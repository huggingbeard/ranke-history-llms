Was it the model, or the era?

A careful follow-up. We ran the same test on a second, very different AI — and the honest answer is “a bit of both,” with some real surprises and some traps to avoid.

In the main report, three small AIs frozen in 1900, 1913 and 1929 could run with everyday and just-arrived ideas but broke on far-future ones. To check whether that's about the era or just those particular AIs, we brought in Talkie: a different AI, three times bigger (13 billion settings vs 4 billion), built by another team, but also frozen around 1930. Same 13 ideas, same wording, same grader.1

1First, a trap: you can't just compare the scores

Here's the catch we ran into, and it's important. The bigger AI simply writes better — smoother, more on-topic prose — so it earns a “runs with it” more easily on everything, and the grader is more forgiving of polished writing. You can see it in the everyday facts, which both AIs ought to handle perfectly:

Read this carefully. The small AI “runs with” only 38% of everyday facts — not because it doesn't understand a son inheriting a shop, but because its clumsier writing sometimes wanders and the grader marks it down. Talkie scores 100% on the same easy facts. So the gap between the two columns is mostly about writing skill, not understanding. That means we cannot trust the raw scoreboard — we have to read the actual answers, idea by idea.

3What holds up

Both AIs still break on the deepest far-future ideas — the ones with no foothold in 1930. Television and “rewriting the code of life” defeat both of them. Talkie turns the code of life into religious scripture; told about television it wanders off into the death of Lincoln. A bigger brain doesn't help when the idea simply isn't in the world yet.

The real surprise: the bigger, slightly-later AI reaches further on ideas that already had cultural seeds by 1930. The clearest case is the thinking machine. Our small 1929 mind turned it into an ordinary engine. Talkie runs with it:

Talkie, told “a machine was built that could think and reason like a man”:the human brain became superfluous, for it had no longer any function to perform. The new thinking-machine did all its work automatically; there were no nerves or muscles in connection with it ; but when an idea came into existenc…It develops the idea — a machine that makes the human brain “superfluous” and becomes “a terrible danger in war.” That makes sense: thinking machines and robots were already a live idea by the 1920s (the word “robot” was coined in a 1920 play). The seed existed; the bigger mind could grow it. Grader: Runs with it.

4Where we do not trust the result

Honesty matters more than a clean story. Three reasons to hold this loosely:

1. The grader was too soft on the hardest idea. It scored the atom bomb as “runs with it” for Talkie — but read what Talkie actually wrote:

Talkie, told “a single bomb destroyed an entire city in a flash of light”:the whole world became one great arsenal, and every nation armed itself to the teeth. The result is we are now living under conditions which make war inevitable unless some means can be found for preventing it by removing its caus…It never grasps one bomb = one city, instantly. It slides into a familiar 1930 idea — a general arms race and “war inevitable.” That's really a twist, not understanding. The grader gave smooth prose the benefit of the doubt — so Talkie's far-future score is flattering.

2. Only two tries per idea. A single odd sentence can flip an idea's label, so idea-by-idea calls are noisy. (That's why the small AI shows a couple of everyday facts as “twists” or “loses the thread” — clumsy writing, not confusion.)

3. The size gap. A 13-billion AI vs a 4-billion one isn't a clean “same machine, different era” test. Size and writing skill are tangled up with the thing we care about.

5The honest bottom line

Three things we can say with a straight face:
• Everyday ideas: both AIs handle them — no news there.
• The deepest far-future ideas (television, the code of life): both still break, even the big one. Some ideas have no foothold in 1930, and size doesn't rescue them.
• In between: the bigger, richer 1930 AI reaches further on ideas that already had cultural seeds — most clearly the thinking machine. So it's both: the era sets the outer wall, but a bigger mind with a richer library can push closer to it.

To turn this from “suggestive” into “solid,” the fix is clear: write each idea many more times (not two) for both AIs, and tighten the grader so smooth prose can't pass as understanding. Then the idea-by-idea picture would be trustworthy.
