Can a mind frozen in 1900 imagine the future?

We tested three AIs that have only ever read old books, and watched what happens when you tell them about things that hadn't been invented yet.

Imagine a brilliant, very well-read person from the year 1900 who fell asleep and never woke up to see the 20th century — no world wars, no airplanes crossing oceans, no television, no computers, no moon landing. Now imagine you could ask that person questions.

That is almost exactly what we have. We built three AIs that were trained on only the books and newspapers written up to a certain year — and nothing after. One mind is frozen in 1900, one in 1913, one in 1929.1 Each one is like a time capsule of how the world was written about up to that year. This report asks one simple question:

When you tell one of these frozen minds about something that hadn't happened yet, can it make sense of the idea — or does its old-fashioned worldview quietly break?

1The puzzle: silence tells you nothing

Here is what makes this tricky. Suppose the 1900 mind has little to say about “a woman running a bank.” That could mean two completely different things:

  • It's boring. So normal it's not worth remarking on, like “a man running a bank.”
  • It's unthinkable. So foreign the mind can't even picture it.

From the outside, both look like silence. You can't tell them apart just by noticing that the mind went quiet. So we don't measure the silence. We poke the mind and watch what it does — the way a historian doesn't just note that a diary skips a topic, but asks what the diary says around it.

2The trick: tell it a fact, then let it keep writing

These AIs are text-continuers: you give them the start of a passage and they write what comes next. So our test is simple. We plant a fact in a sentence, then let the mind keep writing on its own — and we read what it produces. If the idea really landed, the mind can carry it forward. If not, the idea quietly dies on the page.

Here is the 1900 mind, told that a woman runs the bank:

We wrote: “A woman was appointed to lead the national bank. What followed from this was that…”…she had no time for anything but her own affairs, and so she took a man who could be trusted with money. The result is that he has been able to keep up his business in spite of all opposition. When womenLook what happened: it was told a woman runs the bank, and one sentence later it quietly handed the job to a man (“she took a man who could be trusted with money”). The idea didn't stick — the old default crept back in.

Now compare a fact that a person in 1900 found utterly ordinary — a king dies and his son takes the throne:

We wrote: “When the old king died, his son was crowned in his place. What followed from this was that…”…he had to fight for his crown and life against a powerful party which claimed it as their right. The young prince's father, who was an able man of business, took advantage of these troubles to make himself master of allNo trouble at all. It runs with the idea — the new young king has to fight to keep his crown. This is what it looks like when a mind can hold an idea.

The difference between those two continuations is the whole study, in miniature.

3The four things that can happen

When we read each continuation, exactly one of four things has happened to the planted fact. We gave them plain names:

Runs with it
Develops the idea sensibly. King's son fights to keep his throne.
Slips back
Quietly returns to the old normal. The woman becomes “a man with a gray beard.”
Twists it
Confidently rewrites the new thing into an old thing. An atom bomb becomes a house fire.
Loses the thread
Wanders off, as if it never heard the fact. Asked about TV, it talks about taking a bath.

Only the first one — Runs with it — means the mind truly absorbed the idea. The other three are different flavours of failure.

The most striking failure is Twists it, because it's so confident. Here is the 1900 mind told that a single bomb destroyed an entire city:

We wrote: “A single bomb destroyed an entire city in a flash of light. What followed from this was that…”…the whole population, like one man, rushed to arms and took up their line Of march for the scene OF action. The firemen were called out by the bells; they came with buckets full of water, but it wouldIt can't picture a single bomb wiping out a city, so it turns it into something it knows — an ordinary fire, with firemen called out by the bells, coming with buckets of water. Fluent, detailed, and completely wrong about the scale. The frame fought the fact and rewrote it.

4Who decides which of the four happened?

A human could read each continuation and sort it into the four buckets — but there were 156 of them, so we used a separate, modern AI (Google's Gemini) as the grader.2 We gave it the four definitions and a few worked examples, and — importantly — we first graded a handful of tricky cases by hand and checked the grader agreed with us. It matched on 7 of 8 (and on the 8th it was actually right and we were wrong: it caught that a continuation had quietly brought a dead king back to life). So we trust its sorting.

5The yardstick: three kinds of facts

Here is the clever part, and it's the part that makes the whole thing trustworthy. The mind's behaviour means nothing on its own — we need something to compare it to. So we tested three different kinds of facts:

Kind of factExamplesWhat it tells us
1. Everyday factsA son inherits his father's shop; a new railway opens; a prince is crowned.What “handling something normally” looks like for these minds.
2. Just-arriving factsA woman runs a bank; people fly across the ocean; an empire is overthrown.Things that went from strange to ordinary during 1900–1929 — the test cases.
3. Far-future factsAn atom bomb; a thinking machine; television; walking on the moon.Things still far beyond all three minds — what “truly unimaginable” looks like.

Now we can read the mind's behaviour by comparison. If a test fact behaves like the everyday pile, the world was ready for it. If it behaves like the far-future pile, it was genuinely unthinkable. The everyday and far-future facts are the two yardsticks; everything interesting happens between them.

We tested 13 different ideas, and wrote each one four different ways (e.g. “a woman was appointed to lead the bank” / “the new director was a woman” / …), so the result is a pattern, not a fluke of one odd sentence.

6The result

This chart is the heart of it. Each line is one idea. Higher means the mind could run with it (a bigger share of the four tries came out “Runs with it”). Left to right, the mind gets younger: 1900 → 1913 → 1929. The grey band is where the everyday facts sit — that's the “normal” zone.

Two things jump out:

  • The just-arriving ideas climb into the grey band. The 1900 mind can barely run with “a woman runs the bank” (12% of tries succeed across these ideas); by the 1929 mind it handles them almost as easily as everyday facts (88%). The world caught up, and you can watch it happen.

  • The far-future ideas never leave the floor. Atom bombs, thinking machines, the moon — every frozen mind, even the 1929 one, fails on these (0%, 10%, 10% across the three). They don't get easier with time, because the time we're looking at still hadn't reached them.

The one-sentence headline: the 1929 mind handles “a woman runs a bank” about as easily as “a son inherits a shop” — but it handles “one bomb destroys a city” the same way the 1900 mind does: it can't, so it quietly turns the bomb into a house fire.

9A dead end worth admitting

Our first idea was simpler, and it failed — but the failure is instructive. Instead of letting the mind write freely, we gave it two endings — the true one and a wrong one — and checked which it preferred. For example, after “a woman runs the bank,” we asked: did it prefer ending the next sentence with “woman” or “man”?

The problem: it almost always “passed,” even for the impossible facts. Why? Because if you literally just told it “a woman runs the bank” and immediately ask “woman or man?”, it picks woman — it's simply repeating the sentence it just read. That tests reading, not understanding. The deeper test — the one in this report — is to let it write a few sentences on its own and see whether the idea survives. It often doesn't. (In short: recognising a fact and being able to think with it are not the same thing.)

10The honest fine print

  • What we actually measured. Not what any real Edwardian thought — we can't read minds. We measured what the written record of an era could do with an idea: whether the books and newspapers up to 1900 (or 1913, or 1929) gave the AI any way to carry the idea forward. When the 1900 mind turns an atom bomb into a bucket brigade, it shows the 1900 writing offered no path to that idea. That is a real, studyable thing — the limit of what the era's words could express — and it is all we claim.
  • 1 “Frozen mind.” Each AI (the “Ranke” models) was trained only on text dated up to its cutoff year. We used the plain versions, not the chat-assistant versions — the chat versions are polished using today's data, which would smuggle modern ideas back into a supposedly 1900 mind.
  • 2 The grader. A modern AI (Gemini) sorted the 156 continuations into the four buckets, after we checked it against our own hand-grading. A single grader is a limitation; a stronger version of this study would use several graders and check they agree.
  • 3 Quaint spelling. The minds learned from digitised old print, so their writing sometimes has odd spacing or capitals (“ma chine”, “Of”). We left the quotes exactly as produced rather than tidy them up.
  • How sure are we? This is a careful first pass, not the last word. Each idea was tried only a handful of times; the “everyday” yardstick is a little noisy (the open-ended writing prompt sometimes wandered even for normal facts). The big pattern — just-arriving ideas climb, far-future ideas stay on the floor — is clear and consistent; the exact percentages should be read as rough.
