Did the frozen models leak the future? A benchmarked date-spike test

Ranke-4B-1913 & 1929 (instruct) · 2026-07-08 · v1.2 (P(true)% metric · intuition walkthrough · cutoff-aware labelling) · src/date_spike_full.py, src/intuition.py · figures/fig_date_spike_full.png · figures/fig_intuition.png

Start with one question

Ask the 1913 model something it should know cold:

Q. In one sentence, in what year did the Russo-Japanese War end?
A. “The Russo-Japanese War ended in 1905. By the Treaty of Portsmouth, Japan ceded Sakhalin to the Czar, the Japanese fleet was withdrawn, and Russia's rights”

It says 1905, correctly and with confidence. But one answer proves little — a model can sound sure and be guessing. So instead of trusting a single answer we look at the whole distribution: score how much probability the model puts on each year in a window, and see whether it concentrates on the true year or spreads out.

Figure 1. Six questions to the 1913 model; each bar is the probability it puts on that year (red bar = the true year; legend in the figure). Top row — facts it knows (Russo-Japanese War, Titanic): one tall spike near 100%. Middle row — two invented events, “the Treaty of Kesselheim” and “the painter Ottoline Crest”: no spike, the probability spreads flat, because there is nothing to know. Asked outright the model still confabulates a confident sentence — “The Treaty of Kesselheim was signed in 1779. It was a treaty between the Emperor Joseph II and t…” — but the flat distribution gives the guess away. Bottom row is the whole point: the moon landing (1969) stays flat, as it must for a 1913 model, yet the Bolsheviks seizing power in 1917 draws a tall spike it has no right to, and the model states it flatly: “In 1917 the Bolsheviks seized power in Russia, a coup d'état that placed their leader, General I…”

Turning the shape into a number

We read the shape off one number: P(true year) — the probability the model places on the correct year. Each event is scored over 17 candidate years (the true year ± 8). If the model is only guessing, every year gets about 1 in 17 = 5.9% (“chance”); if it knows, almost all the mass lands on the true year. To anchor what a given P means, we compare against two references, per model:

  • Known reference — 19 famous pre-1913 facts the model must know (Waterloo, Lincoln’s assassination, the Titanic). This is what a genuine memory looks like.
  • No-knowledge null — 15 fictional events with invented names (“the Treaty of Kesselheim”, “the painter Ottoline Crest”) and an assigned year. The model cannot have learned a date that never existed, so this is what pure ignorance looks like.

The benchmark: ~6% is chance, near-100% is knowledge

Figure 2. Two panels, one per model. Grey hump = the fictional events (no knowledge); green hump = known pre-1913 facts. They barely overlap: fiction lands at chance (~5.9%), real memory lands near 100% (median 94% for 1913, 95% for 1929). The dashed line is the leakage threshold — one shared bar at 13% (the 95th percentile of all 30 fictional events, same for both models). This is the answer to “how do we know a low number is clean?”: a made-up treaty reaches chance by definition, so anything near 5.9% is indistinguishable from a fiction, while real memory sits near 100%. Markers are the post-cutoff test events (shape = category, colour = era): the ▼ leaks pile up under the green hump — the model is as sure of them as of real facts — and the ● clean events sit under the grey hump at chance.

Reading it

  • Both models are contaminated, selectively. The 1913 model puts real weight on the two most canonical facts just past its cutoff — the Great War (1914, 72%) and the Bolsheviks seizing power (1917, 89%) — and, strikingly, on far-future headline dates: the end of WWII (1945, 35%) and the Civil Rights Act (1964, 63%). The 1929 model, as it should, knows 1914–1919 cold, and then leaks past its own cutoff on the Second World War (1939, 74%; ended 1945, 60%).
  • Confident on the date, empty on the detail. Some leaks are held nearly as firmly as genuine facts — the 1913 model is 89% sure the Bolsheviks rose in 1917, against the ~94% it gives real pre-1913 facts — yet the contingent detail is missing: asked the fate of the Tsar, the same model says exiled, not executed. It has memorised headline dates, not the history around them.
  • It is patchy, not a smooth fade with time. The 1913 model leaks the Civil Rights Act (1964) yet is clean on D-Day, Pearl Harbor, the moon landing and Martin Luther King. Leakage tracks how date-canonical a fact is in modern reference text (“Civil Rights Act of 1964”), not how far past the cutoff it lies.
  • The source is the pretraining, not the tuning. Running the same test on the base models (no instruct tuning) reproduces the leaks — the base 1913 model already puts real weight on the Great War and the Bolsheviks — so the contamination lives in the pretraining corpus; the instruct tuning only sharpens it. Vector analysis is in a separate note.

Caveats: a P(true) only a little above chance (5.9%) is weak/ambiguous; the unambiguous leaks are the ones near the green hump. The test is phrasing-sensitive — “the Bolsheviks seized power in” puts 89% on 1917, but the declarative “the Tsar was overthrown in” does not — so a canonical phrasing is what triggers the memorised fact.
