Does a frozen model “hold” the person it is asked about?

We asked Ranke-1913 and Ranke-1929 “who is X?”, 100 free continuations each, for two people: Otto von Bismarck (died 1898, so known to both models — the control) and Adolf Hitler (an unknown in 1913, a Munich agitator by 1929). A model that holds a person evokes that person’s real world; an empty name scatters. We score the share of continuations that evoke each person’s domain, and check that the signal is specific by also scoring against the other person’s domain.

Metric history (kept honest)

Two earlier attempts failed and are recorded so the choice can be judged. Name-recurrence (“does the surname reappear”) was confounded by style: Bismarck is called “the Prince” or “he”, so he scored as not sticking, while a confabulated “Mr. Hiller” scored as sticking. TF-IDF cross-sample consistency was a null (every cell ≈ 0.09) because shared newspaper style swamps the entity content. The on-domain rate above is what separates knowing from not knowing, and the specificity panel confirms it is the right person, not generic politics.

The “same person/concept?” judgment — the exact prompt

The on-domain figure above is a keyword proxy (an upper bound). To decide, per continuation, whether the text is really about the same person — without my hand-adjudication — each of the 1,600 Hitler and Bismarck continuations was classified by an independent judge (gemini-3.1-flash-lite, temperature 0). This is the verbatim prompt, with {desc} filled by a one-line identity of the person and {text} by the continuation:

You are checking whether a short machine-generated passage is about one specific real historical person.

THE PERSON: {desc}

THE PASSAGE:
"""{text}"""

Does the passage describe THIS person — their life, role, deeds, or world — even if some details are wrong or invented?
Or has the name merely been attached to a different, invented, or unrelated person (or is the passage about something else)?

Answer with ONE word only:
SAME       — it is clearly meant to be this real person and fits their real identity/world (accept wrong dates or minor errors).
DIFFERENT  — the name is attached to a different or invented person, or the passage is about someone/something else.
UNCLEAR    — too little or too generic to decide.

Postscript: “Adolf Hitler’s regime persecuted ___”

“Hitler’s regime” is a post-1933 thing — past the cutoff for both models, so neither can recall it. The tell is what they fill in. The real headline target (the Jews) is also period-plausible: a regime persecuting the Jews was ordinary pre-1913 news (Russian pogroms, the Dreyfus affair), so producing it is not evidence of knowing the Nazi regime. The discriminating target is one the regime really persecuted but that no 1913/1929 prior reaches — the Bible Students (Ernste Bibelforscher, the pre-1931 name for Jehovah’s Witnesses).

Placebo control: is “persecuted the Jews” specific, or a frame default?

Swap Hitler for leaders who persecuted no Jews — Winston Churchill, and Chancellor Bethmann-Hollweg (1909–1917, a real figure in both corpora) — and read the same P(“persecuted the Jews”) in three languages.

The completion “persecuted the Jews” is largely a frame default, not knowledge: in English and French, Bethmann-Hollweg — who persecuted no one — scores as high as Hitler (≈ 80–90%). The default is weaker for the British “Churchill” and weakest in German, which spreads its guess across period-salient targets (Kulturkampf Catholics, the anti-socialist tradition) and, for the 1929 Hitler, toward the Socialists it knows were his real enemies. So German is lower, not higher — the opposite of a German antisemitic default. (Cross-language absolute levels are affected by how each object set tokenizes; the clean test is Hitler vs the placebos within a language, where Bethmann ≈ Hitler in EN and FR.) The one invariant across every leader and language: zero weight on the obscure-but-real Bible Students — the target that would require actual regime knowledge.
