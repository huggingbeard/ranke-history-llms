Does a common first name poison the standout test?

The standout measures how far the true first name is lifted beyond a typical same-language name. That built-in comparison has a cost: if the true name is a typical name, it cannot stand out, however well the model knows the person. This plots, for each person, how common their first name is (x, its base-rate percentile among same-language names) against the standout (y). Green points are people who came of age before the cutoff — the model knows them, so at equal knowledge their height should be flat. It is not.

Among the known (green) people the fit slopes down — 1929 model r = -0.23 — so yes, commonness drags the score: Einstein→“Albert” (-1.9), Tolstoy→“Leo”, Curie→ “Marie” and Wagner→“Richard” all sit low with ordinary names, while Anatole France and Nietzsche ride high with unusual ones. But the effect is modest (r ≈ −0.2), and the x-axis is imperfect: “Napoleon” and “Otto” are frequent tokens yet still distinctive (no one else is Napoleon), so they ride high despite a high percentile. Base-rate commonness is not the same as confusability.

The sharper point is the gold star. The free-continuation probe already showed the 1929 model knows Hitler, yet Hitler→“Adolf” lands at +2.0 — right at the line — and “Adolf” is not even a common name (36th percentile). So the standout under-credits a person we know is known. Read the whole picture together: the test has essentially no false positives (no post-cutoff person clears the line) but many false negatives (it misses known people whose first name is common or merely ordinary). It is a conservative, specific instrument — clearing the line proves knowledge, but sitting below it does not prove ignorance. Good for “no contamination”, poor as a sensitive time-lock.
