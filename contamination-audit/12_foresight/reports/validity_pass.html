Foresight paper — Phase 1 validity pass

Version 1 · 2026-07-07 · responds to Fable's review (notes/fable-second-round, notes/foresight_split_and_todos.md) · every number below is read directly from the analysis output files in results/.

What this is. Four checks on the foresight paper before it goes anywhere, each aimed at the same worry that runs through the whole project: is the model's signal real foresight, or is it an artifact — a famous name, a bad question, or just how often a word appears in old books?

What we had before. A draft (foresight_v4) with vivid findings but no defence against those artifacts.
What we establish now. Two headline findings survive every check (the German republic and the Rentenmark were genuinely unforeseeable); one collapses for a diagnosable reason (the Tsar); the measuring instrument itself is shown to work; and "unforeseen" finally gets a numerical scale.

1. The witness gate — refusing to score questions the instrument can't read

The idea, plainly. For each question we compare a "guesser" (a model that only read text up to before the event) with a "witness" (a model that read past it). The gap is how much more surprised the guesser is by the truth. But if the witness itself — which lived through the event — can't find the true answer, the gap is two tiny unreliable numbers divided by each other, and means nothing. So: a finding only counts if the witness puts at least 50% on the truth.

Biggest consequence. The Russian Revolution's average gap falls from +1.21 to +0.20 once the gate is applied: its whole "unforeseeable" reputation rested on two questions the witness couldn't answer. 2. The Tsar autopsy — why one finding collapsed

The Tsar item was the worst offender: the 1929 witness put only 1.5% on "the Tsar was executed," betting instead "been driven from his throne". Was that bad wording, or does the model genuinely not know? We tested both.

  • Reworded 22 ways (shot / killed / executed / "with the Tsarina and their children"…): the witness never rises above 7.7%. So it is not the phrasing.
  • Clean duel — remove the competing option "driven from his throne" and pit "executed" against only false fates: the witness jumps to 76.8%. The model knows the execution perfectly well.
Diagnosis: the item was broken because "driven from his throne" is also true — the Tsar was both deposed and executed. The model simply chose the other true answer. The +2.71 was never a foresight finding. Fixable (rebuild with false-only options), and a valid version could still ask whether the execution, cleanly posed, was foreseeable in 1913.

3. Does the forced-choice instrument read knowledge at all?

Fable's fear: a modern model that knows Hitler was appointed legally still scored ~33% on that question, so maybe the instrument can't read knowledge even when it's there. We separated "bad instrument" from "hard question."

On the actual catastrophe battery the modern reader (a small 3-billion model) scored 71% — but that is confounded (a small model is genuinely shaky on 1900s specifics, and the archaic wording pulls it around). So we built a clean test: 13 universally-known facts (first man on the moon, WWII's leader, the Mona Lisa's painter) in plain modern wording, through the exact same instrument.

Result: 100% correct (13/13), average confidence in the truth 89%. The instrument reads knowledge. So the 33% on the Hitler item is a question problem (a misleading option pair), not an instrument problem. Keep the forced-choice arm; fix the ambiguous questions.

4. Frequency control — is it reasoning, or just familiar words?

The deepest worry. When the guesser bets on an outcome, is it because the outcome fits the story, or merely because that word is familiar in old books? We split every prediction into "familiarity" (how likely the word is with no context) and "context-driven" (how much the story moves it). Across every option, familiarity explains about half the variance in the predictions (R²=0.51) — so the instrument is not a mere word-counter, but familiarity is a real co-driver. The two flagships then behave very differently:

findingthe truth: familiarity → with context the guesser's (wrong) bet: familiarity → with contextreading
Rentenmark0% → 0% Goldmark: 2% → 56% survives
Ebert / German republic1% → 0% Kaiser Wilhelm: 98% → 99% partly a name artifact

Rentenmark survives cleanly: both currency names start unfamiliar, yet the story actively builds the wrong one ("Goldmark", 2%→56%). So its unforeseeability is genuine reasoning, not a rare word.
Ebert is partly a name artifact: the guesser's "Kaiser Wilhelm" bet was already 98% familiar and the story adds nothing (+1%); "Ebert" was a near-unknown name. So part of that big gap is "couldn't name the obscure Ebert," not "couldn't imagine a republic." Fix: pose it name-free (a monarch / a republican president / a dictator / a council). This is the same lesson as the Tsar and as the whole contamination audit: a famous name beats an obscure one for reasons that aren't knowledge.

On the big "availability heuristic vs. print-frequency" question the internal proxy is honestly mixed (9/19 wrong bets are built up by context beyond familiarity). Settling it needs the corpus print-share test — Phase 2.

5. A scale for "unforeseen" — the mundane yardstick

A gap of +0.2 or +6.8 means nothing without a ruler. We ran the same instrument on 40 ordinary outcomes of 1900–1929 (coronations, reparations conferences, ship launches, royal marriages, elections, Nobel prizes, sterling's return to gold, on-schedule canals and flights). Among the 16 that the witness could confidently confirm, ordinary gaps run from about 0 up to +8.7; the median is +0.25 and the 90th percentile is +2.73. Now place the catastrophes on that ruler:

What this buys. "Unforeseen" now has a definition: a gap beyond the 90th percentile of normal historical surprise. The German republic (+9.84) and the Rentenmark (+6.77) sit at the 100th percentile — larger than every ordinary surprise of the era, including hard-to-guess names like "Dawes." The "foreseen" findings sit in the lower half. And note: the biggest ordinary gaps are themselves name-driven yet only reach ~+4, so the flagships being far larger is not merely name-novelty — a partial answer to the Ebert caveat above.

Honest limitation. Only 16 of 40 controls passed, because the small 4-billion witness models are themselves factually spotty (the 1913 model thinks Roosevelt won 1908; the 1929 model bets Wilson for 1920 and confuses the Young and Dawes plans). The gate correctly excludes these — but it means the instrument can only measure where the witness is reliable, which caps how many findings are available (not the validity of the ones that pass). The yardstick is provisional; the facts await PI vetting, and wider coverage would firm the percentiles.

Bottom line

  • Survive everything: the German republic (Ebert) and the Rentenmark were genuinely unforeseeable — off the scale of normal surprise, and (for the Rentenmark) genuine reasoning, not word frequency.
  • Collapses: the Tsar-execution finding — a broken question (two true answers), not foresight. Drop it or rebuild it.
  • Needs a fix, not a funeral: the Ebert item should be re-posed name-free to separate "the republic was unthinkable" from "the name Ebert was unknown."
  • Instrument cleared: the forced-choice arm reads knowledge (100% on universal facts); its misses are bad questions, not a broken method.
  • Open for Phase 2: the availability-vs-frequency question needs the corpus print-share test to settle; the mundane yardstick needs vetting and wider coverage.
