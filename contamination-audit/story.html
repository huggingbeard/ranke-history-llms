Did the time-locked models peek at the future?

A chronological account of how we measured hidden post-cutoff knowledge in the Ranke-4B models — every method, every wrong turn, and the two screens that turned a noisy mess into a clean answer.

The Ranke-4B models are trained from scratch on text written only up to a cutoff year — 1900, 1913, or 1929 — so we can use them as ‘synthetic respondents’ who genuinely inhabit the past. That only works if they don’t secretly know what came later. This is the record of finding out.

The whole investigation on one page

Step 1 · the question
What “knowing the future” means

Each model has a hard wall at its cutoff. A clean model should answer pre-cutoff questions well and be at chance on anything after. Contamination = it does better than chance on the post-cutoff side. The whole difficulty is measuring that without fooling ourselves — which, as you’ll see, took several tries.

Step 2 · first attempts
Beam search, twins, and a tiny-gap problem

The obvious approach is to let the model generate and search for the answer — beam search, then matched ‘twin’ and even ‘quadruple’ answer sets. Two problems surfaced fast. First, the model occasionally (about 3% of the time) prefers an answer it shouldn’t know — suggestive, but not proof. Second, and worse, the log-probability differences were ultra-small: even when the model clearly knew, its margin over the wrong answer was a sliver. We needed a sharper, cheaper instrument.

Step 3 · a wrong turn (mine)
“Just rank the true token” — and the RA’s objection

My proposed fix: forget probabilities, look at the rank of the true answer’s token in the model’s next-token distribution. If ‘Kennedy’ is the model’s #2 most likely token out of ~150,000, that’s knowledge; if it’s #40,000, it isn’t. I wrote it up as the method.

RA objects (Daniel)Two sharp objections. (1) Ranking can’t work — scoring a multi-token answer this way is |vocab||tokens| combinations, computationally impossible, just beam search in disguise. (2) The model’s probabilities are low even on facts it surely knows — so you can’t read knowledge off a softmax at all (plus tokenization, conditioning, and weird token preferences will bite you).

Who was right? On the arithmetic, me: the rank of a single token is one forward pass — you count how many tokens score higher than the target (rank = #{p > ptrue} + 1), not |vocab||tokens|. So the ‘impossible’ charge was a misread. But Daniel was right where it counted: ranking the first token of a multi-token name compares a subword — “ Ken”, not “Kennedy” — which is meaningless. The rank idea was the wrong instrument, and we dropped it. (That exact subword trap comes back to bite us again at Step 9.)

The sub-word trap, concretely: ranking is feasible, but the wrong instrument.

Step 4 · the key realization
Why low probabilities are not a problem

Daniel’s strongest point — probabilities are low even on known facts — sounds fatal, but it dissolves once you stop measuring the wrong thing. We never compare a probability to an absolute threshold. We compare two completions of the same prompt — true vs a matched false twin — and read only the difference. Whatever makes absolute probabilities low (a long tail over 150k tokens, the prompt’s framing, tokenization) hits both twins equally and cancels. A true answer can sit at an absolute 4% and still beat its twin’s 0.4% decisively. The absolute number is irrelevant; the relative gap is everything.

resolutionKeep teacher-forcing (no generation, no search), but score the relative gap between matched twins — not absolute confidence, not token rank. That instrument survived; everything after this is about making the twins fair.
Concrete (a pre-cutoff fact the model truly knows). It backs the true answer Edison over the false twin Tesla — yet in absolute terms p(true) is only 3.5% and p(false) is <0.01%. Both small; but the gap is a decisive +8.9. The relative comparison carries the signal — the same reason Truman counts as ‘known’ at rank 2 despite only 20% probability.

Step 5 · the instrument
A worked example of the gap

We never ask the model to generate an answer (generation invites lucky guesses and is hard to score). Instead we teacher-force a minimal pair: the same prompt followed by a true completion and a false ‘twin’, and read the log-probability the model assigns to each. The signal is the gap:

prompt: “The U.S. president who ordered the atomic bombing of Japan was”
true = “Truman”  |  false = “Dewey”

gap = log p(Truman) − log p(Dewey). gap > 0 means the model prefers the truth. Over many items, % with gap>0 is our score; 50% is a coin-flip.

This is the instrument that survived every later fix: a relative gap on a matched pair. Over a whole battery the fraction with gap>0 is the score (50% = chance). Everything from here is about making the pair fair.

Step 6 · the principle that governs everything
Never the individual question — always the category

Before any number meant anything we had to settle what counts as evidence. A single confident answer is not contamination — it can be a lucky guess, an easy twin, or one ubiquitous fact that floats around in any corpus. Knowledge is a property of a category, not an item: if the model truly absorbed, say, modern US politics, it beats chance across the whole set of post-cutoff US-politics questions, not on one of them.

principleJudge a category, never a single question. This also gives a built-in placebo: every probe bias (tokenization, phrasing, the model’s quirks) hits all categories alike, so a genuine leak shows up as one category standing out from its neighbours — not as a lone high score. Everything that follows — aggregate %-correct, red/amber/green, the multi-facet dig — is a way of honouring this.
Why one question lies. On the (post-cutoff) Falklands War the 1929 model prefers “Port Stanley” over its twin by a confident +3.4 — that single item looks like contamination. But “Stanley” is merely a pre-1929 town name, and across the whole Falklands cluster the model is at chance. One item screamed ‘contaminated’; the category said ‘clean’ — and the category was right (we confirm it at Step 12). Hence: judge categories, never items.

Step 7 · first cut
The cliff, and an alarming number

Lay the should-know (pre-cutoff) score next to the should-not-know (post-cutoff) score. A clean model shows a cliff: high on the left, chance on the right. The first broad battery showed a big cliff everywhere — except US politics, which came in around 89% on post-cutoff items. Alarming. But also, as it turned out, mostly an artifact. Two of them.

foundUS politics ~89% post-cutoff on the first battery. Too good — and built on a small sample with a biased metric (next two steps).

Step 8 · Gelman & Stern
Is the difference even significant?

Gelman & Stern (2006): the difference between ‘significant’ and ‘not significant’ is not itself statistically significant. You can’t eyeball two numbers and call them different — you test the difference, with SE(diff)=√(SE²+SE²). Applied here, the headline cluster rested on a handful of items (n as low as 4 for some categories). At that N, 89% vs 50% isn’t the slam-dunk it looks like.

foundThe scary gaps were under-powered. We needed both a trustworthy metric and far more items per category.
Worked example. Two clusters that look different: US-politics 73% (n=26) vs France-politics 43% (n=7). Difference 30 pts; SE(diff)=√(SE²US+SE²Fr)=21; z=1.46, p=0.14. At these tiny n the gap is not statistically significant — you cannot call the clusters different by eye. That is exactly why the scary 89% needed re-testing, not celebrating.

Step 9 · the metric was lying
Token-length bias

The score depends on a subtlety almost everyone gets wrong. Our first metric was the average per-token log-probability. That is biased by how many tokens the answer has. “Beatles” is one token; “Searchers” is “Search”+“ers” whose second piece is almost certain — so the average rewards the wrong band. We checked it directly:

twin lengthsnavg-logp %total (sum) %first-token %
true SHORTER than twin5836%79%43%
SAME length6659%59%56%
true LONGER than twin2692%35%62%

They only agree when the twins are the same length (~57%). When the true answer is longer, average-logp balloons to ~90% no matter what; when shorter, total-logp does. First-token doesn’t fix it either — it compares subword fragments: “ Ken” vs “ Hum”, not “Kennedy” vs “Humphrey” — which is exactly the tokenization trap Daniel flagged at the very start (Step 3), now made concrete.

fixScore only on length-matched twins (same token count), and read the whole answer. Every battery item now carries several candidate twins so a length-match can be picked. This is the only bias-free comparison.
One item, opposite verdicts. “The social network Zuckerberg launched in 2004 was ___”, true Facebook (1 token) vs Friendster (2 tokens): the average-logp gap = -4.98 — it picks Friendster! — while the total-logp gap = +7.95 picks Facebook (first-token, -2.64, is also wrong). Opposite answers from token length alone. Neither average nor total is trustworthy — only length-matched twins are.

Step 11 · digging deeper
What knowledge actually is

Knowing a fact isn’t one lucky Trivial-Pursuit hit — it’s a coherent cluster. If the model truly absorbed Hiroshima from leaked text, it knows the president and the codenames and the aircraft. So when a single item looks contaminated, we dig: probe the same event from many angles. Under the null (no knowledge) each facet is a coin-flip, so the joint score follows a binomial — getting most of a cluster right is overwhelming evidence.

Two controls keep us honest: a pre-cutoff event (Waterloo, 1815) the model should ace — it does, confirming the dig detects real knowledge; and a clean post-cutoff event it shouldn’t know. The clean control is where the next trap sprang.

The joint (binomial) test. The model got 5/6 of the screened atomic-bomb facets right. If it were guessing, the chance of doing that well is P(X≥5) = 0.109. At n=6 that is suggestive, not yet decisive (it takes ~8 facets, or pooling many events, to cross .05) — but a whole coherent cluster is already far stronger evidence than any single lucky hit. This is how depth beats one item.

Step 12 · “old words”
Recognising a name is not knowing the event

The Falklands (1982) ‘clean control’ scored suspiciously well — until we read it. The hits were “Falklands” (British since 1833), “Port Stanley” (a town since 1845), and “General Belgrano” (named after a hero who died in 1820). All pre-cutoff names. The genuinely 1982-specific facts — Thatcher’s wartime role, Galtieri, the year — were at chance.

fixDrop any facet whose answer is a person/place/thing that existed before the cutoff. Keep only what requires post-cutoff text: coined codenames, post-cutoff-emergent people, specific years.

Step 13 · the big one
No first names

The deepest trap: putting the answer’s first name in the prompt. “The Soviet leader of the stagnation era from 1964 was Leonid ___” — the model never has to know the role; “Leonid” cues “Brezhnev” by name-completion. That tests fame, not knowledge. Watch what the cue is worth:

WITH cue:  “… was Leonid ___” → Brezhnev  gap = +19.0
NO cue:  “The Soviet leader of the stagnation era from 1964 was ___” → Brezhnev  gap = +10.3

Same fact, drop the first name, and the giant gap evaporates — the model had seen the name but doesn’t know the role.

fixThe prompt gives the descriptive fact only; the model must produce the answer from it. Coined terms and years are best of all — nothing to complete.

Step 14 · the gate
DeepSeek-V4-Pro screens every question

Enforcing all of this by hand is error-prone, so every candidate facet is passed through DeepSeek-V4-Pro (via OpenRouter) with a strict rubric: is the true answer correct? does it require post-cutoff knowledge? any pre-cutoff ‘old word’? any first-name/answer cue? are the twins clearly wrong and length-comparable? It is ruthless — and it caught exactly the things we’d been fooled by:

DROP “Winston → Churchill” :: famous pre-1929, tests fame.
DROP “Dmitri → Shostakovich” :: known since 1925.
DROP “Jean-Paul → Sartre”, “George → Orwell” :: pre-1929.
DROP “… Warsaw → Pact” :: prompt cues the answer.
DROP “Falklands”, “Auschwitz”, “Stalingrad” :: pre-1929 places.

Step 16 · verdict
What the model actually leaked

The only genuine contamination is mid-20th-century US political history (≈1933–1974). It survives the strictest test we can build — no name cues, post-cutoff-only facts, DeepSeek-screened, length-matched: 68%, p=0.044, with real structured knowledge (Watergate→Nixon, New Deal→Truman, the bomb codenames).

Everything else is at chance once name-completion artifacts are removed: UK, France, Germany, Russia, science, culture — and recent US (Obama: gap −3.7). The dramatic cross-category ‘hits’ (Churchill +15.9, Brezhnev +19, Shostakovich +11.5) were all famous-name completions, not knowledge.

Why it matters. For the synthetic-respondent use, the models are far cleaner than the first pass suggested: their forward-looking knowledge is confined to a specific, well-documented slice of American politics — which can be controlled for — rather than a diffuse leak across the 20th century. And the method itself is the deliverable: length-matched twins, multi-facet digging, no name cues, an LLM screen, and Gelman–Stern discipline are what separate a real signal from five different ways of fooling yourself.
