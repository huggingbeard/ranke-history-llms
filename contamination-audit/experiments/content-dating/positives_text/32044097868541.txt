INTRODUCTION 

Planning for the future development of urban communities has been practiced for centuries, but the establishment of planning boards or commissions in the United States under State enabling legislation started in 1913 when the New York and New Jersey Legislatures each adopted laws authorizing cities and villages to appoint planning commissions and to appropriate money for their work. It was not, however, until thirteen years later that more adequate State legislation of this type was adopted. Here New York led the way with its City, Village, and Town Planning Acts of 

1926 and 1927.

During the interval, zoning in the United States started in the City of New York with its Zoning Resolution of 1916. 

It may be defined as the regulation through the police power of the uses of land and the heights of buildings and the areas and yards about them.

NEED FOR REGULATION BY PLANNING AND ZONING 

The need for such types of regulation has been brought about by the evils of unregulated growth. The more rapid this has been, the more obvious the evil, and the more urgent the problem of finding a remedy for it. Zoning is now generally considered a part of the planning process, but enabling legislation in New York is still differentiated. Likewise the machinery, both for preparing the zoning ordinance and for administering it, is generally distinct from that relating to the planning process. It is logical, however, to deal with both subjects in a single pamphlet such as this, as it is important that there be a coordination of procedures if the objectives common to both are to be reached. In essence, zoning may be said to be an integral part of the wider field of planning. [9]

The need for both planning and zoning powers was first obvious in the urban areas of the State. But the powers provided by the Legislature may be applied to all areas within the State. Such use, however, is permissive and not mandatory. The growth of motor vehicle traffic has brought serious problems to the unincoporated areas of the towns, partly from the spread of residential development into rural areas, and partly from the growth of business along the roadsides of main highways. The latter trend has brought with it blight from billboards, ugly gas stations, and hot-dog and other refreshment stands. So, today, all parts of the State can benefit materially from the proper and efficient use of the powers which the Legislature has made available to them, subject only to their own initiative in setting up local organizations to apply and administer these powers. 

ZONING AND PLANNING PROGRESS 

Although on the basis of area a considerable portion of the State is still without the benefits accruing from the regu lation of the use of land, the heights of buildings, and the extent of open areas about them in the form of yards—and although many communities throughout the State and the public facilities which serve them are being developed without the guidance of a local planning board or commission, nevertheless, on a population basis, a very encouraging picture of planning and zoning progress is presented. 

Excluding the City of New York, 52 per cent of the popu lation of the rest of the State lives in municipalities which have the benefit of official municipal planning boards. If the 

City of New York is included, this percentage rises to 78. 

Similarly, outside the City of New York, 64 per cent of the people of the rest of the State live in communities protected by zoning ordinances. If the City of New York is included, the percentage enjoying the protection of zoning rises to 84. [10]

PRELIMINARY STEPS REQUIRED 

A considerable amount of education of the public is required before official action is taken in either planning or zoning. This is clearly indicated by the way in which zoned areas and municipalities with planning commissions are now concentrated within those areas where regional planning organizations have been active and have engaged in such educational work. 

Unofficial planning committees may well be the first step, as they very often lead naturally to the formation of official planning boards and the appointment of official zoning commissions. The local community, in the form of a neighborhood, is often the place where the need of planning and zoning is most obvious, and community plans formed by local groups of citizens may well serve as the basis for town, village or city planning.

ASSISTANCE IN PROGRAMS FOR PUBLIC WORKS AND SERVICES 

In recent years, extensive programs of public works, financed largely by relief appropriations, have given an I impetus to both planning and zoning. Communities which had comprehensive plans for their future development, involving a program of public works, have been able to make efficient use of such funds and those with zoning ordinances in effect have been protected from the possibility of misfit structures in areas where they did not belong. Adequate zoning has been a prerequisite to financial assistance by the Federal Housing Administration to subdividers undertaking private developments.

COUNTY PLANNING A LATER DEVELOPMENT 

Provision for county planning was made by the State Legislature in 1932, such powers being likewise permissive rather than mandatory. This legislation also provided for the establishment of regional planning boards to serve a group of municipalities or a group of counties where they are con- [ 11 ]

fronted with large-scale problems which extend beyond their separate jurisdictions. The Legislature has, in addition, provided for the adoption by the several counties of optional forms of county government which include provision for county-wide planning agencies. These have also been authorized by special laws applying to Nassau and Westchester where new charters adopted by public referendum provide for the establishment of county planning commissions with broad powers.

County planning may be of use in both metropolitan and rural counties; in the former because of the complexity of the problems involved and the density of population; in the latter because of the difficulty and expense (often involving wasteful duplication) of having each individual town and village make separate arrangements for preparing its plans and ordinances.

SAVINGS VERSUS COSTS 

In both planning and zoning undertakings, there inevitably arises the question of cost. Both procedures are relatively inexpensive as compared with the benefits to be derived from them, but good planning and zoning cannot be achieved without some public expense. When one compares this with the possible savings due to an efficient program of both public and private construction, such planning expenses become almost negligible. As has often been pointed out, the real question is not whether a community can afford to plan and zone but whether it can afford to do without such services and subject itself to the risk of resultant losses. "A City Planning Primer," published in 1928 by the United States Department of Commerce, stated: "The experiences of hundreds of American communities have been carefully analyzed to find out how city planning actually works out in practice. The answer is emphatic Planning pays.' [12]

The same publication stated that: "The cost of preparing such a plan is so reasonable and its advantages are so great that the arguments for it are unanswerable."

By far the largest cost in both planning and zoning lies in the making of initial maps and studies. Continuation studies and plan administration usually require only nominal outlays. 

The initial cost may well be regarded as a long-term investment paying large dividends over a long and indefinite period. [ 13 ]

MUNICIPAL PLANNING PROCEDURES 

Almost identical powers are available to all political subdivisions of New York State which care to avail themselves of the services of planning boards. Due to the fact that the planning powers of cities, villages and towns have been sepa rately provided for, the pertinent statutory provisions will be found in a number of distinct laws. (No attempt will be made in this manual to cover in detail special planning and zoning powers given in special instances by the Legislature to various political subdivisions of the State.)

HISTORY OF PLANNING LEGISLATION 

As already explained, provision for city and village planning commissions was first made in 1913 (Gen. Mun. Law, Article 12-A). The City of Rochester established a City 

Planning Bureau and a City Planning Advisory Board under amendment to its charter adopted in 1917 (Chapter 505). 

The City of Syracuse established a City Planning Commission in 1920 under a special act adopted in that year (Chapter 447), but its commission was reorganized in 1938 under the provisions of a new city charter.

The basis for the present planning powers and procedures, however, was adopted in 1926 (see Appendix). 

Although several city and village planning commissions. were organized under the 1913 legislation, they function now, for the most part, under the broader form of later legislation. 

For this reason, the 1913 legislation, although still available to cities and villages, has not been reproduced in the Appendix. 

In 1927 similar powers were given to towns (see Appendix). 

Relatively minor amendments were made in succeeding years, but the 1938 Legislature adopted substantial amendments to [ 14 ]

all three of these laws giving cities, villages and towns greater control over subdivision plats. (See Appendix.) These amendments were worked out by a joint committee of the State Conference of Mayors and the Association of Towns in cooperation with the Division of State Planning. Some minor adjustments were made in 1939. These clarified the 

Town Law and made uniform in all three laws the provisions regarding changes in zoning district lines.

SUGGESTIONS FOR PLANNING PROCEDURE 

While the procedure of a planning board must conform to the State and municipal legislation under which it operates, a board can profit from the experience of earlier boards and from certain standards of practice that have come to be recognized. The following suggestions are drawn from these sources and are presented as a guide both to citizens interested in planning and to those who have been assigned the task of administering planning in their community. 

PRELIMINARY EDUCATIONAL WORK 

As mentioned in the introductory chapter, considerable educational work is generally necessary before the establishment of an official planning board by municipal ordinance. 

There need not be a great number of backers for such a step at the start. One or two men or women sufficiently interested and sufficiently persuasive can start the ball rolling. Individuals and interested groups can obtain from the Division of State Planning and other readily available sources the necessary information on the values of planning and on its operation in other places.

ORGANIZATION OF THE BOARD 

Planning board members are generally unpaid, although New York City, under its local charter, has a full-time, salaried planning commission. The exceptional circumstances in this case justified a special procedure. 

To be effective, a city, village, or town planning board should be given operating funds in proportion to the size of its job. Its organization and the development of its program of work present some difficulties. 

Its membership should be selected both for broad comprehension of community needs and problems and to some extent for specific knowledge in one or another field of community development. So far as possible, it should be broadly representative of principal community interests. Bearing in mind that in cities and villages two of the five members must be [21]

officials. A good board might, for instance, include among others a real estate man, one or two business men, an architect or engineer, or an outstanding woman. Beyond the above generalizations, however, no general rules can be laid down as to board personnel. Much must depend upon local conditions and possibilities.

The planning board members are not expected to be either technically qualified or in a position to make the plans themselves. This is a technical job which would ordinarily be delegated to a technical staff working under the general direction of the board, or to a professional planning office engaged by the board and working in very close cooperation with it. 

Such assistance should be made available early in the board's operation. Under compulsion of operating for any considerable period of time without funds or technical assistance, the planning board becomes discouraged and ineffectual. 

Headquarters should be provided in a public building and should include space for drafting tables, files, and records and a meeting place for the board.

ELEMENTS OF THE PROGRAM 

In operation, the board will find its work during the initial planning period divided into two parts: 

1. The making of surveys and studies leading to the preparation of a master plan; and 

2. The approval of subdivision plats and the handling of routine matters of business, including consideration of and reporting upon such current planning problems as may be referred to the board by the governing body. 

The board necessarily will be handicapped in making intelligent decisions with respect to these current problems without the guidance of the master plan, still in course of preparation. Nevertheless, as a matter of expediency, it will have to do the best it can with these current considerations until such time as it is better equipped. 

The planning process involves: (1) The survey, or gathering of facts bearing upon the physical development of the [22]

area and trends therein; (2) the analysis of these facts and preparation of a comprehensive plan based thereon which, when adopted, becomes the master plan; (3) keeping the 

F plan before the officials and citizens making important decisions affecting the community's growth, thus promoting the carrying out of the plan.

SURVEY OF EXISTING CONDITIONS 

The first requisite of planning is an adequate base map of the municipality. This is a map of existing conditions and proposals already officially adopted, upon which the various elements of the plan for the future can be shown. It is essentially a street map, but should also show railroads, any other important means of transportation, principal lands in public ownership, and such semi-public lands as cemeteries, airports and country clubs.

The base map should be up-to-date and reasonably accurate. Municipal maps are frequently found to be woefully out of date and surprisingly inaccurate. If this is found to be the case, the officials and the municipal engineer should be consulted and steps taken to secure an accurate and up-todate map.

The size of the map is limited by the size of a normal drawing board. Three feet by six feet is a convenient size to handle. The scales generally used for municipal maps are one hundred feet or multiples of two hundred feet, i.e., one, two. four, six or eight hundred feet to an inch. For most purposes, the scale of the map will depend upon the size of the municipality. If the longest dimension of a municipality is say, two miles, a 200-foot scale makes a convenient sized map. If four miles, a 400-foot scale may be used, etc. For large cities, the base map may be prepared in sections. 

The planning staff may prepare a tracing of a municipal map at a scale suitable for its purposes, but should not be expected to do field surveying for making an original map. 

Prints from the tracing of the base map should be used for showing the salient information about the community. [ 23 ]

MAKING THE MASTER PLAN 

A list of the features to be shown on the master plan has been included in the preceding summary of planning legislation. The first step in its preparation is a careful analysis and appraisal of the facts brought out by the survey of existing conditions. For this work qualified technical advice is essential, preferably through employment on the planning board staff of a planning technician, the part-time services of a planning consultant, or both. Small communities may have to make a start with only such general advice as they can. obtain from a county planning board, a regional planning agency, the Division of State Planning, or extension services of universities or federal agencies.

Separate graphic studies of different systems of improvements, such as highways, parks, local transportation, etc., should be made and then supplemented by studies of the various systems together on one map to insure their coordination. Consideration should be given to official proposals of other agencies such as the county, State or Federal government, or a regional planning agency.

The master plan consists of a graphic plan together with its supporting data, supplementary maps, and written proposals. It is quite different from the official map as it is not binding upon the legislative body. It is the planning board's best current judgment of the future development of the area under its jurisdiction which it believes it will be practicable to carry out as part of a long-time program. The master plan is the yardstick with which the planning board will compare any proposed new improvements that may be referred to it later for report to the legislative body. It should be flexible and be amended by the planning board from time to time as new improvements are adopted by the legislative body, new subdivision plats are approved, additional pro- [ 25 ]

posals are added, or previous proposals become impracticable as a result of changing conditions.

For a small community, all the principal physical features of the master plan can sometimes be shown on a single map. 

Generally, however, a series of maps is called for, each of which shows a separate type of facility, such as highways, parks and playgrounds, transportation routes, etc. The graphic plan should be prepared in such form that it can be reproduced by photographic process. Often the original plans can be prepared in black and white and prints may be colored for display purposes.

The report which accompanies, and is a part of the graphic master plan, should describe and explain the proposed improvements, their need, and their justification. Some of the larger municipalities have prepared their reports in sections adopted and printed separately. It is also desirable to include a long-range financial program showing how the municipality may finance the carrying out of the proposals. 

The preparation of the plan report is properly the function of a properly qualified technician, but it should be worked out in close cooperation with the members of the planning board, preferably with the assistance of a local staff. 

CARRYING OUT THE PLAN 

The work of the planning board is by no means concluded upon presentation of the master plan and report to the legislative body of the municipality. Plans do not get carried out by virtue of their logic alone or their appeal to thoughtful and far-sighted citizens. There are many and diverse interests in every community. Newly elected public officials have their own ideas about what would be popular with their constituents. Constant watchfulness by broadminded civic groups and close cooperation between the planning board and other public officials are required to make the plan an ultimate success.

From time to time as the legislative body accepts specific proposals from the master plan, it should give formal approval thereto by incorporating such proposals into the [26]

official map, thus declaring its intention to carry them out as soon as it is expedient to do so. This is a very definite step toward carrying out the plan and the planning board may and should recommend to the legislative body, from time to time, [ those master plan proposals which it thinks should be given such an official status. 

Among the other routine duties of the board which will be effective in carrying out its purpose are: (a) Consideration of and the preparation of reports upon such public and semi-public improvement projects as may be referred to the board in accordance with the terms of the act. (b) Adoption of rules and regulations to govern the land subdivider in submitting land subdivision plats for approval. (c) Consideration of, and action upon, all subdivision plats submitted, using the master plan as general guide. (d) Continuous study of the master plan with a view to I making such adjustments as may, from time to time, seem desirable. (e) Filing of certified copies of the master plan and all modifications thereof with the municipal clerk and engineer (or town highway superintendent of a town). (f) Assistance to the local legislative body in the preparation of a long-range capital improvement program and budget to cover a period of from five to ten years, showing improvements in the order of their relative importance. If local or general economic conditions are too unsettled to permit more definite budgeting over a period of years this program will serve as a guide. If economic conditions are relatively stable, the program may well become a series of reasonably definite commitments. 

Local leadership is never more essential than in carrying out the approved recommendations of the planning commission. A park system with facilities so designed and located as best to serve the needs of all groups, a coordinated street system related to traffic requirements and topographic features, a program of public building planned for economy, utility, and beauty-such things are not accomplished merely by considering the need for them or by indicating them on an official map. [ 27 ]

It takes persistent, sustained effort by the citizens to carry an idea through the process of detailed physical and fiscal planning, over and around the hazards of opposition to the goal of realization. The power of the press and other mediums of communication, the backing of informed and influential citizens, and the support of various community organizations will all be necessary. 

GAIN RATHER THAN LOSS OF CONTROL BY LOCAL LEGISLATIVE BODY 

It should be noted that in establishing a planning board under these State planning enabling acts, the local legislative body relinquishes none of its authority or power of final decision in the advancement of public works or other municipal affairs, but gains adequate control, through the planning board, of land subdivision by private developers who otherwise are subject to little or no municipal control. It also secures technical, comprehensive assistance in obtaining facts to aid in the solution of a large number of other problems over which the legislative body has jurisdiction. Through these facts, the activities of the various municipal departments may be coordinated. 

Under the general provisions of the Town Law, most towns do not require building permits, but if the specific provisions applicable to planning and zoning are availed of, these towns may require such permits before new buildings may be erected or existing ones altered or enlarged. [ 28 ]

MUNICIPAL ZONING PROCEDURES 

The village and town zoning provisions are combined with the planning powers, each under a single article of the Consolidated Village Law and Town Law respectively. The city zoning provisions appear as a separate article in the General City Law. Each is given in full in the Appendix. 

There is more variation in the zoning provisions of the State enabling acts for cities, villages and towns than in those relating to planning, but in this case also powers are available to all these units, covering the entire geographical area of the State. The subject of municipal zoning will be dealt with in the same general sequence of subjects as was followed in discussing municipal planning.

HISTORY OF ZONING LEGISLATION 

The initiation of zoning in the United States came as a result of a report submitted in 1913 by a Heights of Buildings Committee of the Board of Estimate and Apportionment of the City of New York. It recommended an amendment to the city charter which was adopted by the State Legislature in 1914 (Chapter 470) and authorized the Board of Estimate and Apportionment to divide the city into districts and to regulate the heights of buildings, the area of courts and open spaces, the location of trades and industries, and the erection of buildings designed for specified uses. Based on the recommendations of a Commission on Buildings Districts and Restrictions appointed under this amendment, the city adopted a Zoning Resolution July 25, 1916.

The following year the State Legislature adopted (Gen. 

City Law, § 20, subdivisions 24, 25) an amendment to the

General City Law giving similar powers to all cities in the State, except first class cities with a population of not less than [ 29 ]

240,and not more than 450,000, which exempted only the City of Rochester to which the same Legislature gave equivalent powers under an amendment to its city charter (Chapter 505).

In 1920 (Gen. City Law, Article 5-A), provisions were included for a board of appeals, for court review, and specifying procedures in making amendments and changes in district lines. In 1925 (Gen. City Law, § 20, subdivision 24), there was added to the general powers the right to regulate the density of population in any given area. Other minor amendments have been added, including an extension of the application of the act to all cities except a city having a population of more than one million. This excepted only the City of New York which had zoned itself, as explained above, and gave to Rochester, which had been zoned in 1919, for use only, under the special provisions of its charter, the same zoning powers as were given to the rest of the cities in the State.

Villages and towns were not given the right to zone until several years after such rights were given to cities. In 1921 (Vill. Law, § 89, subdivision 30) the first law permitting village zoning was adopted and in 1923 (Vill. Law, Article 

VI-A), this was amended to make it comparable to the city law. Town zoning was first authorized under Chapter 322 of the Laws of 1922, which was amended in 1926 (Chapter 714) to make it comparable with the then existing Village Law. Later amendments to both Village and Town Laws have kept them in line with the city law. (For Town Zoning 

Law, now available, see Appendix.)

At the present time, therefore, all cities, towns, and villages, which include all geographical areas within the State, are in a position to adopt zoning regulations of similar character and have almost identical rights. 

RELATIONSHIP OF ZONING TO PLANNING 

Zoning in New York State has progressed faster than comprehensive planning as in 1939 there were 254 zoned municipalities in comparison with 157 municipalities with planning [ 30 ]

commissions. Nevertheless, zoning is properly an integral part of planning and gives the best results when done as a part of a comprehensive planning job. As good zoning requires almost as extensive basic mapping, surveys, and development studies as the preparation of a master plan, there is little economy in trying to do the zoning job alone. 

It is certainly true that a zoning ordinance based on, and supported by, a comprehensive plan for the community is on a firmer foundation than one for an unplanned community. 

It should, therefore, be much more likely to receive judicial approval in case of legal attack. Both the town and village zoning laws state that "such regulations shall be made in accordance with a comprehensive plan," which definitely implies that the planning process should either precede or go along with the zoning process. This is of course equally desirable in a city.

SUGGESTIONS FOR ZONING PROCEDURE PRELIMINARY STEPS 

As

The legislative body of the municipality will generally await some expression of desire for zoning on the part of local property owners before taking any step in that direction. 

This may well appear in the form of a petition by the property owners to the legislative body asking for the appointment of an agency to prepare a zoning ordinance and map. explained on page 32, this agency would be a zoning commission in a village or town, but in a city the type of agency may vary. In any case, its task is the preparation of a report to be submitted to the legislative body for final action. The size of a zoning commission is not specified in the zoning laws, but it should generally have at least five members. [ 35 ]

While, as already explained, the Town Law provides that any existing planning board may also be designated the zoning commission, there is nothing in the city or village laws to prevent the use of the same personnel on both groups. 

They must, however, function as distinct bodies. 

DANGER OF COPYING OTHER ORDINANCES 

In drafting both ordinance and map, it should be appreciated from the start that every community has its own peculiar needs and problems and that the zoning ordinance for one may not be at all applicable to another. Copying an ordinance designed for another community is therefore not a safe procedure and may do more harm than good. But a study of ordinances for similar municipalities is generally helpful. It should be borne in mind also that the way the various districts are laid out on the zoning map is often more essential to reasonable and effective zoning than is the phraseology of the text of the ordinance. 

GATHERING OF DATA 

The first step of the zoning commission or other responsible agency should be to ascertain, tabulate, and map all pertinent facts as to existing conditions throughout the municipality and immediately adjoining area. Much of these factual data can best be shown on maps. One map should show all public and semi-public properties, including streets and roads, parks and playgrounds, school and other public building grounds, airports, railroads, navigable waterways and the like; together with such geographical features as lakes and principal streams. Another map should show the existing use of private property whether for business, industry, or residence, or special types of each; or for agriculture or forest lands. For simplification, the indication of such uses may be grouped in rather broad classifications with individual definition of only such uses as are likely to be made subject to special regulations.

Air maps, now usually to be obtained at comparatively small cost, are the best sources of existing uses of land and other needed planning information and more than pay for [36]

;ך themselves in time saved and mistakes avoided. In rural towns, where no good map exists, by far the best and cheapest way of making such a map is by tracing from an air map or pantographing to the desired scale.

In addition, data should be collected on the amount and distribution of population, its past rate of growth, and estimates of future trends as to number, character, and distribution. Information concerning the extent of local industries, the amount and kind of employment offered by them, and the likelihood of industrial expansion will be very helpful in determining the probable future development of the territory under study.

AREAS TO BE COVERED 

Armed with the necessary maps and factual data, the zoning agency should then proceed to draft the regulations. of the various types of districts which it may deem best suited to carry out the purposes of the zoning laws and to lay out the boundaries of these districts on the map of the municipality. Village and city zoning ordinances should apply to the entire area of the municipality. A town zoning commission may apply the ordinance and map only to that part of the town which is outside the limits of any incorporated village or city lying within or partly within the town (a restriction similar to that in the planning law), but should generally apply it to all of such unincorporated area. 

In large towns which have several populous centers that have not been incorporated or which have ribbon developments along main highways, recourse may be had to piecemeal zoning by zoning only such populous portions of the town. To avoid discrimination and possible upsets by the courts all areas of similar character should, however, be zoned at the same time and the remaining unclassified area should be marked "undetermined" to indicate that at some future time an appropriate classification will be assigned to it. 

Interim ordinances, prepared without a map to preserve the status quo until complete regulations with a map can be established, should be discouraged. [ 37 ]

KINDS OF DISTRICTS 

The number of kinds of districts required will vary with local conditions. The reasonable minimum would appear to be a single residential district and a single business district. 

Generally in rural areas it will be well to have two types of residential districts, a business district, and an industrial district. It may also be desirable to establish a district to be reserved for agricultural and recreational uses from which residential subdivisions would be excluded. In a large city it will generally be desirable to have a light and a heavy industrial district, a central business district, a neighborhood business district, and four or five types of residential districts. 

This does not mean that there need be, in any case, only one area set aside for each of these various districts. There may be many areas scattered throughout the municipality falling within each kind of district.

Some of the earlier zoning ordinances had a series of maps showing the types of districts; for example, one showed various types of use, another permissible heights, and a third the area regulations. Modern practice uses only a single map with a single set of districts, and the height and area regulations are usually applied to the separate types of use districts. 

Billboards and advertising signs should generally be confined to business and industrial districts, possibly subject to special restrictions even in these districts. Court decisions have sustained their exclusion from residential districts.

Height and area restrictions are most important in urban areas and should be greatly simplified in strictly rural areas. 

In some cases, only use zoning may be established in the latter, but the special limitation of height and area in every district is supported by numerous considerations of safety, light, air, health, and general welfare.

METHODS OF CONTROLLING HEIGHT AND AREA 

The height of buildings may be restricted by specifying the maximum permissible number of floors, or height in feet, or both. Area restriction should provide for proper yards or open spaces at the front, sides, and rear of buildings and for [ 38 ]

building lines along the frontage of all streets, roads, or other thoroughfares. Side yards and rear yards, especially, should be sufficient to insure access of ample light and air to dwellings and to safeguard against spread of fire. Building lines or building set-back lines along thoroughfares should be established with a view to keeping buildings well back from traveled ways and should be placed at a distance back from the roadway right-of-way in proportion to the volume and objectionable character of traffic upon the road or street in question. 

In rural areas, business buildings usually should be held back to the line established for residential structures. 

DENSITY CONTROL 

Control of population density can be provided in one of several ways. The ordinance may specify the maximum number of families per acre for which new buildings may be designed. In computing such densities, the gross acreage is generally used which includes the area of any interior streets within the area being developed and half the area of any abutting streets.

A more usual and more easily understood method of controlling density is to specify the minimum lot area per family to be housed on the lot. For example, there may be a requirement of 5,000 square feet of lot in a one-family detached house district, equivalent to a lot unit of 50 by 100 feet, and a requirement of 2,000 square feet of lot per family in a multifamily district. The latter would be equivalent to 13 families to the gross acre if 40 per cent of the district was in streets and other public open spaces.

A third method of controlling density would be the requirement of a certain number of square feet of open space on a lot for each family housed thereon, or a requirement for a minimum relationship between open space and floor area. 

For example, there may be required in an apartment district, one square foot of open space for each two square feet of gross floor area of the building. This would mean that a three-story apartment house in such a district would be required to leave 60 per cent of its lot area vacant, and a [39]

six-story apartment house 75 per cent. This third method of density control is particularly applicable to multi-family districts.

ENACTMENT OF ORDINANCE 

The agency designated to prepare the zoning ordinance should first submit a preliminary report and hold hearings thereon. It should then submit its final report to the legis lative body, whereupon its responsibility in connection therewith is terminated. The legislative body should then hold a public hearing, or hearings, at which parties in interest and citizens shall have an opportunity to be heard. In a municipality covering a large area, it is desirable to hold a series of such hearings at meeting places distributed about the municipality for public convenience. 

The final action by the legislative body should be taken in the manner laid down in that municipality for the adoption of other municipal ordinances.

DANGER OF ILL-CONSIDERED AMENDMENTS 

To lessen the danger of ill-considered amendments and to insure that all changes in the ordinance and map are consistent with general planning requirements and objectives, all proposed amendments should be submitted to the local planning board, where one exists, for report thereon before final action by the legislative body.

PITFALLS TO BE AVOIDED 

Zoning must be well done to be beneficial. Bad or illconsidered zoning may do real and permanent injury to the municipality concerned and lead to severe administrative difficulties. On the other hand, ease of administration is not always a sign of good zoning as an ordinance that meets with no objections is likely to be so loose as to be ineffective in securing the protection it was intended to give. 

A wise board of appeals can do much to avoid legal controversy and to clear away unnecessary and unreasonable restrictions while still preserving the intent and objective of [ 40 ]

the zoning ordinance. However, by misinterpretation or other extension of its powers, or by being too liberal in granting exceptions, such a board may completely nullify the effect of a zoning ordinance. It is well, therefore, to set forth clearly in the municipal zoning ordinance the exact powers and duties conferred upon this body by the legislative body, in addition to certain appellant jurisdiction given it under the State statute.

Reference has already been made to the danger of trying to copy directly zoning ordinances of other municipalities. 

Among the other pitfalls to be avoided in preparing zoning ordinances and maps are: (1) Failure in the first instance to make zoning a part of a general planning program. (2) Lack of proper consideration of the zoning regulations of adjoining municipal areas, creating problem arcas along the municipal borders. (3) Prevention of new and improved forms of largescale housing by too rigid restrictions designed primarily to regulate the placing of one-family dwellings on single lots. (4) Over-extensive provision for business, industrial, and apartment house uses. It is not unusual to find zoning ordinances setting aside enough area for business purposes to allow for the greatest imaginable business expansion over a period of hundreds of years. The same is true, in perhaps less degree, of industrial and apartment house districts. Such over-extension of these districts checks the development of single residences and, in the long run, depreciates property values. With neither protection of residential investments nor any considerable demand for other uses, a large proportion of the property affected is almost certain to lie fallow or, if improved, to fall gradually into blight and decay. (5) Failure properly to relate apartment house districts to existing and economically feasible street and utility capacities. (6) Attempt to accomplish through a zoning ordinance things for which zoning is not intended and the laying down of obviously unreasonable regulations. [ 41 ]

NEED FOR RURAL ZONING 

There has been some tendency to consider zoning primarily as an urban need, and for rural agricultural communities to feel that they do not require it and that it may interfere with some of the processes which are necessary to the production and marketing of their crops. This may have been due in the past to a failure to approach the subject from the rural angle and to work out simple zoning ordinances for rural towns which do not need the variety of restrictions that are essential in a more intensively developed area. As a guide for such towns, a suggested simplified rural zoning ordinance has been prepared by the Division of State Planning and published as its Bulletin Number 32. This is an adaptation of urban zoning to fit rural conditions, but for towns entirely outside the influence of large urban or metropolitan centers, it is proposed that the ordinance be still further simplified. 

For example, some rural towns may suffer no growing pains except those occasioned by the cumulative cluttering up of the State and county highways extending through their territory. It may be worthwhile, in such a case, to enact a zoning ordinance simply to keep billboards, gasoline stations, roadside restaurants, and other such commercial structures in their proper locations in such towns. 

This would preserve the beauty of the countryside which attracts the tourist and vacationer and, in a very real sense, provides a cash crop to the rural dweller. 

In certain rural areas of the State the increasing cost of government and the inability of agriculture to maintain itself on an economic basis have made it obvious that there should be a gradual withdrawal of unsuitable lands from active farming use and gradual resettlement on other lands of struggling families who have been unable to produce enough to pay for even a small part of minimum public service such as schools and roads. On the basis of a comprehensive plan for land uses, it may be possible to zone lands of this type against agricultural settlement and use. Rural populations may thus be brought into closer concentration upon better lands or moved to locations more readily accessible to State [42]

services, with benefit both to the people concerned and to the State at large.

For rural towns adjoining suburban territory, however, there may be many more needs for zoning than at first appear. 

Such territory, usually, is subject to many forms of sudden speculative development. It represents the city in its embryonic stage when regulation to insure orderly and healthful development is most important and easiest to apply. Broader need for zoning will be found also in rural towns with a high degree of "estate", or recreation, or non-farm use, where orderly development is important to protect the town's chief asset-attractive rural environment. However, no matter how limited may be the need for and purposes of zoning in rural areas, general zoning procedures and zoning coverage must, under the present statutes, follow the course. outlined above. IV. COUNTY PLANNING 

County planning is relatively new, but has spread rapidly in certain parts of the country during the last few years and there are now several hundred county planning boards or commissions in the United States. New York State counties, however, do not yet have zoning powers.

HISTORY OF LEGISLATION 

In New York the movement has spread more slowly, however, as up to 1939, only ten counties had established official county planning agencies, although two others had semiofficial agencies and one an unofficial agency. Most of these official county boards were established under the county planning enabling act adopted by the Legislature in 1932.

RELATION TO OTHER TYPES OF PLANNING 

County planning has objectives similar to those of municipal planning but on a somewhat broader scale and over larger areas. It applies to the counties a process of discovering existing conditions, trends of growth and future needs that in essentials is similar to the planning procedure applied to office units of government. It provides a plan to arrange the county's physical features in order to secure economically for its inhabitants the maximum of comfort, convenience, health, and beauty.

Like the cities and villages, the counties are organized communities and have many public functions, including police protection, water supply, sewage disposal, recreation, health protection, construction and maintenance of highways, roads, streets, and bridges, and many others. Some of these functions are performed directly by the county or town governments, while others are performed by special districts such as water districts. A county plan will provide a means of determining the need for proposed physical improvements, the order of their urgency, and their permanency when constructed, without conflict between various agencies. The plan will involve periodic inventories, a long-range program, and regulations governing both public and private activities. 

There is necessarily an overlapping in the areas covered by the various types of planning-municipal, city, village, and town, county, State, and Federal-but there is no serious overlapping in function, as the different types may be concerned with the same land but on a different scale, or from a different point of view. Along the border line of adjacent [ 45 ]

communities, a local project common to both also becomes the concern of the larger governmental unit which includes both. 

Inter-city, inter-village, and inter-town problems may thus be subjects of importance to the county; inter-county problems to the State.

Applied to a county, this means that it would be a function of the county planning board to coordinate its plans for highways, parks, and other physical improvements, or for future uses of land, with those of the State and larger regions. It may cooperate with the State and regional planning agencies by furnishing information and offering suggestions which would harmonize the larger projects with county interests and welfare. It should also cooperate with the local planning boards within and along its boundaries. It may receive and adopt, as far as practicable, suggestions from those local boards. It will be serving the best interests of the county by promoting the establishment of additional local boards and by assisting all of them in their work, particularly in tying in the plans of adjoining communities. 

County planning has, therefore, a definite and distinct function to perform, the lack of which constitutes a missing link in the chain of planning activity which extends from the Nation and the State down to the village or neighborhood.

PLANNING UNDER COUNTY CHARTERS 

Reference has already been made to the establishment of planning in Nassau and Westchester counties under their county charters. Their provisions deserve special mention. 

In each case a county planning department is established, the head of which shall be a county planning commission, appointed by the county executive, subject to confirmation by the Board of Supervisors, and serving without pay. In 

Nassau, town representation is provided. In Westchester, each city is represented, two members represent all unincorporated areas, and three members represent all villages. 

The powers of the planning board in respect to the master plan and the provisions for its adoption, in whole or in part, by the Board of Supervisors, are quite similar in each case to those given in the general county planning law. But some additional powers are given.

There has not yet been an opportunity to see how these new Nassau County provisions will work out in actual practice. WESTCHESTER COUNTY 

The Westchester County planning provisions are given in Chapter 617 of the Laws of 1937 and are relatively brief. 

Specified powers conferred by special acts of the County Board and not mentioned in the general county planning law are approval of plats; the protection of property of the county, including parks and parkways, against direct encroachment, and the areas bordering on them against deleterious uses; and the preparation and submission annually of recommendations in regard to a proposed capital budget and capital program.

OPTIONAL COUNTY GOVERNMENT LAWS 

Still another method of establishing county planning in counties of New York State is provided by the optional county government laws adopted by the Legislature as Chapters 862 and 863 of the Laws of 1937. The first of these is the Optional County Government Law, Chapter Eleven-A of the Consolidated Laws and the other is Article 18 of the County Law. These include provisions of both the Desmond and 

Buckley Acts introduced at that session. 

Four optional forms of county government are provided for under the Desmond Act and five under the Buckley Act. [ 50 ]

Adoption involves a general referendum at the time of a general election. Provision is included for county planning boards or commissions with powers varying from those in the general county planning act to the special powers included in the Nassau Charter. 

The Desmond Act provides for advisory and planning boards appointed by the county executive and upon which may be conferred the powers specified in the general county planning law and such other powers of planning and zoning as may be given them by the board of supervisors of the county or governing bodies of local political units. The 

Buckley Act authorizes a county planning commission, with provisions for subdivision control, preparation and recommendation of a master plan and its adoption and enforcement, and the continuance of zoning powers of local government units subject to the master plan. 

As stated at the beginning of this chapter, Monroe County has a Division of Regional Planning under a county manager system of government. This was set up under the original 

Buckley Act of 1935 (County Law, Article 2-A) and was the only county to take advantage of the provisions of that act. To date, no one of the forms of optional county government under the 1937 provisions have been established. 

SUGGESTIONS FOR COUNTY PLANNING PROCEDURE MAPPING AND SURVEYING PROGRAM 

As in municipal planning, the first requisite is an adequate base map of the area to be studied, but this is much less apt to be readily available for an entire county than for a city, village, or town.

In the preceding chapter reference was made to the advantages of air maps, particularly in planning rural towns. This is even more true of county planning and several county planning commissions have started their work with the acquisition of an aerial map. A county-wide mosaic at a scale of 2,000 feet to the inch (a natural scale of 1 to 24,000) with sheets at 400 feet to the inch for more detailed studies. would be an excellent combination. [51]

Line base maps on tracing cloth will also be needed and these should be in the following two forms: (a) A skeleton base map, which would be in the nature of an outline map showing only the principal transportation routes, larger parks, and the boundaries and names of all political subdivisions. A suitable scale would be 1 to 62,500 (approximately one mile to the inch), 4,000 feet to the inch, or 2,000 feet to the inch, depending on the size of the county and the extent of the development. (b) A detail base map which would show, in addition to features on the skeleton base, all railroad lines and all streets (classified as existing, officially proposed main highways, and local mapped streets not yet laid out on the ground). In general, a scale of 2,000 feet to the inch is desirable, but in urban counties 1,000 feet to the inch may be preferable.

Many of the basic surveys and studies for the county can be shown on prints of these maps. Among the principal things which should prove useful are the following: (a) Preparation of a map showing existing land uses. 

These may be broadly classified as urban and rural. The urban lands may be further classified as industrial, business, and residential; and the rural lands as agricultural, rural residential (country homes), and forested lands. Further classification would show the lands devoted to recreation, institutions, cemeteries, and municipal water supplies, all of which may be basically either urban or rural. 

The detailed classification of lands which might be shown on a land-use map (using a print of the detail base map) will vary with the rural or urban character of the county. The former may require several subdivisions of agricultural and open development areas, while the latter should show separately the various types of urban development. PLANNING STUDIES 

As these factual surveys progress, a start can be made on the planning studies which should lead up to the county master plan, a necessary objective if county planning is to be effective. Here adequate expert guidance is essential.

These studies should relate primarily to land uses and communication facilities.

The former require studies of the probable future use of land, indicating on a print of the detail base map the expected extent of urban and industrial uses, and non-urban requirements for such uses as agriculture, forestry, outlying parks, and other types of recreation. The latter require the mapping of the location and character of such future public improvements as roads, airports, and public and semi-public transportation facilities.

From an analysis of these studies a general development plan for the entire county should evolve, shown either on a single map or a series of maps, supplemented by a written. description and supporting data. A master plan should then be adopted by the planning board, but, as already explained in the summary of the general county planning law, this is limited, in counties establishing boards under that act, to a plan for those projects in the acquisition, financing, or construction of which the county may be called on to participate.

MAINTENANCE OF PUBLIC INTEREST 

In addition to these somewhat routine procedures described in the general county planning law, it is essential that an active public interest be maintained in the planning movement in general and in the work of the county planning board in particular. This involves well prepared publicity through printed reports, newspaper articles, and public addresses. [55]

V. 

REGIONAL PLANNING 

Under this heading will be discussed the powers and procedures by which two or more counties or political subdivisions of counties may create and operate joint planning boards.

UNDER GENERAL LEGISLATION 

Provision for this was first made in New York State by Chapter 539 of the Laws of 1925, but, as explained in the preceding discussion of county planning, this law also provided for the use of the same powers by single counties. Two of these established boards under this act, but no regional agency, as defined in the preceding paragraph, was created thereunder.

The legislation adopted in 1932 (Chapter 137) replaced the earlier act, again providing for both county and regional boards, the latter to be established either by two or more counties, by a single county in collaboration with any or all of the cities, towns, and villages therein, or by two or more counties in collaboration with any or all of their local municipalities.

The regional board may contain representatives of the municipalities selected in a manner to be determined by the board or boards of supervisors.

The purpose of this regional planning provision is to permit the creation of planning boards with a real jurisdiction coterminus with actual planning problems. Only in rare instances is there any logic in the location of established county boundary lines. Community interests and community. development frequently break over these lines. So do important planning problems. Wherever this happens to any large extent, there is likely to be much advantage and economy [56]

in having one planning commission instead of two or more at work. 

Rural counties with extensive submarginal land-use problems, may find particular advantage in combining county and municipal forces in planning. Each of the participating governmental units, under this procedure, retains all of its independent legislative functions.

The detail powers and procedures for regional planning boards are identical with those established for county planning boards in the same act and will not be repeated here as they have been described in the preceding chapter. Although the machinery is at hand no regional boards have yet been established under these general provisions of law. 

SPECIAL REGIONAL AGENCIES 

There is however one official regional planning board in New York State and that is the Niagara Frontier Planning Board serving the counties of Erie and Niagara, set up in 1925 under a special act, Chapter 267 of the Laws of 1925 (as amended by Chapter 232 of the Laws of 1927). The 

Board is empowered to study the needs and conditions of regional and community planning in Erie and Niagara counties and prepare plans adopted to meet such needs and conditions, and through designated agencies, collect and distribute information on regional and community planning and zoning in both counties. The Board consists of thirteen members: the mayors of Buffalo, Lackawanna, Lockport, Niagara Falls, North Tonawanda, and Tonawanda, three members of the Board of Supervisors of Niagara County, and three members of the Board of Supervisors of Erie County. The chairman of the board is the thirteenth member and is elected by the other twelve who are ex-officio members. The County of Erie and the County of Niagara and all cities, towns and villages in such counties may in their discretion expend, out of the public moneys, funds to defray the expenses of the board and to further its purposes. 

Mention should also be made of the unofficial regional planning which has been done in the metropolitan district center- [57]

ing around the City of New York and reaching into the three states of New York, New Jersey and Connecticut. A comprehensive regional survey and plan of this area was undertaken in 1921 by the Committee on a Regional Plan for 

New York and Its Environs, financed by the Russell Sage

Foundation. These studies were published in a series of volumes, the last appearing in 1931, and covered an intensive analysis of the physical, economic, industrial, social, and legal problems involved and a master plan for future growth under the name of the Graphic Regional Plan. The Regional 

Plan Association, Inc., has now superseded the original committee. It was established in 1929 and is functioning as a non-profit membership organization charged with keeping the Regional Plan up to date, bringing urgent parts of it before local officials, and maintaining a continuing program of research and education. 

Similar organizations have been established in other metropolitan centers in other states and this procedure has proved an effective one where adequate financing is available. [58]

VI. ASSISTANCE AVAILABLE FOR LOCAL PLANNING AGENCIES The Conference of Mayors and the Association of Towns are both prepared to render assistance to local planning agencies in the way of general advice regarding procedures for solving their local problems. The New York State 

Federation of Planning Boards, organized in 1937 under the joint sponsorship of these two organizations and the Division of State Planning, has provided a medium of interchange of views through its bulletin entitled, "New York State Planning News," and also has been conducting a series of sectional conferences throughout the State at which members of local planning boards and other municipal officials have discussed their own particular problems. Regional and county planning agencies have also rendered valuable assistance in the establishment and organization of local planning boards within their districts.

While the concern of the Division of State Planning is primarily with State-wide problems, particularly those which are not definitely assigned to a single State department, it is also definitely charged by the act which established it with a responsibility of advising and cooperating in local planning, and it, therefore, has been cooperating along these lines with the three agencies mentioned above. It can perhaps be of particular service in advising county and regional planning organizations within the State. It recognizes that State planning can only be successful if it is built on a firm foundation of local planning. Likewise local planning must be fitted. into a State-wide pattern for both uses of land and communication facilities.

It was felt from the start by the Division of State Planning that one of the most profitable ways to help local planning boards is to provide a means of informing each [59]

board about the activities and programs of others throughout the State. With this in mind, a directory of regional, county, and municipal planning organizations was published in 1937 with a review of the past activities of each of these organizations. Later editions of this directory were published in 

1938 and 1939, summarizing the work accomplished by each board during the preceding year and including a zoning supplement showing which municipalities had zoning ordinances, the date of their adoption, and the type of ordinance.

The Division has also taken an active interest in planning legislation and has cooperated with the State Conference of Mayors and the Association of Towns in formulating the extensive and important amendments to the planning enabling acts adopted in 1938. It is prepared to continue its study of additional legislative needs in regard to planning and zoning. 

It is hoped that this manual in itself will provide a convenient source of reference for existing local planning agencies and for citizen groups interested in the establishment of such agencies. It is being made available by the New York State 

Planning Council as a contribution to the promotion of better and more effective planning and zoning within the State of New York. While only physical problems have been stressed, the general field of planning is a broad one and should, in the future, make substantial contributions to the solution of social and economic problems as well. § 81. Board of appeals. 1. The mayor of any city to which paragraphs twenty-four and twenty-five of section twenty of this chapter are applicable, except a city having a population of more than one million may appoint a board of appeals consisting of five members, each to be appointed for three years. They shall also hear and decide all matters referred to them or upon which they are required to pass under any ordinance of the common council adopted pursuant to such two paragraphs. The concurring vote of four members of the board shall be necessary to reverse any order, requirement, decision or determination of any such administrative official, or to decide in favor of the applicant any matter upon which they are required to pass under any such ordinance or to effect any variation in such ordinance. 2. Appeal, how taken. Such appeal shall be taken within such time as shall be prescribed by the board of appeals by general rule, by filing with the officer from whom the appeal is taken and with the board of appeals of a notice of appeal, specifying the grounds thereof. An appeal stays all proceedings in furtherance of the action appealed from, unless the officer from whom the appeal is taken certifies to the board of appeals after the notice of appeal shall have been filed with him that by reason of facts stated in the certificate a stay would, in his opinion, cause imminent peril to life and property, in which case proceedings shall not be stayed otherwise than by a restraining order which may be granted by the board of appeals or by the supreme court, on application, on notice to the officer from whom the appeal is taken and on due cause shown.

The return must concisely set forth such other facts as may be pertinent and material to show the grounds of the decision appealed from and must be verified. 

If upon the hearing, it shall appear to the court that testimony is necessary for the proper disposition of the matter, it may take evidence or appoint a referee to take such evidence as it may direct and report the same to the court with his findings of fact and conclusions of law, which shall constitute a part of the proceedings upon which the determination of the court shall be made. The court may reverse or affirm, wholly or partly, or may modify the decision brought up for review. 

Costs shall not be allowed against the board, unless it shall appear to the court that it acted with gross negligence or in bad faith or with malice in making the decision appealed from. [73]

All issues in any action or proceeding for any of the purposes herein stated shall have preference over all other civil actions and proceedings. § 179-d. Conflict with other laws. Wherever the regulations made under authority of this act require a greater width of size of yards or courts, or require a lower height of building or less number of stories, or require a greater percentage of lot to be left unoccupied, or impose other higher standards than are required in any other statute or local ordinance or regulation, the provisions of the regulations made under authority of this act shall govern. Wherever the provisions of any other statute or local ordinance or regulation require a greater width or size of yards or courts, or require a lower height of building or a less number of stories, or require a greater percentage of lot to be left unoccupied, or impose other higher standards than are required by the regulations made under authority of this act, the provisions of such statute, or local ordinance or regulation shall govern. § 179-e. (Official maps.) Every village may by resolution of its board of trustees establish an official map or plan of the village showing the streets, highways and parks theretofore laid out, adopted and established by law, and such map or plan is to be deemed to be final and conclusive with respect to the location and widths of streets and highways and the location of parks shown thereon. Such official map or plan is hereby declared to be established to conserve and promote the public health, safety and general welfare. The clerk of every village which has established such an official map or plan shall immediately file a certificate of that fact with the clerk or register of the county in which said village is located. § 179-f. (Planning board; appointment; terms of office.) The terms of the two officials of the municipality shall terminate with the terms of the mayor selecting them. The respective terms of the other members of the board first appointed shall be one, two and three years from and after their appointment. If the planning board shall decide at any time during the term of the performance bond that the extent of building development that has taken place in the subdivision is not sufficient to warrant all the improvements covered by such performance bond, or that the character and extent of such development requires additional improvements previously waived for a period stated at the time of fixing the original terms of such bond, the planning board may modify its requirements for any or all such improvements, and the face value of such performance bond shall thereupon be reduced or increased by an appropriate amount so that the new face value will cover the cost in full of the amended list of improvements required by the planning board. $261. Grant of power. For the purpose of promoting the health, safety, morals, or the general welfare of the community, the town board is hereby empowered by ordinance to regulate and restrict the height, number of stories and size of buildings and other structures, the percentage of lot that may be occupied, the size of yards, courts, and other open spaces, the density of population, and the location and use of buildings, structures and land for trade, industry, residence or other purposes; provided that such regulations shall apply to and affect only such part of a town as is outside the limits of any incorporated village or city. Such regulations may provide that a board of appeals may determine and vary their application in harmony with their general purpose and intent, and in accordance with general or specific rules therein contained. § 262. Districts. For any or all of said purposes the town board may divide that part of the town which is outside the limits of any incorporated village or city into districts of such number, shape and area as may be deemed best suited to carry out the purposes of this act; and within such districts it may regulate and restrict the [80]

erection, construction, reconstruction, alteration or use of buildings, structures or land. 

All such regulations shall be uniform for each class or kind of buildings, throughout such district but the regulations in one district may differ from those in other districts. § 263. Purposes in view. Such regulations shall be made in accordance with a comprehensive plan and designed to lessen congestion in the streets, to secure safety from fire, panic and other dangers; to promote health and general welfare; to provide adequate light and air; to prevent the overcrowding of land; to avoid undue concentration of population; to facilitate the adequate provision of transportation, water, sewerage, schools, parks and other public requirements. Such regulations shall be made with reasonable consideration, among other things, as to the character of the district and its peculiar suitability for particular uses, and with a view to conserving the value of buildings and encouraging the most appropriate use of land throughout such municipality. 

8 264. Method of procedure. The town board shall provide for the manner in which such regulations and restrictions and the boundaries of such districts shall be determined, established and enforced, and from time to time amended, supplemented or changed. However, no such regulation, restrictions or boundary shall become effective until after a public hearing in relation thereto, at which parties in interest and citizens shall have an opportunity to be heard. At least fifteen days' notice of the time and place of such hearing shall be published in a paper of general circulation in such town. $265. Changes. Such regulations, restrictions and boundaries may from time to time be amended, supplemented, changed, modified or repealed. The provisions of the previous section relative to public hearings and official notice shall apply equally to all changes or amendments. § 266. Zoning commission. In order to avail itself of the powers conferred by this article, such town board shall appoint a commission to be known as the zoning commission to recommend the boundaries of the various original districts and appropriate regulations to be enforced therein. Such commission shall make a preliminary report and hold public hearings thereon before submitting its final report and such town board shall not hold its public hearing or take action until it has received the final report of such commission. Where a plan commission already exists it may be appointed as the zoning commission. $267. Board of appeals. Such town board shall appoint a board of appeals consisting of five members and shall designate its chairman. Of the members of the board first appointed, one shall hold office for the term of one year, one for the term of two years, one for the term of three years, one for the term of four years, one for the term of five years from and after his appointment. If a vacancy shall occur otherwise than by expiration of term, it shall be filled by the town board by appointment for the unexpired term.

Approval of plats; additional requisites. Before the approval by the planning board of a plat showing a new street or highway, such plat shall also in proper cases show a park or parks suitably located for playground or other recreation purposes. In approving such plats the planning board shall require that the streets and highways shall be of sufficient width and suitable grade and shall be suitably located to accommodate the prospective traffic, to afford adequate light and air, to facilitate fire protection, and to provide access of fire-fighting equipment to buildings, and to be co-ordinated so as to compose a convenient system conforming to the official map and properly related to the proposals shown by the planning board on the master plan; that the land shown on such plats shall be of such a character that it can be used safely for building purposes without danger to health or peril from fire, flood or other menace; that suitable monuments have been placed at such block corners and other necessary points as may be required by the board and the location thereof is shown on the map of such plat; and that the parks shall be of reasonable size for neighborhood playgrounds or other recreation uses; that all streets or other public places shown on such plats shall be suitably graded and paved and that sidewalks, street lighting standards, curbs, gutters, street trees, water mains, sanitary sewers and storm drains or combined sewers shall be installed all in accordance with standards, specifications and procedure acceptable to the appropriate town departments, or alternatively that a performance bond sufficient to cover the full cost of the same as estimated by the planning board or other appropriate town departments designated by the planning board shall be furnished to the town by the owner. Such performance bond shall be issued by a bonding or surety company approved by the town board and shall also be approved by such town board as to form, sufficiency and manner of execution. Such performance bond shall run for a term to be fixed by the planning board, but in no case for a longer term than three years, provided, however, that the term of such performance bond may be extended by the planning board with the consent of the owner for an additional period not to exceed three years. If the planning board shall decide at any time during the term of the performance bond that the extent of building development that has taken place in the subdivision is not sufficient to warrant all the improvements covered by such performance bond, the planning board after due notice and public hearing may modify its requirements for any or all such improvements, and the face value of such performance bond shall thereupon be reduced by an appropriate amount so that the new face value will cover the cost in full of the amended list of improvements required by the planning board. In the event that any required improvements have not been installed as provided in this section within the term of such performance bond, then such performance bond shall be forfeited to the town and upon the receipt of the proceeds thereof the town shall install such improvements as are covered by such performance bond.

Where the enforcement of the provisions of this section would entail practical difficulty or unnecessary hardship, and where the circumstances of the case do not require the structure to be related to existing or proposed streets or highways, the applicant for such a permit may appeal from the decision of the administrative officer having charge of the issue of permits to the board of appeals or other similar board, in any town which has established a board having power to make variances or exceptions in zoning regulations, and the same provisions are hereby applied to such appeals and to such board as are provided in cases of appeals on zoning regulations. The board may in passing on such appeal make any reasonable exception and issue the permit subject to conditions that will protect any future street or highway layout. 

Any such decision shall be subject to review by certiorari order issued out of a special term of the supreme court in the same manner and pursuant to the same provisions as in appeals from the decisions of such board upon zoning regulations. § 281. Planning board; changes in zoning regulations. The town board is hereby authorized, by general or special rule applicable to the zoning regulations of that part of the town outside the limits of any incorporated village or any portion of such zoning regulations, to empower the planning board simultaneously with the approval of any such plat either to confirm the zoning regulations of the land so platted, as shown on the official zoning maps of the town, or to make any reasonable change therein, and such board is hereby empowered to make such change. The owner of the land shown on the plat may submit with the plat a proposed building plan indicating lots where group houses for residence or apartment houses or local stores and shops are proposed to be built. Such building plan shall indicate for each lot or proposed building unit the maximum density of population that may exist thereon and the minimum yard requirements. Such building plan, if approved by the planning board, shall modify, change or supplement the zoning regulations of the land shown on the plat, provided that for such land so shown there shall not be a greater average density of population or cover of the land with buildings than is permitted in the district wherein such lands lie, as shown on the official zoning map. Such building plan shall not be approved by the planning board unless in its judgment the appropriate use of adjoining land is reasonably safeguarded and such plan is consistent with the public welfare. Before the board shall make any such change in the zoning regulations there shall be a public hearing, preceded by the same notice as in the case of the approval of the plat itself. On the filing of the plat in the office of the county clerk or registrar, such change, subject, however, to review by court as hereinafter provided, shall be and become part of the zoning regulations of the town, shall take the place of any regulations established by the town board, shall be enforced in the same manner and shall be similarly subject to change. § 282. Court review. Any person or persons, jointly or severally aggrieved by any decision of the planning board concerning such plat or the changing of the zoning regulations of such land, or any officer, department, board or bureau of the town, may present to a special term of the supreme court a petition, duly verified, setting forth that such decision is illegal, in whole or in part, specifying the grounds of the [ 87 ]

illegality. Such petition must be presented to the court within thirty days after the filing of the decision in the office of the board. 

Upon the presentation of such petition, the court may allow a certiorari order directed to the planning board to review such decision of the planning board, and shall prescribe therein the time within which a return thereto must be made and served upon the relator's attorney, which shall not be less than ten days and may be extended by the court. The allowance of the order shall stay proceedings upon the decision appealed from.

The planning board shall not be required to return the original papers acted upon by it, but it shall be sufficient to return certified or sworn copies thereof, or of such portions thereof as may be called for by such order. The return must concisely set forth such other facts as may be pertinent and material to show the grounds of the decision appealed from and must be verified. 

If, upon the hearing, it shall appear to the court holding special term that testimony is necessary for the proper disposition of the matter, it may take evidence or appoint a referee to take such evidence as it may direct and report the same to the court with his findings of fact and conclusions of law, which shall constitute a part of the proceedings upon which the determination of the court shall be made. The court may reverse or affirm, wholly or partly, or may modify the decision brought up for review.

Costs shall not be allowed against the planning board, unless it shall appear to the court that it acted with gross negligence or in bad faith or with malice in making the decision appealed from.

All issues in any proceeding under this section shall have preference over all other civil actions and proceedings. § 283. Issuance of licenses and permits in certain towns. If, in any town of the first class at the time of the enactment of this chapter, there exists a building department, set up as an adjunct of the planning board, all licenses or permits, whenever and in the manner required by any building zone ordinance or building code, shall be issued by said department, and all fees therefor be collected as provided by the requirements of the building zone ordinance or building code. § 284. Separability clause. If any part or provision of this article or the application thereof to any person or circumstance be adjudged invalid by any court of competent jurisdiction, such judgment shall be confined in its operation to the part, provision or application directly involved in the controversy in which such judgment shall have been rendered and shall not affect or impair the validity of the remainder of this article or the application thereof to other persons or circumstances and the legislature hereby declares that it would have enacted this article or the remainder thereof had the invalidity of such provision or application thereof been apparent.
