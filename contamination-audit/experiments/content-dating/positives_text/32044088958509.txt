CHAPTER I 

1866-1879 HATEVER doubts and disagreements people of Chris- Wtian faith may have on pinag et theology and religious practice are apt to be put aside and disappear when an appeal is made to them in behalf of children. All enlightened nations have by law and at times by treaty given their attention to the welfare of children. There is hardly any stronger instinct or principle in human behavior. 

When Dr. Henry A. Coit, Rector of St. Paul's School, saw in the rural neighborhood about him those who had been left orphaned and destitute by civil war, he could find no peace for his soul until he had done what he could to afford relief. The need here was no greater than in multitudes of other places, but it met the eye of one who, while of a commanding spirit, had a heart of the deepest and tenderest human sympathy. He has said of our Christian calling that it is its privilege "to serve, console, comfort and encourage those with whom we have to do, to contribute our mite toward lessening the great mass of human sorrow, toward drying up the mighty overflowing stream of human tears."

In the little pamphlet containing a report for the fiscal year, April 1, 1873-April 1, 1874, Dr. Coit has stated the brief history and the purpose of the Home, as follows: 

The Orphans' Home was opened April 1, 1866, with six little children, whose plea was that they were homeless, friendless and needed Christian care. The number has gradually increased to twenty-three. There have been about thirty children in all at different times under our roof. The object is to train them for a useful life by giving them the elements of an English education, habits of industry, punctuality and obedience, and those principles which are profitable for this world as well as for the next. The Home is entirely dependent upon the voluntary gifts of the charitable for its support. Thus far, by the goodness of a kind Providence blessing

the work, and moving the hearts of good people, the yearly expenses have been met and no debts have been incurred. Miss Carter continues her earnest and faithful care of the children. She has been efficiently aided in the school by Miss Wheeler, the last six months.

The Home is about three miles from Concord. There are six acres of land, the Bishop of the diocese holding the property as sole corporator.

It was in a cottage still standing on the Hopkinton road, at the eastern corner of the Long Pond road, that the Home was opened on that first of April, 1866, with its six children. 

Miss Roxana Jones, from her home on Dimond Hill, came to take charge of them.

Meantime Dr. Coit was preparing for the purchase of the permanent site of the Home in the triangle between the new and old Dunbarton roads, where one looks down upon St. 

Paul's School and eastward towards the city of Concord. 

This lot of five and three-quarters acres was once a part of the Enoch Coffin pasture, which stretched back to Turkey River, Enoch Coffin being a resident of the city proper, living opposite the first meeting house of the town and the old cemetery. 

The order of events was as follows:

On February 14, 1829, the selectmen laid out a new highway from the house of Samuel Bradley, where the School cow-barns now are, past the present Upper School and across the pond, to Moses Bullen's house, which later was the first schoolhouse of St. Paul's School. The lot thus severed from its large pasture was deeded by Enoch Coffin on March 19, 1831, to Luther Dimond, who transferred it on March 16, 1833, to Waterman Dimond. On May 28, 1836, Waterman

Dimond, in order to provide a home for his parents, deeded to Abner Dimond, Jr., about a quarter of an acre at the apex of the triangle, where the roads meet. A small house was built there the hollow of whose cellar is plain to see. When this little homestead was no longer needed by the aged Dimonds it was purchased for his own protection by Charles Hall, descendant of Samuel Bradley, who removed the house

because it was so near his own. Years later the quarter of an acre was generously donated to the Home by Mrs. 

Harriet A. Hall, widow of Charles. 

Waterman Dimond, on September 16, 1841, deeded the main lot with its buildings to William F. Holt, a cutler. 

This title arrests our attention; without doubt he was employed at the old mill where we know that pocket knives were made at the time.

About fifteen years later, i.e., June 3, 1856, Holt deeded to 

Alfred W. Parker, which is arriving within the memory of the oldest inhabitant. Mr. Parker had the pioneer spirit of change and adventure. He came from Northfield to 

Fiske Road, from Fiske Road to the Orphans' Home hill, from which he went westward just beyond Turkey River, and from thence to Henniker, where he disappears from our view. He was always building or renovating a home. 

He sold to Dr. Henry A. Coit on February 8, 1867, and Dr. 

Coit, on May 29, 1871, to Bishop Niles, as a corporation sole, according to Dr. Coit's own statement in the printed report already quoted. Bishop Niles and Dr. Coit joined on 

December 8, 1875, in a final deed of the whole property to the trustees of the Orphans' Home. 

The site, thus happily chosen, had a history beyond that of Enoch Coffin's pasture. It had been the home of one of the very early Concord schools. The house stood on the south side of the lot, just west of the present entrance. 

Before the day of Long Pond water a great water tank stood there with its windmill. In digging for its foundation the bricks of the old schoolhouse chimney were disturbed. 

It was a very central location for the western part of the town. Hither came children from the Iron Works, Stickney

Hill, the Hopkinton road and the road leading to town. 

In these latter days they are again coming, but from all parts of New Hampshire.

It is a fair conjecture from the date of Mr. Parker's deed that it was in the Summer of 1867 that the removal was made from the Hopkinton road to the present home. At

a reunion in later years the journey was graphically described by one of the original six children as having been made in a hayrack under the direction of Mr. Woodbury Flanders. 

It is certain that in the season of 1868 the household was permanently established in Millville, as appears from a poem published in the Horae Scholasticae of St. Paul's 

School, Vol. 3, No. 3, dated December, 1868. The lines are entitled "The Orphans," and are signed C. P. P., which are the initials of Charles Pomeroy Parker, a student at the School and later at Oxford University, and then successively a master in the School and professor of Latin in Harvard University. Two extracts are given: 

THE ORPHANS 

The little Orphans who they are and where 

Their origin and home, O Muse, declare! 

And last, not least, O Heavenly Goddess sing 

The Sunday Night collection, direful spring 

Of woes unnumbered to the luckless wight 

Who carries money with him Sunday night. The mansion stands upon a lofty height 

Where all the School may see it day and night.

The forest tree its timbers strong affords To build the house with good substantial boards; 

Northward the building faces, and defies 

The wintry blasts that whistle through the skies,

While from its lofty lookout it commands 

A view of Millville and th' adjoining lands.

From the beginning, the boys of St. Paul's School took a keen interest in helping the work of the Home. A group of them, including Charles Parker, had, for the fun of the thing, formed a little club under the name of the Shaker Brotherhood, whose officers were Brother Nehemiah, Hezekiah, etc. 

It took their fancy to solicit money for the orphans, and one may see in the early printed reports mention of substantial sums which were handed to Dr. Coit by the "S. B. 

Society." The poem gives a hint of the way the money was gathered.

In June, 1874, an act of incorporation was obtained from the legislature, authorizing not only the support of needy children, but of the families of deceased clergymen, and of persons devoted to the relief of the sick and indigent. Of these three classes of beneficiaries the children only have thus far received the attention of the institution. The act is as follows: 

THE STATE OF NEW HAMPSHIRE 

In the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and seventy-four.

AN ACT TO INCORPORATE THE TRUSTEES OF THE ORPHANS' HOME AT CONCORD 

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives in General Court 

Convened: 

SECTION 1. That William W. Niles, Henry A. Coit, Joseph H. Coit, 

Josiah Minot, Albert R. Hatch, and their associates, are hereby incorporated and made a body politic under the name of the Trustees of the Orphans' Home of Concord. Said corporation may take, hold and manage such property as may be obtained by donation or otherwise not exceeding fifty thousand dollars, for the purpose of maintaining, educating or otherwise aiding children who have lost one or both parents, or are otherwise destitute, and of aiding and supporting the families of deceased clergymen, and of persons devoted to the care, nursing and relief of the sick and indigent. They may also receive all such minor children as may be bound to them under indenture by their surviving parent or guardian according to the laws of this state relating to master and apprentice and shall have the same rights and be subject to the same liabilities, in regard to children so bound, as other masters may lawfully have. 

SECTION 2. William W. Niles, Henry A. Coit and Joseph H. Coit, or any two of them, may call the first meeting of the corporation, at which, or any subsequent meeting, associates may be admitted, vacancies supplied, and by-laws established, not inconsistent with the laws of the state. 

SECTION 3. This act shall be subject to alteration, amendment or repeal, by the general court, and the same shall take effect and be in force from its passage. [Approved, June 26, 1874.] 

JAMES A. WESTON, ALBERT R. HATCH, 

WILLIAM H. GOVE,

Speaker of the House of Representatives. 

President of the Senate. Governor.

The first Board of Trustees, named in the act, were the Rt. Rev. W. W. Niles, Bishop of the diocese, Rev. Dr. Henry 

A. Coit, Rector, and Rev. Dr. Joseph H. Coit, Vice Rector, of St. Paul's School, Josiah Minot, a prominent lawyer and former judge, in Concord, and Albert R. Hatch, another prominent lawyer of the State, resident in Portsmouth. 

Bishop Niles was president, and Dr. Joseph Coit, Treasurer and Clerk.

At this date began the annual printed reports of the Home. 

The first meeting of the Trustees was held at the Home on September 30, 1874, at which officers were elected and bylaws adopted. The date of the annual meeting was at first set for the Wednesday following the last Tuesday in September.

At the meeting of 1875 a form of indenture was adopted for the commitment of minors, in which the parent covenanted not to interfere with the custody and control of a child committed to the Home for the purposes intended by its incorporation, so long as the Home chose to continue that custody and control, not to exceed, however, any age that might be specified in the contract.

In this early form there was no agreement on the part of parent or guardian to pay for the board and education of the child, the implication being plainly that they should be furnished free of expense. For many years children were committed under these agreements. They operated beneficially to check the sudden impulses of parents to snatch away their children for temporary and selfish and foolish reasons. At the same time it was always understood that they were not absolute and final, and in conceivable cases would not overrule the great principle of public law and policy which allows to parents the care of their own children. 

At the same meeting Mrs. Mary B. Coit, wife of Dr. 

Henry A. Coit, was appointed Assistant to the Treasurer; and she afterward was styled Treasurer of Current Expenses. 

During the year which ended in April, 1876, the Home lost by death Mrs. Clarissa Carter, a relative of the Lady in

Charge, who, despite increasing years, had given to the children and the work of the Home unwearied care. 

In the same period a fence of posts and boards was completed about the entire lot. It was a contribution from the boys of St. Paul's School, managed by the Shaker Brotherhood, which was still active in support of Dr. Coit's plans for the welfare of the Home. 

In 1877 it was decided to extend the fiscal year so as to reach September, 1878. This arrangement by which the business year ran from September 1 to September 1, and corresponded with the school year, continued for the next decade. 

By September, 1878, great improvements had been made in the buildings and therefore in the comforts of living. 

The original house was of two stories, with its broad side toward the new road. A long shed ran back to what originally was a barn but had been transformed into very useful living quarters; which building is still standing and known as the Old Schoolhouse. The improvements consisted in removing the shed, moving the main building backward so as to join the other, and adding a wing containing dormitory, kitchen, bathrooms and laundry.

With the increase in room there was some increase in number, so that, with twenty-five children, Dr. Coit could say in 1879 that the number had never been so large. Mr. 

Woodbury Flanders was helping to improve the grounds and to teach the boys to be useful in such work as was fitted to them, especially in the vegetable garden. 

At the end of the year 1879 an attempt was made to enlist a more general aid from the women of the diocese by establishing a board of twelve lady managers, to be nominated by the Bishop and approved by the Trustees. Their duties were: to visit the Home; to give their sympathy and encouragement to the Lady in Charge; to make such suggestions and recommendations as might seem helpful; to enkindle an interest in the work within their parishes and neighborhoods; and to study and devise means of supporting the wants of the Home.

Undoubtedly good resulted from this device; but the names of the lady managers are not found on the annual reports or other records. It is probable that they served, according to their individual interest and activity, as a visiting committee, and did bring much sympathy and encouragement to the rather isolated workers at the Home.

Besides the money which has been recapitulated there were, of course, and have been always, numerous gifts in kind, which in 1880 filled over four pages. One of these was "a brown cloth sacque" from some Miss. Another was "10 pounds coffee, 5 bushels potatoes, one dozen cans tomatoes, one barrel of Graham flour, from the Sixth Form, 11

St. Paul's School." These are but samples of the great variety both in value and bulk of the contributions which are annually received. Whether it is a set of toys from a child or a barrel of apples from a farmer, these gifts are all precious, not only for the pleasure they bring but for the thoughtful interest and love which they show in the wide community of the State. 

In the year 1881 the number of children was twenty-eight. 

The Women's Auxiliary, recently organized in the diocese, having made the Home one of its special objects of interest, had begun to send boxes of clothing and other useful articles. 

These and other such contributions were sorted and arranged and served as a storehouse from which to draw supplies at all seasons.

In the following year Miss Mary J. Millard is recorded as being added to the staff. Her cheerful and efficient help came to be highly appreciated in the years that followed. 

She showed a truly marvellous skill in fitting the stores of accumulated gifts to all occasions and needs. It must be admitted, however, that in later days when taste in dress was quite generally diffused some of her adaptations might have drawn a faint protest. 

On December 26, 1882, John L. Farwell of Claremont was elected a Trustee, to fill the vacancy caused by the death of 

Albert R. Hatch of Portsmouth. Mr. Hatch was a prominent lawyer of the State, whose knowledge and advice had always been at the service of the Home, although it had never been necessary for him to give much of his time to the details of its work. 

By the death of Miss Sarah L. E. Carter on July 7, 1883, after a service of fifteen years as head of the Home, a great loss came to the household. She was born in Savannah, Ga., the daughter of the Rev. Abiel Carter, an Episcopal clergyman. Her mother was the daughter of the Rev. 

Abraham Beach, an eminent divine of his day in New York.

Since her parents died when she was very young, she was adopted by her uncle, Dr. Moses Carter, of Amesbury,

MISS SARAH L. E. CARTER

Mass., who afterward removed to Concord, where others of her kindred had lived. She was the granddaughter of the 

Jacob Carter who once owned the mills on Turkey River and built the brick mansion in which St. Paul's School began its life. Of a truly religious nature, Miss Carter was wholly devoted to the Home and took the place of a true mother to all the children under her charge. She sought no other interest or diversion, and bequeathed all her earthly possessions, which were considerable, to aid in carrying on the work which she was called to leave. Her loss was long looked upon as irreparable.

The place of Miss Carter was filled, at the meeting of the Trustees in December 1883, by the choice of Miss Alice D. 

Jack, of St. John, N. B.

A passage from the report of 1884 will bring an approving smile to those who are working with children today. It shows that the elements of human nature are pretty constant from generation to generation:

In too many cases the children are sought as hired workers, from whom much is expected, much demanded, with small allowance for their years, their antecedents, and their peculiarities of temper and disposition, and with very little care of that moral and religious discipline which alone can supply the motive and power of selfcontrol. The motherless need mothers and the fatherless need fathers, and we cannot expect that at the age of say fourteen, fifteen, or sixteen, our girls, whom we have been teaching and helping for a few years only, will come forth wholly bent upon hard work, with the wise thoughtfulness of full-grown women, and with no love of dress and no desire for occasional amusement and excitement, from which even their seniors are not always exempt. 

In the year 1885 the Rev. W. S. Emery of St. Paul's 

School held a short service and gave instruction on Wednesdays, during term time. In the winter that began the year, seven cases of scarlet fever were successfully managed with the very kind and able attendance of Dr. F. A. Stillings. 

They were followed in the summer by whooping cough, which went through the family quite impartially. 

In September of this year Miss Jack was obliged by serious

illness to withdraw from her work as head of the household. 

But in the short time of her direction of affairs she had won the entire respect and confidence of all interested in the Home. She carried away the assurance of their affectionate remembrance. 

In June, 1886, Miss Frances C. Chapman of Sackville,

N. B., succeeded to the position vacated by Miss Jack. 

The Rev. Thomas J. Drumm of St. Paul's School took up in the year 1887 Mr. Emery's work, and held a very useful short service with instruction every Wednesday afternoon of the School year.

Mrs. D. C. Roberts of St. Paul's Church and other ladies were successful in raising money for the much needed laundry. From about twenty parishes in the State $425.87 was contributed.

The year 1888 was fraught with change. Early in January Miss Chapman resigned her charge as head of the house after a wise and efficient administration and to the regret of her associates and the children. Until November, when 

Miss Fanny M. Keyes of Ashland took the position of 

Matron, the work was carried on by Miss Millard, assisted by Miss Charlotte G. Ide and Miss Mary A. E. Stockett. 

Both Miss Ide and Miss Stockett had a long and useful connection with the Home. The former came from Claremont where her family had been identified with church work. 

Miss Stockett came from Maryland. She was for a long period in charge of the boys, and many are the tales of the firmness with which she held them to the various duties that were expected of them. No one who knew the Home of her day can forget the dignity with which she marshalled her flock into chapel and disposed them in their seats. She had a stately presence and an eye to threaten and command. 

The death of Mrs. Mary Bowman Coit on August 12, 1888, was a loss which it was hard to measure. She had been more than Treasurer of Current Expenses for the twenty years of the Home. Both Dr. Henry and Dr. Joseph 

Coit have spoken of her as its virtual founder. For the

MRS. MARY BOWMAN COIT

helpfulness of her example it is certainly fitting to quote some of the words written of her when her loss was fresh: 

Her loving heart and wise and calm judgment began the work, and continued it with patience and watchful interest from the day it was opened in April, 1866. The stream of her gifts has flowed unceasingly from the first. It was her daily habit to think and care and do for others, and the Home had a large share in her loving interest. To her the ladies in charge, especially our dear and unforgotten friend, Miss Carter, always turned for a sympathy which was unfailing, and for advice which could be safely trusted and followed. Her perfect humility and self-forgetfulness, joined to an unvarying sweetness of temper and regard for the feelings of others, made her always just and kind in thinking and speaking of those with whom she was associated. Children were won by her, for they have penetration to see who really care for them; and she had a brightness of mind and quickness of perception which gave her insight into their peculiarities. 

The Rev. Mr. Drumm continued his services on Wednesday afternoons, and in addition took on from this date the responsible duty of Treasurer of Current Expenses. 

A large, new brick building, later known as the Girls' House, was begun in the summer of 1888 from plans of Dow and Wheeler, of Concord, and much progress was made upon it in this season.

The fiscal year of 1889 was extended to November 1 to bring it into conformity with that of the diocese. There is a close connection between the two, since the printed report of the diocese contains also that of the Home. 

The great event of this year was the completion of the new building at a total cost of $9,836.44. The laundry, dormitories, store closets and other accommodations were quite complete, for the time and location. The heating was by steam. A large well was dug, and a tank, windmill and pump were installed, by which water was led into the house. Mr. Isaac A. Hill made a valuable contribution to the Home by giving his care and oversight to the work from the beginning to its end.

1890-1899 'XPERIENCE having shown that to accomplish the 

EXP 

ends aimed at by the Home love should be the motive power, a system of rewards and prizes was adopted in 1890.. 

Every child's faithful work received a small pecuniary recompense, graded according to age and capacity. It was felt that this for the most part worked well and gave the children a new motive for being diligent and thorough. 

It was noticeable this year that benefactions took largely the form of goods and merchandise. While contributions of money increased and filled three pages of the annual report, those of useful articles increased still more and required ten pages of close type to record them. In these days a barrel of flour is seldom seen by a family, but then it was a common gift to the Home, together with fruits, hams, and all necessary articles of house furnishing. 

From thirty to thirty-five children were cared for in 1890. 

The permanent funds of the institution now amounted to $10,050, and a commodious new building, completed and equipped, was occupied by the household. 

Early in 1891 Miss Keyes returned to Ashland "at the call of filial duty," and greatly to the regret of every one. 

Besides being postmistress in Ashland Miss Keyes had a fine and long record there as a school-teacher. At the time of her departure from the Home no one suspected that her own hold upon life was so slender. She died in Ashland, at the home of her father, on December 14, 1891. Her place as head of the Home was filled on June 29 by Mrs. Sarah G. 

Moreland, aunt of the then Rector of the Church of the 

Good Shepherd at Nashua. There were thirty-five childrensixteen boys and nineteen girls-with Miss Ide in sole charge in the schoolroom after Mrs. Moreland's arrival. 

Mr. Drumm's useful services as Chaplain ceased, and he 16

was succeeded by the Rev. James P. Conover, also of St.

Paul's School. 

In this year an interesting report was made to the Trustees by Mrs. Bertha O. Niles for a committee appointed by the 

Bishop as official visitors of the Home. The committee consisted of Mrs. Niles, Mrs. Schutz, Mrs. O. Morrill, Miss 

Edith Minot, Mrs. L. D. Stevens, Mrs. Nathaniel White, Jr., and Mrs. Bethune. They were confident that from their forty-three visits, made within eighteen months, much benefit had accrued, not only in encouragement given to the workers in the Home, but from the increased knowledge gained by the committee of conditions there and of the many needs still existing. These had largely been supplied through their instrumentality. 

The fortnightly meetings of the Women's Auxiliary were held at this time at the Home, to the pleasure and profit of all. The system of prizes was continued throughout the year, and, it was thought, with good results. 

Hon. Josiah Minot, an original Trustee and friend of the 

Home, died December 14, 1891. His death deserves more than a passing notice, since he ranked among the ablest lawyers and most astute business men of the State. Graduated from Dartmouth College at the age of nineteen, he became judge of the Court of Common Pleas at thirty-three, and resigned from the bench to receive the appointment of Commissioner of Pensions from his friend and law-partner, President Pierce. In later years he was Treasurer of the 

Northern Railroad, and President of the Concord Railroad and of the Mechanicks National Bank. His interest in the 

Home never ceased, and his generous gifts have been continued by members of his family to the present time. 

At the close of 1892 thirty-seven children were under the care of the Home. 

On November 16, 1892, John H. Pearson, of Concord, was elected a Trustee. Like Judge Minot, whose place he filled, he was a very prominent citizen of the State, who took a leading part in railroad direction and control.

He also had experience with newspaper ownership and with manufacturing. 

The main improvement of the year was the purchase of iron bedsteads for the dormitories, and window screens for the whole house.

In the year 1893 the average number of children was forty-two.

Much attention was given to the improvement of school accommodations. The old room was enlarged by opening another into it; and by fresh paint and paper all was made pleasant and attractive. And since the number of children had outgrown the easy mastery of one teacher, Mrs. Sarah 

E. H. Clark came to the work for five days in the week from her home in the neighborhood.

At this time the lack of sufficient water in the house was felt as a hardship, and serious effort was directed to the problem of securing it from the city. 

The year 1894 found forty-five children resident at the Home, with Mrs. Clark, as before, supplying the additional teaching, Mrs. Moreland, housemother, Miss Millard indefatigable in her large province, and Miss Ide and Miss Stockett faithful and assiduous in their efforts. This is substantially the summary made by Dr. Coit in his annual report.

Through the action of the city government and the water board, Long Pond water had been led to the Home on its devious path of about five miles to a point about two miles from its source. For the speedy accomplishment of this desirable object, and other difficult matters of business at this time, the Trustees owned themselves largely indebted to the energy of their associate, Mr. John H. Pearson. 

The expenses of the household were necessarily increasing, and the management looked eagerly for increasing revenue. 

Had it not been for a successful sale made at the Home by the Women's Auxiliary in February, the two ends could hardly, for that year, have been made to meet. 

At this period, by bequest or purchase, the Home was in

possession of several farm mortgages in payment of which there had been default or the fear of it. A special messenger was sent to Kansas and Colorado to ascertain facts which could not be known here.

On February 5, 1895, the Home lost its great friend, Dr. 

Henry A. Coit. How vivid is the recollection of that bitter winter day of his funeral, when friends of St. Paul's School came from all points to show respect to his memory and to assist in that last office of love and reverence!

For the first time in over twenty years the annual report was penned by another hand than his own. From it a few words are here taken:

From the day when the first matron, with seven or eight little children, took possession of the house he had secured for the work, April 4, 1866, until the last week of his life, Dr. Coit carried the Home in his heart. His interest in it and care for it never waned. He gave time, money, sympathy, counsel, and pitiful concern, year after year, as freely at the end as at the beginning. Engrossed as he was by grave cares and responsibilities, with every hour of his day filled up with the important duties of his office, he still found or made opportunities for constant visits, for watchful supervision of all details; and was never too busy to give a hearing to those who had the charge of the institution, and to aid them by counsel, by sympathy, and when necessary supply their needs from his own purse. 

Wonderful was his compassionate interest in the children. He knew them all by name, the history of each one, the traits and character of each one, and not a month passed without tokens coming from him of deep and tender regard for them. He himself looked after their religious instruction, baptized them himself, and for many years prepared the older ones for confirmation and the Holy Communion. . . . The Home will not soon forget him- -we shall not soon see his like again among mortal men; but we shall cherish his memory, and take from his grave the inspiration of a faithful service of God and of a genuine love of our fellow men.

Mrs. Clark, after being for several years a most capable and acceptable teacher, was obliged by the state of her health to withdraw from the work. Her place was supplied by Miss M. F. Kallam, who shared the duties of instruction with Miss Ide.

In this year a fire-escape was attached to the main building, and some improvement was made in the condition of fences and gates. 

On November 25, 1895, the Rev. Dr. Joseph H. Coit, having become Rector of St. Paul's School, resigned his offices of Clerk and Treasurer of the Home. The Rev. Charles W. 

Coit, son of the late Dr. Henry A. Coit and the Rev. 

Thomas J. Drumm, both of St. Paul's School, were elected 

Trustees. Mr. Drumm was elected Clerk and Treasurer.

At a special meeting of the board on January 24, 1896, Hiram A. Tuttle, of Pittsfield, a former Governor of New 

Hampshire, was elected a Trustee. 

In August, 1896, Miss Charlotte G. Ide, after many years of devoted labor for the children, relinquished her duties and retired from the Home. 

At the close of this year there were thirty-four children in the Home. There were forty-two within the year. It was with regret that several meritorious applications were refused for lack of accommodations and funds. The failure of some farm mortgages to pay either principal or interest brought real embarrassment.

The year 1897 was made memorable by the erection of the Boys' House, from designs by Henry Vaughan, of Boston, the distinguished architect, whose remains now rest in the crypt of the Washington Cathedral. The upper floor of the three-storied brick building, which adjoins the main building or Girls' House on the west, is devoted to a chapel, very complete and suitable for all the uses of the Home. The lower stories contain dormitories, common rooms both for larger and smaller boys, and other rooms. This noble benefaction came from the Rev. Charles W. Coit, although for a time his name was withheld from public knowledge in connection with it. It was given as a memorial of his parents, Dr. Henry A. Coit and Mary Bowman Coit, whom he considered as joint founders of the institution. Mr. 

A. H. Campbell, of St. Paul's School, gave close attention and superintendence to the entire work of construction.

At the same time Mr. John H. Pearson set aside the equivalent of $25,000 in railroad securities as a gift to the permanent endowment. The regular income from this fund amounts to $1,050 yearly, though there have been periods of suspended payment in times of railroad depression. 

Since the death of Mr. Pearson the fund has been held by the Trustees of the John H. Pearson Trust. This addition to annual income was a great encouragement to the work of the place, and ever since has remained so. 

It was also in this year that Mrs. Charles Hall gave to the 

Home the quarter of an acre at the apex of its triangular lot, where the old and new roads meet.

No account of the Orphans' Home would be complete that omitted mention of the unpleasant events of May, 1912, although the printed report of that year does omit them altogether. Certain facts of discipline became widely known in the city and aroused a warm feeling of indignation even among prominent and sober-minded citizens, not to speak of the noisy demonstrations made by those of another class. 

Some out-of-town newspapers gave their attention to the matter for several days. 

A boy was severely whipped for theft. It was not done in anger but with a conscientious purpose to do what was right both for the boy and the morale of the Home. There had been in the past other cases of discipline probably as severe. 

But for a brief time general attention was so concentrated upon this as to bring about a public hearing of the case in the local court. All the facts were fully shown. After argument by the County Solicitor and other counsel and the citation of precedents, the case was dismissed by the court as furnishing no ground whatever for legal action. At the trial, however, it was frankly admitted for the Home that a mistake of judgment had been made. And Bishop Parker, by a candid statement published in the Concord Monitor, made it clear that the Home had no thought, under a puritanical sense of

responsibility, of pursuing any harsh system of punishments. 

With his healing words public demonstration and criticism ceased.

The public was unquestionably right, and its feeling thus openly expressed had an instant and lasting effect.

The annual report for 1915 contained an instructive statement by Miss Harriman, the Matron, of her aims and accomplishments during the two years in which she had been head of the household. It showed beyond question that in physical well-being, in recreational, industrial and educational opportunities there had been a happy progress. The annual reunion of alumni was repeated and thus became an accepted and welcome event.

At this time there was an increasing zeal in devising better ways of bringing up dependent children. Societies were springing up for study and practice in this field. A new literature came into being, full of a philosophy quite beyond the stolid ideas of condescending charity. It called upon psychology to study the very mind and soul of the child, and upon psychiatry to heal the wounds or diseases discovered by that study. It frowned upon institutionalism, that is, the gathering of many such children into one house where they ran the risk of being handled en masse, and of losing individuality, diversity of interest and personal ambition. In the autumn of 1915 the Church Flyleaf published an 

Orphans' Home number in which Miss Harriman gave a graphic account of a day at the Home. A day now would be different for several reasons: The children go to the public schools. There is more paid help in the kitchen. Then the oldest girl (of sixteen) rose at 5.45, to prepare the ladies' breakfast and the cereal for the children, and at 6.30 she rang the rising bell. The older children dressed and helped the younger into their proper clothes. It was a busy scene, for there were many things for fifty children to do. After breakfast, at 7, the children scattered to their several tasks, all scheduled and known: washing the breakfast dishes, sweeping up the dining room, cleaning the ice-box, picking up in the basement, making dormitory beds, doing the halls and stairs. Meanwhile the boys had their appropriate duties, in the chapel, dormitories, stairs, hall and cellar, not to forget the wood-pile.

A chapel service of fifteen minutes began at 8.30, to which the girls marched in line, followed by the boys. Then all to school until 12.15, except for a fifteen-minute recess, with a cracker or two and an apple for each child. The oldest girl has spent her morning in the kitchen helping to get the dinner, which is ready at 12.15, and which four girls come directly from school to put on the table; all sit down at 12.30. 

From 1 to 2 a squad of boys do the dishes and put the dining-

room in order, while girls are mending in the Girls' House and Boys' House. At 2 the smallest children have their nap, and the others go to school for an hour and a half. 

From 3.30 until supper at 6 is the regular play time, while for the individual boy or girl there would be many other bits of time at their command. Outdoors, gymnastic apparatus, including swings, trapezes, rings, horizontal bars, ladders, etc., were in constant use by someone except when all were in bed, at the table or school. 

Miss Harriman fills in a multitudinous detail, but we can imagine most of it-the hockey, coasting, berry picking, swimming, the cooking classes, Mr. Trask's carpentry classes; on rainy days, the stamp collections, games, marbles, the store; and on Sunday evenings the singing in the Common Room.

Those who have seen it closely know how wholesome the life is, how well ordered, how punctual to hour and minute, and, in its entirety, how well adapted to promote a co-operative disposition and character.

October 8, 1917, occurred the great fire. It was Monday, which is wash day. Some shavings and light wood were thrown on the fire, in the basement of the Girls' House, in order to force steam for the laundry. The strong draft would easily carry some of this up the chimney and drop it on the roof. This is believed to be the cause of the fire, which appeared first on the roof of that house when the household had seated themselves for dinner; for the roof was of shingles and not of slate. It is true that the fire was attributed at the time to defective insulation of electric wiring in the attic, which was full of stored clothing and other combustible material. Whichever was the source it progressed rapidly, so that by half-past twelve the roof and attic were burning fiercely. Though a hydrant was close by, the pressure was so weak that at first only an intermittent stream could be brought to play. But after a while hose was laid from the high-pressure hydrant near the Upper School and the fire was brought under control. At three o'clock it was entirely extinguished. 

One can imagine the speed with which the boys of St. 

Paul's School arrived on the scene and strewed their outer clothing on the fence and bushes. While the roof and upper story were blazing they helped to save a prodigious amount of property from the two lower floors before the fire gutted them, which it finally did. Sparks innumerable rose and many fell upon the wooden building called the Old School

House. The boys swarmed over that building as though the roof were flat, and put out every incipient blaze. One of them was found trying to chop a hole through the roof at that point, though his reason was never known. It is due to the boys of St. Paul's School that the Old School House is standing today.

The Superintendent and her assistants, as well as the Concord Fire Department, received the formal thanks of the Trustees for their careful and unflagging attention to all the details of their duty on that trying day. 

The Girls' House was a wreck, but all else was safe including the beautiful Roche Annex, as yet unused, and with its varnish hardly dry. Within a fortnight plans by Mr.

Since there had been in late years some readjustment of

duties amongst the numerous officials of the institution, the President thought it expedient to state in his report for 1918 the then existing method of work, as follows: 

The Superintendent has the full charge of the management of the Home, of the selection of workers, of the purchasing of food, supplies and clothes, and of the admission and placing out of children. 

Seven ladies, appointed annually by the Trustees, form a Board of Managers, who assume the routine management of the Home and its expenses.

There is a Visiting Committee, six from the City of Concord and six from St. Paul's School, appointed yearly by the Trustees, and twelve from congregations outside of Concord, four being appointed each year for a three year term by the Bishop. Each visitor is expected to visit the Home at least once every year, to report suggestions and criticisms to the Board of Managers, and to interest her own community in the work and needs of the Home as far as possible. 

There is a Chaplain appointed annually by the Trustees, who is responsible for the spiritual welfare of the children, and a Treasurer to care for the invested funds, gifts, and receipts, and to pay all bills and salaries.

The Board of Trustees is the ultimate authority in all matters that concern the work and welfare of the Home.

In writing the annals of the Home it seems necessary to record, as has just been done, the changes that occur for a year among those who are carrying on its work or contributing toward it. But the real history of the Home lies in the lives of the children who inhabit it, in their growth and development. We get some vivid glimpses of these things from Miss Malcom's first report of a year of experience as Superintendent.

Here are six children whom she enables us to see distinctly:

Three were taken into the house in January from a farm. 

There was no fire in their home. The mother was in a hospital with a young baby, the father at work, but at a distance. The youngest, a girl of four, had been taken into a neighbor's house from the roadside with her feet frozen. 

Immediately they were surrounded with every comfort. 

The Home took guardianship of these children and pledged itself to "stand between them and circumstances beyond their childish powers to change or improve. 99 

Another, a girl of eight years, had lost her mother, and her father was dying in a sanitarium. Two years before she had been placed by the father with a friend and his wife. 

In this home she was made to wash clothes, and scrub floors, and failing to please was whipped with a razor-strap. 

The Orphans' Home found a real home for this child. 

Next is the case of a little boy who had been in the Home seven years, shy, sensitive, high-spirited, to whom no one had ever been able to get near. A foster home was found for him where he is happy and making his foster parents happy.

An older child, a girl of fourteen, had been in the Home twelve years. She had twice been placed out as "mother's help," and twice had signally failed to give satisfaction. 

But having a special talent, a wealthy lady was glad to take her and give her all advantages of culture and training to develop it.

These cases illustrate the inner life of the Home, the delicacy and diversity of its problems, and they seem to justify all the interest and generosity of the great public who support the devoted people that are dealing with them. 

The beginning of the technical work on which the Home had entered is thus described in the annual report of 1922. 

The first psychological work began in July and ended in September, 1922. This survey was based upon the Stanford-Binet Scale, supplemented by the Myers Mental Measure group test. In all thirtysix children were examined individually, and a group test was given to twenty-two children who were available at one time.

The tests showed what was literally true, that not one of the thirtysix children had advanced standing in school. One case showed a retardation in school of four years, but the history of the child showed his case to be one of neglect in a bad environment. Another case showed five years of school retardation due to mental inferiority. 

Several children show a slight lack of intelligence, but the majority are normal, and with proper care and kindly encouragement should advance mentally as other children. 

Miss Rose S. Hardwick, A.M., who made these tests, devoted painstaking effort to her work. She highly favors the continuance of the placing-out system adopted by the Trustees. 

We cannot forget, in connection with this work, the very splendid co-operation of the late Dr. Frederick H. Knight, Superintendent of the New England Home for Little Wanderers, who offered and provided opportunity in that institution, free of charge, for the further study of special "problem children."

At this time or a little subsequently Coit House was aided in the study of these special children by the following psychologists: Dr. F. L. Wells, Miss Margaret S. Child, Mrs. G. W. Allport, and Miss Elizabeth M. Hincks.

The name of Coit House, perpetuating like that of Harvard College or Yale College, the name of an honored founder, without implying in itself anything of charity or dependence, has proved a satisfactory answer to the desire that has long been felt for a title more pleasing than that borne by the original charter.

Principally because of the influence and leadership of Dr. and Mrs. Drury, the Home had for several years been making a study of the more modern methods of child care. Steps already taken in this direction gave confidence in the practical wisdom of the movement. At one of the first reunions

of former children an old boy of the Home, now become a Boston physician and college lecturer, reproached us for backwardness, and confidently advocated the placing out of children in private homes. His remarks were unfavorably received at the time. But at the date at which our narrative has arrived the placing-out system was in full force. If objections to it were still entertained they had ceased to be urged, and the organization as a unit moved forward to a hopeful future.

Unquestionably some good was relinquished in giving up the older method. A longer period of childhood spent at the Home under the watchful care of kind and cultivated ladies, with the weekly services of the Chaplain in the beautiful chapel of the Home, and the intimate connection with the old chapel of St. Paul's School and the Rector and the clergy there, could not fail to make many and happy impressions on youthful characters. Modern students of child psychology, however, were placing more stress on the value of normal home life, or that of the closest substitute for it, than upon institutional and religious influences, however superior they might seem to be. And of course it is always true that the placing-out home may be selected with reference to religious advantages. 

Mr. C. C. Carstens, Executive Director of the Child

Welfare League of America, became well known by his public addresses and by personal acquaintance to officers of Coit House. His wise counsel has been much sought in these gradual advances in child management toward a method more sympathetic toward the instinct of the child itself.

Parker's connection with Coit House began when he was a small boy in St. Paul's School and became a member of the famous Shaker Brotherhood, which solicited contributions for the Home on Sunday evenings. He remembered that connection with pride. As master and clergyman at the

He was early trained by his ministry among the rural people of Dunbarton and Weare in the gift of sympathetic and tolerant counsel. The simplicity of the request provokes a smile. But in all such interviews and occasions Bishop Parker was a true friend and helper, as he proved to be in all the multitude of problems that presented themselves to those having charge of the welfare of Coit House.

A request was made in December, 1928, by Miss Ruth Berolzheimer, Agent for the Child Welfare League of America, that Coit House, considering itself as a member agency, should contribute annually to the support of the League a quota of one per cent of its operating budget. This would have been a considerable amount. At a meeting on 

January 10, 1929, after discussion of the subject, the Trustees could not bring themselves to acquiesce in the proposal or to promise more than the annual membership fee of $25. 

A prospect was opened at this time of an extension of the gas line to and beyond Coit House. Authority was given to introduce gas into the house for cooking and other purposes; and this was done early in the autumn. 

At this meeting authority was also given to join in a petition for the discontinuance of the highway on the east and north sides of the Coit House land, since it lay altogether within the territory of Coit House and St. Paul's School and was not a part of any through line of traffic. 

This pamphlet history of Coit House is also owing to a vote passed at the same meeting which requested the writer to prepare it for publication within the space of two years. 

Miss Knox, of the N. H. Children's Aid and Protective 

Society, and Mr. Carstens and Mr. Cheney Jones, of the 

Child Welfare League of America, were present on this occasion and were principal contributors toward an interesting examination into the working of Coit House and an appraisal of its possibilities as a Study Home. A month later it was voted to adopt in a general way the recommendations of Mr. Jones and Mr. Carstens for equipping 

Coit House as a Study Home by the employment of a psychiatrist, a salaried physician, and an additional social worker, with a motor car. In accordance with this vote certain improvements in quarantine and other arrangements of the house were made to prepare for the enlargement of the work, and the psychiatrist began her interesting task. 

In brief, the present procedure, as stated by the Superintendent, is to give entering children a psychometric

examination to determine their mental capacity; and, if of sufficient age, vocational tests to show the trend of their aptitudes. The so called "problem child" receives in addition a psychiatric examination to ascertain, if possible, the springs of emotional unbalance. After psychometric and psychiatric examinations, if both are necessary, comes the endeavor to find the right foster home for the individual child.

By virtue of the by-laws of Coit House which provide that the Bishop of the diocese shall be president of its board of 

Trustees, the Rt. Rev. John T. Dallas became a Trustee upon his election to that office; and he has been the President of the institution and the warm friend and promoter of its work since 1926.

In closing this imperfect sketch of the life of Coit House the writer cannot withhold his tribute of admiration to the motive and spirit of those its founders and early friends who gave the institution a place in the community and guided its work with so much of unselfish and truly Christian devotion. 

That work has outlived most of them and must outlive those who are carrying it on today. May it gather friends from year to year, and, with that of kindred societies, enlist and hold fast the interest of all generous-hearted people! For it is, without all peradventure, a precious work.
