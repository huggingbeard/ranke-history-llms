FOREWORD 

The Church has always favored stability of the pastoral office. 

In the code she provides that those who are appointed for the government of a parish should be established permanently in that office. And although she admits two degrees of pastoral stability, she implicitly shows her preference for the more stable pastorate. 

This may be gathered from two considerations. In the first place, while she allows removable pastors to be declared irremovable by the bishop with the advice of his chapter (or diocesan consultors) she insists that only with her permission may irremovable pastors be declared removable. And secondly, she lays down that all new parishes must be considered irremovable pastorates unless the bishop after taking counsel with his chapter (or diocesan consultors) has, owing to peculiar circumstances, made them removable. 

However, no pastor whether removable or irremovable enjoys absolute permanence of office. The salvation of the people is the supreme law; it is the chief reason for the very stability of the pastoral office. Hence, a pastor's right to a permanent incumbency must yield to the salvation of the souls committed to his charge. In other words, he may be removed from his pastoral office. 

The pastor may not be summarily removed at the mere whim or caprice of his ordinary. He has rights which are surrounded by numerous safeguards-provisions demanded or suggested by the natural law and supplemented by the positive ecclesiastical law. 

Not only must there be a sufficient reason for removal, but also a special process has to be faithfully followed, varying according as the pastor is irremovable or removable. And lest there might be any danger of arbitrary procedure, the Holy See has granted the pastor the right of final recourse to itself. 

The present work consists of two parts: the first, an historical conspectus tracing the development of the legislation on removal from its vague form in the Decretals to its mature form in the Code; and the second, a commentary explaining and interpreting the law as it exists today.

CHAPTER I NOTION AND CHARACTER OF THE ADMINISTRATIVE REMOVAL OF PASTORS ARTICLE 1 

Notion

Administrative removal or, as it is also called, economic or disciplinary removal in general may be defined ¹ as: 1 An act of the legitimate ecclesiastical authority by which a beneficed cleric, when there is a grave cause, i. e., the good of souls, is removed from the possession of his benefice without any juridical solemnities and is transferred to another benefice or ecclesiastical office or is given a suitable pension..

The administrative removal of a pastor, then, is an act of the ordinary by which a pastor for a grave cause is removed from his parish without a trial in the strict sense and is transferred to another parish or is given some ecclesiastical office or a suitable pension.

This definition will become clear upon analysis. (a) Administrative removal is an "act of the ordinary." 

The term ordinary here is taken in the sense of canon 198. It includes not only the Roman Pontiff but also, within their respective territories, a residential bishop, an abbot and a prelate nullius, their vicar general, an administrator, vicar apostolic and all those who by the provisions of law or of approved constitutions succeed during a vacancy to any of these offices. (b) A pastor. In this context a pastor is a priest (not a moral person) on whom has been conferred the title of a parish, removable or irremovable, with the care of souls to be exercised under the authority of the local ordinary. The term also includes quasipastors of the secular clergy, parochial vicars of a non-religious moral person and ordinary assistants who have a benefice.2

ARTICLE 2 

Distinctive Features

A still clearer notion of administrative removal will be obtained by contrasting it with judicial deprivation and transfer. Deprivation in general is defined as: An act by which a cleric is dispossessed of his office. It may be incurred either ipso facto or by an act of the legitimate Superior, i. e., ab homine." 

Deprivation takes place ipso jure when it is inflicted by the law itself, i. e., when it is incurred by the very act of committing the crime to which deprivation is attached as a penalty; it is ab homine when it is decreed by the authority of a superior or applied either by a condemnatory sentence or by a particular decree.10 

Considered in the manner in which a deprivation ab homine is applied it is judicial, economic or simple. Judicial or processual deprivation is one in which the rules of a trial, or at least of a process as outlined in titles XXX-XXXII of the Fourth Book of the Code, are followed and in which the deprivation itself possesses the character of a vindicative penalty. Economic administrative or disciplinary is that which is applied by means of an economic or administrative process. This method was instituted precisely for the good of souls and is not necessarily intended as a punishment. Judicial or processual deprivation is deprivation in the strict sense; economic non-penal deprivation is removal. The third, simple deprivation, is that which a superior for a just cause and in natural equity may apply without the observance of any formalities.11

Since simple deprivation has reference only to removable offices which are not benefices, it has no bearing on this subject.12 

Deprivation of the pastoral office (using deprivation in the strict sense) is a vindicative penalty either incurred ipso facto or inflicted by the sentence of a judge for grave crimes specifically mentioned in the law as depriving the pastor of his parish.

By comparing the definitions of deprivation and removal the points in which they differ will be more clearly seen. How do they differ? (a) Suppose a parish is irremovable. The pastor can be deprived of that parish only because of certain grave crimes which are set forth specifically in the Code.¹³ Some of these crimes by the very commission of them bring deprivation; others need the sentence of a judge. The pastor can be removed from that parish for any cause which renders his ministry in it harmful, or at least inefficacious, apart from his personal guilt or innocence.14 Suppose now that the parish is removable. The pastor can be deprived of such a parish not only in those cases mentioned specifically for an irremovable pastor, but also for other reasonable causes.¹ Insofar as the reasons for removal are concerned he is on exactly the same footing as the irremovable pastor. 15 (b) Deprivation is a vindicative penalty the primary purpose of which is the punishment of the pastor and the restoration of the public order disturbed by his crime. Removal is not directly nor usually intended as a punishment; its proper and immediate purpose is the welfare of souls. Since removal resembles transfer in some respects and may therefore be mistaken for it, it will make for clarity of ideas to compare them. Reiffenstuel,20 defines transfer as:" The change of a cleric from one office or benefice to another, made for a just cause by legitimate ecclesiastical authority." It implies two things, namely, the loss of the office or benefice from which one is changed and the acquisition of the new office to which one is changed. Transfer may be voluntary, as it is when the cleric gives his consent, or compulsory when, for a just cause, it is forced upon a cleric who, whether innocent or guilty, is unwilling and refuses to be changed.21 

Compulsory transfer is either penal, or economic, or simple. It is penal when it is inflicted as a vindicative penalty after a judicial process, and when at the same time there is a demotion involved; 22 economic when it takes place after an administrative process; simple if the rules of natural equity are observed. In this treatment only compulsory economic transfer will be considered. Transfer of a pastor is, as defined by Cocchi,23 "An administrative act of the competent ordinary by which a pastor, for the good of souls, is changed to another parish from one which he administered successfully." Administrative removal at first sight may appear somewhat like transfer and hence it is necessary to make a closer examination of the two concepts. (a) The most obvious difference between transfer and administrative removal is in the general cause which justifies recourse to each. Transfer looks to the good of the parish to which the pastor is to be changed, removal to the good of the parish, from which he

CHAPTER II THE STABILITY OF THE PASTORAL OFFICE 

Before treating of the history of removal there are certain historical facts which it will be well to recall. Removal and the stability of the pastoral office are so intimately connected that the history of the one can be adequately understood only in the light of the other. The intimate connection between stability and removal and the importance of stability in relation to removal may be gathered from this fact which has always to be borne in mind, namely, that up to the promulgation of the decree Maxima Cura in 1910, removable pastors might lawfully be dispossessed of their parishes for any just and reasonable cause; and validly (since they had no jus in re to their parishes) without any cause whatever.¹ This chapter then will give a brief historical outline of the stability of pastors. 

ARTICLE 1 

Before the Decretals

For the first three centuries there were no parishes in our sense. 

The bishop was the sole pastor; his cathedral was the parish church and his diocese the parish. With the peace of Constantine the faithful multiplied in town and country. Churches were built to accommodate the ever-increasing number of Christians. While priests of the bishop's presbyterium ministered to the faithful in the city churches per modum actus, as it were, the priests who ministered to the spiritual wants of the Christians in the rural churches took up their residence in the country. These latter were the forerunners of the parish priests of today and from their office has developed the pastoral office as now understood. 2 The introduction of the system of benefices had much to do in stabilizing the pastoral office. In the sixth century there began the practice of giving those priests who were attached to rural parishes the use of certain lands. Three centuries later this property, or 

¹ Claeys-Bouuaert, De Canonica Cleri Saecularis Obedientia, pp. 331-332. * Wernz-Vidal, Ius Canonicum, II, n. 721. 77

rather the revenue from it, was attached not to the person of the priest, but to his office, thus constituting a parochial benefice.³ 

Thus the office of pastor received a new quality-permanence, stability or irremovability. This is objective perpetuity, for the priest appointed to the office of pastor was appointed for life and remained in it for life, unless after a formal trial he was found guilty of a very serious offense. In other words he was irremovable. This characteristic of the pastorate was unknown in the early centuries, for the office of parish priest did not yet exist. The development of rural parishes in the fourth century was the first step towards irremovability. Then for the first time priests lived at or near the churches assigned to them, unlike their brethren who officiated in the city churches. Nevertheless these rural priests might be removed for any reason which the bishop deemed sufficient. However, if a cleric, as the Council of Chalcedon (451) demanded, was ordained for a particular church, he was dedicated permanently to the service of that church.7 When benefices were developed between the ninth and twelfth centuries and each parish had its own property from the revenues of which the incumbent was to be supported, the title of irremovability was no longer based on the incardination by which a cleric was attached to a certain church for which he was ordained. The priest who had such a benefice had a perpetual right to receive its revenues; nor could he be deprived of the benefice except for certain grave crimes of which he was found guilty after a criminal trial.8

Here it may be of interest to trace the practice in the United States. Naturally, in the beginning the dioceses were very large in territory and sparse in population; priests were few and communication was very difficult. Hence it is not surprising that there were no parishes in the true sense; there were no strictly parochial boundaries. As late as 1810 the bishops, in order to meet the needs of the times, agreed to delegate their jurisdiction to one another. 

This jurisdiction was within limits sub-delegated to the priests.

CHAPTER III ADMINISTRATIVE REMOVAL UP TO THE DECREE MAXIMA CURA ARTICLE 1 A. From Decretals to Council of Trent 

As was stated in the beginning of the preceding chapter, there were no pastors except the bishops during the first three centuries. 

Hence the question of removal could not arise. It was only when the pastoral office became irremovable, as it did especially with the development of benefices, that there could be question of removal. 

As a matter of fact there is no legal doctrine on the subject prior to the end of the twelfth century. It is likely that throughout that period a pastor could not be dispossessed of his parish except for specified crimes and after a strictly judicial trial.¹

By the time of the Decretals, with the stability of the pastoral office firmly established, one would expect some legislation governing removal. Indeed there is in the Decretals a certain amount of legislation which, although it does not deal directly with removal, nevertheless as interpreted by the decretalists and later canonists, became the basis of the modern administrative method. 

There are three texts in which canonists see the germ from which the principle of administrative removal has developed. He makes a notable exception, however, in these words: "Si autem episcopus causam inspexerit necessariam, licite poterit de uno loco ad alium transferre personas ut quae uni loco minus sint utiles alibi se valeant utilius

B. The Council of Trent 

The Council of Trent did little towards developing the existing legislation on administrative removal. ARTICLE 2 The Teaching of Canonists 

It is well known that canonists have played a very important part in the development of legislation. Their doctrine was often adopted and given the force of law in the decretals of the Roman Pontiffs. 

They not only interpreted the law but they also drew conclusions which were only implicitly contained in the law.10 From their study of the Decretals and of the Council of Trent canonists concluded that, even though a pastor had committed no canonical crime, he might, nevertheless, in the interest of the common good be removed from his benefice whenever his ministry was unsuccessful. Thus Nicolaus de Tudeschis (1386-1445), more familiarly known as Abbas Panormitanus,12 commenting on the text of Urban 

III regarding the exchange of benefices, disagrees with Joannes 

Andreae (1275-1348) and holds that a bishop might transfer a pastor against his will if his ministry was unsuccessful, provided, however, he would give him another benefice at least equally good. 

He observes that while Urban III gave the bishop the power to transfer persons from one place to another, the Pope made no mention of the consent of the pastor. In confirmation of his teaching he adduces the text of Innocent III, on which he remarks: 

Quod si scandalum imminet Ecclesiae propter persecutionem praelati, potest talis praelatus licite privari per superiorem, licet ipse non sit in culpa huius persecutionis scandali, sed propter bonum publicum toleranda sit talis privatio, dato tamen illi bono cambio. 

The galaxy of eminent doctors of the seventeenth century confirmed and illustrated still more the principle of removal as expounded by earlier canonists. Such authorities as the Jesuits, Pirhing (1606-1679), Leurenius (1646-1723), and Schmalzgrueber (1663-1735), the Benedictine, Engel (d. 1674), the Franciscan, Reiffenstuel (1641-1703) and Fagnanus (1598-1678) are agreed that a priest, even of blameless life, may be transferred from his parish or forced to exchange it for another, if the good of souls should require it.

B. Procedure 

Administrative removal is something altogether different from judicial procedure; it has nothing in common with the obsolete summary process, for it is not a trial at all. Administrative removal is of gradual development. Its foundation was laid chiefly in the jurisprudence of the Holy See. A study of some of the decisions of the Congregations will reveal the origin and development of the process of removal, which reached its full stature in the decree Maxima Cura and in the Code.

ARTICLE 4 Inadequacy of Legislation 

That in certain circumstances a bishop might have recourse to the administrative removal of a pastor was generally admitted. But in the absence of definite legislation a bishop could not be certain that a particular cause would be sufficient or that a particular method of proving the existence of that cause would be recognized. 

In this state of uncertainty bishops, as a rule, resorted to removal only when an evil became intolerable and when all other means of eradicating the evil had been tried in vain. However, despite the caution with which the bishops employed this extraordinary measure, pastors occasionally protested against what they considered abuse of authority, and either refused to submit to the decree of removal or had recourse to the Holy See.69 The Holy See upon recourse examined the acts and gave its decisions according to the merits of each case. Of course these decisions carried the force of law for the persons concerned only. Nevertheless they suggested a directive norm on which a bishop might with greater confidence base a decree of administrative removal. Guided by the decisions of the Holy See, bishops usually followed a uniform practice. Thus as soon as a bishop learned that there was trouble between a pastor and his parishioners he admonished the pastor and requested him. to remove the cause. If the pastor disregarded this warning and if the trouble grew worse, the bishop before taking any further steps had to furnish himself with proof of the existence of the cause. 

The statement of witnesses would be accepted as sufficient.70 Satisfied as to the existence and proof of a sufficient cause, the bishop did not at once issue a decree of removal. He had to determine whether the cause, e. g., aversion of the people was permanent or temporary. To discover this he permitted the pastor to absent himself from the parish for six months. If at the end of another six months the cause still existed, he should persuade the pastor to resign his parish or exchange it for another. In case of refusal, the bishop might oblige him to accept another parish or resign with pension. 69 Greco, La Rimozione Amministrativa dei Parroci, p. 23.

CHAPTER IV DECREE MAXIMA CURA 

It is related that Pius X on the night after his election (August 4, 1903) conceived the idea of codifying the scattered laws of the Church. Next year the idea took the form of a resolution and the work of codification was begun.¹ 

Among the proximate causes of codification was the absence of adequate legislation on some important matters, one of which was the administrative removal of pastors. It is only to be expected, then, that administrative removal should engage the attention of the Code Commission. The Consultors and Cardinals of the Commission after diligent study succeeded in drafting clear and definite legislation on administrative removal. This they submitted to the 

Holy Father for approval. Taking counsel with the Consistorial 

Congregation, and seeing the pressing need for this legislation, the Holy Father ordered that it should be promulgated immediately. 

Hence the Congregation on August 20, 1910, published the famous decree Maxima Cura, which became law throughout the whole 

Latin Church.

This decree will now be studied. Since the decree contains so much that is entirely new, it seems preferable not to compare it with the indefinite provisions of the former law; it is proposed rather to consider it in itself and under the following aspects: purpose, content, territorial extension, subjects and binding force. 

A. Purpose 

The end and purpose of the new legislation was to supply a definite legal remedy by which pastors for certain specified causes might be administratively removed in the interests of the souls of their parishioners. Hence the prologue of the decree says:

In his (remediis) potissimum est parochi amotio, quae economica seu disciplinaris vulgo dicitur, et nullo iudiciali apparatu, sed administrativo

Heretofore, while the law on penal deprivation was clear, the law on administrative removal was too indefinite to be practical and effectual. The new legislation was established to remedy this situation. It was devised to expedite the removal of a pastor when the spiritual good of his parishioners demanded his removal; to determine precisely what causes justified removal, what procedure was to be followed and what provision was to be made for the incompetent or unworthy pastor so removed. 

B. Content 

Since it is proposed to draw freely on the decree Maxima Cura in the second part of this dissertation, only a brief outline of the decree need be given here. The decree consists of a prologue or introduction and eight titles which comprise thirty-two canons. 

The first title consisting of one canon sets forth in nine sections the causes for which removal is allowed. These causes in substance, are: (1) insanity; (2) incompetence and ignorance; (3) mental or bodily infirmity which renders a pastor unequal to his parish duties; (4) animosity of the people; (5) loss of esteem among serious-minded good-living people; (6) an occult crime which in the prudent judgment of the ordinary may soon become public and thus cause grave scandal among the people; (7) maladministration of temporalities with consequent injury to the church or benefice; (8) neglect after two warnings of such important parochial duties as the administration of the sacraments, visiting the sick, explaining the catechism and the gospel and fulfilling the obligation of residence; and (9) disobedience, after a second warning, to the ordinary's precept in serious matters, as for example, if the ordinary should command a pastor to beware of familiarity with a certain person or family, to attend to the cleanliness of the church, or not to be overexacting in parish dues and the like.

Decree "Maxima Cura" 39 The second title, consisting also of only one canon, in two paragraphs states in a general way the order of procedure. The first paragraph mentions first the invitation to resign; then upon refusal of the pastor to resign, the decree of removal; finally, in the case of recourse against this the review of the acts and the confirmation of the previous decree. The second paragraph stresses the fact that the particular provisions which affect the substance of the process must be observed under the pain of invalidity.

The third title, which contains five canons (3-7), prescribes the persons besides the ordinary necessary in the procedure, namely, synodal or pro-synodal examiners and pastor consultors. It states, furthermore, the rules for the election of those officials, their term of office and the number to be selected for a particular case and their sworn obligation of secrecy. 

The fourth title (8-13) lays down the first steps in the procedure proper. It deals with the invitation calling for the pastor's resignation. It provides that this invitation be formal and generally in writing, that it be accompanied by the reasons (general in the case of an occult crime) why the resignation has been demanded, and the arguments supporting these reasons. It prescribes also that a decree of removal can be issued, unless the pastor within ten days after receiving the invitation, either resigns his parish or shows that the alleged reasons were unfounded; that for a just cause the ordinary may, with the consent of the examiners, grant the pastor ten or even twenty more days to make up his mind or to prepare his defense. Finally, it ordains that the ordinary after the pastor's resignation declare the parochial office or benefice vacant.

The fifth title (14-21) is concerned with the decree of removal to which the ordinary may proceed, if the pastor within the time allowed has not handed in his resignation, nor demanded an extension of time for consideration nor undertaken a defense. This title defines the action which the pastor may take after he has been formally asked to resign, e. g., the preparation of his defense and the presentation of the witnesses for examination. In the examination of the pastor and of the witnesses the regulation excludes all judicial formalities and the repudiation of the witnesses, requiring only what is necessary to establish the truth. This same title provides further that, after the pastor has been given adequate op-

portunities for his just defense, the question of removal is to be discussed by the ordinary and the examiners and settled according to a majority of secret votes. Finally, if the majority favors removal the ordinary issues the decree which must always mention that the pastor was invited to resign, that thereupon the pastor set forth his allegations in his defense and finally that the ordinary asked and obtained the vote of the examiners. 

The sixth title (22-25), De Actorum Revisione, treats of the method to be followed in the case of recourse. It declares that the remedy against a decree of removal is a recourse to the same ordinary who reviews the acts with the aid of two pastor consultors. 

The recourse must be made within ten days from the receipt of the decree. After introducing his recourse, the pastor has ten days to bring forward his allegations. All the ordinary and the pastor consultors have to do in reviewing the acts is to see whether there were any invalidating defects of form in the process, or whether the cause adduced for removal was really present or not. Here a majority of secret votes determined whether the recourse was to be admitted or rejected. 

The seventh title (26-29) defines the provision to be made for the pastor who has accepted the invitation to resign, or who has been removed from his parish in an administrative manner. It directs the ordinary, after taking counsel with the examiners or (in case of recourse) with the pastor consultors, to transfer the pastor to another parish, or to assign him some ecclesiastical office, or to grant him a pension-as the case demands and the circumstances permit. It warns the ordinary that only a priest who is worthy and fit for parish work and administration is to be given a parish. 

If the priest is worthy and competent he may, as equity and prudence demand, be given a parish which is equal, inferior, or superior to the one whence he has been removed. If he is to be given a pension, then the rules of law regarding pensions must be observed, e. g., perpetual pensions cannot be imposed by the ordinary on a benefice without the permission of the Holy See. Furthermore, when the good of souls demands removal, the question of making provision for the priest must not be allowed to interfere with, or impede, or delay such action. And, lastly, the pastor who has resigned, or who has been removed, must leave the rectory as

Decree "Maxima Cura” 41 soon as possible, handing over (generally to the substitute) everything that belongs to the parish. In case of sickness, however, the pastor is to be allowed, if necessary, the exclusive use of the rectory until such time as he can, in the prudent judgment of the ordinary, conveniently be transferred to some other place. 

The eighth and last title (30-32) defines who are the subjects of this legislation. The active subject is the ordinary. The vicar general, however, is excluded unless he has a special mandate. This title. also prescribes (canon 31) that administrative removal can have no place the while the pastor is being tried for some crime in an ecclesiastical or civil tribunal but that it becomes available only after the verdict has been rendered, provided the circumstances do not call for some other adjustment or procedure. Meanwhile, if the crime involves infama facti the ordinary may prevent the accused from exercising the care of souls and the temporal administration of the benefice.

The decree concludes with a personal command of the Holy Father regarding the immediate election of pastor consultors and the method of election of examiners. 

C. Juridical Value 

As the final words of the prologue imply, the decree established legislation for the whole Latin Church. The decree emanated, it is true, from one of the Congregations which as a rule have no legislative competence. However, in this case there is question of a general decree which was promulgated at the command and with. the special approval of the Sovereign Pontiff. Hence the decree has the force of law. It not only had universal but also exclusive application. This follows from the final words: "Praesentibus valituris, contrariis quibusvis non obstantibus." Hence all previous particular customs as well as particular laws contrary to the decree Maxima Cura were abrogated."

D. Extension 

The decree was binding on the universal church. In its own words:

Decretum per hanc (Consistorialem) Congregationem edi iussit (Pius PP. X) quo novae normae de amotione administrativa promulgarentur, eaedemque canonicam legem pro universa Ecclesia constituerent. The decree, however, did not apply to the Oriental Church. As at present so also at the time of the promulgation of the decree Maxima Cura, it was the common teaching of theologians and canonists that the Orientals are not bound by pontifical constitutions except in three cases: (1) when there is a case of faith or morals; (2) when pontifical law is merely an interpretation or declaration of the natural or divine positive law; (3) when there is express mention of the Oriental Church. Since the decree Maxima 

Cura was only a positive disciplinary decree, which did not expressly mention the Eastern Church, it must be concluded that its provisions concerned only the Latin Church." 

This legislation per se was binding also in regions subject to Propaganda. It has been seen what territory was subject to the new law. The question now is: what persons were affected by it? The answer is contained in canon 30 of the decree:

The precise rules of the decree Maxima Cura applied, therefore, to all those who on any title whatever administered a parish as its proper rectors. It made no difference what name these rectors were called—whether they were called perpetual vicars or desservants or by any other name. The following then were subject to the new law: 10 (a) pastors properly so called; (b) curates who possessed a benefice to which the care of souls was attached; (c) perpetual vicars; (d) and all others in charge of parishes as their proper rectors. The decree made explicit reference to desservants and perpetual vicars as coming under the new legislation. It excluded all those who administered parishes in the name of another, as "Vicarii oeconomi" who had temporary charge of a parish, "vicarii adjutores" who assisted a pastor unable, through sickness or old age, to fulfill all his pastoral duties and finally all substitutes, assistants, and chaplains.

The application of the decree Maxima Cura to those removable rectors would only have the effect of defeating in great measure (by the addition of prescribed formalities) the very end and purpose of the decree.

HISTORICAL SUMMARY 

There was no legislation on administrative removal prior to the Decretals. Even in the Decretals there is little more than an adumbration of such legislation, as in the texts of Urban III, Clement III and Innocent III. Neither did the Council of Trent legislate directly on this matter; at most, it only indirectly admitted the principle. However, the teaching of the later decretalists and of the post-Tridentine canonists together with the jurisprudence of the Holy See left no further room for doubt regarding the principle. But, as a matter of fact, in the absence of precise and definite legislation, recourse to administrative removal was rather rare; it was adopted only as a last resort when matters had reached a crisis. Bishops generally felt the need for clear and certain legislation on administrative removal. At the Vatican Council the bishops of Germany and of the province of Naples so expressed 

14 ITQ, X (1915), 495; Cappello, De Administrativa Amotione Parochorum, p. 119.

themselves. No action, however, was then taken, owing to the abrupt interruption of the Council. It remained for Pius X in 1910 to give to the Church an adequate, fully-developed body of regulations which in respect to removal clearly defined the relations between the ordinary and those priests who held an office or benefice with the care of souls. This body of laws is substantially the law as it exists today in the Code.

A pastor may be removed for permanent mental infirmity, provided that he is thereby incapacitated for his parochial duties and that in the judgment of the ordinary the good of the parish cannot be provided for by the appointment of an adjutant vicar. The pre- 

Code law 37 insisted on the removal of a pastor who became incurably insane or who so lost his reputation and authority through temporary insanity that it would be thought harmful to retain him in office. The Code returns to the more equitable and indulgent legislation of the Decretals.38

The phrase infirmitas mentis here is quite comprehensive. It embraces all psychical disorders which are not of a passing nature, whether they are due to lesion or diseased action of the brain or of the nervous system.39 Those disturbances are many and varied. The principal ones are complete insanity; monomania, which is a mental derangement in respect to one object or subject; imbecility, which may result from a serious illness or from an intense and prolonged grief, or from old age and which impairs the power of judgment; neurasthenia, which makes its victim abnormally irritable and depressed, lessens his intellectual power and saps his strength of will; and hysteria, which is a certain type of neurosis affecting especially the central nervous system.40

Popular Animosity 

Popular animosity, even though unjust and not universal, is also a sufficient cause for a pastor's removal, provided that it is such as to hinder him from exercising a successful ministry and may be foreseen as not ceasing within a short time. This is taken almost word for word from the decree Maxima Cura.69 It is one of the few causes which have their roots in the Decretals.70 It was deemed a sufficient reason in the practice of the Holy See at least as early as the year 1757.71

The word odium is best translated animosity. It means more than mere indifference or antipathy.72 It may express itself in insults and demonstrations against the pastor and injury to parish property, but such manifestations are not necessary.73 It consists then in positive hostility of the people against their pastor, in a state of opposition which results in their neglecting or refusing his ministrations.74

Surely, such a pastor cannot be said to be exercising a fruitful ministry even though he may retain the affection of his Catholic subjects. 

It makes no difference for the purpose of the canon whether the animosity of the people is just or unjust; for the whole principle underlying administrative removal is the salvation of souls. The animosity would be just, i. e., deserved, if the pastor himself was responsible, as e. g., if he indulged in violent outbursts of temper or in unrestrained vituperative language. It would be unjust if it proceeded solely from the malice or evil disposition of the people. 

In all cases, however, the animosity must be directed against the pastor not as a pastor but as an individual.79 The reason for this is evident; the substitution of another pastor would not improve the situation but would rather encourage the people in their wilfulness.80

If it has spread to a majority or to one-half of the people of the parish the case is clear also. It is difficult to conjure up a set of circumstances in which a pastor's fruitful ministry may not be hindered in the face of the people's intense hatred for him. It might be verified, e. g., if the pastor had an assistant who had so completely won the confidence of the people that they approached him in all their spiritual necessities and faithfully fulfilled their religious obligations. Ordinarily, however, this is not the case when a pastor is universally and with good reason hated in his parish. Even a most God-fearing and well-instructed congregation will usually suffer spiritual loss when its pastor has deservedly incurred their hatred.

The Code nowhere determines what constitutes a short period. 

Much depends upon the circumstances of each case. It can be said that as a rule, if the animosity has lasted for six months and is not likely to cease in a very short time, removal is in order as far as the time element is concerned.85 The ordinary will naturally do his utmost to remove the cause of the animosity rather than the pastor; and only when there is no alternative will he, as a last resort, reluctantly employ the remedy of administrative removal.

One's reputation (existimatio) is the opinion which others entertain about one; it is the value, good or bad, which others set upon one; it is their judgment of one. If everyone's reputation is among his most priceless possessions, a pastor's reputation is still more important by reason of his office. There is question here not of a bad reputation, but of the loss of a good reputation. The two ideas are not the same. The former is infamy; the latter is simply loss of esteem. Infamy always entails loss of esteem; but loss of esteem does not always entail infamy. They may differ in their cause, the one always arising out of some fault which is equivalent to a crime, the other arising not necessarily from a crime but from other causes also.88 It is the latter the legislation has in mind in this connection. 

Whether the loss of one's esteem in a particular case is a serious matter or not depends upon the character of those amongst whom one's reputation is lost. Hence the legislator is careful to state that the pastor's loss of esteem must be among virtuous and seriousminded persons. The virtuous are persons of recognized honesty and integrity. The serious-minded are those who see things in their proper perspective and act accordingly. Naturally the character judgment of the unscrupulous carries no weight and is therefore disregarded; and similarly, the opinions of the garrulous and unstable have little influence on others. The pastor may lose his reputation because of his levity of conduct (levis vivendi ratio). Because of his priesthood and office the pastor is committed to a life of solid virtue. He must lead a truly virtuous interior life. That interior sanctity will manifest itself in his outward conduct. As long as his outward conduct is irreproachable he will be presumed to be a good priest. But, on the contrary, once his outward behaviour savors of levity or frivolity, even by simple neglect or omission, he may be suspected of being a sinner in his heart and consequently may lose the esteem of his people. 

Hence, although he is not guilty of any external, or at least public, mortal sin, he can suffer that loss of esteem which will make him an unprofitable servant.90 Levity of conduct, in short, is an unconcealed habit which externally is venially sinful or which has at least the appearance of sin, and which leads the people to think that their pastor is not leading a truly priestly life. To determine in a particular case whether a pastor's conduct is frivolous the ordinary will have to weigh not merely the conduct itself but also the temper of the parishioners. An occult crime 120 is one which has not become generally known or one which occurred under such circumstances that it can and ought to be prudently judged that it will not easily become generally known. CHAPTER VI NECESSARY OFFICIALS AND WITNESSES IN THE REMOVAL PROCESS 

Before discussing the various steps in the process of removal it may be well to make some general remarks about the officials who take part in the procedure. If a removable pastor is involved these officials are the ordinary, a notary and two synodal or pro-synodal examiners; in the case of an irremovable pastor in addition to these there are other officials, namely two pastor consultors. 

The term ordinary for the purpose of removal embraces the Roman Pontiff for the entire Church, and the following within their respective jurisdictions: the residential bishop, the abbot and prelate nullius, the apostolic administrator, the vicar and prefect apostolic and all those who, by the provisions of the law or of approved constitutions, succeed during the vacancy to any of these offices.¹ Whether the vicar general also is competent is not certain. Of course, he is competent if he is possessed of a special mandate. He is competent, too, in urgent cases sede impedita.2

And if canon 1585 has no application to the second and third parts, then of course without question the presence of a notary is not required for the validity of the removal process.

Nor does the phrase cuilibet processui compel the inclusion of the processes of the second and third parts under the prescriptions of canon 1585 of the first part of the Fourth Book. A good case might be made for this all-embracing force of the phrase, if only one type of process had been considered in the first part, De Iudiciis. As a matter of fact, however, the part, De Iudiciis treats of several different processes-the contentious, the criminal, the matrimonial and the ordination processes. It does not, therefore, seem to be any undue restriction of the phrase to confine it to the pro cesses of the first part of the Fourth Book. 

Again if canon 1585 were intended to apply to all three parts of the Fourth Book it would have been inserted as an introductory canon to the whole book and would not have been placed in the body of the first part. This conclusion is based on what seems to be a uniform method employed throughout the Code. Thus, whenever there are canons which are of general application throughout different parts or titles, these canons are so placed as to form a kind of introduction to those parts or titles. There is an example of this practice in the third part of the Fourth Book.

Witnesses 

Although the process of removal is not judicial, nevertheless it admits everything that the natural law requires for the administration of even-handed justice. Hence it allows the introduction of witnesses. But since it is a summary process it allows only as many witnesses as may be necessary to establish sufficient proof. 

The Code 63 provides expressly for the admission of two or three witnesses, whether invoked by the pastor or ex officio. This recalls the scriptural dictum 64 that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall stand. Although the Code specifically mentions two or three, that does not necessarily mean that it excludes a larger number.65

However, the purpose of witnesses is to establish proof. Consequently, if the ordinary judges that the pastor is introducing witnesses merely to prolong the process or cause delays, he may exclude them after consulting the examiners or consultors, as the case may be. Canon 2148, § 1. When an irremovable pastor has forfeited the right to his parish either for any of the specified reasons or for the general reason mentioned in the previous canon (2147) and explained at length in a previous chapter, the ordinary may immediately initiate removal proceedings. Though the law does not demand it, the ordinary, if he foresees any hope of success, will seek a happy solution by an appeal to paternal methods. If possible, he will strive to remove the cause. If this is not possible either from the nature of the case or for some other reason, he will exhort the pastor to resign his parish voluntarily. This exhortation, however, is not part of the legal process and does not do duty for the processual invitation; it has no juridical effect whatever.¹

If the ordinary intends to remove the pastor, before taking any legal step he must prudently judge that there is a cause for removal and that this cause is sufficiently serious to justify removal. 

This presupposes that he has secured full and adequate evidence.2 

The source of the evidence is a matter of indifference provided that it is reliable. The ordinary may have gained his information upon inquiry in the course of a canonical visitation of the parish; he may have received numerous complaints from the parishioners; he

He has to summon two synodal or pro-synodal examiners. It is not required that these examiners enjoy seniority. 

The ordinary sees to it that the examiners and the notary take the oath of secrecy as prescribed by canon 2144. The session then opens, the notary being present to write the acts. The ordinary discusses with the examiners whether all the conditions for removal laid down in the law are verified; whether the cause and its gravity are supported by sufficient proof; and, finally, whether an invitation to resign should be communicated to the pastor.* 

The ordinary will conceal nothing from the examiners which has a bearing on the merits of the case; he will allow them full liberty of discussion and not seek to influence them for or against removal, but will present the facts and arguments just as they are.5 

In a word, he will not show any displeasure, if, as duty requires," they express their opinions respectfully, faithfully and sincerely.

This choice and its consequences are dealt with in the next canon (2150). Again, he may in due time acknowledge the decree by requesting an extension of time for the preparation of his defense. 2151. Or he may attack the reasons alleged for removal by the ordinary. The procedure to be followed in that event is outlined in canon 2152. Finally he may take no action at all; he may simply maintain silence. 

This last course is the one which is envisioned by the above canon (2149). If the pastor does not reply within the time assigned in the decree of invitation it may be because the document was invalid or inadequate or because the document had not come to his notice or, finally, because he was legitimately hindered from acknowledging the invitation. 

The ordinary has the duty, before taking further legal action, to investigate why the pastor has not made some answer to the decree of invitation. Naturally the ordinary will first assure himself that the document was duly executed according to the prescriptions of the previous canon (2148). He will examine the acts and will investigate whether a time limit had been specified for the resignation and whether the cause for removal as well as the proofs of the reality of the cause had been set forth in the original document. CHAPTER VIII PROCEDURE FOR REMOVABLE PASTORS 

According to canon 454, § 5 pastors who belong to a religious institute are all, ratione personae, removable at the will of the religious superior notifying the local ordinary and at the will of the local ordinary notifying the religious superior. These pastors are removable ad nutum and hence their removal is not governed even by the administrative process prescribed for the removal of removable pastors. The religious superior may remove them without obtaining the consent or approval of the local ordinary; the local ordinary may remove them without obtaining the consent or approval of the religious superior. Nor is either obliged to inform the other of the reason for which he removed the religious pastor. 

However, if either considers himself aggrieved by the action of the other he may lodge a recourse with the Holy See. This recourse is in devolutivo, i. e., even while the recourse is pending the act of removal is effective. Whether the Holy See will overrule or confirm the act of removal depends on whether the superior concerned acted arbitrarily or acted reasonably. Hence although the religious superior and the local ordinary need not acquaint each other with the reason on which they base their action in removing the religious pastor, they must nevertheless in case of recourse be prepared to defend their action to the satisfaction of the Holy See. 1 Quasi-pastors, as stated elsewhere ¹ are not removable ad nutum; they are removable only ad normam iuris. In other words their removal follows the rules laid down for the removal of removable pastors.

It has also been seen that for the removable pastor there is no such right and that therefore the ordinary's first decree is definitive. The removable pastor, however, as well as the irremovable has the right to lodge a recourse with the Holy 

See against the ordinary's final decree.

Since removal is an administrative and not a judicial process, the ordinary's verdict is not a sentence but a decree, and hence the legal remedy against the verdict is not an appeal but a recourse. 

It follows also that, not the Sacred Roman Rota or any other tribunal, but some superior should be competent to receive the recourse against the ordinary's final decree of removal. By positive law this superior is not the metropolitan or other intermediate superior but the Holy See itself. The competent authority is one of the Sacred Congregations.¹ There are only two Congregations which have authority to decide in matters of removal-either the Congregation of the Council or the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith. CONCLUSION 

A pastor of the secular clergy, whether he be removable or irremovable, can be removed from his parish by the ordinary only for a just and grave cause. The Code expressly mentions five such causes and implies that there are others. It lays down the general principle that a cause which renders a pastor's ministry harmful or even unsuccessful is sufficient to justify that pastor's removal. 

There would seem to be two exceptions which must be made in the application of this principle, viz., a crime which involves ipso facto deprivation of office and also a crime which is the object of a specific administrative penal process, e. g., non-residence. 

But even when a sufficient cause for removal is known to exist, the ordinary cannot immediately issue a decree of removal. To act validly there is a certain procedure which he must follow. This procedure takes two different forms corresponding to the greater or lesser stability which the pastor enjoys in his parish. 

The procedure which the ordinary must follow in the case of an irremovable pastor is the more complicated of the two and includes: (1) The ordinary's discussion with two synodal or pro-synodal examiners, regarding the existence and seriousness of the cause for the pastor's removal. (2) The ordinary's oral or written invitation requesting the pastor to renounce his parish within a certain time and stating the specific cause justifying the request as well as the general nature of the evidence for the existence of the cause. (3) a) The ordinary's acceptance of the pastor's resignationif the pastor resigns. b) The issuance of the decree of removal—if the pastor is contumacious. c) The granting of an extension of time at the pastor's request, provided the spiritual interests of the parish are safeguarded. (4) The ordinary's consultation with the same examiners a) regarding the defense arguments advanced by the pastor 142

Conclusion 

143 in case the latter, within the time assigned in the invitation or granted at his request, chooses to dispute the grounds on which his resignation was demanded. b) The ordinary's notification of the abandonment of removal action in case he is convinced of the strength of the pastor's defense arguments. c) The ordinary's issuance of a decree of removal in case he is convinced of the insufficiency of the pastor's arguments. (5) The ordinary's consultation with two pastor consultors a) regarding the reasons advanced by the pastor against the first decree of removal, in case the pastor interposes his recourse in suspensivo within ten days (tempus utile) from the date of notification of his removal and prosecutes his defense within ten days from the date of recourse. b) The ordinary's confirmation of the first decree of removal-a confirmation given in the form of a final decree and admitting only of a recourse in devolutivo to the Holy See, the parish remaining vacant pending a timely recourse to the Holy See. c) The ordinary's reversal of his own original decree of removal in case he is convinced that the pastor's defense is adequate. In that case the pastor is in the position he enjoyed before his resignation was demanded.

The procedure which the ordinary must follow in the case of a removable pastor includes: (1) The ordinary's paternal but canonical admonition exhorting the pastor to renounce his parish within a certain time and indicating the reasons for the request. (2) a) The ordinary's declaration of the vacancy of the parishin case the pastor resigns. b) The ordinary's issuance of a decree of removal-if the pastor wilfully neglects to make answer. c) The ordinary's accession to the pastor's request for an extension-provided he prudently judges the spiritual interests of the parish will not thus be jeopardized.

(3) a) The ordinary's discussion with two examiners regarding the reasons submitted in writing by the pastor against the reasons alleged in the invitation. b) The ordinary's repetition of the paternal admonition exhorting the pastor to resign and addition of a threat of removal for the pastor's failure to renounce his parish before a certain date—all this in case he is convinced of the insufficiency of the pastor's defense. c) The ordinary's notification to the pastor of the abandonment of removal action—if he is convinced of the adequacy of the pastor's defense. (4) a) The ordinary's execution of his threat of removal in the event that the pastor fails to resign within the time assigned. The only recourse from this decree of removal is a recourse in devolutivo to the Holy See. b) The ordinary's prorogation of the time for resignation provided he prudently judges that the spiritual interests of the parish will not be jeopardized. 

The removable as well as the irremovable pastor must be provided for either by being given another parish or by being given some other kind of office or benefice or by pension. The only exception to that rule is a pastor who has shown himself contumacious. In determining what manner of provision is to be made for the pastor, the ordinary must take counsel with two examiners or with two pastor consultors according to the circumstances of the case.

BIOGRAPHICAL NOTE 

Maurice Connor, born on January 21, 1901, at Clashmealcon,

Causeway, Co. Kerry, Ireland, made his primary and intermediate studies in his native county-at Killury and Ballinillane National 

Schools and at St. Michael's College, Listowel. Entering All Hal lows College, Dublin, in September, 1919, he attended lectures at University College, Dublin-a constituent college of the National University of Ireland-from which in 1922 he received the degree of Bachelor of Arts. Upon the completion of his theological course in All Hallows College he was ordained a priest on June 20, 1926, and in October of the same year took up duty in the diocese of Sacramento, California, into which diocese he had been incardinated. In the fall of 1934, at the desire of his bishop, he enrolled in the School of Canon Law at the Catholic University of America, from which he obtained the degrees of J. C. B. and J. C. L. in the years 1935 and 1936 respectively. 153
