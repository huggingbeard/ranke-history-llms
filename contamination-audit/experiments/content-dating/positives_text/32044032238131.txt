Dear Mr. President:

It is a pleasure to transmit to you this "Special Report" which summarizes the most recent thinking of major Department and Agency heads on the rehabilitation and employment of our handicapped citizens.

Our Advisory Council has charted a course for the future which we shall make every effort to follow. This includes workshops or seminars for employers, special efforts for the handicapped in the anti-poverty program, visits by Federal officials to workshops for the retarded, stepped up activity in jobs for the handicapped in the agricultural environment, continued emphasis upon eliminating architectural barriers in Federal buildings and special emphasis upon the 20th observance of National Employ the Physically 

Handicapped Week (October 4-10, 1964). 

No "Report" would be complete without reference to your continued encouragement and support and to the special cooperation given by Governors and municipal officials. Together, we shall work for a brighter tomorrow for the physically handicapped, the mentally retarded and the mentally restored. Respectfully,

PRESIDENT'S COMMITTEE ON EMPLOYMENT OF THE HANDICAPPED 

Welcome to this rare meeting of the Advisory Council of the 

President's Committee. I have not prepared a "report" from the

Committee since Mr. Freeman's May 1 report is so current and so fresh. It shall serve as our report.

I say "rare" because we don't meet very often; we get together not on any regular schedule, but only when there are pressing problems that deserve our collective best thinking. Our last meeting was 

December 14, 1961, two and a half years ago ... a meeting presided over by our dear friend Mel Maas, whose memory will always be strong in our hearts.

Before coming here, I did my homework by studying the minutes of all past Advisory Council meetings. The thing that strikes me most is that these meetings are characterized by a minimum of talk and a maximum of action, rather than the other way around, as is so often the case. 

The reason isn't hard to understand. Just look around the table and see who's present. Some of the most action-packed people in Washington are here today. 

I'm sure you have attended more than your share of "Johnny-Come- Lately" meetings where the group finally wakes up to problems that have been on the doorstep for a long time, and concludes that, well, after all these months or years, maybe somebody ought to be doing something.

This Advisory Council is not at all like that. This Council seems to have a sixth sense of spotting problems at the time they are beginning to show their heads. Council action is swift and intelligent. Progress is noteworthy. Let me freshen your memories with three examples:

A number of years ago, the Council realized that architectural barriers were preventing many handicapped men and women from holding jobs and leading lives of independence. An Ad Hoc Committee was formed. It laid groundwork that was to be a foundation for the entire national effort that followed. Go to the World's Fair; see all the special accommodations for the handicapped; and thrill at the knowledge that much of what you see had its start right here. 1

As another example, the Advisory Council quickly saw that automation was going to have a great impact on future jobs for the handicapped. 

But what kind of impact? So another Ad Hoc Committee was formed.

It conducted a national survey and wrote a brilliant report that still is being read and referred to, all over the country. 

As a third example, the Council was aware that special steps would have to be taken in order to open up more jobs in the Federal Government for the mentally restored and mentally retarded. Another Ad Hoc 

Committee, another set of recommendations, and look what we have today: the Federal Government out in front, as an employer, in hiring the mentally restored and mentally retarded. 

Now let me make this clear: I'm not saying that this Advisory Council is a modern David who slays Goliath after Goliath with his trusty slingshot, and nothing more. I am saying that this Council has given pushes to programs where pushes are necessary, and therefore has contributed greatly to the welfare of the handicapped in America. 

and all the infrequent meetings in will carry on in the same grand tradition. Good luck! 

I am sure that today's meeting times to come

DISCUSSION ITEMS

ITEM ONE: 

The Problem: 

What can Council members do through their Departments and Agencies to help promote the 20th observance of 

National Employ the Physically Handicapped Week this fall, October 4 - 10?

Federal agencies can help make this forthcoming NEPH Week the most effective ever conducted, through two lines of action: (a) An internal campaign of reaching all personnel within the agency; (b) an external campaign of reaching outside "publics" with which the agency has regular contact.

Proposed Programs: 

Heads of agencies can speak in behalf of the handicapped at suitable occasions during October, if convenient. Or they can mention the subject in other speeches scheduled during that month. Or they can ask their Information Offices to arrange for radio or TV interviews in which they discuss their agencies' activities in hiring the handicapped. 

Agencies can make thorough internal distribution of President's Committee material posters, bumper strips, etc. to create a heavy impact.

Agencies can use the week to publicize the stories of handicapped employees making good in spite of their disabilities.

Agencies can spread the word to the "publics" they are in contact with, either through their usual means of communication or through special bulletins, letters or brochures. Agriculture, for example, in its radio programs in October, can urge jobs for handicapped farm workers; 

Commerce can spread the word through its publications reaching the business community; etc.

Heads of agencies can send letters to all personnel urging equal consideration for the handicapped. Such letters have been found most effective. 

Agency coordinators for the handicapped can be made the focal points of all NEPH Week activities working in close cooperation with top management, the personnel department and the public information department.

ITEM TWO: 

What are the implications and opportunities for increased jobs for the handicapped under the President's Anti-Poverty program?

The Problem: 

Poverty seems to strike the handicapped even more severely than the able-bodied.

A New York City survey showed that the unemployment rate of the handicapped was twice as high as the able-bodied; and that 60% of the employed handicapped had poorer jobs than before they incurred their disabilities.

A national study by the Institute for Social Research of the University of Michigan showed that 36% of handicapped breadwinners had incomes below minimum family budget needs, while only 22% of the able-bodied had below-minimum incomes. 

The Department of Labor's study on mental retardation pointed out that the unemployment rate of retardates qualified to work is at least four times higher than for other males in the same age brackets. Also, this and other studies show that much retardation in America is not organic but rather environmentally-induced, caused by being raised on the wrong side of the tracks.

The physically and mentally handicapped at the lower end of the economic scale can be particularly disadvantaged; the combination of poverty and handicap can be disastrous. Special aid is needed. 

Proposed Programs: 

The President's Anti-Poverty program, the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964, offers hope for the handicapped, provided it pays special heed to their special needs.

It calls for a national Job Corps of young men whose health and education make them least fit for useful work; a Federally-financed program of locally-inspired community action to meet the problem at the local level; a volunteer corps, trained to fight poverty in many ways; special loans and special training to help the poverty-stricken break out of their environment; expansion of existing programs such as Area Redevelopment, Manpower Development Training and Vocational Education. 

In addition to being on the receiving end, many handicapped persons might well be on the giving end, such as serving in the volunteer corps. 10

ITEM THREE: 

The Problem: 

What additional steps should be taken to increase employment opportunities for the mentally restored? 

Mentally retarded?

The new Federal program of placement of the mentally retarded received much initial emphasis; continued "push" is needed to reach every level of the Federal Government. The Federal program of hiring the mentally restored has been in effect much longer, but without the same stress; this program needs more emphasis. 

Proposed Programs: 

Mentally retarded: Basically, Government officials need to be convinced the mentally retarded can work. To spotlight abilities, the President's 

Committee, Vocational Rehabilitation Administration and National Asso ciation for Retarded Children should develop a program of visits to sheltered workshops by top Government operational and personnel officials as suggested by President Johnson. 

Initially, as a trial, a visitation program should be planned for one city with an active rehabilitation program for the retarded. Ultimately, the visits should go on in every city with a workshop for the retarded. 

Mentally Restored: A series of one-day seminars on employment of the mentally restored should be held under CSC auspices throughout the country. They could take place in CSC regional offices, drawing officials from Federal agencies throughout the regional office area. A trial seminar could be held in Atlanta, Ga.

Participants at the trial seminar might include an outstanding psychiatrist (Dr. Rives Chalmers); CSC regional medical officer (Dr. Martin); a panel of vocational rehabilitation, employment service and VA officials; a 

Junior Chamber of Commerce person. The VR-ES-VA panel could stress sources to which Government can look for qualified restored persons; the JCC person could explain his organization's role in helping ex-mental patients.

For this program to succeed, Federal agencies should be encouraged to make travel funds available so that field officials might attend. 

No attempt would be made in Washington to dictate precise format of these seminars. Local-level planning would be encouraged. 11

ITEM FOUR: 

What national effort should be made to improve rehabilitation and employment opportunities for the handicapped in the agricultural environment?

Following is an abstract of recommendations in the final report of the 

Ad Hoc Committee on Employment of the Handicapped in Agriculture (March 1963): 

1. The President's Committee act to strengthen and extend agricultural and rural non-agricultural rehabilitation and employment services coincidental with farm and rural safety programs. 

This should be done through cooperation of the National Safety Council, Agriculture Department and Labor Department, working with HEW 

(Vocational and Technical Division, Office of Education, and voca tional Rehabilitation Administration) and other farm organizations.

2. 

States should be requested to cooperate with above organizations and agencies. Governors' Committees on Employment of the Handicapped should take action in cooperation with existing State farm safety committees.

The Agricultural Extension Services, Department of Agriculture, Division of Vocational Rehabilitation and the Employment Service should be included in the planning. Governors' Committees should take the lead in bringing these agencies together with others to devise and coordinate State and local plans with existing activities (PL 565, Section 8). 3.

Initial tryouts or demonstrations should be conducted in selected States before embarking on a national plan. Funds for demonstrations should be requested from VRA or other appropriate agencies. 4.

Cooperating agencies will be urged to furnish and exchange pertinent information for use in rural communities. Federal extension service should be used to assist in establishing contact between 

State Vocational Rehabilitation Administration offices, State Employ ment offices and prospective rehabilitants. 

This report has been accepted by the Executive Committee of the President's Committee. The Advisory Council has not had the opportunity to discuss it. 12

ITEM FIVE: How can Council members cooperate in breaking down architectural barriers?

The Problem: 

Many handicapped persons are denied the opportunity of Federal employment because of architectural design which denies them access to Federal buildings.

The Background: 

A Sectional Committee of American Standards Association, under the sponsorship of the President's Committee and the National Society for Crippled Children and Adults, has developed an approved Standard 

Al17.1-1961, "Making Buildings and Facilities Accessible to, and 

Usable by, the Physically Handicapped. The adoption of this 

Standard by architects and builders is being urged in practically every community across the country by volunteer citizen groups. Prominent civic, cultural, commercial, religious, educational and transportation facilities have been built or are planned with the specifications contained in this Standard incorporated in the design. 

Proposed Programs: 

The Specifications of General Services Administration in all new Federal construction parallel this Standard in guarantees of access and use for handicapped employees and visitors. 

Existing Federal buildings have been modified to some extent where budgetary considerations permit renovations. There is a compelling need to extend this modification program to more Federal buildings particularly those drawing heavy public traffic, such as post offices. 

In leasing private structures for government use, accessibility should be a primary consideration.

Those agencies responsible for imparting Federal matching grants for State and local construction of hospitals, libraries, educational facilities, housing, etc., should act as watchdog committees in their review of designs and plans, and suggest to local authorities the needs of the handicapped where such considerations have not been included in the blueprints. 13

ITEM SIX: 

How can the severely handicapped benefit more fully from the stepped up vocational rehabilitation and employment services provided in the communities? 

In Government service? In private industry?

Stepped up rehabilitation and employment services should include an objective of reaching a greater number of the severely handicapped not just an increase in the number of lesser handicapped served. 

This will require a modification of the practice of measuring program success in terms of numbers rehabilitated and placed. 

Allocation of funds should be sufficient to permit longer term and more costly training and placement services and the type of personnel necessary to perform these services. 

The Federal programs for the employment of the retarded, the noncompetitive employment of a limited number of handicapped (not to exceed 100 nation-wide) who have demonstrated their ability to perform duties under temporary appointment and whose impairment prevents them from qualifying for permanent placement under competitive rules, the 700 hours (work record) employment of handicapped, and the Coordinators for Employment of the Handicapped are resulting in increased employment of the handicapped in Federal service. 

These programs should be expanded in number of agencies actively participating and number of handicapped, particularly severely handicapped, considered and employed. Results and experiences from the 

Federal programs should be used to illustrate and demonstrate to other levels of Government and private employment procedures most efficient and successful in hiring the handicapped.

Seminars should be conducted for the purpose of informing employers, public and private, about new training methods, placement techniques and medical procedures which improve the skills, work attitudes and overall employability of the severely handicapped. 14

REPORT OF THE PRESIDENT'S COMMITTEE ON EMPLOYMENT OF THE HANDICAPPED A CHRONICLE OF EVENTS: 1963 - 1964 

The world lost two champions of the handicapped this past year: 

President John F. Kennedy and Major General Melvin J. Maas. Their contributions live on as everlasting memorials.

The affairs of the handicapped, however, remain in strong, willing hands, under President Lyndon B. Johnson and President's Committee

Chairman Harold Russell. 

The handicapped made appreciable gains in employment over the last 

12 months.

Placements of the handicapped by the nation's local public employment offices totaled 278,000 close to the previous year's 280,000 and still far ahead of the 256,000 placements the year before. 

Placements of disabled veterans numbered 102,000 not far behind the 105,000 total of the year before but well ahead of the figure of two years ago. 

A record 110,000 handicapped persons were rehabilitated this past year under the State-Federal program of vocational rehabilitation well ahead of the 102,000 total of the year before. 

The Veterans Administration treated a record 713,000 disabled veterans in its hospitals during the year past. About 1,000 disabled veterans of 

World War II and the Korean Conflict still were in training under VA's vocational rehabilitation programs.

Civil Service Commission reported that nearly 9,000 handicapped people found jobs in the Federal Government last year -- well above the year's previous record of 8,000.

One reason for this heartening showing seems to be a basic strengthening of America's attitudes toward equality for all its citizens, the handicapped included. 17

Another reason, of course, lies in the many-sided activities of the President's Committee and Governors' Committees across the nation in cooperation with public and private agencies and organizations. 

Some highlights: 

In the field of the mentally restored and mentally retarded, a number of landmark events have taken place. 

A Guide to Job Placement of the Mentally Retarded, prepared in cooperation with the Bureau of Employment Security and the National Association for Retarded Children, found immediate acclaim. An initial printing of 50,000 was exhausted and a second printing is going fast. 

A companion Guide to Job Placement of the Mentally Restored, made possible by the Ida S. Latz Foundation, has just been written by former New York Times columnist Dorothy Barclay Thompson. Publica tion will be in late 1964. "So You're Going to Hire the Mentally Restored" and "So You're

Going to Hire the Mentally Retarded," flyers aimed at employers, were printed two months ago; the stock of 100,000 of each is almost depleted. 

The National Association for Retarded Children, in cooperation with the President's Committee, has launched an awards program honoring the employer in each State and in the nation doing most to further job opportunities for the retarded. 

The Civil Service Commission, National Association for Mental 

Health and President's Committee co-sponsored an experimental orientation program on the mentally restored for top officials of six Government agencies. The program will be extended to other agencies as well.

The Federal Government's new program of employing the mentally retarded has been called to the attention of all State Governors by the President's Committee; so far, 35 Governors have expressed interest in considering similar programs for State jobs. 

There has been heartening progress in the project of eliminating architectural barriers against the handicapped a joint venture of the President's Committee and the National Society for Crippled Children and Adults. Here are evidences of a growing groundswell: 18

So far, 12 States have enacted legislation specifying that all buildings erected with State funds be fully accessible to the handicapped. Eleven more States are planning similar action. 

Sixty-six major cities have conducted inventories of public buildings to learn which could be used by the handicapped. Most of the cities are planning directories for the handicapped, based on the inventories.

Among the many national organizations passing resolutions supporting the elimination of architectural barriers are the veterans' organizations, International Association of Governmental Labor Officials, the Church of Latter Day Saints and many more. 

There is much action in every State in the Union, ranging from a full day's workshop in the State Senate in Alaska to the passage of a strongly-worded resolution of support by a union federation in Wyoming. 

More than a year ago, the President's Committee and the National Inventors' Council announced a contest for the invention of a stairclimbing wheelchair, with $5,000 to be awarded to the winner (compliments of an anonymous donor).

Some 500 entries were received; they have been narrowed down to. a dozen finalists by scientists at the National Bureau of Standards; a panel of three judges has been appointed; selection of the winner is expected by early summer.

The Women's Committee has been most active these past 12 months, with many worthy achievements such as these:

An exhibit of arts and crafts by the handicapped in Washington's Department of Labor Building was introduced April 29 by Mrs. Lyndon 

Johnson and Mrs. Willard Wirtz. Purpose of this exhibit, and others to be held elsewhere, is to promote the sale of quality works of art and craft products by the handicapped. "VOICE," an orientation program for women's groups, has been held in Washington, D. C., and in a number of States. As an outgrowth of Washington's VOICE program, the city's Business and Professional Women's Clubs have organized a speakers' bureau and have arranged for an "Employers' Night" dinner-meeting devoted to jobs for the handicapped. 19

American Women in Radio and Television adopted the "Hire the Handicapped" program as its one-and-only community service project for the year.

Other major women's organizations also have adopted the program, and have been distributing President's Committee material to all local chapters. 

This year's "Ability Counts" writing contest for high school students drew a record-breaking 60,000 entries from across the country. 

As in years past, the DAV has donated prize money for the first five winners, and the AFL-CIO State Federations have brought all State winners to Washington for the Annual Meeting plus sightseeing. 

The Employer Committee has selected as Employer of the Year the Caterpillar Tractor Co. of Peoria, Ill., an industrial pioneer in hiring the handicapped. Nearly one-tenth of its total work force 

3,000 out of 33,800 workers are handicapped. 11 

The Labor Committee announced the distribution of a new film, 'They Have What It Takes," a dramatic demonstration of selective placement of the handicapped produced by the AFL-CIO for the President's Committee; and also the printing of wallet-sized full-color "Hire the Handicapped" calendars by the International Brotherhood of 

Electrical Workers.

Juan B. Ferrer, a vocational counselor who has devoted his entire life to the service of the handicapped in Puerto Rico, is this year's recipient of the Public Personnel Award of the Public Service Committee. 

Among many other things, Ferrer organized and started Puerto Rico's rehabilitation program for disabled veterans; co-authored a law providing training to the handicapped and helped found the Governor's Committee for Employment of the Handicapped.

Dr. John S. Young, specialist in physical medicine and rehabilitation in Denver and former Chairman of the Colorado Governor's Committee, has been named Physician of the Year by the Medical Committee, for his dedicated efforts in furthering the welfare of the handicapped. 

Distinguished Service Awards, the President's Committee's top honor, were approved during the past year for Senator Lister Hill; Kenneth 

King of the Columbia Savings and Loan Association in Denver; Gallaudet 

College for the Deaf; Mrs. Esther Van Wagoner Tufty, Washington correspondent; the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers; Walter Mason of the AFL-CIO; the Joseph Bulova School of Watchmaking; "Plays for 

Living," and P. J. Trevethan, Executive Vice President of Goodwill

Industries of America. 

20

Promotional efforts of the President's Committee and of Governors' Committees reached new high levels over the past year, as the mass media have devoted more and more time and space to the handicapped. 

Radio and TV stations and networks donated more than $3,000,000 in public service time to "Hire the Handicapped" messages during NEPH Week, 1963, alone. TV network dramas featuring problems of acceptance of the handicapped have been on the rise. "East Side West Side," for example, recently highlighted a paraplegic's drive for acceptance. 

Articles on the handicapped have appeared in nationally-circulated magazines such as Factory Magazine, the Executive, Management Review, The Office, and a host of others aimed directly at employers; as well as mass-based magazines such as Look, Life, Reader's Digest and Catholic

REPORT OF THE SECRETARY OF LABOR 

Since my last report in March of 1963, the Bureau of Employment Security has maintained its forward-looking program on behalf of the handicapped, as shown by our operating statistics, development of new tools, and improvement of existing ones. In addition, a number of national developments closely related to bettering the lot of the handicapped have challenged and involved the Bureau. New legislation for the mentally retarded and the mentally restored, amendments to the Manpower Devel opment and Training Act, amendments to the Vocational Education Act, and changes in the Selective Service System with respect to rejectees, have triggered a number of significant activities in the Bureau and State employment services.

Operating Statistics 

Total nonagricultural placements for the 1963 calendar year show a slight decrease over the previous year. This was reflected in the number of handicapped placements, down slightly from 280,000 to 278,000. As a percentage of total placements, however, the ratio of handicapped placements was maintained. A similar trend was noticeable in the slight decrease in number of applicants referred to the Employment Service by the State vocational rehabilitation agencies as compared with last year. 

It should be observed, however, that over the past five years this number has doubled, going from 11,000 in 1958 to nearly 23,000 in 1963. 

Technical Aids Developed 

In the series of Interviewing Guides for Specific Disabilities, three important guides were published and made available to our local offices and other professional workers. They cover Mental Retardation, Visual 

Impairments, and Legal Blindness and Blindness. In addition, a first draft has been prepared on psychiatric disabilities for use with the mentally restored. As with other guides, however, this guide will be reviewed with experts in the field before it is printed. 

The Training Manual on Selective Placement has been revised, up-dated, tried out and reviewed by staff in selected State offices and is being readied for reproduction. The operating manual on services to the handicapped has also been rewritten. In addition, a portion of the management section of the BES manual has been revised to facilitate staffing and organization of services to the handicapped. 22

A new Employment Capacities Form for obtaining medical information has been developed and approved by the Bureau. This form is in two parts: one for reporting pertinent physical capacities information, and the other for reporting mental capacities information for use with the mentally retarded and mentally restored. 

Also in preparation is a new booklet describing the services available to the handicapped in the local offices of the Employment Service. 

Special program letters have led to revisions of some of the Employment Service-Vocational Rehabilitation agency State agreements and promoted more cooperation between the Civil Service Commission coordinators and the Employment Service.

Of special interest to those who work with the blind are the arrangements which the Employment Service has made with selected local offices for providing a testing service for blind transcribing machine operators.

Arrangements for this service have been made with State agencies serving the blind.

Surveys and Studies of Special Problems 

About a year ago, the Employment Service made the observation that the number of people released from mental hospitals had increased at the rate of 20% a year and that in some States the number released from public mental hospitals was equal to the number of handicapped from all causes who registered with the Employment Service during the same period. The Employment Service has, over the years, reported successful arrangements with mental hospitals for placing the mentally restored. 

In view of the increased demands for service to this applicant group, a nationwide survey of local office arrangements with mental hospitals was conducted. Only some of the highlights of the survey can be mentioned in this report.

More than 200 hospitals were included in the survey--138 were public mental hospitals, and 32 were Veterans Administration mental hospitals. 

Also included were over 30 general hospitals with neuropsychiatric facilities; however, only 5 private mental hospitals were included. 

Program arrangements with these hospitals go back as far as 1940, although there have been many new arrangements with mental hospitals during the last few years. Formal arrangements exist with 38 hospitals --informal arrangements with the rest. 23

The percentage of time provided by Employment Service counselors or interviewers assigned to work with these hospitals ranges from 1 to 90%. One of the more striking features of the survey is that all but three States report insufficient staff to meet the requests for services. One State said that a counselor working with a State hospital was placing 15 to 20 mentally restored people a month, but with more help, the Employment Service could double the placements. 

In one State, the Employment Service staff member serving on the hospital team was hired by the hospital. 

The number of admissions to the hospitals having arrangements with the Employment Service totaled 224,000 during the past year. On the basis of previous experience in the United States about 80% of the admissions could be expected to be released from public mental hospitals in a year. This gives some idea of the magnitude of the problem. Although it is true that, at this time, the proportion of released patients seeking to enter the labor market is not known, expert opinion places the figure at 30%.

Based upon this survey, the national office of the Bureau is currently preparing guidelines for the States to facilitate expansion of their services to this group.

REPORT OF THE ADMINISTRATOR OF VETERANS AFFAIRS 

I am pleased to furnish this report to the Advisory Council which shares our aims, efforts, and concern for the rehabilitation and employment of our handicapped people.

The Veterans Administration has continued its primary role in the care, treatment, and rehabilitation of veterans. As a major Federal employer, it has actively carried out and supported the program of the President's Committee internally and through its service organizations, state, community, and other public relationships. Following are the more significant accomplishments for the reporting period: 

Additional Vocational Rehabilitation for Blinded Veterans 

On August 16, 1962 the President signed PL 87-591. This law extended to July 30, 1975 - the time during which a blinded veteran can be provided vocational rehabilitation by the VA. It makes these benefits available to any blind veteran of WW II or the Korean conflict who has not previously been provided vocational rehabilitation. Additionally, it provides for giving further vocational rehabilitation training to a veteran who has already been rehabilitated for a service-connected disability, but later becomes blind due to a worsening of his service incurred disability.

Vocational Rehabilitation of Veterans Disabled Other Than in a Period of War

On October 15, 1962 veterans who incurred disabilities in active service between WW II and the Korean conflict or after the Korean conflict became eligible for vocational rehabilitation through the enactment of PL 87-815. This is the first time that vocational rehabilitation benefits have been made available to disabled veterans other than those disabled during an actual period of war. Now, the veteran who becomes disabled in the "cold war" period receives the same vocational rehabilitation benefits that heretofore were available to veterans of WW II and the 

Korean conflict.

Special Emphasis on Rehabilitating the Seriously Disabled 

In recent years the VA has been emphasizing motivation of seriously disabled veterans for purpose of encouraging them to attempt rehabilitation in spite of the seriousness of their disabilities. In many instances a serious disability is so disruptive of the individual's drive and so 37

devastating to his ego that rehabilitation for the purpose of restoring employability will never be attempted unless he can be persuaded that rehabilitation can become a reality for him. In November of 1963 the VA published a special circular which requires the identifying of all veterans rated for a serious psychiatric disability incurred in peacetime service and all disabled veterans rated 100 per cent disabled. 

These veterans are then contacted usually by mail the first time: Then there are follow-up personal visits to the home to interest and encourage them to employ all possible means to develop their remaining abilities for a productive, useful life. This approach was used for the seriously disabled veterans of the Korean conflict who had not attempted vocational rehabilitation on their own initiative. Over 1200 seriously disabled veterans of that conflict who had not attempted vocational rehabilitation were encouraged to enter training. A majority of those who entered were successful at becoming rehabilitated and suitably employed. This experience has prompted VA to make a real effort to motivate those seriously disabled veterans of the cold war who have not yet attempted training.

The Psychiatry, Neurology, and Psychology Service of our Department of Medicine and Surgery is studying the employment status, employment potential and vocational adjustment of veterans with a serious psychosis following treatment and discharge from psychiatric hospitals.

In one large group of these patients who were most seriously ill and subsequently discharged from the hospital, 27 percent were constructively employed and adapting to community life. An additional portion were working on a limited part-time basis. The very significant aspect of these findings is that their work was accomplished on a regularly scheduled, full-time basis. Of additional significance is the realization of need for greater development of vocational training as a part of psychiatric treatment, the use of sheltered workshops, transitional therapeutic work programs and day and night hospitals.

It is believed that these programs can contribute to even greater job placement and vocational success with the most seriously ill psychiatric patients. Development of these programs is under way. 

Promoting Employment Opportunities for Persons with Histories of 

Psychiatric Disability

In January 1963 the VA published the findings of a study which showed that a large proportion of non-hospitalized veterans with rated psychiatric disability are employed and have achieved satisfactory job adjustment. Two reports were issued, one a pamphlet for the general 38

public entitled "They Return to Work," the other a technical report describing in more detail the methodology of the study. "They Return to Work" has had widespread distribution through the VA and the President's Committee to State and local committees for employment of the handicapped, Federal agency coordinators of employment for the handicapped, employment service and rehabilitation agency workers, mental health and other interested groups. Newspaper releases throughout the country have helped to inform the general public of the main findings of the study. By thus publicizing factual information to help dispel existing misconceptions and prejudices about the vocational potential of the psychiatrically disabled, the VA and the President's Committee have contributed to current efforts to promote job opportunities for persons with histories of mental or emotional disturbance. 

Mental Health 

In line with the President's mental health program and with recent legislation, the Veterans Administration is operating centers in urban areas that offer a wide range of treatment and services to meet the needs of psychiatric patients.

One of these comprehensive mental health programs, for example, has been created at the West Side VA Hospital in Chicago. 

Just about every practical tool developed in psychiatric research has been incorporated into some phase of the combined hospital-outpatient services program.

In the hospital itself is an intensive treatment inpatient area of 105 beds. 

Here the full hospital regimen is available, with the treatment programs being enhanced by research and clinical support of two large medical schools.

In the outpatient service building adjacent are supporting programs, some of them by law restricted to veterans whose mental illness resulted directly from military service.

There is a Mental Hygiene Clinic with both day and evening services available to veterans with service-connected disabilities. 

And there is a Day Treatment Center to which veterans may come while bridging the gap between hospitalization and return to community life. 39

Then, available in each veteran's hometown, is a combined psychiatric and pharmacy program available to service-connected veterans who cannot make the trips in to the Chicago clinic. The VA pays the bills for this fee-basis treatment at home. 

For the non-service-connected veterans, a form of outpatient service has been developed within the hospital, known as Continued Bed Occupancy Care. The veteran quits the hospital but returns periodically for a period of treatment limited to one year for medication or psychotherapy. 

An integral part of the program in both hospital and clinic is the Social Work Service, which must counsel and supervise the veteran once he leaves his hospital bed. West Side Social Workers go further than that. 

They watch over all former mental patients from any VA hospitals who have returned to Illinois. In both hospital and clinic they have set up group therapy classes which families of patients are encouraged to attend, and which are supervised by psychiatrists. 

Day and evening hours of the clinic were found necessary to accommodate working veterans. Many have received intensive treatment in VA hospitals and are now back at work. 

During 1963 alone, the mental hygiene clinic recorded 18,666 patient visits. At the end of the year, 1,691 veterans all service-connected 

were under active treatment programs. 

Many of these former patients require continued medication and received more than 15,000 prescriptions during the year. These prescriptions were filled at the outpatient pharmacy or in the veterans' home towns and paid for by VA.

In this way, VA has kept veterans on the job, restored them to their families as competent members of the community, and made more hospital beds available for intensive treatment. 

The Day Treatment Center which the VA has developed in recent years assists former mental patients in making the often difficult transition from hospital to community life. 

Newly discharged patients sometimes have difficulty with strangers or members of their own family. Insecure, they cannot accept jobs or even approach employers. Too often, they returned to home situations where conflicts which had contributed to their breakdown still exist. 40

To counteract this, the VA developed the Day Treatment Center where former mental patients may join in social activities, take part in workshop programs in woodworking, watch and radio/TV repair, etc., or benefit from art and painting therapy or music. 

Twenty-four such VA Day Treatment Centers are now in operation in urban locations. 

Both the hospital and outpatient service in Chicago benefit from affiliations with two large medical schools the Illinois University Medical School and the Chicago Medical School. The schools provide a deans' committee to supervise treatment and assist in research. Many of the hospital's psychiatrists and psychologists hold teaching posts, while the schools' faculty in turn provides outstanding consultant services not only to the full-time staff but also to the residents in the psychiatric training program.

It adds up to a modern approach to mental illness which is helping to empty hospital wards and return the patients to their communities. 

Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation The vital role of our Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation program continues to be the maximum rehabilitation of hospitalized veterans. 

Everything feasible is being done in the rehabilitation of every hospitalized patient, whether for his return to the community as a productive citizen or for a much better life for him in a sheltered environment. 

Some brief examples are: 

1. Patient A, aged 38, was transferred from a State Hospital where he had been for five years. There was strong indication that his illiteracy and inability to find work after four years in the Navy greatly contributed to his breakdown. 

At time of transfer he was not privileged, had been on maximum security wards, and was considered uncooperative and dangerous. After a few weeks, he was given privileges and assigned to Educational Therapy to learn to read and write. 

He was assigned to the patient incentive therapy program but the pace was too great; he became highly disorganized and was again placed on a closed ward. He subsequently reappeared in Educational Therapy and the building process began once more. This time he was assigned to the photography section and made fine progress. He has since been granted an MHB discharge and his relatives are delighted with his rehabilitation. They plan to assist him in setting up a photography shop. 41

2. Patient B is a B/K amputee. Following a prior hospitalization period, he was placed on discharge as a picture framer with a large concern. This had been his first job in many years. Keeping in close touch with Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, he was encouraged to work out certain problems which permitted him to continue in his job. On re-entry into the hospital, his job was filled by someone else. We have been able to place him with a national golf club manufacturer, where he is working happily as a lathe operator. 

3. During Patient Achievement Day Exercises at one of our hospitals in May 1963, the principal speaker was a former star professional basketball player. Presently a paraplegic, he is coach of the flying wheel chair Jets of the Pan-Am Airlines. He also is fully employed by Pan-Am as an accountant and his presentation on the need for rehabilitation was enthusiastically received by the patients. 

Restoration Centers 

The VA Restoration Center at Hines, Illinois is designed to help disabled patients who have attained maximum hospital benefits return to the community in whole or in part self-supporting. This program not only helps the veteran and his family but makes it possible to treat more patients within the VA's available facilities. The Hines Center is providing rehabilitative care to a daily average of 101 veterans. Since the program began, more than 150 restorees have been discharged as having received maximum restorative care.

A second Restoration Center attached to the VA Hospital in East Orange, New Jersey will commence operations during this fiscal year.

Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Program The Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Program contributes substantially to the employability of over 400,000 disabled veterans each year by furnishing them prosthetic devices and repair services. While we have no statistics to indicate specifically how many of these veterans are 42

Many new and improved prosthetic devices have been developed in recent years through our Prosthetics Research and Development Program. A Patellar-Tendon-Bearing below-knee artificial leg which eliminates the previously required steel knee joints and leather thigh corset; a Total-Contact plastic socket and hydraulic knee mechanism for above-knee artificial legs; a new type foot which eliminates the need for a separate ankle joint; and a greatly improved "Long Cane" for use by blinded veterans represent only a few examples of improvements which are making it easier for the prosthetically handicapped veteran to obtain and hold a job in competition with non-disabled people.

Development work also is continuing on electronic reading machines and guidance devices for the blind, and encouraging progress is being made. A guidance device and obstacle detector which will allow a blind person to travel where he wishes without fear or assistance, and a reading machine with which he can read a business letter or the numbers on paper money without the assistance of a sighted person will substantially widen the vocational prospects of these people and there is definite promise that these things should be possible within the next few years.

ACTIVITIES OF THE CIVIL SERVICE COMMISSION IN FURTHERING EMPLOYMENT OF THE HANDICAPPED IN GOVERNMENT 

It is a pleasure for me to meet with you again for our annual assessment of our joint efforts to restore more handicapped people to useful places in American Society, and to present this report of the Commission's activities to promote employment of the handicapped in the Federal Civil Service.

Our activities for the last year were concentrated on the new program for employment of the mentally retarded, and on developing a more effective approach to promoting placement of unusually difficult cases such as those recovered from a mental illness and those with serious handicaps who have demonstrated ability to do a job under temporary appointment but whose handicaps prevent them from obtaining eligibility for permanent placement under competitive rules.

The very fact that we have been able to devote increased attention to these categories of handicaps which require special placement assistance is, I am convinced, a tribute to the progress achieved to date in winning wide acceptance of the physically handicapped throughout the Government. 

Selective placement of the physically handicapped has, by and large, been accepted as a valid selection technique by Government personnel officials who are responsible for selecting the best people available in order to assure accomplishment of their agency mission. No amount of selling or emotional appeal can be expected to move these officials to employ the handicapped to any appreciable extent if the handicapped had not in the past proved themselves to be valuable employees. Today, it is the exception rather than the rule when selection officials pass over a fully rehabilitated physically handicapped applicant for Federal employment whose examination score places him within reach for selection from a register. When this happens the cause for non-selection usually lies elsewhere, e.g., poor references from previous employers, unpleasant personality, and other factors unrelated to the handicap. 

Our goal is to win this same acceptance for applicants whose only handicap is that they have a history of a mental or emotional illness and those whose mental capacity limits the type of position for which they can be considered. 47

While considerable progress has been made in promoting these difficult placements, particularly as exemplified by the successful launching of the new program for employment of the retarded, much more needs to be done and this effort will continue to be our major emphasis in the upcoming year.

Civil Service Retirement: Continued Utilization of Handicapped 

Employees in Lieu of Disability Retirement

Heads of Departments and Independent Establishments: 

PURPOSE 

This letter is issued to: (1) emphasize the desirability of reassignment of disabled employees in lieu of disability retirement whenever feasible and (2) provide instructions for expanded and more explicit certification of agency reassignment efforts on Standard Form 2801-A, Superior Officer's 

Statement. 

BACKGROUND 

We have a continuing problem of employees whose disability retirements are approved because of injury or illness which prevents them from performing the duties of their positions efficiently and safely, but who could continue in productive employment if provided the opportunity through selective placement in available positions for which they are qualified. This problem is frequently brought to our attention by the employee concerned and by organizations interested in the employment of the handicapped. 

Executive Branch policy on employment of the handicapped emphasizes the desirability of continued utilization, through reassignment, of on-board employees who because of illness or injury are unable to continue in their positions. This was specifically spelled out in the White House Policy 

Statement of September 6, 1961, directed to Heads of Departments and Agencies, as follows: "Disability retirement should be considered only after every feasible effort at reassignment has been made." As part of our role

AGENCY RESPONSIBILITY FOR UTILIZATION OF QUALIFIED EMPLOYEES 

Each agency, of course, is responsible for separating by means of disability retirement the eligible employee who is truly unfit for useful and efficient service because of physical or mental disability. However, there is a further responsibility to retain the skills and services of the employee who, though unfit for service in his present position, is medically and otherwise qualified to perform service in another available position without detriment to himself or the Government. 

Apparent eligibility for disability retirement should not automatically trigger the processing of a retirement application. Instead, where feasible, efforts at reassignment should begin when the necessity for it becomes evident rather than permitting continuation of a situation which is going to degenerate. Superior officers should be alert for indications that the employee is experiencing increasing difficulty in the performance of his duties. If the difficulty is the result of a physical or mental impairment, the superior officer should attempt to effect reassignment to a position within his jurisdiction. If no positions exist for which the employee is qualified, the case should be referred to the agency coordinator for the employment of the handicapped having jurisdiction over the place of employment or, if a coordinator is not available, to the personnel office for consideration of: Reassignment Knowledge of the employee's education and work experience may indicate that he can still perform efficiently in other work. 

Reassignment need not necessarily be limited to positions of the same grade or class. The possibility that the employee would be willing to accept reassignment to a lower grade position with less strenuous physical or mental demands is not to be overlooked. 

Retraining Agencies should also give serious consideration to the possibility of retraining disabled employees for positions for which they have the basic qualifications and capabilities. Although an initial expense is involved in retraining, the result will justify the expenditure if the employee and the position are carefully matched beforehand.

Once an employee accepts reassignment to a position for which he is medically qualified, he voids his eligibility for disability retirement based on his inability to perform the duties of his previous position. To encourage the employee to accept reassignment on a trial basis and yet retain his eligibility, agencies can exercise the option of placing the employee on a detail assignment of up to six months. If performance in the new position is mutually acceptable to agency and employee, consideration can then be given to making the reassignment permanent. 63

(3) Efforts at reassignment of disabled employees should not end when there are no available positions within the coordinator's jurisdiction. As stated in FPM Letter 339-2 of September 22, 1961, coordinators are responsible for maintaining effective liaison with the coordinators of other Federal establishments accessible to them in the same geographical area for the interchange of information relative to agency needs and specific job opportunities. This avenue should be explored if no positions for which the employee qualifies exist in the employing agency. 

AGENCY CERTIFICATION OF REASSIGNMENT EFFORTS 

Standard Form 2801-A, Superior Officer's Statement, is a required accompanying document for all applications for disability retirement. In item 11 of this form, a disability applicant's superior officer presently is required to state what efforts have been made to assign the applicant to any other job for which he is qualified. In addition to this statement by the superior officer, this space now will be used for a certification by the responsible agency coordinator or, in the absence of a coordinator, the personnel officer, as to what further efforts he has made towards reassignment. 

This certification is to be a brief resume of actions taken or a statement telling why the circumstances of the case preclude reassignment. 

If there is not sufficient space on the form, the coordinator's certification may be made on an attached piece of paper. SF 2801-A will not be revised until we have had sufficient experience with the new procedure. 

AGENCY RESPONSIBILITY TO THE EMPLOYEE 

Contact with disabled employees is necessary to properly appraise their qualifications and work out reassignment. Where the issue of disability retirement versus reassignment is involved, the employee should be given a balanced presentation of the advantages and disadvantages of each course of action.

In considering disability retirement, the employee should have a firm estimate of his annuity benefits and be made aware of Retirement Act provisions for discontinuance of disability annuity upon recovery or restoration to earning capacity. Again it is important that he should know that eligibility for disability retirement is contingent upon inability to perform useful and efficient service in the grade or class of position occupied and that if he accepts reassignment to a position for which he is not disabled, and later finds the job not to his liking, he cannot retire from it on account of disability. The employee should also know that if he accepts another position with a lower salary, it may reduce the amount of his insurance coverage under the life insurance program. Separation from service from a position for which the employee is physically and mentally qualified may also result in loss of health benefits and life insurance coverage unless he is eligible for retirement on an immediate annuity with at least 12 years of creditable service. 64

COERCION NOT TO ENTER REASSIGNMENT EFFORTS 

Nothing in this letter is to be construed as justification of an attempt to coerce an employee to accept reassignment or retraining against his will. The Civil Service Retirement Act is specific concerning the eligibility requirements for disability retirement and the employee who meets those requirements is entitled to be so retired. The Commission fully recognizes that most disability applicants are in fact totally disabled for all employment. To the extent that positions are available, however, employing agencies are expected to assure that those employees who are qualified for further Government service in another grade or class of position have the opportunity to serve.

FPM LETTER NO. 339-5 SUBJECT: 

Employment of Severely Handicapped Persons Who Have Demonstrated Ability to Perform Duties Under Temporary 

Appointments

Heads of Departments and Independent Establishments: 

During the past year we have noted a few very worthy cases of severely handicapped persons who have demonstrated the capacity to become self-supporting through temporary employment in a competitive position but who cannot normally obtain eligibility for permanent placement under competitive rules. 

To further the program for the utilization of the handicapped by making it possible to give permanent employment to a limited number of such persons, the Commission recently approved a general Schedule A authority. The authority section 213.3102(u) will permit the exception of not more than 100 positions when filled by severely handicapped persons who, under temporary appointment, have demonstrated their ability to perform satisfactorily the duties of the position. Only persons who are currently serving in a competitive position under temporary appointment may be appointed under this authority. Its use is subject to prior approval of the Commission and carries a June 30, 1965, limit.

The stringent limitation on the number of positions that may be filled under this authority dictates extreme selectivity on the part of agencies in submitting requests for exceptions under it. For example, this authority is not intended to be used for the exception of blocks of positions for a particular category of handicapped employees. Exception will be approved for only the most seriously disabled and deserving employees who have fully demonstrated their ability to carry on the duties of the position in a satisfactory manner through an adequate trial period under temporary appointment.

Criteria for Determining Who May be Considered Severely Handicapped

Agencies may request this Schedule A exception for (1) those with severe hearing or visual impairments, (2) wheel chair cases, and (3) persons with multiple impairments which indivi lually would not be considered as seriously handicapping but which would in combination be so regarded. 

There may, of course, be other severe impairments that could also be considered qualifying for the benefits of the Schedule A authority. 

Agencies may request an advisory opinion in such cases from the medical officer in the Commission office having jurisdiction over the area where the position is located. 

How to Apply for an Exception Under Section 213.3102(u) 

Application for exception of a position under the conditions prescribed in section 213.3102(u) should be made to the Commission's regional office having jurisdiction over the area where the position is located. The following information must be submitted by the agency office desiring the exception: (1) a request for the employee's appointment under section 213.3102(u); (2) the job title, grade, and description of duties; (3) a report of a current complete medical examination of the proposed appointee; (4) a statement that the employee entered the competitive position under a temporary appointment other than a 

30-day special needs appointment; (5) a performance evaluation of his work under his current temporary appointment; and (6) a statement of the reasons why the proposed Schedule A appointee would have little or no opportunity to compete successfully for career-conditional appointment. 

The Commission's regional medical officer will forward the agency's request and supporting data with his recommendation to the Medical Director in the central office who will, in turn, prepare a final reconmendation to the Commission on the case. 

Reporting Requirements 

In order to give us information on how well the employees hired under this authority are working out on a long-term basis, agencies are requested to furnish the regional medical officer with a report of each employee's progress at the end of his first six months of service under Schedule A. 

This will help to afford a basis for an evaluation of the program. 67

To make certain that the limit of 100 positions to be excepted under this authority is not exceeded, the Medical Director in the Commission's central office will maintain a roster of the excepted positions and their incumbents. The employing agency must report immediately to the Chief of the Medical Division in the central office by letter when any individual whose position is excepted under this authority is separated. (Such report should include a brief statement of the reason for the separation.)
