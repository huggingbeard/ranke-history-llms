SHALL, the writer of this memorial, from the time of her brother's enlistment and tragic death, has been a great "American patriot" and active in all patriotic work, particularly in regard to the Grand Army of the Republic and the boys who wore the blue.

She has given of her life many years of activity at the State Soldiers' Home, Sandusky, Ohio, in giving entertainments at the Assembly Hall and cheering the sick in the hospital. Veterans affectionately called her "Mother Bickerdye" and "Angel of the Home." She was later matron of the Madison Home for five years, where she was beloved by all its members for her many deeds of kindness.

In the local Corps, since she became a member in 1895, she has held the positions of President, senior and junior vice presidents, treasurer. color bearer. etc. In the Department of Ohio

Woman's Relief Corps she filled the offices of chaplain, instituting and installing officer, press correspondent and department president. She filled all these offices to her honor and credit. Furthermore, she won a contest over the wet and dry of over 60,000 majority, receiving a Swiss diamond ring as trophy.

In this memorial, given to a beloved brother, a tribute should be given her for her years of fine service. 

E. H. MACK,

Editor of The Register.

EMOGENE NIVER MARSHALL

Foreword 

EDWIN W. NIVER, son of John B. and Sarah A. Niver, was born near 

Centerton, Huron County, Ohio, November 6, 1844. He enlisted October 

7, 1851, in the Third O. V. C., Company I, when not quite seventeen years old. He died at Andersonville, Georgia, Confederate prison, June 19th, 1854. The Third O. V. C. encamped near Monroeville. Well do I remember in January, 1862, when the regiment in companies passed through Greenfield, Huron County, Ohio, en route for the Southland. It was a sad, yet grand sight-horses prancing, the winter day cold, but sun shining. The good people were ready with a bountiful lunch. They did not dismount. At last, all too soon, the order was given to march. With many a hand-shake and 

God bless you, they passed from sight, amid the weeping of mothers, wives, sisters and friends, with the air of Dixie wafted back to them. Passed from sight, some never to return, some to die of sickness, some of wounds in hospitals, some on the battlefield, some in prisons. They passed the first night in Shelby, from there to Camp Dennison, fifteen miles north of Cincinnati. Were in camp there about six weeks; from there to Jeffersonville,

Indiana; remained there about six weeks; from there to the South where active service began.

This book is not intended to give the history of that regiment, but a memorial to my brother, Edwin White Niver-a tribute to the memory of a boy who gave his life for his country, not on a bloody battlefield, although engaged in many, or in a hospital, but languishing in prison pens, preferring death to dishonor in swearing allegiance to the Confederacy. 

He was home two different times-two weeks each time, on sick leave. Once on his return to Louisville after sick leave he participated in the hunt for the rebel Morgan, who was terrorizing the section around there, and being unable to join his regiment on account of the Morgan raid. 

The letters herein published in this little book are the only ones preserved. Many were lost owing to the death of my mother, in September, 1864. My brother had passed over the border nearly three months before, but we were not aware of it until just one month later. [5]

MY MOTHER 

"I sat beneath the same old tree, And I have heard the birds sing the same old song,

When the mother I loved stood near by me 

And her presence was dear-I shall treasure it long. 

Her hair was sprinkled with white, like the pureness of snow, And her words were the sweetest that ever I heard. 

Yet her cheek was so paled the health did not glow, But there was youth in her voice, and she sang like a bird.

Until the news of my brother, then her voice was hushed 

With anguish untold, her heart was crushed.

The Letters of Edwin W. Niver 

Written While Serving in the Union Army During the Civil War Between the North and the South The first letter written at Camp Dennison was not dated. And as the "History of Third O. V. I." says that the sabers were received on Janaury 

20th, it must have been written after that date. 

Dear Parents and Friends:

I take this opportunity to let you know I am well and having a good time. Have good quarters, better than I expected. The boys are all very well satisfied, we have got our sabers. Now I suppose we will go to Texas or be disbanded. It would be a pleasant place here if it were not for the mud. We have had a great deal of rain since we have been here, although the weather is warm. Camp Dennison is situated in the valley of the Little 

Miami river, surrounded by hills two hundred and three hundred feet high. 

I have just been on a scout across the river and had a good old time of it, only one man getting upset in the river, he coming out all safe and sound, getting only a little wet. I write in a hurry, not having much time. Give my respects to all. No more at present. 

Camp Dennison, Feb. the 9th, 1862. 

Dear Parents: It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well and hope that these few lines will find you all the same. We have got marching orders and will start for Louisville Monday the 10th. When you get this we will be in Kentucky I suppose. It is all excitement here now about going to Kentucky and expecting a good time. We drew our revolvers and carbines today. It is very sickly in camp now all but our regiment, there are only about two or three sick now. I suppose I must quit writing now. 

I have no money nor nothing. Don't write till you hear from me that we have gone or not, for we may not go yet for a while. No more at present. 

Bowling Green, Ky., March 14, 1862. 

Dear Parents:

I am well and in Bowling Green. Expect to go on to Nashville in two or three days. Sheffield was in Camp last night. We came across him here at Bowling Green, or rather he came across us. His reg't. is six miles below Nashville, Tennesee. We have had a good time marching here. We stopped at the Mammoth Cave one day. Here is a ticket I got three miles in the cave. I drank some of the sweetest water from a spring about three miles and a half I ever drank. It was about two hundred feet below the surface. 

Joe Williams is at Nashville now.

It is a wonder the rebels ever gave up Bowling Green without a struggle, they were so well fortified. We have been paid off now, but did not get much. Expect to be paid off again. No more at present. [7]

EDWIN W. NIVER (Picture Taken at Galion)

Nashville, March 26, '62. 

Dear Mother: I am well and hope you are the same. We are now in Nashville in Wood's division, but the report today is that we are transferred to another division down the Mississippi, perhaps to New Orleans. I don't have much time to write often for there are so many sick that it fetches those that are well on duty about every other day. None of our boys are sick; we have been pretty lucky about that. This is a small letter, but I have no time to write more. 

Savannah, April 24. 

Dear Parents: I take this opportunity to let you know that I am well and the rest of the boys are well. Stub Sykes is dead. He died the 17th of 

April. It is very sickly since the battle near Savannah. 

Our regiment was not in the battle. We were on a scouting expedition and were not there soon enough to take an active part in it, although we had a small skirmish a few days before. We charged into Lawrenceburg and took the town, and Companies I and G chased the rebels eight miles, but could not catch them. We are at Savannah now but intend to cross the river tomorrow. We were within seven miles of the battle and we thought we were close enough, although we were willing to go if needed, for I tell you it was a bloody battle when 1,000 were killed by one broadside of our gun boats.

It is supposed that about 13,000 of our men were killed in this battle. It is not known how many rebels were killed. Every house in this city was full of wounded the day after the battle. I tell you it was a dear bought victory, but a glorious one. The rebels fought like devils, but they were crazy with whiskey and powder or they would not fought as they did. 

The ground for three miles was covered with dead, so you could not step without stepping on some one. But the battle of Corinth is expected to be a still larger one and if our side wins the battle I think there will not be much more fighting. 

Give my love to all. No more at present. 

Dear Father: 

Battlefield of Shiloh, May 6th.

I am well and hope you are all the same. We received two months' pay today, twenty-six dollars. I would send it home but I tell you it is a hard place without money. If I should get sick I could come home, but if I sent it home I could not. It is not hard work or those to go home that are sick if they have got money. We are now within two miles of the rebels. We have them surrounded now and if they get away this time they are smarter than I think they are.

There are 300,000 of our men here now and I think we have got them this time, if we have there will not be much more fighting. No more at present. 

3d O. V. C., Co. I, Wood's Division. 

Dear Parents: 

Battlefield of Shiloh, May the 13th.

I am well and hope these few lines will find you all the same. We are now within a few miles of Corinth where the rebels are stationed in large forces, quite as many as we have, although we have some two or three hundred thousand here now and more coming every day. When they do fight it will be a hard one I am afraid. Our company has gone out scouting to- [9]

day. Our reg't. or part of it had quite a fight the other day with rebel pickets (our scouts) but had to retreat, being outnumbered by the enemy. 

Some of our reg't. is in sight of the rebel camp every day and who knows but our company will have a little fight today. I should like to have been with them but had to stay in camp. They have detailed me for chief cook for twenty days, so I am excused from all duty. I have it pretty easy now, having nothing to do but to see that the cooking is done. We have to cook by the company now, so there are four beside myself cooking. Now the report is that Seigel is here. But I do not know that it is so, but if it is so the fight will open before long, although there is fighting every day, but merely to make them keep their distance. I wish you would send me some postage stamps. It is impossible to get them here, for there are none to get, but everything else. I think it is a good plan to have suttlers along or we would go pretty hungry sometimes. Give my best wishes to all inquiring friends. Tell Grandma I wrote to her but perhaps it did not go. 

6th Division, 3d O. V. C. 

Dear Parents: 

Camp Stanley, Murfreesboro, Tenn., May 18th, 1862. 

I received your letter yesterday and was glad to hear you are well. Our regiment is very healthy and so is the whole army. There are but very few sick, considering the number of troops here. Everything is still here and it is a mystery to all why the army does not make an advance. Our regiment is going to move camp tomorrow. 

You said Fred Bernerd was coming home, but that is a mistake as he has no intentions of going home. You wanted to know if I received my back pay. Yes, I received what was due me. There are several months back yet to the whole regiment. They never pay all, but always keep three or four months back.

There is no news to write as you get it all as soon as I do, I suppose. No more at present. 

Dear Mother: 

Camp Near Corinth, May 26, 1862.

It was with pleasure that I received your letter. As for myself, I am not very well.

Dick Waggoner has been very sick, but is getting better now. Our captain is sick and is going home--in fact, we have no commissioned officer now. Our line of battle is within 1½ miles of the enemy. Our line of battle extends 24 miles. There is skirmishing every day, but there is no telling when the battle will open. I think they intend to starve them out. Deserters tell us that they are on half rations now. I should like to hear the battle open today-the quicker, the better for I do not think we will go any farther if we whip them here, for I should like very much to be at home next Christmas to eat roast turkey. 

Give my love to Em and Mate and the rest of them. No more at present. 

Dear Sister:

Camp Near Corinth, May 29th, 1862. 

It is with pleasure that I find this opportunity to write to you. The regiment has gone after the enemy with the rest of the cavalry. I was not able to go with them. The darned rebels have all left Corinth and we have [ 10 ]

got to follow them now still farther. I was in hopes that we would not have to go any farther South, but we have got another 100 miles farther to go, and perhaps farther. And it was all our general's fault. He either was afraid to attack or he wanted them to run so he will make a little more money by it. I know he gets a good many curses for not fighting them while we had them. Our second lieutenant died yesterday and our captain has gone home, and first lieutenant is at home, so you see "Co. I" is pretty hard up. Third Ohio is pretty hard up, take it all through, for they cannot raise 

500 men. The colonel is sick, besides a number of other officers are sick. 

Charles Sanders' cousin, by the name of Miller, died the other day, and if they take us farther South it will be more sickly than ever. But no more at present. Give my respects to all. 

This letter was mailed June 6th at Cairo. 

Dear Mother: 

Thursday Eve., July 11, 1862.

I am now at Columbus, but have got our transportation passes and leave tonight for Cincinnati. (The letter is so torn that the above is all I have of it, but the writer, my brother, had been home two weeks on sick leave and was on his way back to his regiment with his captain, Gaylord, who had also been home on sick leave.-E. N. M.) 

Dear Sister: 

July 13, 1862.

I am at Louisville now. We have had a very pleasant time and arrived here last night. We start in the morning for Nashville. Colonel Morgan has been playing the devil in Kentucky, and great apprehension for the safety of this town having Morgan here, and threatened to appear when least expected. But there is no danger of that, for he dare not do it. 

I thought you said he was a prisoner down on the island, but you are mistaken.

Well, I cannot think of anything else this time, and supper is ready. 

Louisville, Ky. (It was supposed this Morgan was imprisoned on Johnson's Island, but it turned out to be another Morgan.--E. N. M.) 

Dear Mother: 

July 17th, 1862.

It is with pleasure that I can say that we had the best supper, “Cap” and myself. We marched 40 miles today, reaching Springfield, Ky., and a good Union man invited us to supper and spend the evening, to which we, of course, accepted. Where, by thunder, I saw a pretty girl and we were very highly entertained and having pretty nigh fell in love (with the music, I mean), which was splendid. "Cap" said she was the best player he ever heard. We are under Captain Gaylord, 100 men of us, scouting after Morgan. 

We slept last night with one eye open, you better believe. Tonight we are in Springfield, stopping at the hotel. I do not suppose I or the "Cap" will get to the company in a month. Well, we are having a good time of it any way, although it is very dangerous work as it is. 

We leave tomorrow morning for somewhere, I guess. I don't know. 

Give my respects to all inquiring friends. That grand party lady particularly, I mean. [11]

Dear Parents: 

July 28th, 1862.

I am well and at Louisville at present. We returned last night from our chase after Morgan. We chased him out of Kentucky. I do not suppose we will get to our regiment for a month yet. We had a good time on our scout last time. We lived at the hotels most of the time and had a Kentucky dance once and had a good time in general. We went after Morgan's plan. 

He went along, taking the property of the Union men, such as horses, for instance, so we served the secesh the same way, but he is a darned coward. 

He had eight hundred men with him and we had only three hundred with us. And he ran like the devil from us at Richmond, Ky., but we were reinforced the next day so at to make 1,400 men, but I am mad at not getting to our regiment sooner. The captain is as fast as I am. He cannot get away before I do. 

Well, no more at present. Give my respects to all. Louisville. 

Direct to E. Niver, 1st Convalescent Cavalry, near Louisville, Ky. 

Dear Parents: 

Camp Near Winchester, August 16th, 1862.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well. We started from Louisville the 15th, and the rebels burned the bridge down that runs across Red River, just this side of Nashville, two hours before we got there, so we had to turn back to Bowling Green, where we will have to stay a day or two till the road is repaired. Lieutenant Centers overtook us at Louisville with the boys. Tell Al White he is a smart fellow to not come with the rest of the boys. Give my respects to Philena Wilcox and the rest. Direct to the regiment. Bowling Green. 

Dear Parents: 

August 18th, 1862.

It was with pleasure that I received your letter yesterday and was glad to hear that you were all well. We have orders to march tomorrow morning, I suppose for Chattanooga. We had a new recruit yesterday, right from the old country. He came to Cleveland and, hearing a great deal of the 3d 

Ohio, he took a notion to enlist. He was in New York at the time of the great riot there. He used to be a reporter for the London News. I sent my picture the other day. I suppose you have got it before this time if you get it at all. I heard that Sam White had got home and a good many of the 

123d Regiment. A good joke on Tom White, who was taken for a deserter. 

1 guess he won't wear soldier's clothes any more. When you write again I want you to send me the directions so I can write to Mary White. 

Well, no more at present. Give my compliments to Miss P. M. W. 

Dear Parents: 

August 22d, 1862.

This letter is so badly torn as to be illegible, but I think must be near Stevenson, Alabama, as there is a part of a word "Stev--" and the remainder torn. He says the rebels are pretty thick around here now. They are having another fight. The regiment has not been paid off yet since I have been away, and I do not know when they will. Send me some money if you can. No more this time. [12]

Dear Mother: 

Murfreesboro, Tenn., September 1st, 1862.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well. We are now within 

30 miles of Nashville. We have been marching north for the last five days and on our march we lost all of our clothes and company equipage. Our wagons broke down and were left back. We sent after them the next morning with eight men, who got whipped out by the guerillas and captured all our clothes. If you can make me a couple of shirts and send them to me I should like to have you do so. You know how to make them better than I can tell you. Some kind of woolen-anything but red. You can send them by mail just as you do a letter. Some of the other boys got some by mail. 

We are now at Murfreesboro, Tenn. No more. 

Dear Sister: 

Louisville, Sept. 28th, 1862.

It is with pleasure that I can say that we are at Louisville again. We had a pretty long march of it, 35 days on the road with old Bragg ahead of us part of the time, with our supplies cut off, so we had to make the citizens toe up, but we made the rebels get out of Mumfordsville when our army got there, and so we got to Louisville after all. I wrote for Ma to make me a couple of woolen shirts. Tell her that I would rather have a couple of calico shirts. She can send them by mail the same as a letter, by putting on three or four postage stamps. The talk is that we are going to Cincinnati to rest and let the new troops fight awhile. Well, as I have such a poor chance to write, I guess I will quit. 

Dear Parents: 

Louisville, Ky., Oct. 13th, 1862.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well. I am at Louisville acting as patrol guard of the city. The regiment is now at Perrysville, about 

80 miles from here. The dead and wounded of recent battles are coming today-about 5,000 of them-—and a great many of them who were residents of this city. I may stay here in the city this winter as a patrol guard. 

We have received no pay yet and there is no knowing when we will get any. It is rather hard to be strapped here in the city, but that cannot be helped. I suppose there are a good many of the 3d Ohio here. Our horses died on the road from Nashville here, so we have no horses to ride and have to stay here. Give my respects to all inquiring friends. 

Write Co. A. Barracks, Louisville, Ky. 

Dear Parents: 

Louisville, Ky., Oct. 24th, 1862.

I am well at present and hope you are the same. I am not now at the barracks. I am at camp near Louisville. I suppose you have heard ere this of the fight our boys had with Morgan and how it terminated. I suppose the most of Company I are now at Columbus, Ohio. You wrote that Fran and Dick were dead, but that was a false report. Dick says the rebels never made the bullet that can kill him. Henry Sweetland was also taken prisoner but made his escape with another of our Company. It is expected Morgan will be in here to take Louisville. They are moving all the canvas or stores across the river, but let him come-he will meet a sweet reception. Some of our new recruits did not soldier long. Morgan took it into his head to release [14]

them. You said you sent me a couple of shirts which I have never received as yet. Direct your letters the same as ever to the regiment, Louisville, Ky. 

Dear Sister: 

Nashville, Tenn., December 4th, 1862. 

I received your letter last night just after my return from a scout and having a little fun with the guerillas in this vicinity. The devilish cowards 

I will slip up on our forage trains and fire upon them. We have the fun of chasing them off. Some of the boys have gone out today to give them a little fight if they are so disposed to accept one. We are not paid off yet, but expect it every day.

The talk every day is that we will get pay tomorrow, but I have never seen it yet.

You said the boys were all at home. We heard that they were all exchanged and were on their way back here again. Give my respects to Grandmother White and all inquiring friends.

I expect you will think this a long letter, but it is so "thunderin'" cold outdoors to write, I'll stop. 

Nashville, Tenn., Dec. 5, 1862. 

Dear Mother: 

I received your letter this evening and was glad to hear from home.

You are complaining about my not writing oftener than I do, but I think I write pretty often. It must be that you do not get all my letters. You wanted to know if I would not rather be a prisoner than where I am. I do not think I would, as those that are prisoners need not have been prisoners if they had accepted the offer that John Morgan offered them. He told them if they wanted to fight very bad he would pass them back to their Regiment, to which some of them came back. No doubt but what they did fight as hard as any 200 men could fight while they were fighting. I don't think I would rather be a prisoner than where I am. So far I have never received the shirts. I would like to know what Mary it is that wants me to send my likeness, but I have not had any chance to get it yet. I expect we will get some pay tomorrow, then I will write again. 

Dear Parents: 

Nashville, Tenn., Dec. 13th, 1862.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well, hoping that you are all well at home. We had a great fight at Hartsville, a few miles from here, that is, some of our sixty-dollar men let John Morgan take them. Only think of it, 5,000 men and one battery of artillery to let that rebel dog take them with only 200 men that our regiment would not run from. Well, that is all we can expect from hurried soldiers. They were brought to this city yesterday and received the sentence to wear a white rag to Columbus and march through 

Indiana and be discharged in disgrace as cowards. You wrote to me to send my likeness, but there is no chance to get it taken here. There is nothing here hardly, and what there is you cannot get for love nor money. What you do get you have to pay six prices for. Butter is 75 cents per pound here. 

Eggs $1 per dozen, and everything sold at similar prices. I wish you would send me some postage stamps, for it is hard work to get them here. Well, it is getting so dark I cannot see to write. No more. [15]

Nashville, Tenn., Dec. 29. 

Dear Mother:

It is with pleasure that I have this opportunity to write to you, even though I cannot say I am as well as usual. I have not been well for a day or two as I should like to be. F. Van Horn has also been quite sick, but is getting better. I went to the theater last night and met with quite a misfortune. I either lost my pocket book or somebody took it. At least it is gone and all the money I had with it, so I am strapped again. I sent $20 home which I suppose you have got before this time. It is so disagreeable to be strapped in town. I wish you would send $10 of it to me by mail. It will get here. I guess the most of the army has moved toward the enemy, and even now I can hear the distant roar of battle. The report here is that the rebels are retreating to their entrenchments to give us battle there. 

Well, no more at present. 

Dear Mother: 

Nashville, Tenn., Jan. the 11th, 1863.

It is with pleasure I can say I am well enough to write today. I have been quite sick for a few days, but I feel better today. I am now at Barracks No. 1, Nashville. I wrote to you that I had lost my pocket book and all the money I had was in it, and wanted you to send me some, but you need not send it until you hear from me again. I think perhaps I will get a chance to get home for a short time when the railroads get in running order again, although you must not be disappointed if I do not. I suppose you have heard of the great battle near Nashville (Stone River). The 3d Ohio has got her name up this time. They were in the fight three days and lost but a few men. Co I did not lose a man. 

Well, no more at present. 

Dear Mother: 

Louisville, Ky., Jan. the 15th, 1863.

I am happy to say that I have got back to Louisville again. I have been quite sick, but am much better again. I started from Nashville last Sunday and reached here today. I suppose you have received that money I sent home by Lyman Benham. I wish you would send me back the $20 so I can come home for a while. The doctor says I can go home if I pay my way home myself. I am now at Barracks No. 1, Louisville, Ky. Please send it as soon as you get this letter. (He came home, and when he came, took us by surprise. My father happened to be in town when the train came in and a cousin brought him home. 

He was very thin and had grown tall. I was so shocked when I saw him I ran and hid. He inquired and soon found me.-E. N. M.) 

Dear Parents: 

Louisville, Ky., Barracks No. 1, Feb. the 16th, 1863. 

I am once again at Louisville, having arrived last Saturday night. I stopped at Galion a couple of nights. I am getting some photographs taken there and Lan is going to send them to me. Perhaps he will send one to you instead of sending it here. I saw Ira Crow when I got off the train. He says he is going home the first of next month. There is not much chance for a discharge, but I am going to try for one I guess. 

Give my respects to all inquiring friends. [16]

Dear Parents: 

Louisville, Ky., February the 24th, 1863.

I am still at Louisville and having a good time. I am well at present, but I suppose I will not stay here long as I think of going to the regiment soon, perhaps this week, but may not before next week. I got a letter from 

Philander Bartow last week. He said he sent you a photograph of mine. I sent one to Mary the other day, supposing you had got one. I wish I had stayed a day or two longer so as to have seen Uncle John White. I am glad to hear he has got home. As for me there is no chance for they have stopped discharging here for the present. I guess I will go to the regiment as they need men there for the next battle, so as not to get defeated in this one. The rumor here today is that Vicksburg is ours, that the rebels had evacuated the place, but I guess it is not so; and the report is that Rosencrans is advancing on the enemy. What are the prospects at home of this war ending-discouraging as ever? I suppose so. You will say well, perhaps it is, but let 'em slide. So no more this time-only tell Tom White that 

I won't go to California this spring. You ask how I am feeling and if the old trouble of bowel complaint is bothering me. At times I am feeling somewhat weak, the rations received are not conducive to health in that particular. However, I hope I may escape from its ravages in the future. It is a weakening process and the proper remedies are not forthcoming as they are at home.

My respects to all inquiring friends. 

Direct to 3d Ohio Cavalry, Co I, Barracks No. 1, Louisville, Ky. 

Dear Parents: 

Murfreesboro, Tenn., March the 8th, 1863.

I have again joined the regiment and am feeling quite well at present. 

I came through with the Greenfield boys and arrived here today. The boys from Centerville went first and had gone to Nashville after horses which were given us here. The regiment has gone out on a scout and will probably have a fight before they come in to camp. There was heavy cannonading in the direction they went. You say you have not received that photograph of mine. I should think Lan was smart for not sending it. He wrote to me that he had sent one to you, as he thought you would be more apt to get it, and I am sorry I have not got one more of them so as to send you one, but perhaps you will get it yet. I wish you would send me some postage stamps, for there's none we can get here. Well, no more at present. 

Camp Stanley, Murfreesboro, Tenn., March the 15th, 1863. 

Dear Parents:

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well, and I hope these few lines will find you the same. We have just received our arms and horses, that is the paroled boys. So they are ready for Morgan and his band again. 

We have had very pleasant weather for the last few days, warmer I suppose than you will have for a month or two. Captain Colver, of Company R, has gone to Columbus, Ohio, to see the governor for the purpose of getting this regiment to Ohio to recruit, and I hope he will be succesful for I think they need it, for out of the eleven hundred and fifty, there is not over three hundred left.

We are now encamped but a few miles from the rebel army, and on the very outpost of our picket lines, so when the enemy makes an attack we [17]

will be the first to have a fight, but I think there is no danger of an attack. 

We will have to be the attacking party or there will be no fight yet for a while. Well no more at present only I wish you would send me some postage stamps for I cannot get any here for there are none, only what the boys get from home. Well, no more. 

Murfreesboro, Tenn. 

Dear Sister: 

Camp Stanley, Murfreesboro, Tenn., March the 20th, 1863.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well. We are still lying in camp near Murfreesboro, but expect to soon move. It is thought that the army will soon move on the enemy at Shelbyville. General Rosencrans had a grand review of our cavalry yesterday, so I suppose he intends doing something. I suppose you have heard that Delos Ashley was wounded. He was shot very bad and it is thought he will not recover. The first report seems to be confirmed that the enemy has evacuated Vicksburg and are coming here to attack Rosencrans, but I suppose if it is so you will hear of it as quick as we will. The army seems to be in fine spirits and anticipate fine sport when they come, so let them come. We have had very fine weather for the last week, but today it is very windy and I guess we will have some rain. 

Well, no more at present. 

Dear Mother: 

Camp Stanley, Murfreesboro, Tenn.

It was with pleasure that I received your letter, but learned with regret that such stories about me were afloat at home, but as I have heard nothing about it, I think there must be some mistake somewhere, and as my not drawing any pay-it is a lie, and I thank the gentleman very much. for his kind feelings towards me for writing such as you say. I know who wrote such a letter. I must say I think I do but he positively denies it. I think he wrote it, but it is well he does deny it for the man that slanders me must be my enemy or retract. Well, no more of this nonsense this time. 

Mr. Cal Ashley arrived here yesterday. Delos Ashley is yet alive but is not expected to live. We had a good time on picket last night, for it rained all the time. Well, there is nothing new to write this time, so no more. Dear Parents: 

Murfreesboro, Tenn., April the 27th, 1863.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well. We just came in from a long scout yesterday evening. An expedition of 15,000 cavalry was sent out for the purpose of getting in the rear of the rebel army and burn the railroads, in which we were quite successful. The 3d Ohio Cavalry burned. several bridges and two trains of cars and a large amount of cotton and part of the town of McMinnville. In all we burned some $300,000 worth of property for the rebels on this expedition. We also took some 200 prisoners from Morgan. Since I have been back this time I have been on scouting expeditions 20 days, so you see I am not quite the play-off that Mr. Jealous

Pate pleases to term me in his very interesting letters. Well, we have very good times here, the weather is fine, but very warm, and I think the word warm would be better expressed by calling it hot. We were paid off last week, two months' pay, but I do not like to trust it by mail, for the guerillas are capturing some of our trains every day or two. When I get a chance I will send some home, if there should happen to be somebody going home. 

Tell Pa to pay Tom that five dollars and I will pay him as soon as I get a chance to send it. Well, no more at present. 

Dear Sister: 

Murfreesboro, Tenn., May the 9th, 1863.

It is with pleasure that I can say I am well and I hope these few lines will find you the same.

We have splendid weather here, only it is very hot. There is some excitement here now about the battle in Virginia. There has been no news yet today. Our company was on picket yesterday. Two rebel deserters came in and gave themselves up. They say if their soldiers were permitted to vote their army would be two-thirds for the union. How is it around 

Centerville for northern copperheads? Are there any? I hope not. It would not be well for them to have the 3d Ohio sent back to take care of them. I have some respect for the rebs in arms, it shows them not to be cowards. Well, the soldiers will settle with them when they get home. They had better be emigrating South if they wish to get off without any broken bones. You would think so if you had been here to have heard some of the expressions of the copperhead mob of Dayton.

Well, I can think of nothing new to write, so no more at present. 

Dear Sister: 

Murfreesboro, Tenn., June the 2nd, 1863.

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well. We have moved camp. since last I wrote to you and our regiment is excused from picket duty for a while and put on provost duty. We have very pleasant weather, and cherries, mulberries, strawberries, etc., are ripe, and plenty of them. We went out the other day and had a little fight with the 8th Regulars, Rebel 

Cavalry. We lost two men killed and several wounded. We took 75 prisoners, how many killed we did not learn, as we had to retreat for we surprised them in the morning before they were up, but we had to retreat or we would have had their whole army upon us and, of course, we did not go intending [ 19 ]

to fight them, merely to give them a little surprise and retreat and, of course, the "boys" like no better fun than to give the enemy a surprise, take a few prisoners, have a little run, etc.

Well, give my respects to all inquiring friends. So no more at present. 

Murfreesboro, Tenn., June the 23d.

Dear Parents: 

It is with pleasure that I can say that I am well and I hope these few lines will find you the same.

Sergeant Spaulding will be the bearer of this. He will be at Centerville recruiting. Pa, I wish you would get me a pair of light Cavalry boots made and send back by him. I would send you the money for them, but I have loaned the most of it to the captain, as I had no chance to send it home until now. If you will get them I should like to have them. Number six will fit me by having them made very high in the instep. I would like to have calf boots made fine foot fashion. I would not ask for them if I could get boots here to fit me. Sergeant Spaulding will stay about a month for the purpose of recruiting for Company I, so as to give the boys a chance to enlist in the Cavalry, and I think they will never be sorry for it, as I think it is by far the best branch of the service. We have very fine weather here now with the exception of it being a little too warm for comfort. Well, no more at present. 

Dear Parents: 

Huntsville, Alabama, July the 19th, 1863.

It is with pleasure that I have the opportunity to write, it being the first opportunity since we left Murfreesboro. We have been marching nearly four weeks now, skirmishing and harassing the enemy's rear, but the last of them have crossed the Tennessee river, so we will perhaps keep still for a few days at Huntsville, Alabama. We have for fifteen days subsisted entirely on the surrounding country that we have marched through. 

Our wagon trains were not able to keep up with us in our forced marches. 

We received the news here yesterday that Port Hudson was taken, which I suppose is so. It was announced to us by the firing of artillery. Oh, yes, we have plenty of ripe fruit here now. Peaches and apples are ripe. The boys are all well and with anticipations of soon seeing the rebellion crushed, if the rebs all ran as old Bragg does. Our regiment had a fight the other day with a whole brigade of rebs, which constitutes four regiments, and scattered them. We only lost one man killed and seven wounded. The rebs lost 

17 men killed, whom we buried, one of them being a colonel. As for the wounded we did not ascertain as they took them away with them. 

Well, no more. Give my respects to all inquiring friends. 

Direct to Murfreesboro. 

Dear Parents: 

Winchester, Tenn., August the 4th, 1863.

It was with pleasure that I received a letter from you yesterday, and was glad to hear that you were all well. You say that you heard that we were in Georgia. That is a mistake. We have not been there yet. We have been to Huntsville, Alabama, only. Rebs are played out here now and 

I guess they will be everywhere else before a great while. Ohio did very well, I think, in the capturing of Morgan and his guerilla band. The only pity is that they took him alive. It was a good thing that the 3d Ohio did [ 20 ]

not have the taking of him. If they had, there would be no John Morgan now. We have not seen a reb in three weeks and the boys say they are spoiling for a fight. Deserters say that Bragg's army is perfectly demoralized, and are scattering all over the country. They say they think there is no hope for the Confederacy and are glad to return again to the old Union. 

Well, no more this time. Please excuse pencil writing, as we have but poor conveniences for writing.

Dear Parents: 

Camp near Stevenson, Alabama, August the 31st, 1863.

Again I have the opportunity to write you a few lines to let you know that I am well and hope this will find you the same. We are slowly closing around Chattanooga. Well, I must tell you about the adventure we had the other night, the 28th of August. About 50 of the 3d Ohio were detailed to ford the Tennessee river and capture a rebel picket post. I was one of the detail, also Charles Sanders and W. Van Horn. The Tennessee river is about a half a mile wide where we crossed. Well, we got safe across but were not so lucky in getting the pickets. We got only one of them, so you see we had a pretty cold bath of it, for we were about an hour crossing. The rebs could have shot the last one of us, but it was lucky for us they were asleep and did not discover us until we were within fifteen rods of them, and then they ran, throwing their arms away as they fled. So it turned out well for us having only one man wounded. The report was today that the enemy had received 10,000 reenforcements and today it is thought Rosencrans is trying to draw a siege with the rebs here. He is so slow in advancing on them 

I think he is forming a junction with Burnsides and trying to surround them.

Well, no more this time. My respects to all.

Dear and Respected Parents: 

Woodville, Alabama, Oct. the 30th, 1863.

It is with pleasure that I again have the opportunity of writing and let you know that I am yet alive and well, and I hope these few lines will find you the same. We have had quite a tour since last I wrote to you. I suppose you have heard of the great rebel raid through Tennessee. Well, our regiment was one of the many chosen to drive them out of the state. We chased them some two hundred and fifty miles during which time we had four fights with them and I was engaged in all of them and went through with only two horses shot. We lost two men in our company, and it is not known what became of them, killed or wounded.

Our regiment made one charge and it was pronounced one of the best ever made in this department. We took more prisoners than there were in our regiment and but very few but had a saber wound and we know not how many we killed of them. That accounts for them wearing our uniforms of which they captured a very large supply. There were a goodly number missing out of our regiment, the exact number I have not ascertained. Oh, yes, tell Emma I must have her photograph in exchange for the picture I sent her. We are now lying at Woodville, where we lay so long last summer. Oh, yes, I liked to forgot there was great rejoicing here at the announcement of the election of John Brough by 60,000 majority. The home [ 23 ]

vote was indeed good news. Well done for Ohio home vote—a great deal better than we expected.

Well, no more. Give my respects to all inquiring friends and also tell the populace at Centerton that their friends of the 3d Ohio are well and my compliments and best wishes to the fair sex of Centerton. 

Dear Parents: 

Woodville, Ala., Nov. the 8th, 1863.

Your letter of the 7th of October has just reached me-the first letter for some time. I had almost begun to think you had quit writing. Well, I have not much to write, as it has been only a day or two since I last wrote. 

I suppose you are all well satisfied with your governor-elect. You say the copperheads all show fight. Well, we will be home next summer if we live and the boys say they will re-take the place back again. The boys think they will not have the two you mention to fight. They will have some boys who have studied fighting for the last three years. We will teach them how to draw a saber and charge on the enemy-the northern copperheads. We are having very pleasant weather here at present and have very good times, nothing to do but hunt "bushwhackers" and guerillas. And the few we catch we have the consolation of shooting, for it is the orders to take none prisoners. The general says he don't want to be bothered with them. 

Well, no more at present.

T. T. and his father better keep mum, for we honor the bushwhackers more than northern enemies.

November 8th is the last letter I have. It was written just six days before he was captured by the bushwhackers. He may have written later and the letters lost. We wrote so frequently, as the letters indicate, and the lapses are perhaps letters lost, as his last letter reads that he had written only a day or two before. 

My mother was very ill when on November 23d a messenger came to inform us that my brother was a prisoner in the hands of those guerillas. 

The messenger came from the funeral of Comrade James Mann, whose body was sent home to Steuben, four miles from our home. Van Wert, Ohio, December 22, 1882. 

Emmogene N. Marshall, Greenwich, Huron County, Ohio: 

Madam: I received your letter of the 13th inst., and, of course, feel that I am anxious to do what I can for an old comrade's friends. My memory, however, has not retained distinct impressions of the events of the war. [ 26 ]

The reason principally is that I then was very young, only fifteen when I went in, and nineteen when I came out, and the occurrences since the war in my history, being much more important to me, much of what transpired has been effaced. I will therefore hold myself to no responsibility on account of the statement of facts which I now give to you. At a point on the Memphis & Charleston railrcad, not far from Woodville, Ala., in that dreary month of November, 1863, the 3d regiment of Ohio volunteer cavalry was stationed. The woods in the immediate vicinity were infested with those most abominable creatures, called guerrillas. They were lying in wait alongside the roads, to attack the Union soldiers wherever they could find a 

GAYLORD M. SALTZGABER Cɔ. I, 3rd O. V. V. C. 

Van Wert, Ohio force much inferior to their own, and then never giving honorable open warfare. These miserable creatures were too cowardly to join the army in defense of a cause they pretended to espouse; but, like human hyenas, hung upon the outskirts of the Union forces whenever trees, and crags and rocks furnished them shelter, and from thence murdered those whom they dared not face. On the 14th of the month, Company I, of the 3d, of which Ed 

Niver and myself were members, were out doing picket duty, perhaps a mile from the camp. The Company was in reserve upon the road, and then from the Company was sent out two men as videttes or advance pickets. I forget now who those two men were but, anyhow, being short of fresh meat, it was [ 27 ]

concluded that several of us would go foraging after some. So Ed Niver. 

James Mann, J. Wilcox and myself started. Carefully watching the undergrowth of timber upon either side of the road, to see that it contained no hidden foe, we passed through a kind of defile in the mountain, and down into a beautiful valley upon the other side. In about the first field we came to, were some sheep grazing. A little farther on were some hogs. After securing a hog, we turned back, and it was but the work of a few moments for us to kill one of the sheep and fasten its hind legs to our carbine slings, and start upon our return. Cheerfully then we retraced our steps, thinking of the pleasure we would bring to our comrades in camp, with our plentiful supply of pork and mutton. We were upon our horses, and had the hog fastened to two, and the sheep separate. We thus went nearly to the vidette post, when suddenly there flashed from the brush and precipitous hillside upon our right the furious glare and roar of a dozen gans, and they were upon us. Niver was upon the side of the hidden foe, and his horse and probably himself was pierced by the leaden missiles hurled by those fiends. Schooled to such emergencies, it was but the work of a moment for me to throw off the carbine sling that held the dead sheep, and my horse started and alarmed at the unexpected assault fairly flew, as if shot from a catapult, and went perhaps one hundred yards before I could check it. Then 

I turned and rapidly fired two or three times at the spot from whence came that murderous volley and eleven of the villains were visible. They had been too cowardly at first to show themselves even to fight a single foe, for J. W.'s horse had been hit and he had escaped, and Mann was down, and 

Niver was surrounded; the rail that held the hog being fast, he could not get away. Oh! the cowardly hidden villains. Knowing the folly of going back to make myself a target for those devils, I again turned and galloped back to the post, and roused our friends as soon as possible. They were in the saddle and hurried back, but when we reached the place of the shooting, Niver and his miscreant captors were missing. Tracking them as well and as rapidly as we could, we found Mann in a crevice of the rocks, wounded to death. They made him go a little ways, when he begged of them to kill him. One of the bushwhackers stepped forward to do so, when the leader of the gang, interposed, saying "he would die, anyway, and they must make tracks.' Mann was taken to camp and lived several days. We followed 

Niver and his captors to the Tennessee river, where we could see them just crossing upon the other side, with poor Niver in the boat. We had no boat to follow, and if we had, we argued it would do no good, because the shades of night were falling, and on the other side of the river were the regular forces of the rebel army, who would in all probability have captured us, had we crossed. It was a terrible sight to see our unfortunate comrade thus torn from us, and feel that we were unable to prevent it. It seemed so much worse for him to be taken that way, than to be shot dead in our midst in the fiercest battle; such deaths we were used to, and there was something noble and heroic in one dying upon a battle field, fighting openly for one's country, but we had read much of the horrid cruelties practiced upon our captured soldiers, and we could only conjecture and shudder at Niver's fate, wretched, weak and perhaps wounded. He was left without friends, and without hope. Our records show that Edwin Niver died June 19, 1864, at 

Andersonville prison-pen. How and when we heard it, I don't know, but I remember we talked about Eddy's mother, and his bereaved sister, and we instinctively felt how their hearts would ache, oh, so bitterly, and the full [ 28 ]

measure of our sympathies went out to them. Ed was a good boy, and we all loved him. I believed we learned he had been an only boy at home, but he had not been spoiled; he always showed those companionable qualities which endeared him to his soldier comrades. 

This, madam, furnishes, I believe, all the knowledge I can communicate. to you in answer to your inquiries. Very respectfully.

G. M. SALTZGABER. 

EDWIN W. NIVER

Mrs. E. N. Marshall, Greenwich, Ohio: 

Havana, Ohio, January 1, 1883. 

Dear Madam: Yours of December 13th, received in due time, but have neglected to reply until this time. As to the date of Ed's capture, I could not give it, but the place I shall never forget, as I was not with the party when he was taken. James Mann and Edd were back of the balance of us boys, perhaps forty rods. We heard two shots fired, we turned our horses and went in pursuit of them. We found Jimmy Mann lying down behind a big rock badly wounded, but Edd was gone; poor boy. We took Jimmy back to camp, which was about one-half mile away, and then went in pursuit of Edd. They had at that time one hour the start of us. We came to the river bank but Edd was just landing on the opposite side. Night had come on and [ 29 ]

we returned back to camp. Jimmy died two days afterward. We sent his remains back to Steuben for burial. The place where this happened, was at a station on the Memphis & Charleston railroad; he was captured about one mile south of the station. It is a very barren country at the foot of the mountain. Our camp was between the mountain and the railroad, not very iar from an old mill, or in a turn in the road, in the woods. Dear Madam: I received your note of inquiry yesterday. I hasten to answer. I can probably give you more accurate information in regard to the capture of your brother than any one else, as I am about the only survivor of the party. The date I do not remember, but the affair is as vivid in my mind as if it had happened but yesterday. We were encamped near 

Woodville, Alabama, our company was on picket duty that day. G. M., Ed. 

James Mann and myself (without orders) went foraging southeast from Woodville, about one and a half miles from our reserve picket post. We came to a field, I should think containing about six acres, in which were some half dozen hogs; we fired several shots, and finally killed one, took it and started for camp; had not gone far when we saw on the side of the mountain to our right and about sixty rods off a flock of sheep. I fired at them with my carbine and brought one of them down; went and got it; put it on Mann's horse in front of him. Edd and I put a gambrel through the legs of the hog, hung it on a piece of fence rail and a tened it in front of our saddles, with our over-coat straps. Started for camp, little thinking of the dreadful catastrophe in store for us. The road we had to travel was just wide enough for two horses abreast, and on either side in places the small cedars were so thick we could not see into them ten feet and over them a high hill or a small mountain. When we arrived at the top of the hill Edd and I were about five rods in front of Mann. Ten guerillas came out of the brush in front of us, and one in the rear, between Mann, Saltzgaber and us. 

They caught Edd's horse by the bridle and tried to catch mine, but I put spurs to him, and he dashed through them. Then they all fired at me, missing me. My horse fell about thirty feet from them, threw me off, and immediately got up again, I jumped on his back and put spurs to him, but it was no go, he fell, shot through the hips. I unsnapped my carbine from my saddle, fired a parting salute at them, took to the bushes and escaped. The man between us and Mann, shot and wounded him, from which wounds he died. I hurried to camp, and we started in pursuit as soon as possible. One of the rebs got on Edd's horse, with Edd behind him, and two others got on Mann's horse, and went to Gunter's Landing, on the Tennessee river. The rest of them took to the woods. Our men got to the Landing shortly after [ 30 ]

they had crossed with Edd, but too late to rescue him. There was not a word spoken between us, and it was all the work of an instant. Excuse this pencil. Wife sends respects. Anything you think of I have forgotten to mention, write me, and I will answer. Truly yours,

J. M. WILCOX.

Andersonville Prison and Its Terrors

As Seen By Corporal H. J. Peters, Who Was Incar cerated in That Famous Bastile as a Prisoner of War

The conditions existing at Andersonville prison during the Civil War are vividly portrayed by H. J. Peters, who was a corporal of Company E, 126th O. V. I., at the time of his imprisonment in that dreaded bastile.

His letter was written many years afterward, but evidently his memory had not failed in recalling some of the terrible sufferings endured by soldiers of the Union Army who were imprisoned at Andersonville. His very interesting letter was written to Emogene Marshall, who had requested information concerning the capture and death of her brother, Edwin Niver.

The letter follows: 

Dear Mrs. Emogene Niver Marshall: 

I hope you will excuse pencil and poor scribbling of the pen and the untasty manner in which I give you part of my military experience in that statricidal war, not being very well and almost 83 years old, and President of the 126th O. V. I., having to arrange for our Reunion on August 20 and 21,

I am pretty busy just now. Best regards and well wishes to you; would be pleased to meet you at Grand Rapids.

So many Union prisoners had accumulated at Richmond in Libby, Smith's and Pemberton Landings, Belle Island and Castle Thunder, near their military operations and far from their source of supplies, they concluded to move them to more remote parts. Howell Cobb, who was U. S. Treasurer under President James Buchanan, suggested Andersonville, Ga., located by General Winder, Nov. 27, 1863, sixty miles south of Macon and 1,600 feet east of the Macon & Albany Railroad, in Macon county-not in Sumpter county, as many think, for the county lines cross the space between the Stockade and the village, which is in Sumpter county. 

The prison was erected in the form of a parallelogram, the longest diameter from north to south, 750 feet wide and 1,010 feet long, comprising about 15 to 22 acres, made from pine logs hewed upon two sides, planted close together five feet in the ground and twenty feet high. Work was done by slaves drafted from farmers nearby. Another similar stockade was erected 12 feet high, with 100 feet of space between the two, this being for offense and defense. If the one stockade was forced by a revolt of the prisoners, the second formed another defense, and should any force operate from the outside, the outer stockade would be a protection for the Confederate troops. 

Each angle was earthworks, mounted with 17 canon, charged with grapeshot and canister, ready for instant service in case of mutiny among the prisoners. Upon the inner stockade was 60 sentry boxes for the guards, who were armed with loaded muskets and had full view of the area within. 

The prison was entered by two massive north and south gates. Railing about three feet high and 17 feet from the stockade constituted the "dead line," which was fatal to touch or pass. No shelter but the canopy of heaven [49]

except such as the prisoners could contrive putting up-storm blanket, shirt or coat, digging holes in the ground, putting up huts with sun-dried bricks.

All timber was cut down when a disinterested spectator, Mr. Ambrose 

Spencer, of Americus, Ga., said to Winder the trees would give grateful shelter to the prisoners. Winder said that was just what he didn't want. “I want a pen here where the Yanks will rot as fast as they can be sent." 

The ground declined from the north and south towards the center to a swamp of a slimy black muck, alive with magots. A stream about three feet wide and four or five inches deep, like a venomous serpent, crept through the swamp, called Double Branch. Its water was at no time fit to drink. 

From this life-destroying stream 35,000 heroes were compelled to quench their thirst. The filth from the Confederate camp's sinks, stables, cemetery, and from the village located on the hillsides above the stockade were washed into the stream by the rains, whence it was carried through the stockade. 

Prisoners invariably were brought in through the south gate. Rations, hauled in the same wagon that hauled the dead to the cemetery, were brought in through the north gate. When Ambrose Spencer, a Southerner and Christian, suggested to General Winder that a little consideration should be given by the attendants of the pen by which the prisoners could be made more comfortable, Winder replied that he would rather blow the 35,000 

Yankee scoundrels to hell than go to heaven himself.

In June they added about five acres to the north of the grounds. In 1864 there rolled into this slaughtering pen captives plucked from the picket post, skirmish line and battle front, until their numbers aggregated 35,000. 

The loyalty of these captives was not only tried by the march and bivouac, shot and shell, but by hunger, disease, nakedness and death, with no mother to whisper words of comfort or wipe away the clammy perspiration of death. Never before were prisoners starved, hunted and mangled by bloodhounds, tortured in stock, pillary, tied up by the thumbs for attempting to make their escape, and at the mercy of a fiendish guard perched upon massive pallisades with loaded muskets, ever ready to shoot to death unarmed, starving captives who were plucked from the battle front, now doomed to inhale corrosive atmosphere, drenched with cadaverous dews, hungry, emaciated, battling with vermin, racked with pain, shelterless and naked, the fierce rays of a burning sun, storms and lightning peeling into the unscreened faces of the living and the dying. 

In the months of July and August there were probably 12,000 seriously sick, and a large number of these would gather at the south gate upon the signal of the beating of a drum, calling sick roll, where so-called doctors would prescribe sumac berries, sweet gum bark, corn meal sourings, as the war mimics of drugs. Some of the sick were carried in old coats or blankets on sticks, arms or backs of comrades; others would creep, crawl or slide, with shrieks, groans and frequent halts, through the hot sands. The miseries and infirmities witnessed during these sick calls beggars description. Every form of suffering that is possible for disease to induce was visible. Dysentery, diarrhoea, smallpox, scurvy, gangrene, swollen and distorted limbs, blistered backs with fly-blown sores, recently united fractures came apart, old sores opened, faces puffed, gums swelled, teeth dropped out, small abrasions caused arms and legs to rot off, nostrils, mouths, bronchial tubes poured out streams of offensive blood and corruption, blindness, failing of mental powers and insanity. HOW ANDERSONVILLE PRISON PARK WAS ACQUIRED AS A NATIONAL LANDMARK 

The following communication from an official of O. M. Mitchell Post, No. 1, of Georgia, to officers and ladies of the Women's Relief Corps, explains how the famous prison property was secured by the W. R. C.: 

To Mrs. Belle C. Harris, President, Mrs. Sarah D. Winans, Chairman, and

Ladies of the W. R. C.: 

I have been asked in few words to tell you of the part the Department of Georgia had in purchasing and preserving Andersonville Prison Pen property, seventeen or eighteen acres, which at one time was occupied by 

33,000 prisoners.

It is needless to say why we felt it should never pass out of the hands of the friends of the thousands of those who suffered and died there. 

September 24th, 1884, seventeen ex-Union soldiers met in the city of 

Atlanta, accepted a charter, and were enrolled as O. M. Mitchell Post, No. 1, of Georgia. The Department of Georgia was organized January 25th, 1889. 

Early in 1884 we set in motion to see if the "Pen" and adjacent ground could be purchased. Proper action was taken, a payment made, subject to investigation of title in May, 1890. It was found that in order to hold legal title, the Department, under the law, would have to become a corporation. 

Charter was drawn up by our attorney, applied for and granted by the Superior Court of Chatham County at the December term, 1890. Question arose with owner as to a roadway to the railroad station. Finally this was settled. Warranty deed was executed April 7th, 1894.

In the meantime work of clearing up the ground had been done, taking off underbrush, taking away stumps, and making the ground more presentable.

At the time of the purchase the line of the stockade was clearly defined by the remains of the enclosure, which had been built of hewn timbers, deeply set in the ground, and projecting twelve or fourteen feet above. This had been fired, and the upper portion burned, the lower portion remaining. 

Soon relic hunters began digging them up, and in about two years it had been all carried away.

In justice to the memory of my dearly loved comrade, the late James 

P. Averill, who for years was, so to speak, the right hand of our "Lizbeth" in the Andersonville work, I quote from a history of Andersonville, written by him: "Every patriotic citizen of our reunited country is justly proud of the valor and chivalry of the American soldier as developed by the late Civil War. That this sentiment is universal, and growing stronger, is but the natural outgrowth of the sterling character of the true American. "This just pride is shared alike by the Northern and Southern soldier in every act of heroism which so brilliantly illuminates the annals of the war. A generous enemy is every ready to enthusiastically applaud deeds of self-sacrifice and patriotic devotion in a cause to which one has dedicated his life, his fortune and his sacred honor. "The part which the prisoners at Andersonville had in demonstrating the superior patriotism of the American soldier is unrivaled. as [59]

There is certainly nothing in the world's history that surpasses it. In the midst of suffering indescribable they refused the comfort and safety temptingly proffered them by the enemy and remained faithful unto death to their colors. "The story of Andersonville has become a familiar one in every household. Time, the great healer, is rapidly obliterating the harshness and bitterness connected with it. The heroes of Andersonville are all either resting beneath the sod or rapidly passing away. There is now left naught but the memories (softened and hallowed) and the great lessons of patriotism to future generations. The sacred spot where these heroes suffered and died will continue to be the Mecca towards which the steps of all Americans may turn for patriotic in spiration. 

"Probably the most interesting feature of this historic locality is what is known as 'Providence Spring,' thus appropriately named by the heroes of Andersonville. The story is universally familiar and will continue to live in history as one of the most intensely interesting episodes of the Civil War. When the famishing soldiers had reached that moment when they could not much longer endure, with naught but the contaminated water of the creek to sustain them, during a severe electric storm, this living stream broke forth, bright, pure and sparkling, bringing renewed life and hope. Its opportune appearance may be easily explained from natural causes, yet it will always be recognized as an especial evidence of the infinite power and mercy of Divine Providence. Not only was it providential that this supply of pure water should come at this time, but also that it came from within the "dead line," where, by the prison laws, it was protected from being trampled and defiled. "Over this spring the National W. R. C. has erected a beautiful pavilion, tile-roofed and supported by granite pillars, with cement floor, thus perpetually preserving it from the elements. "The sacred waters now flow from a tastily carved fountain of marble, at the spot where they originally burst forth, the gift of the National Prisoners of War Association. The stream is still flowing, as in days of yore, and will continue to flow on forever, ready to refresh all who come, with its cool, sweet waters. The hallowed memories which cluster about it shall endure until the lapse of time. Let him who drinks from this sacred fountain bow his head in reverence."

The creek, which in those days ran dark and muddy through the stockade, now sparkles and dances in the sun bright, clear, pure and undefiled, eloquently inviting us to banish all unpleasant memories of the past, and accept the joy and peace which the heroes of Andersonville did so much to bequeath to us.

Before I close I must ask your President's attention to the recommendation made in my report at Toledo at the 43rd National Encampment. At the 29th Annual Convention of the W. R. C., August, 1896, deeds to the property from the Department of Georgia were delivered to your body. 

What they have done and the result of their labors will be told you by 

Mrs. Winans. Ladies, I thank you.

W. M. SCOTT.

Personal Memoirs of Joseph T. Marshall 

Joseph T. Marshall enlisted from Erie County, Ohio, on the 5th day of August, 1862, to serve three years, or during the war. He was mustered into the United States service at Monroeville, Ohio, on the 30th day of August, 1862, as a private of Captain Thomas C. Femald's Company B, 101st 

Regiment, Ohio Volunteer Infantry, Colonel Leander Stem commanding.

This regiment was one of a number of patriotic organizations that quickly responded to the call for volunteers in the dark days of 1862, when the cause of the nation was possibly drifting into defeat. It was recruited from Erie, Huron, Seneca, Crawford and Wyandotte Counties, and rendezvoused at Camp Monroeville, where a regimental organization was effected, with Leander Stem as Colonel, on the 30th day of August, 1862.

On the 4th of September, although not fully equipped for field service, the regiment was hurried by rail to Cincinnati, Ohio, and thence across the river to Covington, Kentucky, to assist in repelling a threatened raid by rebel forces under Kirby Smith. It remained in camp near Covington until September 24th, when it was taken by rail to Louisville, Kentucky, and assigned to the 2nd Brigade, 1st Division of Buell's Army, and received its first baptism of fire at Perryville, Kentucky, October 8, 1862, in which it acquitted itself most creditably, receiving the praise of its brigade and division commanders and losing several men.

It followed in pursuit of the rebels to Lancaster, Kentucky, where it had a lively skirmish with their rear guard. It afterward moved to Nashville, Tennessee, and on December 26 marched with the Army of the Cumberland, General W. S. Rosencrans. Later the 101st was assigned to the 

1st Brigade, 1st Division, 4th Corps, Army of the Cumberland, during its service participating in engagements at Nolensville, Stone River or Murfreesboro, Tullahoma Campaign, Liberty Gap, Chicamagua, Rocky Face 

Ridge, Resaca, Kingston, Dallas or New Hope Church, Kenesau Mountain (two engagements), Chattahoochie River, Siege of Atlanta, Jonesboro, Lovejoy Station, Ga., Franklin, Nashville, Tenn., and a number of minor engagements.

The regiment lost two hundred and thirty-six officers and men by death while in the service, and at all times earned high commendation by its soldierly conduct and bearing. 

Joseph T. Marshall was wounded at Stone River, Tenn., on the 31st day of December, 1862, by gunshot wound in the right hip and was confined to a hospital in the M. E. Church at Nashville, Tenn., for about two months. He was then taken to Louisville, at which place he was confined for about two months. He was also captured the same day he was wounded, but was recaptured in a few days. He was at all other times with his command, bearing a loyal part in all its engagements. He achieved a gallant record for brave and meritorious service. He was honorably discharged at 

Louisville, Kentucky, on the 18th day of April, 1853, by reason of wounds received in the battle of Stone River. 

Joseph T. Marshall was born at Sandusky, Ohio, on the 8th day of

April, 1843, and was united in marriage to Emma Niver at Centerton, Ohio, on the 21st day of May, 1871. He was a member of McMeens Post,

No. 19, Department of Ohio, G. A. R., of which he was Junior Vice Com- [61]

mander for three years, Senior Vice Commander for five years, Commander for one year, Quartermaster Sergeant for one year and Adjutant for ten years. He was assessor for thirteen years, and Constable for one year. 

His wife is an active and honored member of the Woman's Relief 

Corps, Good Templars and Christian Temperance Union.

It seems to be a fact that the men of '61 to '65 were loath to exploit their deeds of heroism-those that suffered most. In looking over these memoirs of Joseph T. Marshall, I notice he gives no particulars of his sufferings and endurance on that bloody battlefield of Stone River and what followed later. I never heard him refer to it but twice, and then as briefly and concisely as possible. 

A guest one evening asked Mr. Marshall to tell of his experiences at the battle where he was wounded. He remained silent a moment and then said: "It beggars description. The battle of Stone River was a terrific slaughter. I was wounded perhaps about 4 p. m., and I remained where I fell all night the 31st of December. It was exceedingly cold, and during the night a Confederate came to me and gave me a drink from his canteen, and asked 

Joseph T. Marshall and his wife, Emogene Niver Marshall. if he could do any more for me. I told him a poor fellow near by would never need his blanket again, and asked him to get it and put it under me. The man did as requested, and after suffering agony the feat was accomplished. 

During the next day some time I was removed to a church in Murfreesboro (Methodist, I think) by the Confederates and placed upon the ground at the door outside, where I remained all night listening to the moans of the wounded and dying, and the horrible sound of saws removing legs and arms, and throwing them out of the windows. The next day I was taken into the church, and refused to have my leg amputated-in fact, they wished [62]

to take the limb from the hip socket. I had my way, however, and was not molested. In a few days I was recaptured by the Federals and removed to Nashville, where I was confined in the M. E. Church hospital for two months, and later taken to Louisville, where I also spent two months."

A lady who visited the hospital daily because interested in his behalf, interceded with the war officials securing his discharge from the army on account of disability, and wrote his brother, William, in Sandusky, to come for him. She secured passage by boat from Louisville to Cincinnati, and then he returned to Sandusky accompanied by his brother, William, who answered to the summons.

In September, 1913, fifty years later, Mrs. Marshall visited Nashville and the church (or spot) where Mr. Marshall was in the hospital. The 

M. E. church had burned down wherein he was the second time, but rebuilt on the same spot and same plan of construction, and is known as

I shall claim a residence in Sandusky as long as I live, the law giving that right to an official of an institution.' "In official as well as G. A. R. and W. R. C. circles throughout the state, Mrs. Marshall is regarded as an ideal person for the responsibilities [67]

she is soon to assume. Friends generally predict for her an administration that will attract wide attention by reason of the fact that she has long been engaged in the work that she will be called upon to do at Madison." 

Veterans Bestow Honor 

That Mrs. Emogene Niver Marshall was held in the highest esteem and respect, particularly among the veterans of the Civil War, is evidenced by a clipping from a Sandusky newspaper of 1913, as follows: "As a suitable recognition of the many kind acts and impartial interest taken by Mrs. Emogene Niver Marshall, the veterans have decided to bestow upon her the honorable title of Colonel of the First Regiment of the Hospital Reserve Corps. Colonel Marshall, we salute you."

MRS. ANN C. McMEENS WAS ACTIVE IN G. A .R. AND W. R. C. 

CIRCLES IN SANDUSKY AND THROUGHOUT STATE OF OHIO 

Mrs. Ann C. McMeens was a nurse during the Civil War and was well known as the Grace Darling of the Grand Army of the Republic. It is fitting, therefore, that an account of the death and funeral of Mrs. 

McMeens, as set forth in a Sandusky newspaper of July, 1893, be reproduced herewith. Mrs. Ann C. McMeens died July 5, 1893. The story follows: 

Mrs. Ann C. McMeens passed peacefully away at her home on East 

Washington street Wednesday morning at 10 o'clock, after an illness of about three months. Although a fatal termination of her sickness had been anticipated, death was somewhat sudden and unexpected at the time, and the news of her demise will be learned with profound regret by the people of this city and vicinity, among whom she was widely known and most highly esteemed. 

Deceased was born in Fredericktown, Maryland, September 3, 1820.

She was the daughter of John H. Pittenger. During her early life she became a resident of Tiffin, Ohio, and was there married to Dr. R. R. McMeens.

About six years later, in 1849, she came with her husband to Sandusky. 

This was the year of the cholera visitation, and Dr. McMeens soon became a prominent practitioner in his profession. At the outbreak of the Civil War 

Dr. McMeens was commissioned as surgeon of the 3d O. V. I. and while serving in this capacity contracted illness that resulted in his death soon after the battle of Perryville, Ky.

During the war Mrs. McMeens engaged as a volunteer nurse in the hospital service at Nashville, Tenn., her husband being officially connected with the hospitals at that place. For a time prior to her husband's death, Mrs. McMeens resided in this city. She afterward served as hospitals at Washington, D. C., and after the war returned to this city, where she has since resided. nurse in In the death of Mrs. McMeens our city loses a noble woman whose life work has been given to philanthropy and whose kindness of heart has in no small degree alleviated human suffering and elevated the sphere of womankind. Since the war her benevolence has been chiefly bestowed in administering to the needs of the surviving veterans and she has always [68]

been foremost in aiding those who were deserving of charitable consideration.

At the time of her death Mrs. McMeens was the chaplain of the Woman's Relief Corps of Ohio. She was identified with the Humane Society and was particularly prominent in local G. A. R. and W. R. C. circles, being known as the Mother of McMeens Post. She was also secretary of the 

Put-in-Bay Telegraph Company and has had charge of Mr. Jay Cooke's fine summer residence at Gibraltar Island since it was built in 1865. 

The funeral was of the quietest possible kind, and only the immediate relatives and friends of the deceased lady were among the invited mourners . . . . A number of members of McMeens Post, G. A. R., named in honor of the deceased lady's husband, were drawn up outside the late residence of Mrs. McMeens, while the funeral service was proceeding The casket was covered with a profusion of beautiful floral tributes . . The pall bearers were Messrs. T. Pitt Cooke, Chas. B. Dennis, Fred P. Zollinger, T. M. Sloane, E. H. Zurhorst, and George Butler.. The ceremony at the graveside was performed by Rev. Dr. Jones.

Facts Concerning Andersonville Prison Park In accordance with Special Orders No. 19, Quartermaster-General's 

Office, June 30, 1865, Capt. James M. Moore, A. Q. M., U. S. A., proceeded to Andersonville, Ga., for the purpose of marking the graves of Union soldiers for identification, and enclosing the cemetery. 

From his report to the department, September 20, 1865, the following interesting facts are obtained: 

Capt. Moore left Washington July 8, 1865, with mechanics and material, for the purpose of carrying out the order. On arriving at Savannah, he ascertained that there was no railroad communication to Andersonville, the roads from Savannah direct to Macon and the one from Augusta, via Atlanta, having been destroyed by Sherman's army and not yet repaired. 

He also found that a sufficient number of teams could not be found in the State to transport one-half of his stores and equipment over the nearly four hundred miles of wagon road. He was, therefore, obliged to await the completion of the repairs on the railroad from Augusta to Atlanta, which was accomplished by July 18. After a trip of six days he arrived at Andersonville.

On the morning of July 26, Capt. Moore began the work of identifying the graves.

The dead were found buried in trenches on a site selected by the Confederates, about six hundred yards west of north of the stockade. The trenches varied in length from fifty to one hundred and fifty yards. The bodies in the trenches were from two to three feet below the surface, and, in some instances, but a few inches, where rains had washed away the earth. Additional earth was thrown upon the graves, sufficient to properly cover them, and continuous mounds were made over each as it laid in the original trenches. So closely had the uncoffined and emaciated remains been buried, that each grave occupied but little over twelve inches in width, and, consequently, the small tablets provided by the Government, measuring ten inches in width, nearly touched each other. 

The prisoners at Andersonville had been detailed to aid in burying their dead companions, and, by a stake at the head of each grave, which [69]

bore a number corresponding with a similar numbered name upon the Andersonville Hospital Record, kept by the Confederates, Capt. Moore was enabled to identify and mark the greater portion of them by neat wooden tablets. 

It is a satisfaction to know that reasonable assurance can be placed upon the correct identity of those thus marked. The number thus properly identified by Capt. Moore and his competent assistants, with name, rank, regiment, company and date of death, was 12,461, there being but 451 that bore the sad inscription, "Unknown." One hundred and twenty thousand feet of pine lumber was used in the construction of these tablets, which have since been replaced by neat marble head stones, similar to those now in use in all of the National Cemeteries. A copy of the record was made and entrusted to the keeping of the Superintendent of the Cemetery, so that visitors are enabled to find the grave of anyone whose remains were properly identified. Subsequently a large number of prisoners of war, who had been buried at Macon, Columbus, Thomasville and Albany, and the remains from various battlefields, were transferred to the Andersonville Cemetery.

The Government secured the title to the soil in which these sacred dead were buried, including a strip of equal width extending to the Central of Georgia Railroad. The cemetery proper contains 25 acres, divided by a main avenue running through the center and subdivided into blocks and sections. Walks have been laid out, the ground cleared, trees and shrubbery planted, the graves and entire grounds sodded with grass, drain tile laid, and the grounds enclosed with a thick, ivy-covered brick wall, a tasty brick residence for the Superintendent was erected, with convenient outbuildings and arbors; a flag-staff, from which daily floats the National colors, and thus the sacred spot has been transformed into a beautiful city of the dead. On every Memorial Day impressive ceremonies are conducted by G. A. R. Posts of the Department, attended by large concourses of people, and, from amidst the thick verdure of the trees, daily requiems are chanted by sweet-voiced feathered songsters. 

Andersonville at the Close of the War

Captain Moore gives the following description of the famous prison as he found it: "The noted prison pen is fifteen hundred and forty feet long and seven hundred and fifty feet wide, containing twenty-seven acres. The dead-line is seventeen feet from the stockade and the sentry boxes are 30 yards apart. 

The inside stockade is eighteen feet high, the outer one twelve feet, and the distance between the two is one hundred and twenty feet. Nothing has been destroyed. As our exhausted, emaciated and enfeebled soldiers left it, so it stands today . . . . The ground is filled with holes where they had burrowed, in their efforts to shield themselves from the weather, and many a poor fellow, in endeavoring to protect himself in this manner, was smothered to death by the earth falling upon him."

The Superintendent appointed for the Cemetery was instructed to "allow no buildings or structures, of whatever nature, to be destroyed, particularly the stockade surrounding the prison pen." This precaution, however (doubtless for the reason that the real estate upon which they rested belonged to private parties), availed nothing. In the course of time the buildings all disappeared. The stockade also gradually melted away, much [70]

of it being utilized for fuel, and large portions carried off by relic hunters, in the shape of canes, etc. A considerable portion of it remained standing, however, for over a quarter of a century, but now nothing remains but the stumps under ground, from which the lines may easily be traced. These are mostly pitch pine, which will last under ground for an indefinite time, unless disturbed.

Influenced by a popular demand to preserve and perpetuate the memorable spot as a memorial to the heroes who had fallen there, action to that end was taken by comrades of E. S. Jones Post, No. 5, of Macon, Department of Georgia, and to them credit is due in large measure for the successful results.

At the Department Encampment in Augusta, January 24, 1890, E. S. 

Jones Post, No. 5, introduced a resolution asking assistance and cooperation of the Department. The resolutions were referred to the Council of 

Administration, which appointed a committee to devise means for the purchase, preservation and improvement of the Andersonville Prison property.

The original purchase of land by the the Department of Georgia consisted of about seventy-two and one-third acres, together with a right-ofway 100 feet wide leading toward the railroad station, including all of the stockade, excepting a small portion of the northwest corner, and the forts and earthworks around it. The price paid for it was $1,500. 

Andersonville Accepted By the National W. R. C. 

At the twenty-ninth national convention of the Woman's Relief Corps, held at St. Paul in August, 1896, a committee from the Georgia Department repeated a former suggestion that the Woman's Relief Corps to take charge of the Andersonville Prison property, which resulted in a resolution "that we accept, as a sacred trust, the Andersonville Prison property, on the conditions proposed by the Department of Georgia, G. A. R." The terms were simple, and the title was accepted, free of incumbrance. 

The entire property has been enclosed by a Page wire fence, with convenient gates.

A nine-room residence was then erected, with well furnished rooms, the building being occupied by the caretaker. "Providence Spring"

Probably the most interesting feature of this locality is "Providence Spring"-so named by the heroes of Andersonville. The story is universally familiar, and will ever live in history-of how, when the famished prisoners had reached that moment when they realized they could no longer endure, with naught but the contaminated waters of the creek to sustain them, during a severe electric storm this living stream broke forth, bright, pure and sparkling, bringing renewed life and hope. Skeptics and philosophers may easily explain its appearance from natural causes, yet it will always be recognized as an especial evidence of the infinite power and mercy of a Divinie Providence. Not only was it providential that this supply of pure water should come at this opportune time, but also that it came from within the "dead-line," where, by the prison laws, it was protected from being trampled and defiled. It is flowing still, as in days of yore, ready to refresh all in need who come. [71]
