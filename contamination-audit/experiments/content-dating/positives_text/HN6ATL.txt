FOREWORD 

Nearly half a century ago I had a good time riding to hounds in Virginia and I have endeavored in the following brochure to recapture an impression of the experience in the hope of giving a little amusement to my friends at this Christmas season.

Through the graciousness of Mrs. Woodrow Wilson, Miss Landon Hicks and of Mrs. F. A. B. Portman I have been enabled to include in the narrative details that had escaped me over the years.

This will convey my compliments and very grateful thanks to these ladies. It is Mrs. Portman, one of the former noted horsewomen of Virginia, upon whom such an attempt as this should devolve and it is to be hoped that she may some day decide to bring out her book "Down Memory's Lane and Bridle Paths." 

Lastly, my thanks are due to Burrall Hoffman who for the second time has kindly helped to illustrate my Christmas booklets. Mr. Hoffman now hunts over similar country in Fauquier County to that described in the following pages.

HE CRISP weather of early December in the Virginia hunting country has a charm and beauty all its own. There, in the home of the holly, the mistletoe, the wild persimmon, and the wild turkey, the frosty mornings slowly dissolve in a genial, springlike mellowness under the winter sun. 

The faint scent of wood smoke pervades the hazy atmosphere; the eye catches fleeting glimpses of glowing red earth. Rolling, grassy fields spread out enclosed by stone walls, post-and-rail, snake and plank fences. The fields are often gashed by the abrupt banks of shallow, wandering streams-called branches and are landscaped naturally with coverts of white oak and pine woods of varied extent. 

Such is the Virginia of my remembrance at the turn of the present century. In these congenial surroundings, the spirit of the cross country rider achieves great heights. Accompanied by horse and hound, he feels inspired to rise superior to all obstacles as he quaffs deep of the excitements and risks of the chase. When the hunt is ended, the slow ride homeward through the quiet countryside in the waning daylight brings perfect relaxation and gratitude for the high privilege of living on God's earth. 

My young sister and I had passed the early months of the year in Morocco convalescing from illnesses [7]

we had contracted in widely separated parts of the world. Late in the year we found ouselves, together with our saddles and my little 

French bulldog, Meese, in Warrenton, Virginia, which lies in the foothills of the Blue Ridge Mountains, bent on getting some riding to hounds. Although our winter home was in Washington, it happened that we had been little in the South. "MEESE" 

From the first the quaint old town with its dignified, pillared court house of red brick appealed to me curiously-as if it were a well known landmark to which I had returned after long absence. This odd impression may have been an echo of the influence of my early bringing up. I had lived in a household which, although all my people had been wholly sympathetic with the northern cause during the Civil War, voiced only tolerance in that late post-war period when the wounds of fratricidal strife were still festering in many distraught minds. 

My father's family was from the Hudson River valley in Columbia County, New York. After having served through the war with the northern forces, he established his winter home in Washington where he had inherited property. He had first gone there as a young man, upon graduating from Williams College years before the war, to study at law while living with his great-uncle, General John P. Van [8]

Ness, in his mansion on the site now occupied by the 

Pan American buildings.

My mother was descended from a Maryland family, her branch of which had resided in old Georgetown before the capital city of Washington was planned; she was distantly connected with a number of families in the southern states. 

In my childhood, she told me of a trip she had made as a girl to Richmond after its occupation by the army of the North and before the war had ended. 

When my mother's party arrived there, having travelled on the same train that bore President Lincoln from Washington, the fallen capital of the Confederacy was still in flames. She stayed at the town house of some relatives left in distress by the siege. One night after a muffled knock at the back entrance, in walked a handsome young kinsman, William Worthington, wearing the uniform of a Confederate officer. 

He stayed only long enough to greet his northern cousin of the same family name and to give her a hurried kiss before dashing away. How he had learned of her presence and contrived to get through the Union lines for this dramatic visit, and how he had been able to return to his regiment uncaptured, in that uniform, my mother never learned, owing to the death of this gallant cousin soon after the war. 

I have taken the liberty of touching upon family matters in an attempt to account for that queer feeling of familiarity with what was an entirely strange environment. [9]

M Y SISTER AND I bore no letters of introduction; none seemed necessary. We felt we were in a hospitable land from the day of arrival. The rambling old 

Warren Green hotel was our headquarters when we came down to hunt. How well I remember the friendly groups around the big stove in the bare office reading room in the mornings! 

There, three young northerners-Ned Center, Wilson Drake and I listened to old Colonel Moses 

Green, Doctor Winter Payne and others of Civil

War vintage, hold forth in long arguments which often generated heat but which never became acrimonious. Of this Warren Green coterie, which in- 

Icluded also Colonel Tom Smith and Colonel Grenville Gains, it was lovingly and facetiously said that if any of them should die and an autopsy be performed, only Confederate flags and mint juleps would be found.

On a non-hunting day one would glimpse people in horsy attire striding about the quiet old town or deep in conversation with farmers from the country. 

Everyone knew each other and the stranger usually was greeted with a cheerful "Mawnin', Sir!" The scene had a decidedly sporting tinge, and whatever were the occupations of the young men of the neighborhood, it was safe to wager that horses, hounds and hunting were the main subjects of discussion at that season. As a matter of fact the breeding of hunters was being widely developed and had become a very profitable business in that part of Virginia. [10]

This brief description of Warrenton late in 1900 would be incomplete without some mention of its little group of British residents. It was said in jocular vein that some of them had come out from England as "mud students," with an exaggerated idea of learning to farm where horse raising was the chief pursuit. I can only say that when I went to the place its charm was much enhanced by the presence of these congenial, sporting gentlemen. One must conclude that they had done well at learning to farm, for many of them owned large places in Fauquier County. At riding certainly they had nothing to learn; most of them were in the top flight "over country." The names I remember best are those of 

Mr. and Mrs. F. A. B. Portman, Mrs. Frank Barker, the Barker brothers, Astley Cooper and Arthur

Dundas. There was also an elderly English Baronet, Sir Harcourt Lees, who lived a rather secluded life at some distance from Warrenton. He kept a pack of beagle hounds and would sometimes invite Drake and myself over on moonlight nights to hunt coons in the woods on his farm. As the little beagles gave tongue, the forest would resound with their grand music to the delight of the Baronet, who could excitedly name each hound by the tone of its distant baying.

This is a cursory impression of a once prosperous county seat. It had found itself in ruins from the ravages of civil strife thirty-five years before, but struggled valiantly against economic collapse to win its way to a new prosperity.

Having brought no horses of our own, I sought [11]

out the widely known gentleman horseman, Jim

Maddux, owner of many famous hunters and show horses. A dapper, active man with a weathered face and quick dark eyes, he lived a bachelor existence in a quaint, old stone house on the Spring Road, with training paddock and great brick stables behind it. 

Formerly known as the Governor Smith house, it was called "Neptune Lodge" by Maddux in memory, I believe, of his first steeplechase horse. He was most obliging and at once arranged to let us have hunters temporarily, assuring us that the Master of the Drag would be delighted to see us at the meet of the hounds the next day. Maddux, a great local character and a veteran cross-country rider, was then, as I recall, Master of the separate pack of hounds used to hunt the wild fox only.

I must confess to having passed much of that night awake in my Warren Green bed conjuring up the probable events of the morrow, for the Warrenton country was renowned for the size and solidity of its jumps. I had ridden to fox hounds and harriers in 

England but never had hunted in Virginia, where the tall timber and jagged stone walls, I had heard, were very formidable by comparison with most of the hedges and ditches I had encountered abroad. [12]

q HAT FIRST MEET was held several miles from town. We drove to it in a light buggy behind a blooded little mare with a mouth like satin. The equipage was furnished by the town livery stable. There were as yet no automobiles in common use. Jeff, the big negro lad who walked our horses to the meet and who returned to town with the trap, was one of the happiest looking mortals I have ever seen. His white teeth were constantly shining in smile or song, and when not otherwise employed, he would practice intricate steps of some "doubleshuffle" or "buck" dance to his own mouth organ accompaniment.

There was a large field out for the Drag-at that time the most popular form of hunting in Warrenton. It was laid to simulate the hunt of a live fox in every possible detail, with long, fast runs and occasional checks over the strongly fenced-in country. 

Some of the riders were splendidly mounted and well turned out, but there was great informality and originality of dress as well as of mounts. The Warrenton country had not then attracted from the north and elsewhere those who later transformed it into a mecca for the ultra-smart lovers of good horses and good hunting.

We were greeted by the Master of the Drag, F. A.

The dress of the men ranged from the immaculate green frock, white leathers and top boots of the Mas- [14]

ter to sketchy, old fashioned garments of country gentlemen and farmers out from sheer love of the sport and for the schooling of green hunters. The ladies, all mounted sidesaddle, made graceful figures in their trim riding habits.

One horseman particularly attracted my notice as I waited in considerable suspense for the Master to start the pack of big hounds moving. His attire was quite odd and denoted a studied though rather bizarre effort at formality. In spite of it, the wearer possessed such natural dignity of bearing that a first impulse to smile was promptly stifled in a wave of sympathetic respect for a fellow sportsman. The gentleman rode a bay gelding "in the rough,' as it were, with a fine head and shoulder but so indifferently groomed, with long rough coat and straggling mane and tail, as to give an impression of mediocrity. 

All was now activity, as the Master and whips took the hounds forward, through a gate and off on a wide cast, the large pack scattering eagerly through the covert. Soon one of the hounds gave tongue. The staccato notes of the galloping Masters' horn rallied the entire pack to that point, and promptly, with a great burst of ringing, exultant voices they dashed pell mell away to a burning scent. The pace they set was fast. After some confusion and crowding at the early fences, the field strung out and there was plenty of room. 

Jim Maddux had supplied us with good jumpers.

My sister, on my left, was taking her fences like a veteran. So far we had encountered no unusually [15]

high jumps. With the confidence born of action and the glorious exhilaration of the hunt I felt ready for anything that might come until on galloping through a depression between two hills I caught sight of my first Virginia stone wall! It dampened my enthusiasm, looming forbiddingly ahead on a slight rise, with sharp, loose-laid flat stones along its top. As I went rushing onward, eyes determinedly focussed upon the deadly barrier, I was conscious of a horse and rider coming abreast of me with the effortless gallop of the thoroughbred. It was the gentleman in the bizarre costume. Just as we were almost up to the take-off for the wall, he unconcernedly turned his head in my direction and called "Nice mawnin', Sir!" As I nodded and felt my horse's mouth with the bit, to steady him, the other horse shot into the lead and gracefully cleared the wall with a foot to spare. My mount also jumped very "big."

Thereafter I found it comforting that even very careless jumpers usually did their best not to rap those relentless stone walls. Subsequently I had the pleasure of getting to know the quaintly garbed rider and found him to be a great sportsman indeed. He was a member of an old family which had not recuperated a fortune lost during the war but still held heads high. The rather unkempt looking horse he rode was a valuable descendant of the well-known sire Torchlight.

My sister at that time had had less experience riding over country than I, but she was a fine horse- [16]

woman and took to the sport instinctively. Her judgment and "hands" when in the saddle were excellent, as I think the following incident which occurred soon after we first went to Warrenton will illustrate. 

Mr. Charles Hurcamp, of Charlotte as I remember, had asked my sister to ride a young hunter of his which had never been hunted by a lady, as he wanted it accustomed to side saddle. The horse proved to be a handsome, nervous chestnut with a rather wild eye. I was very dubious as to the wisdom of my sister's riding this excitable young animal, so it was decided that she would first take him over the jumps in the Maddux enclosure. This was bordered on two sides by an eight foot brick wall and on the other sides by stiff whitewashed board fences. From the first jump the horse showed a will to get out of hand. 

He cleared beautifully but after each obstacle he gathered speed at an alarming rate and rushed the succeeding one in dangerous fashion. His rider did. not fight the animal but sat very quietly and did what she could to calm him, though obviously without much success. We called to her to bring him up to us at the gate and dismount if possible. But at that moment the chestnut bolted out of the course of jumps and bore down the open turf at the side of the field directly towards the brick wall-a runaway. 

Maddux and I stood powerless to prevent impending disaster. Elizabeth, however, did not move in the saddle, and deliberately dropped her hands, permitting the horse to rush as he would at that high wall. It was an alarming sight, but the animal must have understood that his "bluff was called," for at [17]

the last moment instead of dashing futilely into the obstruction he planted his feet in an abrupt halt. His rider turned him sharply to the left, banging sideways into the bricks with no other damage than a marred sidesaddle flap. That was what I call good riding. From then on my sister had no great trouble with the chestnut, which gave her several fair rides in the hunting field. Nevertheless I was relieved when the horse was returned to its owner. [18]

BOUT THAT TIME Jim Maddux told me he owned a "cleanbred" hunter which he would be glad to lend me to ride in the hunt the following day, and this invitation, of course, I was delighted to accept. I went to the stable and found the horse to be a tall, brown thoroughbred with fine points, but a rather comical look-a remote, disenchanted expression. His name-Billy Bowlegswas well chosen; the forefeet toed in slightly. Moreover, the strong forelegs had a recurrent nervous tremble of the knees when in repose, which gave the big horse the appearance of being on the point of falling asleep and then of thinking better of it. Billy 

Bowlegs was what horsemen call "over in front," but I knew that some fine horses over country developed this trait without bad effect upon their performance. Maddux said he had never hunted Billy, but he assured me that the horse was quite sound and a fine jumper although rather difficult to ride.

The field was a large one that day. In the early stages of the hunt Billy proved to be a grand jumper, very fast, but somewhat headstrong. The hounds led us over fine grass country with very stiff timber fences. I was so occupied with my mount that I failed for some time to observe that we were drawing out in front and that only the Master and a whip were in sight. It then dawned upon me that it was 

Billy's firm intention to over-run that pack, if not to pass it! To restrain him from this most heinous [19]

offense against the code of those who ride to hounds, I was hard put, with only a snaffle bit, to keep him at a respectful distance. So strenuous were my efforts to coax the big racer into a less violent pace that as time wore on I realized I was tiring more rapidly than the horse.

The hounds were now crossing some fields enclosed by rather low, old-fashioned snake rail fences. 

As we approached one of these I did what I could to get my flying mount to jump it in the approved fashion-at a slight angle. But Billy startled me by just galloping effortlessly through the fence, cracking the old rails and scattering them in all directions -without changing his stride one iota! Could this be a warning of coming events? After that, I just hung on and pulled for dear life until the hounds lost the scent in a merciful check.

The Master gave me an admonitory but kindly glance, and, on getting my breath, I asked if it wouldn't be better for me to pull out of the field. "By no means," he replied, and congratulated me on having done well so far with the horse, which he said acted more like a steeplechaser than a hunter. During the checks Billy Bowlegs subsided entirely and stood as docile as a lamb, with his air of aloofness and his agitating knee reflexes. When the hounds took up the scent again, he became alert. Later on we galloped through rolling pasture lands. Here occasional up-grades and heavy plowed bottom lands permitted me to come to better terms with my ambitious mount, and he performed in a very creditable [20]

manner. Nearing the summit of one of the slopes,

I spied an enormous fence of planks ahead which I learned afterwards stood at over five and a half feet. (There was, by the way, at least one fence of six feet that was crossed occasionally by the Warrenton

Bracing myself for the big effort I let Billy race towards that fence with apparent determination, but his thoughts must have been elsewhere. To my horror he barely rose, and crashed breast on with a resounding impact that splintered a path through several of the heavy boards. The force of the collision brought him into his only stumble of the day. 

As he went down on his knees I pitched forward astride his neck. With astonishing strength he recovered, literally throwing me back almost into the saddle, and off we galloped again.

After this circus-like performance the trend of the hunt was down grade through a lovely valley, the misty Blue Ridge mountains in the background. Yet

HAD ASKED Mr. Maddux to be on the lookout for a medium-priced hunter for my use during the rest of the season, a more satisfactory arrangement than riding strange horses. Soon he asked me to go over to his stables to see a new arrival he thought might interest me. This was a big light bay horse with a bold eye, good shoulders and hocks, and three white stockings. Maddux told me frankly that he knew very little about the horse except that he was sound, descended from the blazed-face thoroughbred sire, Tecumseh, and that his age was five years. It appeared that he had been lunged over the madejumps in the paddock the day before, without a rider. He performed so naturally and well that jumping bars were put up to six feet and he was sent at them. In his effort to clear, the horse had taken a heavy fall, coming down on the back of his neck. The fact that he jumped at once to his feet and began to quietly nibble grass had excited the admiration of the onlookers.

There was something I liked in the big horse, and I bought him. I must say we took a great many falls, but I never regretted my decision. He was quite fast, a bold jumper and gave me many thrilling rides. I am afraid that we gained rather a reputation for "coming croppers." Once when I asked a friend to suggest a name, the unhesitating reply was Harakiri!

This I discarded as too bloodthirsty, but revised it to 

Harry Carry, and so it remained.

The horse had had no previous training in the [24]

hunting field. Indeed it was whispered that he had begun his career when a colt by drawing a postman's cart, and in his eagerness to jump anything he often made mistakes. But we seemed to lead charmed lives and certainly had a good time, both with the Drag and the live fox packs. 

My final ride on Harry Carry remains a vivid memory. The hunting season was approaching its finish, a glorious early spring Virginia day. The

Drag was a very fast one and Harry Carry's jumping had been perfection. The hounds led us at length through an abandoned farm garden, over a wicked looking picket fence and into a broad road bordered on the far side by a small embankment, along the top of which ran a stiff rail fence fronting a widely spaced row of pointed cedar trees. Several horses had refused this imposing obstacle; it had to be crossed, so the greater part of the field was held up there. I knew Harry Carry would not refuse, and although the width of the road was short for the takeoff, I put him at it. He rose bravely to the high fence and with a great leap was about to clear it perfectly when a young Warrenton gentleman, riding a half trained hunter, endeavored to take our lead. 

His mount refused and, rearing, swerved directly into me, turning my horse completely over in midair. Harry Carry was sent into a smashing fall, broke down the fence as he landed on his nose, and fell upon me. I felt a sharp pain in the side but was able to pull myself out from under and to get both of us out of the way to enable the others to pass through the gap. REFLECT that to all those who love open air life and sports, the companionship and courageous, unquestioning loyalty of horses and dogs have not only contributed much to the pleasure of existence but also have provided a cherished solace in its moments of frustration. In the hearts of many people, regardless of the vital urge of their life work, such contact with nature taps deep springs of emotion which help them to know real happiness, to deal tolerantly with others, and to esteem the vast bounties of Providence. Neither riches nor prolonged leisure are requisite to this. It is a sorry man indeed who cannot own a dog. God's great out-of-doors is within reach of all of us. Memories arising from days in the open spaces are among the most precious as time marches on. 

In Warrenton for a Christmas Eve run with the hounds, we had been invited to have Christmas dinner with the Barrys at their charming home on a hill some miles from town. It was the local custom to make calls on Christmas Day. No one took regular lunch at home, but all the hunting set passed from house to house to offer their friends the good wishes of the season. Several of us made the visits together. 

At each home we would join the merry throng in the dining room around a table loaded with good things and graced by the inevitable bowl of apple toddy.

This drink, a brew of applejack brandy with innocent baked apples afloat on it, was strange to meand more potent than its delicious flavor indicated! [28]

Old fashioned apple toddy is so little known today that I have found it most difficult to learn how it was made. After much inquiry I am at last able to offer this information to my friends. Old recipes graciously furnished me by three charming daughters of Virginia will be found later in this little booklet.

As the visits progressed the guests developed a tendency to lean in comradely fashion, shoulder to shoulder, with those of the merry company around the hospitable boards. I departed from the last house with a deep regard for the charming, sociable people of Warrenton-and a high respect for their apple toddy.

Having engaged the little bay mare and buggy for our drive to dinner that evening, I stopped at the livery stable on the way home to dress, to offer a little Christmas gift to Jeff, to whom I had become attached, and to make certain that the order had not been forgotten. On entering the stable yard I heard loud roars of laughter and the stifled voice of Jeff ejaculate: "O, Lord! Hep me to laff!" He stood there doubled up in a paroxysm of glee over some joke known only to himself. Relative calm followed my appearance and Jeff thanked me for the gift with much dignity, but as I left the yard, the gales of mirth again rang out, Jeff's voice pleading with his Maker to assist him in coping with this mysterious jest.

The night was a dark one as we started the drive to the Barry farm. Jeff had had the forethought to [29]

stow a lantern in the back of the trap. The approach to Major Barry's house passed through many enclosed pasture lands, over which was laid the course of the annual cross country race, the Warrenton Hunt Cup, in which I was subsequently to take part. 

The main entrance gate was familar to me but was succeeded by several inner gates and the road became very indistinct in the darkness. At one point I realized that we were off the road, and proposed making a detour to the left in search of it. My sister, in spite of evening gown, volunteered with alacrity to take the lantern and search on foot in the other direction. 

After she had alighted I made an oblique turn to the left. I had driven some distance when the mare rose before me and suddenly made a scrambling and necessarily restrained leap in her harness. Over a loose stone wall we clattered, coming to rest with a bang, apparently without damage, in another field.

The dim little side light of the buggy, still burning, fell upon the wall as I drove gingerly beside it. I was fortunate enough to discover a gap in it. Then came a call from my sister, and the waving lantern signalled that she had located the road in the other direction.

Guiding the mare carefully over the loose stones of the gap, I drove toward the lantern. About half way there, with a jerk of the traces, the mare disappeared in the darkness; another jerk, and the buggy seemed to descend into a pit; then, up went that wonderful quadruped as she dragged the buggy and myself by a series of tugs up the steep bank of a wide, open trench through which we had made our [30]

miraculous way. With a final bump we reached the level and rejoined my sister, not having suffered a scratch. Elizabeth resumed her seat very dubiously, but this time we arrived at our destination without further adventures. [31]

MERRY CHRISTMAS and a real Virginia welcome awaited us, with a fine oldfashioned dinner a huge turkey at one end of the long table, a ham at the other, to mention but two of the many delicious things-followed by music, waltzes, and a rollicking Virginia Reel.

The last goodnight was ringing out as the sportive mare started us homeward at a smart clip, eyes and ears on the qui vive, lifting each fast-moving hoof high and placing it down upon the road with infinite precision, as is the habit of well-bred young horses at night.

The moon had come up, and as we bowled along the pike in the sparkling, frosty air the shadowy landscape was bathed in silver light. The broad fields, with their ghostly fences, stretched out to dark woods and beyond to the indigo slopes of the Blue Ridge mountains-ever softened by their blue haze which, according to Indian legend, is the smoke from the Great Spirit's pipe of peace.

The hushed panorama lay as if under a spell of expectancy. Could it not be awaiting the strident notes of a fairy hunting horn to conjure the full cry of a pack of phantom hounds leading the panoply of an elfin hunt over the course so often galloped in daylight hours by the hard riding sportsmen of the

YE OLDE VIRGINIA APPLE TODDY RECIPES 

[35]

Furnished by MRS. WOODROW WILSON of Washington, D. C. 

HOT APPLE TODDY 

Be sure to use goblets. 

8 goblets of water 

12 goblets Jamaica spirits (rum) 

2 goblets of brandy (applejack) 

1 large nutmeg, grated 

1 dozen hard green apples

Bake apples with skin on, coring first. Add to other ingredients and put in a container with a lid. 

When ready to serve, take large punch cups, put one apple in each and fill to brims with liquid which has been heated. 

Will keep indefinitely. 

Note: I wonder if Mrs. Wilson is not over optimistic about that delicious sounding toddy keeping indefinitely?-H.P. [36]

Furnished by MRS. F. A. B. PORTMAN of Warrenton, Va. 

OLD SOUTHERN RECIPE FOR APPLE TODDY 

3 dozen apples baked in their peelings 

When done, scrape out pulp with a spoon. 

To the pulp add 

1 gallon of water 

1 pint of rum 

1 gallon apple brandy (applejack) or grape brandy 

234 lbs. of sugar 

1 tablespoon of allspice 

1 tablespoon of nutmeg or ginger 

Run through a seive and let stand four weeks.

If toddy is to be served in a large bowl, bake some small red apples in their peelings and place in bowl with the toddy.

Note: I think it must have been toddy such as is described by 

Mrs. Portman that I was privileged to sample on that Christmas 

Day in 1900.-H.P. [37]

Furnished by MISS LANDON HICKS of Warrenton, Va. 

APPLE TODDY 

Bake two dozen winesap apples in a pan. When done take off skins and take out seeds. Mash through colander. Put in this amount 

1 cup sugar 

2 cups boiling water 

4 bottles brandy (applejack) 

2 bottles whiskey 

Put in jug and shake every morning. Half teaspoon of cloves in jug. 

Note: Miss Hicks has underscored the words "shake every morning." She does not inform us whether it was customary to sample the contents of the jug after each matinal shake!-H.P. [38]
