INTRODUCTION 

The characters in this story, although parading under fictitious names, are living persons. . . .

This is what the author claims, a really true story. 

It is about a man and woman well known in business circles, not only in their home town but also in many other communities throughout the United 

States. 

The plot concerns the life and adventures of a man, who is hardly out of one love-triangle, before he has plunged into another!

This story has been written for three reasons, which will surprise and intrigue the reader as the plot unfolds.

In talking about a tragedy which had occurred, one woman said to the author: "These affairs are always shrouded in mystery. 

Why do they not tell the reason?" 

I replied: "If I am ever involved in one, you shall know the reason.' 99 And then I said, IF I ever do anything, it will be because Life is so miserable I cannot stand it any longer, not because I hate anyone.

A BUSINESS LEADER'S SWEETHEART

A Business Leader's Sweetheart CHAPTER ONE 

"Who buys a minute's mirth to wail a week;

Or sells Eternity to get a toy, For one sweet grape, who would the vine destroy. 

Or! what fond beggar, but to touch the crown, would with the scepter, straight be stricken down." 

How appropriate these lines! I do not recall the author but the words have lived in my mind for years. Now, after I have paid the price, I vividly recall them. 

December 26, 1932. 

I have spent many Christmas days, sad and alone but none like yesterday. As the voices came over the radio, singing Christmas carols, I cried aloud to God. . . to know what

I had done to be made to suffer as I do. I cannot stand it any longer. I do not think there can be any more agony in Hell, than I have suffered in the past two years, and why?

Because I loved a man, not wisely, but too well. 

Why do men make women love them and then

break their hearts? Why did I not kill him when he turned on me and insulted me, after binding me to him for life and I had waited twenty years? He had told me that if we stuck together until he was free and I then turned him down, he would kill ME. (Why, then, did I not do it to him?) 

I said to myself: "If anyone ever makes my life so miserable that I could not live, that person would go into death with me." 

Why did I not do it? 

Does an invisible Power stay my hand? I cannot stand this life much longer. There are many nights I feel like closing the windows and turning on the gas, but one thought holds me . . . 

. . I WILL 

NOT go alone. He kept telling me to be on the square and said: "If you ever double cross me, and I find out, I will kill you. I couldn't stand it to know someone was laughing up his sleeve at me." 

He then added: "We love each other too much to ever end in a tragedy" . . . but what else can it be? 

Oh, God, have mercy on me and help me to bear this pain or end it all.

February 1, 1933. 

Twenty-one years ago tonight, he came for the first time. Tonight I sent him a picture and this note: "This is a picture of a shack in the woods, on the back are wild roses that bloom in the yard.

Am sending it to you for an anniversary present. "On February 1, 1912, twenty-one years ago tonight, you came knocking at my door and I let you

in. You told me that night you despised a liar. "Those were my sentiments exactly. In the years that followed you told me that I would never get the bitter end from you. You also told me that you could be happy and contented in a shack in the woods with me alone, hence the picture. "Keep it to remind you of a woman who loved you with all your faults, not wisely but too well. "The one thing I would like to know is-what became of the undying love you professed to have for me? Tonight I sit alone and review the past while you are loving another woman. Perhaps when you read my review you, too, will remember many things, such as sitting in the coal shed on cold, stormy nights, holding hands in the street car, crying on my shoulder, etc. I will send you one of the first copies printed, until then, Au Revoir." 

I wonder what he did with the picture and what his thoughts were. If he has any heart, soul, conscience or principle, how can he love and kiss another woman? What kind of a woman does he think I am, to stand for such treatment?

I shall tell the story from the beginning, not sparing myself and he who reads may judge. I am not telling this story for sympathy. I am not trying to exonerate myself. I liked the man and I felt sorry for him. I knew he and his wife did not get along together but I had no right to let him call on me while he was living with her. Too well

I see it now. A divorce would have been better for all concerned.

Many will say that I was a fool to trust him and many will say that I have received just what I de-

served. I agree with them. My father always said: "Trust everybody until you prove them unworthy," but after trusting a man for twenty years, the shock is almost too much to bear. 

This story has only to do with our lives after we met and we promised that nothing that ever happened before would make any difference to us, so I shall only say that Mr. Gilmore's family were very poor. He told me his father died a drunkard and his mother worked hard to make a living for her children.

Wilbur had to go to work very young to help support the family and I felt sorry for the little boy and made allowances for the imperfections in the man. He said he drank when he was a young man but he realized what it would do to him and he gave it up and I admired him for it. 

My father had a good position and we always had plenty of the necessities of life. My mother loved her children, but was not demonstrative and we did not feel free to go to her with our worries and troubles. She never told us the things a girl should know about life-sex was never mentioned in our family. In my early teens, I contracted a marriage which threw a gloom over my whole life.

As my father forbade the subject ever to be mentioned, very few people knew the real truth. 

Born on Friday the thirteenth and in the graveyard, what could I, Elinor Wolfsen, expect of life?

CHAPTER TWO 

This is a true story and happened in a town in the middle west.

The man, Wilbur J. Gilmore, was elected president of The Mayflower Lumber Company, about a year before he first called on me. My occupation was designer and dressmaker. I had met this man about three or four years before he came to see me.

On the way to church one Sunday evening, a friend and I passed him out walking. 

My friend said: "His wife is in the hospital (she had an operation) and had better hurry out and look after him." This woman was a distant relative of his wife. The next time I saw him was on another Sunday evening, he and his wife were out riding and this same woman and I met them and stopped to talk. She introduced us and I can still see the look in his eyes as he gazed at me. We were attracted to each other from the first time we met. We attended some of the same dances and became good friends. The K. of P. lodge that he belonged to had dances quite often. When he danced with me he hugged me so tight, many of my friends noticed it and joked about it, and we had a good time laughing about it. 

Little did I dream that an affair started in fun perhaps would end in a tragedy. He had a wife

and step-son.

I was a widow with one son and lived with my mother. 

My son went west January 5, 1910. His wife joined him in May of the same year and in July, 1911, her mother and I went out and took her children, two little girls by a former marriage. 

A couple of weeks before we went, there was a band concert down town one night. I was with a girl friend and we passed this man and a friend of his. He told me afterward that he had a notion to follow me after I left my friend, and ask me to go with him and his wife on the Steam Shovel excursion, the next day. Of course, I would not have gone. He also told me they had a quarrel at Cedar 

Point and when the train arrived in the depot at Marion, she went home one way and he another. 

At that time I had a gentleman friend, a single man, who traveled for a manufacturing company and sometimes would be gone for several months. 

He was gone when I returned from the West and I went to an Elk dance with some friends. Mr. Gilmore was there without his wife and danced with me several times and then asked if he could take me home. (He told me afterwards, he went with that hope.) I said I had company and never thought any more about it. In a few days he called me on the telephone and talked, then went to Dayton to a convention. He sent me a postcard with a picture of a man looking into a woman's eyes and saying: "There is something about you that I like."

When he came back, he called me up and asked if

he could come to see me. Several times he called and I said no, then one day I said yes. 

He came on that night, which was February 1, 1912. I had on a challie house dress, cream ground with a brick colored Persian design, made perfectly plain princess with a train. He thought it was beautiful. I do not say this to be conceited, only for those who did not know me. I had a form men admired and women envied. I was told many times by women, as well as men, that I had the best form of any woman in town. Some women for whom I sewed would say: "I wish you could make me look like you do." I was five feet and five inches tall, had brown eyes and golden brown hair. At that time the hair was fixed on top of the head with puffs and it was beautiful.

That winter my mother was away on a visit and I was alone. As soon as Mr. Gilmore was in the house and the door closed, he put his arms around me and kissed me. I sat on the sofa and pointed to a chair for him to sit on, but he said: "You don't think I am going to sit on one side of the room and you the other, do you?" Then he sat down by me.

He told me how much he liked me and how unhappy he was at home and how he had been married when quite young and divorced his wife because he caught her with another man. He wanted me to hear the story from his own lips as someone would be sure to tell me. (His second wife later did tell me and pointed the woman out to me on the street.) He wanted to come again and I told him to wait a couple of weeks as my friend would go

away again about that time. He had come back a few weeks after I returned from the West. 

After my friend had gone, I let Mr. Gilmore come again. He came four or five nights that week and told me he loved me and would like to have me for his wife. He told me he and his wife were not mated and did not live happily together and that he had his suitcase packed a couple of times and was going to leave but changed his mind. 

Mr. Gilmore was five feet and seven inches tall, had grey eyes and black hair, a type I had never admired. My ideal had brown hair, blue eyes, tall, broad shoulders, fine physique; and the queer part was, that I had met a single man of that type and we corresponded. He called on me several times and I couldn't love him. All the more reason, I thought this was fate. Mr. Gilmore was the only man I ever really wanted to live with, take care of, cook good things for and be a real wife to and how many times he said: "What a wonderful wife you would make! It is a shame our lives have to be like this, but don't worry, sweetheart, if the time ever comes when I can have you in the right way, you will never regret waiting."

I liked his wife and she liked me and invited me to their home. I did not want to go but he insisted. 

He called me up nearly every day and came twice a week. He went to his office on Sunday mornings and called me and sometimes we saw each other at church.

Just before he came up here, Wilbur's step-son was married and he and his wife were living with his mother and step-father. That gave Wilbur a

better opportunity to go out in the evenings and he improved upon it.

The first time I called him "Will," it gave him a thrill and he kissed me and said: "You don't know how sweet that sounded to me." We agreed that no matter what had happened to either of us in the past, that would be forgotten and we would be true to each other; and, if the time ever came that he was free, we would be married. He told me he knew I loved him; he saw it in my eyes one night at a dance.

CHAPTER THREE 

They were having plenty of trouble at home. 

The son had no work and the mother wanted Wilbur to take him into business with him, but Wilbur didn't think it was the thing to do. They quarrelled incessantly.

He came to me and told me his troubles and cried. He told me he received neither sympathy nor encouragement at home. He wanted me to promise to love him and stick to him no matter what happened and assured me I would never regret it. 

He talked about arranging his affairs so we could go away together, but I did not encourage that. 

One day I went to their house and his wife said to me: "Don't you think when people get a divorce, the wife should have half of what the husband owns ?" 

I said: "I don't know anything about it." I did not encourage a divorce for several reasons; I do not believe in it. I was taught to believe a promise was made to be kept. Another thing, he was doing the same thing he had divorced his first wife for, and I loved him enough to wait for him.

He said she would tell the lawyer he treated her mean and he didn't want her to tell that. 

Neither one of us had enjoyed life very much, and both had had unpleasant experiences. He said: "Our meeting was fate. Why could we not have met each other when we were twenty?" I was

thirty-eight and he was forty-one. lovers, yet how we loved each other! 

Middle aged

Twenty-one years ago the house was not so warm as it is now with a furnace and I wore outing flannel nightgowns then. I wonder if any of Wilbur's friends can visualize his being tucked into bed, dressed in one of my flannel gowns!

In the spring my mother came home and, of course, I could not bring him in the house, so we went walking and often sat on a bench in the Catholic cemetery. We went riding (horse and buggy) and drove across the first road south of the cemetery and stopped half way. We always spoke of it as our road, as it was our parking place until it became impassable. 

Sometimes we would come in and sit on the north end of the porch. One night he said: “If you had curtains, we could sit here often. Find out what they will cost and I will give you the money to get them." That was the first money I took from him.

He had offered some but I refused it. He also made me a present of some screens for the upstairs windows that summer. 

One evening my mother and I were walking down town and we met Mr. and Mrs. Gilmore and the daughter-in-law. Mr. Gilmore had the hiccups for two days, and as soon as we met, they stopped. 

The next day his daughter-in-law said to him: "Hiccups stop when you meet the one you love."

I was wearing a black and white checked silk dress that night and he told me to keep it always as a token of love. (I still have it put away in a box.)

When we couldn't see each other we wrote letters and he wanted me to meet him at noon at the post office and walk with him. One day his wife came with him and I was standing in the post office talking to a traveling man I had met at a dance the night before.

Wilbur was so angry his wife noticed it and asked him what business it was of his. I was so excited 

I walked out and left my pocketbook on the desk. 

That evening I walked down to the office to ask about it. As I went in one door, Wilbur came in the other.

Of course, it was fate that brought us together in that way. He was very angry and jealous, but 

I convinced him meeting this man meant nothing to me.

He did not want me to look at another man. He said he wished he could lock me in a room and carry the key and not let anyone see me but himself.

Whenever I went anywhere with another man, Wilbur was miserable and begged me to give up everybody for his sake and not even have an intimate woman friend, for fear she would come to the house and interfere with his coming. 

When Mr. Gilmore first came here, I was friendly with a big, fat, good natured man, a distant relative of my mother. He took me to dances and told me if I wanted anything done around the house to call on him. He would be the chore boy. 

He was useful to move furniture and things of that sort and I invited him to dinner occasionally. He did not live with his wife, said she was not true to

him and if I would marry him, he would try and get a divorce but I said: "No."

Wilbur was so jealous, I had to tell my other friend not to come any more.

As in the case of Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots, there was something about me that (unconsciously to myself) attracted men. Today I suppose this would be called "it," and like Queen Mary, the axe descended on my neck-only in a different way. 

Perhaps the reason I think of Queen Mary is because I am part Scotch. My father was born in

Scotland. 

At that time I had several admirers, mostly married man who wanted to make dates but I would have none of them. One night at a dance one man asked me to go buggy riding and bragged about his horses and said he drove tandem and I replied: "Drive past the house tomorrow morning and let me see your horses." 

Sure enough, about ten o'clock I heard horses' feet prancing along and I went to the window and then called the girls who were sewing for me, to come and see the show. The man was driving two beautiful horses tandem and they were prancing along like a circus.

Another man who wanted to make a date, had a sleigh. Just for fun, I told him to drive past the house. One morning I heard sleigh bells ringing; he was driving up and down the street. 

These men were wealthy and just as good looking as Mr. Gilmore, but money had no attraction for me.

There were other married men who wanted to call on me, one a neighbor, an assistant

Sunday school superintendent to whom I scarcely spoke. 

This shows how little men think of their marriage vows. 

I told Wilbur about this man and how it happened and he thought that was terrible.

CHAPTER FOUR 

In August, Mrs. Gilmore went to visit relatives in Minneapolis (I have post cards she sent me from there). While she was away, Wilbur took me riding on Sunday evenings, and on Labor Day I went to the picnic at the fair grounds with some friends and he drove up.

It was raining, and he came in the Art Hall and told me to come out of the back door and get in the phaeton. We drove down town and he got some ice cream and we went to his house and ate it. There was a girl rooming there and he invited her downstairs but she did not come. 

I was his cousin. 

She thought 

I made her 

Mrs. Gilmore enjoyed her visit. some of the most beautiful dresses she ever had and I took great pains to make her look nice. She had a short fat figure, blue eyes and brown hair and a pretty face.

That winter we became great friends and they invited my mother and me down to their house for dinner and then we played cards. We invited them here to dinner and we played cards and the game often ended in a quarrel between husband and wife. 

When we had company I did the cooking. One night we had roast lamb and he said it was the best he had ever eaten. His wife did not like that very well, but neither she nor my mother suspected we were anything more to each other than good

friends. 

We also exchanged Christmas presents for several years.

Not daring to bring him into the house when my mother was home, I would let her go to a lodge we belonged to and then he would come; and I would go later to come home with her. Other times when it was too cold to walk and it was snowing and blowing a regular blizzard, we would sit in the coal shed, bundled up in our overcoats, just to be with each other.

Is there a woman craving love (and who does not) who would blame me for loving a man who would do that and would kiss my hands and tell me 

I was the only woman he ever really loved?

He did not know what love was before he met me and if he could only have me for his wife he would be the happiest man living, so he said. 

In the meantime, my friend had returned from one of his trips and Wilbur was jealous, so in February 1913, after we came home from a dance one night, I told him I did not care to go with him any longer. He felt pretty bad for a while but recovered and went back to a former sweetheart and later they married.

In the winter of 1913, Mr. Gilmore had to go to Toledo on business and wanted me to go with him. We stayed at the Boody House, ate dinner in the dining room. I remember we had turkey. Then we went to a show. I did not sleep a wink all night.

I was worried for fear someone would find it out. 

Wilbur slept as peacefully as a child. That was my first and last trip. I was worried all the time for fear we would meet someone who knew us; and

coming home, there was a man on the train whom we knew. We scarcely spoke to each other for fear we would attract his attention. 

When Wilbur and I could not see each other, we wrote real love letters that anyone might read, and sent poetry to each other. This piece he sent to me: 

"WHEN THE WIND IS Low" 

"When the wind is low, and the sea is soft, And the far heat-lightning plays 

On the rim of the west, where dark clouds nest

On a darker bank of haze; 

When I lean o'er the rail with you that I love 

And gaze to my heart's content;

I know that the heavens are there above- 

But you are my firmament. "When the phosphor-stars are thrown from the bow 

And the watch climbs up the shroud;

When the dim mast dips as the vessel slips 

Through the foam that seethes aloud;

I know that the years of our life are few, And fain as a bird to flee,

That time is as brief as a drop of dew- 

But you are Eternity."

At that time his wife and his sisters were not on friendly terms and he did not visit them at their homes. He was sick and nervous and I believe he would have died if he did not have someone to tell his troubles to and encourage him. He cried many

times and begged me to promise never to turn him down and always be true to him. 

In the meantime, the step-son had secured a position and he and his wife moved to an apartment. 

Wilbur had a terrible temper and one Sunday afternoon when I was at their house, he was angry about something and kicked the coal bucket over (they had a base burner then). His wife said to me: "His ugly temper will get him into trouble some day." (And it did.)

The next summer Chautauqua was held in a tent in the north part of town and one day my mother and I went. We met Mr. and Mrs. Gilmore there, ate supper together and had raisin pie. We stayed for the evening entertainment. We laughed at an old bachelor who sat with his mouth open and a silly grin on his face, when one of the performers sang: "You can't tell a thing about a woman." I would say the same applies to men. 

We came home on the street car and it was crowded. My mother and Wilbur's wife found seats and we stood in the back of the car and held hands all the way home. We often laughed about being two old fools.

CHAPTER FIVE 

That summer I went to Indianapolis to visit my sister. When I came home, he could not come to the house because my mother was at home, so we met the first night in the Catholic cemetery.

A few days afterwards he wrote me a letter and said: "I shall never forget the time and place that we first met, after you came home; it was one of the happiest evenings of my life. I hope that nothing will ever come between us, sweetheart-don't ever turn me down. I couldn't stand it." 

Several times he wrote: "No matter what comes or goes, we will always stick together. Be true to me, and you will never regret it."

That fall I met a widower, who took me to dances and helped me on committees in the lodge and would do anything for me. Mrs. Gilmore invited us to their house and we played cards and I had them here to spend the evening.

This man was a perfect gentleman until one evening he saw Mr. Gilmore walking home with me.

The next time this man came he acted entirely different, and wanted to love me a little. That ended our friendship.

We had gas stoves and a sitting room upstairs. 

It was warmer and the light better, so after supper my mother would take the paper and her books upstairs and read; and then I would bring Wilbur in the downstairs living room. He told me he and his

wife quarrelled a great deal. I told him to walk out of the room and not answer her if she started it. I also advised him to be good to her and give her everything he could. He told me he treated her better on my account than he would have done otherwise, and if it was not for me, he would leave her.

I felt that I was keeping them together and not taking anything from her, because she did not love him and had an easy life and everything a woman in her circumstances could have. She kept a woman roomer all the time, he said, because she wanted company all the time. Now I know it was to get spending money. I often wondered why she paid only a dollar or two at a time on a millinery bill and why she had a bill at all, instead of paying cash.

I never bought a hat unless I had the money to pay for it and I earned my own living. She had a husband supposed to provide for her. She often said to me: "You are better off than I am. You don't have to ask a man for money. Some day I am going to get a job." Then her husband would get mad and say she was talking foolishly.

Wilbur and I liked to be alone. He was a perfect lover. He wouldn't let me sew or do anything when he was here. We sat with our arms around each other. The course of true love ran smooth for awhile, excepting for the green-eyed monster. 

He wouldn't let me wear flower in my hair at a dance and said I did it to attract other men and 

I belonged to him; and, right or wrong, God must

be the judge, we were surely meant for each other. 

He asked me to give up all other men. 

One Saturday evening a school teacher of uncertain age, came after the dress I had made her and Wilbur was standing on the porch in a dark corner. I think she recognized him, as she only said: "There is a man on your porch." She told me she went to church just for the pleasure of looking at the preacher for he was such a handsome man.

Her sister, another maiden school teacher, admired a handsome man, a widower, and could have had him for a friend, if she had not been too bossy in the beginning. These ladies are now eligible for a pension. They have missed the thrills-the heartaches of love.

Is it better to have loved and lost, than not to have loved at all?

CHAPTER SIX 

In 1915 a friend of Wilbur's (an attorney), named Horatio Durand, wanted him to go on a trip to California. I did not want him to go, but finally consented. They stopped at Yellowstone 

National Park and he wrote me he was homesick and wished he were home and when he got back he "would never leave you again and would make up for lost time and nothing mattered anyway but love, love, love, and if we can't have each other, no one else shall have either of us. There is a way." 

Signed, "Yours forever."

By the time they arrived in Los Angeles, he was so grouchy and disagreeable, his companion could not understand the reason, because he thought he was glad to get away from home. Wilbur wrote me long, loving letters and said my letters were the only pleasure he had on the trip. He even consulted a fortune teller to see if I were true to him. 

She told him something was going to happen, and it did. On the way home they stopped in Denver. 

As they were ready to alight from the train, it gave a jerk and Wilbur was thrown off. He broke his leg. 

He was taken to his cousin's house and from there to a hospital where he stayed for some time. 

As soon as he could, he wrote to me. The day his friend arrived home, I received a letter telling me how he felt. He said I should go down to the

house that evening and hear about the accident from Horatio. 

In the letter he said: "My dear Sweetheart: I wish I had taken your advice and not gone on this trip. I was ready to turn back when we got to

Chicago, but was ashamed to say so. I might have known I would not enjoy the trip, going with whom 

I did. Write me a nice long letter as soon as you receive this and tell me everything, etc. Yours with all the love one can have for another. ways or yours forever, J. or Wilbur."

Most of his letters ended that way. 

Yours al I went to their house that evening and I already knew a great deal of what his friend told. When 

I went home Mr. Durand walked with me and we sat on the porch. When he started home he wanted to kiss me and asked me to go riding with him, but 

I refused. 

He said: "Don't you think I know how to behave?" 

I said: "You do, but you won't." (He had told me once that he did not think any women were decent except his sisters.)

When I wrote, I told Wilbur, and in answer he said: "So! He wanted to kiss you and take you riding. Now listen, sweetheart, when I come home and you should both happen to be at the house at the same time, don't let him walk home with you, no matter what happens, I can't bear it." 

On Labor Day he wrote: "I suppose you are at the Bills' picnic, having a good time and your Bill is lying in a hospital in Denver with a broken leg. 

Oh! if I had only listened to you, I wouldn't be

where I am now. I will always take your advice in the future."

There was a boy from here in Denver for his health at that time and he got this boy to mail letters to me. When Wilbur left the hospital he left a lock of my hair on the dresser. He was afraid his wife would go through his pockets and find it as he was laid up for several weeks after he arrived home. He brought me a pink chiffon scarf from Los Angeles but his wife would not let him give it to me.

As soon as he was able to get around on crutches he wrote me letters and walked to the post office and mailed them. He begged me to come down to the house as often as I could. I wrote back that I did not like to come, it wasn't pleasant, as I thought his wife suspected. 

He wrote: "I know it isn't pleasant for you to come to the house, dear; but, please come for my sake if you love me. The days and nights are so long when I don't see you, I can't stand it." 

Then, again, he would write: "I am afraid you will get tired waiting for me; it has been a long time since we have been alone, but be true to me, sweetheart, and you will never regret it. Just be true to yourself and you will be true to me. matter what comes or goes, we will always stick together."

He always told me not to fall for flattery. When he was left in the house alone he would call me on the phone and sometimes we would walk out Lafayette Street and around Etowa, he on crutches. 

At this time my mother discovered he was the

man who had been coming to see me and she forbade me to bring him in the house. As soon as he was able he came in a taxi to a corner near the house, then walked on his crutches the rest of the way and we sat on the porch, wrapped in blankets, as the weather was cold. When she went out in the evening, I stayed at home and he came to the house. 

One day, an old lady in the neighborhood talked her into letting me have him in the house. 

I told mother she couldn't say who the man was as she had never seen him, and I didn't say it was he, then. When she went to visit my sister in Indianapolis, she told her. She begged me to give him up, and said: "If he did this to his wife, he will do it to you," but I said, "No, this is different, he loves me."

He humiliated and insulted me. Why should I continue to be meek and mild and turn the other cheek? I had defended and protected him for years. Now why not, an eye for an eye? I cannot stand another Christmas alone. I cannot stay in the house. I keep listening for his call on the telephone. He has played an important part in my life for twenty years. He talked to me over the phone on an average of four or five times a week. 

He came to see me on an average of two or three times a week. He called me on the phone every Decoration Day for nineteen years. He came every election night for nineteen years.

He told me I was in a class by myself, no other woman like me and if I were his wife he would be rich. I made my own dresses and coats and often my hats, and upholstered the furniture. He thought that was wonderful.

If he could look in my house now, he would see what a little money and time could do. The woodwork in my room and the one adjoining, has been painted ivory. I had a double casement window cut in the east wall of the southeast room. Both rooms are furnished in lettuce green and gold with a touch of old rose. I had hard wood floors put into the living room and dining room and some of the antique furniture refinished. 

I tried to change the appearance to eliminate his presence, but I can't do it. I dream about him.

I awake in the morning with him on my mind. 

I only see him in the Dr. Jekyll role. 

Mr. Hyde was a nightmare.

Just recently a fashionable wedding took place in one of the churches of this town. The bride came from Chicago to be married in the same church that her parents had been married in twentyfour years ago. The bride wore the dress I had made her mother, white satin trimmed in real lace made princess with a train. Her sister (matron of

It was a pleasure to design and make gowns of this kind.

Money never entered my mind, while I was working. When I worked in the store, one of the girls was always looking forward to getting her envelope and I said to her one day: "Is money all you think about?" She said: "Yes, what do you work for?" 

I said: "Pleasure.' 

I never think of the money and never did when I had a dressmaking shop. It was only a necessity that I had to have.

Later when she found out that I had sued Wilbur, she said: "In my estimation, you are better off without him, money is all that counts anyway." I said: "I do not agree with you." 

A short time ago, Dr. Carl Sawyer resigned from the chairmanship of the Council of "100." 

Why? It does not take a clairvoyant to know the reason. He was instrumental in gathering together the original "32." A group of men he selected, whom he thought had enough interest and pride in their home town to exert themselves and try to make conditions better. Wilbur was one of the original "32."

I am speaking from my intimate knowledge of one man. He went to the meetings because he was a business man and for outward appearances. He

said: "The meetings were tiresome and a bore."

To expand the interest of the community, the original "32" were enlarged to a council of "100," but the same apathy has been apparent. No doubt, the majority are like Mr. Gilmore, thinking only of their own selfish pleasure.

He has left many a business meeting to spend an hour with me. He left a business meeting, pleading illness, to go and see Louise.
