As a student and teacher of history and political science in College and University, as a member of a State Legislature, as Assistant District Attorney, as a general practitioner in a profession which brings one into contact with economic, social and domestic problems, together with my contacts and interest in various business enterprises, both successful and otherwise, I have made an effort to study events in their order and have observed the numerous changes which have taken place.

I have gathered information relating to the past and the present for the purpose of determining the trend of affairs over a long period, and if possible to learn what steps are necessary to adjust ourselves to the various changes which have occurred, these coming slowly at first, then a little more speedily, and finally with such breath taking rapidity that we find ourselves in a world transformed, almost a new creation, within a period of little more than a single century.

What I may say in the pages that follow is not for the purpose of being sensational, nor to create the impression

that I am an extremist. The noise and confusion of rapid change does not appeal to me.

My fondest desire is to live quietly and peacefully, somewhat apart from the pressure and disturbance resulting from mass production and mass consumption. 

My aim is not merely to find fault for the purpose of being critical. Our social, political and economic machinery is not operating as smoothly as it should. On the other hand, if we make a comparison with conditions during the past, we must conclude that we have made progress and if we grasp the opportunities now within reach, a grand and glorious future lies ahead.

In spite of our progress however, the two major social diseases, poverty and war are still with us, so widespread, that they at this moment have assumed almost world wide. proportions.

I have learned that the human race has passed thru great crises and that oceans of blood have been shed because human beings, both as individuals and as groups, have persisted in their refusal to adjust themselves to changing conditions.

Great problems have arisen which demand solution. The failure to solve these problems has in many instances resulted in totalitarianism. Germany, Italy, and Russia are illustrations. 

This question presents itself. Can our problems be solved under our present system as it is now operating, or is it necessary to change or replace it, and if either is required,

how can it be brought about without violence and by a democratic method?

In my study of events and the various changes which have taken place, I have reached the following conclusions. 

1. That periods of prosperity during the past have come primarily as a result of investment and speculative inflation. 

2. That we have never during the past, and cannot in the future, set the wheels of industry turning by the employment of labor alone, but that prosperity must precede employment rather than follow it. 

3. That with our mechanized farms and industries, it is impossible to invest sufficient money under our present system to keep labor adequately employed. 

4. That attempts to furnish sufficient purchasing power by borrowing, taxation and speculative inflation, will result in a series of depressions, each, almost without exception, more severe than the one preceding it. 

5. That in order to solve our problems, purchasing power must be placed in the hands of the consumer independent of taxation and borrowing and in addition to wages. 

6. In many instances today, the result of investments in capital goods is less employment instead of more. 

7. That we cannot set the wheels of industry turning until sufficient purchasing power is put into circulation to purchase the commodities produced by our machines. 

8. That real security can be assured only when purchasing power is created by means other than speculative inflation. 

9. That modern wars are the result of the struggle for

markets and that wars will continue so long as we do not solve our economic problems. 

10. Before the power age, production was the primary factor and consumption secondary. Under our mechanized system consumption has become primary, and production secondary. 

It may be impossible to furnish sufficient facts in one small volume to prove the above conclusions to the satisfaction of a majority of readers. These few pages are written, however, with the hope that the statements and the discussions that follow will at least arouse serious thoughts and encourage more unbiased attempts to adjust ourselves to the numerous changes, which at this very moment are causing us so much anxiety concerning the welfare of this nation.

CHAPTER I THE DEPRESSIONS 

Herbert Hoover, now ex-President Hoover, in his speech of acceptance in 1928 said: "One of the oldest and perhaps the noblest of human aspirations has been the abolition of poverty. 

We in America today are nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before in the history of any land. The poorhouse is vanishing from among us. We have not yet reached the goal, but given a chance to go forward with the policies of the last eight years, we shall soon, with the help of God, be in sight of the day when poverty shall be banished from this nation."

Mr. Hoover expressed a conviction that was general among the American people. Little did he suspect that in less than two years after the delivery of an address which was designed to be prophetic, this nation should experience the most complete financial collapse in its history. In this crash security values dropped from 89.6 billion dollars on 

September 1, 1929 to 15.6 billions on July 1, 1932, a shrinkage of 74 billions, or 83 per cent, thus reducing purchasing power to that extent, in addition to the losses resulting from the decline in prices of commodities in general and real estate values in particular. 

Business conditions became so unsettled that at one time at least sixteen million people were out of employment. 

The depression that started on October 23, 1929 is still with us.

The problem of unemployment and insufficient purchasing power remains unsolved in spite of the various efforts to create jobs for those who have been unable to meet the requirements of a mechanized system. 

Since this nation in its early life adjusted itself to meet existing conditions through the adoption of a fundamental

law known as the United States Constitution, we have experienced eight major depressions, 1819, 1837, 1857, 1873, 1884, 1893, 1907, 1929, together with several periods of decline of lesser intensity between those dates. A few of these were not of lasting effect so far as business activities were concerned, but even the least severe left a trail of bank failures, business failures and bankruptcies, with the inevitable financial losses and consequent distress.

The following discussion of the several depressions is for the purpose of proving (1) that periods of prosperity preceding them were caused primarily by speculative inflation and not by the employment of labor, (2) that without speculation a prosperous condition would not have been possible, (3) that the employment of labor followed rather than preceded business activity, and (4) that periods of prosperity under our present system will inevitably be followed by periods of depression. 

A writer of Industrial History of the United States says of the panic of 1819: "Not the banks only, but business men of all classes had been mortgaging the future beyond warrant. Manufacturers, encouraged by the prospect of adequate protection, enlarged their plants and doubled their output. Land Companies invested borrowed money in property that could not be sold at a profit, and farmers mortaged their land for the wherewithal to make improvements. 

Large sums were sunk in canals and post roads that could not pay dividends on the investments, much less make good the obligations incurred. Confidence in the resources of the country and its ultimate prosperity led men to anticipate its development by a generation, and to risk too much upon the immediate future. 

Hundreds of business enterprises were prostrated, and thousands of apparently prosperous business men were ruined. The closing of factories threw workmen out of employment and the streets of 

Philadelphia, Baltimore, New York and Pittsburgh, and many lesser manufacturing and commercial centers, were thronged with destitute men seeking work. Prices fell and the value of real estate shrank to one third of the speculative period."

Illustrative of the increase in business ventures opening the way for speculation and inflation; in 1807 there were cotton mills with 8,000 spindles consuming 300,000 ΙΟ

pounds of yarn annually. In 1811, 80,000 spindles consumed 2,800,000 pounds. In 1815, 500,000 spindles employed 76,000 persons, with a payroll of $15,000,000 per year. The value of cotton products from 1810 to 1815 increased from $4,000,000 to $19,000,000. In 1800 there were 

500 bales of cotton used; 1805, 1,000 bales; 1815, 90,000 bales. As a result of this the cotton crops outran domestic demand and the price dropped from 24c per pound in 1800 to 16c in 1810.

In spite of losses there were frontiers to be explored, and it was not long until another period of investment, speculation and inflation began. This continued until the second depression which occurred in 1837. The cause of this, stated briefly, is as follows: "In every branch of industry the craze for investment had gone far beyond safe limits. Men did not hesitate to borrow at extravagant rates of interest, and to mortgage future earning power in proportion to their most ardent anticipations. The capital sunk in wheat farms and cotton plantations, in grist mills and cotton mills, in canals and railroads and steamship lines, could not yield immediate profit, and many a promising enterprise was swamped by irredeemable obligations. Impelled by buoyant confidence in its apparently inexhaustible resources, the men of the new frontier scoffed at financial limitations, and the bitter experience of inflation and collapse through which the eastern states had passed twenty years before was ignored."

The price of cotton rose from 6c per pound in 1830 to 114c in 1833, and 20c in 1835. The fever of speculation ran through the South. Twenty million acres were planted to cotton before 1840, and the financial resources of the country were seriously taxed. Cotton lands costing $5 per acre rose to $1,500 per acre. Sugar plantations in Louisiana, a speculative investment, was no less alluring and profitable. The price of slaves in the cotton belt increased in price from $200 in 1790 to $400 in 1840 and as high as $2,000 by 1860.

New territory was being settled and the population was increasing rapidly. 5,000 settlers north of the Ohio in 1790 increased to 3,000,000 in the next fifty years, and the popu- 

II

lation of the states south of the Ohio had multiplied 300 times in the same interval. In 1810 there were only sixteen towns that boasted of more than 3,000 population. In 1840 there were forty-two towns of more than 3,000 inhabitants. 

During this period the labor shortage was met by immigration. Between 1820 and 1830, 150,000 aliens came into this country and between 1830 and 1840 the number quadrupled. 

This added to the population, and with a greater population, consumption of commodities also increased. 

The third financial panic, 1857, like the first and second, was caused by undue speculation. "The extraordinary success of many business ventures tempted men to invest too heavily. The purchase and improvement of land in the new West, the opening of mineral resources, notably coal and iron in Pennsylvania; the building of ships, the construction of railroads, all required large investments of capital that could bring no immediate return commensurate with expenditures. There were 

4,932 failures in 1857 and 4,225 in 1858. The losses reached an unprecedented figure, $387,500,000, but that fell largely on bankers and investors. The rank and file of producers were little affected by the disaster and no prolonged depression of business followed." 

The panic of 1873, the fourth in number, exceeded in extent and severity any that the country had yet experienced. "Every line of business had felt the stimulus of war tariffs and high prices. In anticipation of unusual profits, entrepreneurs borrowed heavily and at high rates of interest to develop iron works and clothing factories, flour mills and abattoirs, mines and oil defineries, without much regard to prospects for disposing of the goods. Unable to market their stock at paying prices, many business firms failed to meet their obligations and went into bankruptcy. 

Another form of speculative mania was reached in Western railroads. The liberal policy of the Government gave promoters a basis on which to solicit subscriptions to stocks and bonds that could bring no return to the purchaser. The sums invested in railway construction during the decade following on the chartering of the Union Pacific aggregated more than a billion dollars. The railroads built between 1867 and 1873 amounted to 32,000 miles, a sum total exceeding the total mileage of 1859. An undue proportion of the available capital of the country was sunk in roadbed 12

and rolling stock. Unable to meet the interest on the bonded debt, much less provide for the payment of the principal, many of these optimistic transportation schemes were forced into bankruptcy."

More than 5,000 failures occurred in the panic year, with a loss of $228,500,000 and the number of bankruptcies steadily increased until in 1878 the appalling total of 10,478 was reached. 

The industrial depression following on the Wall Street panic was even more fatal to productive industries. The sum of the failures for the country at large aggregated 47,000, and the money lost $1,200,909,754, while some three million workmen were thrown out of employment by the closing down of business enterprises. The consequent curtailment in the demand for goods increased the difficulty of the situation."

The panic of 1884 originated in Wall Street in the failure of four large banking firms. This crisis was occasioned largely by over investment in railroads. The mileage built in 1882 and 1883 (18,314 miles) exceeded the construction of 1870 and 1871 by 5,000 miles. In 1884-1885 eighty-one railway corporations holding 19,000 miles of track were placed under receiverships and 37 smaller railroad properties were sold under foreclosure. 

In 1893 the business world was convulsed by a panic of unprecedented severity. Advancing prices had induced speculative investments, and the banking facilities of New York City had become heavily involved. 

The failure of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad, February 20, 1893, caused widespread alarm. The business failures for 1893 numbered 15,242 with total losses amounting to $346,779,899 and the depression extended to every branch of industry. Wheat sold at 50c per bushel and the whole country was prostrated. 

More than 200 Railway Companies, representing 56,000 miles of track and one-fourth of the railway capital of the country, equal to about 21½ billions, went into the hands of receivers between 1892 and 1896.

The proportion of business failures after the panic of 1907 was not so high as after the crisis of 1893, but were up about 50%. Bank holidays were declared in several 13

states. It was largely a rich man's panic confined to the stock market and credit operations. A drop in security values, however, always means reduced purchasing power and is more far reaching than appears on the surface. 

Prior to the outbreak of the World War in 1914 business conditions were such that another panic seemed inevitable. 

The outbreak of the war created a demand for commodities, and as a result of this demand business conditions improved. The ultimate result of war activities was inflation and speculation resulting in the crash of 1929 more devastating in its effect than any that preceded it. Conditions prior to the collapse of 1929 were similar to those that preceded other panics, but speculation, investment and inflation were on a much larger scale. 

Prior to the panic of 1929 there was investment, speculation and inflation in practically every line, but the coming of the automobile opened a new field for investment such as this nation had never experienced. With its development there came into being demands for other products almost without number. Added to this, the demand for war material, while the war was in progress, and for material to rebuild Europe after peace was restored, opened the way for speculation, new investment, and the enlargement of production facilities to such an extent that when the pinnacle was reached the resulting collapse was much more disastrous than any of the other prior business depressions.

There never was a time when inflation became so rampant as during the period prior to 1929 when increased purchasing power was created by the issuing of securities in the form of stocks and bonds followed by increase in prices. In many instances common stock was issued which had absolutely no value at the time of issue, but which was later given a fictitious value by a gullible public willing to buy anything that promised a return in the form of a high rate of interest or dividends, or the possibility of purchasing and selling on an advancing market, failing to realize that a collapse at some time in the future was inevitable. 14

The organization of the United States Steel Corporation is a good example of the extent to which stock could be issued and sold without having sufficient assets to support it at the inflated value. The amount of stock over and above the actual property values, issued by the United States Steel Corporation, was at least $300,000,000. (Some writers place the amount at more than $400,000,000.) This stock, started with no value, was pushed up from 8% in 1907 to 26134 in 1929 and then declined until it reached 214 in 1932. Largely as a result of the present war this stock has again advanced to 58 (Jan., 1940).

The practice of organizing companies issuing stock and 15

selling regardless of value was not limited to the large concerns. The practice became so general that almost everyone who possessed a spark of organizing ability was out to promote his company and reap a harvest. In one instance of which I have personal knowledge, a promoter organized a coal company and capitalized it at $600,000, half common and half preferred stock. He employed one of the ablest lawyers to assist him. All of the stock was turned over to the owner of certain leases, which, as it developed later, had practically no value. $180,000 of the preferred stock and one share less than half of the common stock was turned over to the attorney as trustee to be sold for the benefit of the company as treasury stock. $120,000 of the preferred stock, and one share more than half of the common stock, was retained by the promoters. This $120,000 of preferred stock they sold to the unsuspecting public at par for their own benefit. Of the $180,000 sold, 35% was collected by the promoters as a commission. Common stock held by the trustee was given as a bonus. 

The by-laws of this company provided that the common stock should vote so long as the dividends on preferred stock were paid out of earnings. These promoters, acting as though they were not satisfied until the Company should be wrecked completely, used the common stock held by them to authorize a bond issue of $100,000, after it was made known to them, by the person in charge of the records, that the dividends on preferred stock were paid out of the proceeds of the sale of stock, rather than out of earnings. 

More than half of the bond issue was appropriated by the promoters to pay what apparently were spurious notes. 

Needless to say that this company was wrecked from the beginning and the money paid by innocent investors was a total loss.

It is evident from the above statement of facts relative to investment, speculation and inflation preceding the several depressions, that the periods of activity prior to the depressions did not occur because men were employed, but because there were opportunities for investment, and the 16

investments were furnished by individuals interested in business projects, by loans from banks, or by the sale of stocks and bonds. The investments having been made the demand for labor followed.

To review briefly the causes of the different periods of activity preceding the several depressions; some of the projects to be developed thereby creating a demand for investments were: canals, roads, wheat farms, cotton farms, abattoirs, grist mills, slaves, cotton factories, flour mills, wool factories, steam boats, railroads, iron and steel factories, coal and iron mines, oil wells, refineries, California gold mines, real estate booms, building of towns and cities, telegraph, telephone, machinery both for farm and factory, automobiles, hard surface highways, road houses, gasoline stations, radios, aeroplanes and numerous other projects incident to those already mentioned. With the opening of new frontiers came the rise in security values as well as the rise in prices of commodities and following these the increase in wages.

I do not intend to convey the idea that employment and the paying of wages did not assist in creating conditions, which on the surface indicated improvement, as the paying of wages helped to keep money in circulation. My contention is, however, that the real activity was started not by the employment of labor and the payment of wages, but by the creation of purchasing power by those who made investments. Inflation began in the form of increased prices. 

These prices continued to advance until they reached the peak. Not being able to go any higher, a collapse was inevitable.

Accompanying the numerous depressions and continued fluctuations, which have resulted in serious losses and business failures, have been many changes, which have had a most profound and far reaching effect economically, socially, and politically and which we hope will eventually be for the best interest of the nation as a whole. CHAPTER II CHANGE FROM MODERATE HAND PRODUCTION TO MACHINE OR MASS PRODUCTION 

THE development of machines in industry enables us to produce commodities in huge quantities. Some insist that the machine has been the outstanding factor in causing adverse conditions, such as we have experienced during the last depression. Others insist with equal emphasis that the coming of the machine has opened the way for more employment and consequently the machine is not the chief disturbing element.

When the panic of 1819 appeared, the machine was in its infancy, in fact, there were very few machines. Houses were built by hand with simple tools such as the saw, plane and hatchet. Horse shoes, and even horse shoe nails, were hand made. Cigars were made by hand. Plows, harrows, cultivators, scythes, grain cradles, blacksmith tools, wagons, forks, hoes, rakes, spades, shovels, picks, mattocks, furniture, guns, cooking utensils, spinning wheels, cotton gins, clothing, shoes, hats, and numerous other articles were made by the use of very simple tools.

Equipment for cleaning, spinning and weaving cotton and wool had been developed, but the industry using this equipment was still in its infancy. Railroads were as yet unknown. There was no telegraph or telephone, no sewing machines, no reapers, no binders, no tractors, and practically no iron and steel manufacturing equipment. 

By 1837, the date of the second depression, there was some machinery, but not sufficient to become a disturbing factor. The building of railroads had merely started. Cyrus 

McCormick had in 1831 operated the first horse-drawn machine to cut grain, but it was not until 1842 that seven reapers were put on the market. 19

By 1857, the date of the third depression, the development of machinery had made some progress. Elias Howe had invented the sewing machine. The output of sewing machines in 1853 was 2,266. Six years later the output was 

42,539 and the savings in wages by the installation of the sewing machines reduced the cost to one-fourth that of hand-made articles. 

Cyrus McCormick, who operated the first horse-drawn machine to cut grain in 1831 sold seven in 1842 and 29 machines in 1843. In 1844 he sold 50 and in 1847 he built the first factory and began producing them on a large scale. 

It was not, however, until years later that the real spurt came in the installation of machines, followed by the displacement of those who had produced the various commodities by hand. These machines were called labor-saving devices, but in many instances, we have found that they became labor-displacing devices. 

My own experience on the farm, which could not have been prior to the year 1885, may furnish some information on the development of farm equipment. I can remember well the scythe and grain cradle, both of which were being displaced by the mower and reaper. The flail for threshing grain was used only for threshing rye, so that the straw could be used for binding corn fodder. Threshing machines were primitive compared with the grain thresher and separator of a later date, and with the modern combine. 

Harvest time, considering the number employed, was almost like a picnic, except that there was plenty of exceedingly hard work to be done by those brawny workmen and women, who knew nothing else than hard labor from sunrise until sunset and often even by moonlight. 

Manufacturers of farm equipment now advertise that three men with modern combination harvesters can in one day harvest forty acres of grain, and at the same time thresh it and prepare it for market. Fifty years ago, with the reaper and threshing outfit then in use, possibly 100 men could not have harvested, threshed and prepared for arket as much as can now be done by three men with 20

modern equipment. Only a few years before the last half century when grain was harvested with the grain cradle and threshed with the flail, 300 men could not have harvested and threshed as much grain per day as is now harvested, threshed and prepared for market by the combine employing three men.

Since that time there have appeared on the farm many other machines: side delivery rakes; hay loaders; hay tedders; grain drills; spring harrows; disc harrows; double row corn planters; two to four row potato planters; potato diggers; manure spreaders; hay forks; ensilage cutters; fertilizer distributors; pulverizers; stone pickers; tobacco planters; 12 row seeders; 20 row sprayers; corn shellers with a capacity of as much as 2,000 bushels per day; corn harvesters; corn pickers and huskers; tractors capable of drawing from two to sixteen plows, the largest tractor plowing as much as an acre in five minutes; sugar cane harvesters doing as much work as 200 men; and cotton pickers removing as much raw cotton from the stalk in 72 hours as one man can pick in 11 weeks.

With the development of machinery has also come the increase in quantity. Not more than fifty years ago one or two acres of potatoes was the average for most farms, and a crop of 50 to 75 bushels per acre was considered a fair crop. Today there are a large number of farmers who have an annual acreage of 50, 100, 200, 300, 500, and in some instances as many as 1,000 to 1,500 acres with an average of 250 to 300 bushels per acre, and a maximum yield of 

600 or more bushels per acre.

Some time ago I had occasion to visit a poultry plant which was said to have cost $1,000,000 and was informed that the owner intended to enlarge his flock to half a million hens.

There are dairy companies that milk as many as 1,500 cows on one farm.

Oram Brubaker of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, in 1893 raised for market 25 ducks. Ten years later the quantity had increased to 2,500 ducks. Thirty-five years later 21

his sons marketed 125,000 ducks per year.

A single hatchery advertises 200,000 baby chicks hatched each week. 3,000,000 in a single season.

One farmer in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, has 75 acres planted in asparagus. Another farmer in Cumberland 

County, Pennsylvania, plants 40 acres in celery.

In Bucks County, Pennsylvania, on the original Penn manor, the King farm under the supervision of Karl C. 

King and his partner, Alvan C. Thompson, consists of 6,500 acres. 2,400 of these acres are used for the growing of vegetables.

In 1939, thirteen hundred (1,300) acres were planted in spinach, one thousand acres in green snap beans, usually called "string beans." Broccoli is third in number of acres planted. Other vegetables are asparagus, beets, cabbage, carrots, cucumbers, lima beans, parsnips, rhubarb and turnips. 

Some of the equipment used consists of 29 large field type tractors and 8 small ones, a 12 row tractorized seed drill together with a fleet of 60 motor trucks to handle its fresh vegetables.

These farms serve such centers of population as New 

York, Philadelphia, Boston, Buffalo, N. Y., Baltimore, Md., Richmond, Va., Pittsburgh and intervening cities.

Not so many years ago 50 bushels of corn per acre was considered a good yield. In the corn belt the amount has increased to an average of 100 bushels with some yields as high as 150 bushels per acre and the record held by Clark Dellinger of Clark County, Indiana, with an average yield on 5 acres of 180.1 bushels per acre.

What is true on the farm is true of the industries as well. 

The production of electric household appliances almost doubled from 1919 to 1927. Passenger automobiles increased 1919 to 1928 from 6,771,000 to 21,630,000. From 

1913 to 1928 homes with stationary bath tubs increased from 7,000,000 to 17,000,000; residence telephones from 

2,800,000 to 8,300,000.

In 1861 one McKay invented a machine for sewing soles 22

on shoes.

Workmen with this machine could sew soles on 900 pairs of shoes in a 10-hour day. By using this machine the labor cost was reduced to one-eleventh of the cost of the hand sewn article. In 1931 the shoe industry had the capacity to produce 900,000,000 pairs of shoes, but could sell only 300,000,000.

As early as 1921 there were excess plant facilities of 70% in the steel industry; in 1916 it was 100% in the copper industry; in 1912 the excess lumber mill facilities were over 

300%; and in the rubber tire industry 1200%.

At the World's Fair in New York, 1939, one set of machines owned by the Firestone Tire Company produced a tire complete, going through the whole process from raw material to finished product, ready for market, in four minutes.

During the last few years the output of the cigarette industry has been increased from 600 per minute per machine to more than 2,000 per minute per machine. In July of 

1937 there were 15,290,072,227 cigarettes sold. Three men and a machine can produce about one million (1,000,000) cigarettes per day.

In the cigar industry, by the use of machinery, 30 employees can now produce as many or more cigars than were produced by 100 workmen before the installation of the machines. Two girls with a banding cellophaning machine can do as much work as fourteen girls by hand. Stripping tobacco is also done by machinery with even a larger reduction in the number of employees than in the manufacture of the cigars.

In the steel industry by the installation of the strip steel machines, fifteen men can now do the work of one hundred. 

Before the machines were introduced an apprenticeship of seven years was necessary. By using a machine only a three months' apprenticeship is required.

The following is a recent report on the effect of machinery in the steel industry: "The technological displacement of workers has been especially severe in the steel industry. During the 1930's some 25,000 skilled 23

and semi-skilled hand mill workers were permanently displaced by modern electrically driven continuous strip mills, and an additional 60,000 are on the verge of being displaced. As a result of this displacement entire communities have been turned into ghost towns. 

In one such town, not far from Pittsburgh, where a large steel corporation once employed 5,700 steel workers, not a single one is now employed. Fifty-two per cent of the 50,000 people in this community are dependent upon Government aid for subsistence, while another 12 per cent are trying to get aid." 

In the rayon industry equipment has been installed recently whereby the raw material can be put through processes in six minutes that formerly required ninety hours. 

In the printing industry the linotype machine has displaced the type setter.

In 1901 one man could print 1,000 letterheads per hour. 

In 1929 one man, by the use of improved machinery, could print 20,000 letterheads per hour.

In many industries the electric eye has been installed. 

By the use of this electrical equipment unlabeled cans are picked out; heated billets are passed on to the forger; imperfections in cloth are detected. The electric eye now supplants the human eye in the manufacture of bessemer steel, and in photo engraving, a three color plate can be produced in one-half hour instead of thirty-six hours, which were required before. These are only a small percentage of the uses of the electric eye.

In the sewing industry the stitching has been stepped up during the past few years, from 1,300 stitches per minute to 3,200 stitches per minute. The speed of the machines has been increased generally and many of the operations, such as making buttonholes and sewing on buttons, are practically automatic. Cloth is no longer cut in single pieces, but is laid layer upon layer and piles a few feet in thickness can be cut as easily and more quickly and accurately than the single piece when the cutting was done by hand. 

By the Ashton process for puddling, an 8,000 pound wrought iron sponge ball can be produced every five minutes. A puddler and his helper produced a 600 pound ball 24

in hours. A mechanized plant with eighty men produces as much as 1,000 men by hand puddling.

When canals need digging or huge quantities of earth must be moved for other purposes, a giant power shovel shifts 30,000 cubic yards of earth in 24 hours. Fifteen thousand laborers would take a 10-hour day to do it. The 

Tecumseh Coal Company in its stripping operation near 

Boonville, Indiana, is using a Marion "knee action" dipper with a capacity of 35 cubic yards. One dip of this shovel can move about as much ground as can be hauled on seven 5-ton trucks.

Not so many years ago practically all excavating was done by hand. Today the steam shovel does the work and men have become spectators. 

Each bucket on the world's largest dredge lifts 18 cubic feet and can dig 13,000 cubic yards daily. It can go 112 feet below water level and weighs 3,500 tons. 

In the construction of the Grand Coulee Dam, where from 15 to 20 or more thousand cubic yards of concrete are poured in 24 hours, all of the work in digging the gravel, mixing the concrete and pouring it, is done by less than a score of men. Thousands of men would be required to do this work by hand.

The new Greenwick Point coal pier in Southern Philadelphia has electrically equipped unloaders which pick up a car bodily and funnel its contents into a ship's hold. The handling capacity of this equipment is one hundred thousand (100,000) tons of coal in 24 hours. (More than 2,000 car loads.)

A machine built to exert a pressure of 3,000,000 pounds is at the same time so delicate that it can crack a watch crystal without damaging the watch. A similar machine but not quite so exact in its operation is capable of pressure to the extent of 10,000,000 pounds.

Even within the last few years one saw along the highway hundreds of men digging trenches for pipe lines, sewers, and underground telephone and telegraph lines. Today the trench digger, using not more than two men, has taken the 25

place of the hundreds of men employed previously. The trench digger either deposits the ground on one side of the trench or loads it into trucks. This ground is not even replaced by hand. A mechanical device pushes the ground back into the trench after which a mechanical tamping machine finishes the job many times more quickly than it could be done by hand. 

Machines also invade the office. The young lady clerk armed with modern implements deals with an increased number of ledger entries. Clerks are displaced and managers too, for machines make fewer mistakes, require scant supervision, need no holidays and never suffer from headaches and influenza. 

Large groups of houses were built about canning factories to accommodate the employees, who were brought out from the cities during the canning season. Today those buildings are torn down or have become ghost towns. So much work is done by machinery that very few human beings are required.

Working in a canning factory was quite a help to students during vacation period, and it fell to my lot to do some of this sort of work. At that time I used a capping iron and could close about 4 cans per minute. Today the automatic capping machine can seal at least 275 cans per minute.

The installation of machinery has done the same for other parts of the canning process as was done by the capping machine. Automatic corn huskers, corn cutters, pea threshers and separators, and various other machines, enable the person engaged in the canning business to prepare his product for market with very little human effort, in comparison with the amount used not more than 25 years ago.

In silk mills one employee had charge of one loom. Today one employee can operate as many as sixteen looms. 

In the past a brick maker working with simple tools could in an 8-hour day produce 450 bricks. 320,000 per day is the output of a modern machine. 

In automobile factories in 1904, 1,291 man hours were 26

required to build an automobile. In 1919, 1,600,000 vehicles were built at 313 man hours per vehicle. In 1929, 5,600,000 automobiles were built at 92 man hours per vehicle, and the number of hours per vehicle has probably decreased considerably since that time. A report on the capacity of the 

Chrysler Motor Company in January, 1940, was that this company was producing 5,500 automobiles and trucks per day. Henry Ford has stated that his company has produced as many as 10,000 motor cars per day. General Motors Corporation can probably produce as many or more. 

In one of the large paper factories a machine produces paper 242 inches (20 1-6 feet) wide, 1,200 feet per minute.

A picture of this machine in operation shows one man in charge.

The factory for producing electric light bulbs at Corning, New York, can produce 650,000 bulbs per machine per day, an increase per man of 10,000 times by methods previously employed. 

One of the Berkshire Mills, Reading, Pennsylvania, can produce 444,000 pairs of full fashioned hose per day. 

The modern power plant practically dispenses with human labor. Steel scoops descend to the barges on the river side, close down on a ton of coal, and lift it to an elevated track where conveyors deliver it to the many grates which feed the boilers. Clinkers fall on belts and pass to water troughs and thence to waiting trucks. Coal at one end, clinkers at the other, and in between heat extracted, steam raised, turbines driven and power sent pulsing down the countryside, greater from a single installation than all the power available in England at any one time during the reign of Queen Elizabeth. 

One man controls the crane and the scoop, two men regulate the flow, the pressure and temperature. Two men attend the turbines.

The use of machinery has even been applied to the cracking of nuts. A machine of this type has been developed which cuts holes through the shell, injects gas, and then by passing the nut over a flame explodes the shells. By the 27

use of this machine 90 pounds of nuts can be shelled per hour with the result that half of the 1,400 girls now assorting nuts may be displaced.

The teletype setter has made it possible that a magazine printed in Chicago can have last minute news typed out in New York City, and in doing that the type is automatically set in Chicago.

The teletype used in telegraph offices has practically dismissed the trained telegraphers. 

Twenty-five years ago 20 men working on turning lathes produced about 500 finished pieces. At present a machine operated by two men can turn out 7,000 pieces. 

The bituminous coal output by mechanical means was five times greater in 1928 than in 1923. 

Coal Age recently (1940) in a discussion of Bituminous 

Mechanization says, "With mobile loader installations up 21.2 per cent and conveyor sales at a new high, bituminous mechanization progress was country-wide in 1939." 

It is well to bear this in mind, that the installation of labor displacing devices does not always occur on a large scale at a particular time. Many minor improvements are added frequently which appear unimportant but which gradually increase the output with a constantly diminishing amount of human effort. For example, in a woodworking plant an attachment is installed so that 4 holes are bored at one operation instead of one as was done before. 

A workman in an insulator plant insulated 1,000 feet of wire in a 10-hour day eight years ago. Today he is able to insulate from 3,000 to 3,500 feet in eight hours. 

In a factory producing refrigerating machinery 2½ hours were required to make a compressor wheel. By equipment recently installed the same wheel can now be made in 29 minutes. In the same factory certain pieces which required the work of several men several hours are now made by one man on a turret lathe at the rate of 8 per hour. 

In a tool factory one man doing a certain kind of work could bore 7 holes in one day. This factory has installed a machine that can bore 1 hole in fifteen minutes or 28

per day, one machine thereby displacing about 3½ men. 

A tobacco company has installed automatic feed scrap machines. Four machines required the labor of four men to feed them. One man now oversees the whole operation. 

Three men are displaced. 

In a local chain factory five years ago there was one man to a machine. Now one man operates four machines and these machines are run at a higher rate of speed, an 8 inch pulley having replaced a 10 inch pulley. 

Additions such as these are perpetual, a little here and a little there, scarcely noticeable but of vast importance when we consider the increase in quantity produced and with a diminishing supply of human labor. 

These are only a few illustrations of the installations of machinery. Go into any factory or on the farm and what do we see?

There is scarcely an operation that has not been mechanized to the exclusion of human beings, and according to present indications, mechanization will continue until the means of production will be as nearly automatic as it is possible for the human mind to make it, unless we should fail in our efforts to distribute the product and thereby cause the system eventually to commit suicide.

From what has been written about mechanical improvements, the reader may conclude that I am opposed to the use of machinery.

I see no reason why we shall not continue to improve machinery, so that the bulk of the work commonly known as drudgery should be performed by mechanical means. 

Why should the individual be compelled to labor 162 hours out of every 24 to produce the bare necessities of life as he was obliged to do prior to the coming of the machine? 

Is the human being so incapable of self control that he must be practically enslaved for 16½ hours out of every 24 in order that he should be properly disciplined and kept out of mischief?

Is it not likely that he will be a better citizen if he is compelled to work only a few hours per day for necessaries and 29

will then be at liberty to produce luxuries if he desires to have them, or to pay more attention to the proper care of his own body and the development of his mind, so that he can function more harmoniously as a member of the group, and be a better citizen generally?

Our problem is not to get rid of machinery but to use it properly and learn to distribute the product. 30

CHAPTER III THE TREND TOWARD SPECIALIZATION 

As late as a century ago practically all work was done by hand or by the use of simple tools. The man who was skilled as a carpenter actually built houses. Today he merely puts together the parts that have been made in different factories. 

One factory produces the doors, one factory the door frames. Another factory produces the windows. One mill produces the joists, another the weather boarding. The frame is not held together by wooden pegs as formerly, but by nails and spikes. And so throughout the building, pieces from different factories, each piece created by a different person but none creating the finished product. 

A workman who has been a carpenter for a number of years has informed me that as much can be erected by a carpenter in three weeks, as was done in three months not so many years ago.

The factory builds the parts and human labor merely places them in position.

The shoemaker formerly made shoes. Today the workman in the factory cuts soles, sews on soles, punches holes for eyelets or does some other small part before it is passed to the next workman who is then called upon to contribute his bit, but at no time do any of the workmen produce a shoe.

How many suits of clothing are made by tailors compared with the number put together in factories piece by piece by a large number of workmen, each doing a part but none producing the article complete?

Formerly the wagonmaker built a wagon. Today he probably slips a wheel on the axle, inserts a bolt or even screws on a nut and then passes it on to the next specialist. 31

In modern automobile factories there are miles of conveyors carrying the partly assembled auto past a workman who attaches his special part. The framework then moves on to the next man, who adds another part and so from the beginning to the end of the day, one auto after another passes one workman after another, until they reach the end of their journey complete and ready for market, produced not by any workman in particular but by a group of specialists, each contributing his bit independent of the other. 

Very few housewives bake bread. It is produced by the specialist baker. Canned goods by the specialist canner, meats by the specialist butcher and potatoes by the specialist potato farmer. 

So long as a family could produce the greater part of what they consumed they were independent. Today with our methods of producing in parts for sale, we have lost our independence and are now really dependent one upon the other, even though we may desire to have it otherwise. 

The owner of a factory may think that he is independent, but even he is not. He is dependent upon those who consume his merchandise, and if they cannot consume, the producer, whether he be factory owner or farmer, becomes almost entirely helpless.

What would be the value of Henry Ford's plant if there were no users of Fords to consume the product? And so in every line, producer dependent upon consumer and consumer dependent upon producer. Thus it continues from day to day. The welfare of one dependent upon the welfare of the other, and the misfortune of one resulting in the misfortune and often intense suffering of the other. 32

CHAPTER IV CHANGE FROM THE TRANSFER OF COMMODITIES AND SERVICES FROM PRODUCER TO CONSUMER BY PURCHASE AND SALE RATHER THAN BY BARTER 

I can recall when most of our transactions on the farm were practically without the use of a medium of exchange. 

Farm laborers lived in tenant houses. They were paid a small wage but were furnished with a piece of land on which they could raise their vegetables. Their milk came from the farm cows or they kept one or more cows of their own. 

Meat came from the farm livestock. Chickens and eggs from the farm poultry. Fuel came from the woods, which was a part of the farm. The farm horses furnished the means of transportation. The housewife did the baking with flour from the farm. She also did practically all of the sewing and knitting, and not so many generations ago spun the yarn for stockings, mittens and even for homespun clothing. With all of these commodities and services provided there was not much to buy and therefore money was not so important.

A basket of eggs and butter taken to the country store was exchanged for merchandise not produced on the farm. Even the hide from the animal slaughtered for beef was taken to the local merchant and traded for what was commonly known as store goods.

Farmers worked on the roads to pay their road taxes. 

Farmers also bartered labor in planting and harvesting their crops.

Local stills often changed the grain into a less bulky product, which was then taken to the larger cities, the team 33

transporting the liquor bringing back a load of commodities to be used in the home of the distiller. 

Chickens, potatoes, wheat and practically all other farm products were turned over to the local merchant, in exchange for manufactured goods to be used on the farm. 

The local merchant took the produce to the city and brought back a load of manufactured goods to be exchanged for the farm products of his rural customers. 

The motor vehicle has been responsible to a certain extent for this change in the practice of trading goods for goods. 

Trucks now scour the country buying produce and paying cash for it. This requires the use of money. Even on the farm very little baking is done. Often the milk is sold and butter is purchased. Farm machinery now does the work formerly performed by laborers and the person, who formerly was employed on the farm, now works in the factory or is on relief, and in either event receives his income in the form of cash or a pay check.

Foreign trade was really an exchange of products, manufactured goods for raw material. 

Today all mechanized countries are trying to sell, not trade, and it simply cannot be done. Two countries producing merchandise of the same kind cannot trade as each has sufficient of its own.

If we do not buy nitrates from Chili because we produce them synthetically, that country is much less likely to buy from us.

If we discontinue the purchase of raw silk from Japan because it is displaced by Nylon, Japan will do her utmost to purchase from those who are active customers for her goods. 

When nations are not in position to trade, foreign business must be done on a cash basis and frequently it is difficult to raise the cash.

During the period of small industries most of the product was consumed in the area where it was produced, and there were many opportunities other than on the farm, for the exchange of not only goods for goods but also labor for commodities. 34

The workman who assisted the tailor exchanged labor for a suit of clothes; the employee in the small store exchanged his labor for groceries, notions or whatever merchandise was kept in stock.

Today clerks and assistants are paid in cash and the selfserving stores employ practically no labor, consequently there is no possibility of an exchange of labor for commodities as there was during the past. 

With barter replaced by purchase and sale, the medium of exchange has become more important, and when there is not sufficient of this medium, or it does not circulate, the consumer is without the means to secure that which he is duty bound to consume, if he is to do his part in disposing of the goods produced by the machine. 35

CHAPTER V CONCENTRATION OF INDUSTRY TREND TOWARD MONOPOLY 

Not more than forty years ago in a community with a population of approximately 5,000 people there were at least 

100 cigar factories. Today in that same community there are four machine factories that appear to be holding their own, one struggling machine factory and about a half dozen hand factories which probably will not survive if a wagehour law is enforced.

Automobile factories have dwindled until the bulk of the automobiles are produced by three companies: Ford, Chrys ler and General Motors. 

The greater part of the nation's steel is produced by the 

Bethlehem and United States Steel Companies. 

In the oil business, the flour milling business, the shoe business, the cigarette business, the textile business, the hosiery business, and in almost every other line of importance, the big concern has steadily been growing bigger and the small producer smaller or eliminated entirely. 

Not only have business concerns grown larger in certain lines but they have also taken on different lines. Dupont originally manufactured powder. Now they are engaged in producing an almost endless number of commodities: rayon, cellophane, nylon, lucite, fabrikoid, neoprene, paints, varnishes, dyes, solvents, and numerous other products, plastics in particular. This company also controls General 

Motors, one of the largest producers of automobiles and refrigerators. Also the Remington and Peters Arms Companies.

There are only a few manufacturers of farm machines instead of a much larger number not many years ago. The 

International Harvester Company is producing a large percentage of machinery used on the farm. 

Cigarettes are produced by a few large companies. 

The dairy business, especially in the large cities, is controlled by giant organizations. 

The chain store and self-serving plan have greatly reduced the number of retail companies. 

What has been the result of this merger and consolidation? Small towns usually grew up around small or moderate sized industrial plants. Consolidation meant that the factory was moved from the small town to some centralized point. Many of these towns have become ghost towns. 

Former employees have invested their lifetime savings in real estate, usually a dwelling house. They can not afford to leave it. They are often obliged to travel many miles to seek employment. This means an automobile with all of the expense incident to its upkeep. With a dwelling house which has depreciated in value, and an automobile for the purpose of commuting, one may actually be poverty stricken, while he apparently owns considerable property, especially a dwelling house and an automobile. 

Another effect of this concentration is that large companies are in position to name the price to the consumer. 

Being in position to do so, they can limit production in order to keep up the price. They do not plow their product under but they are careful to arrange production so that there will not be a surplus to force the price down. Instead of killing off the little machines after they are born, they practice birth control and prevent conception entirely. Those same persons might condemn some one else for attempting to destroy enough to cause a scarcity in order to keep the price up. 38

The whole procedure of killing off is ridiculous, of course, but is it much more so to kill the little pigs that happen to have been born, than to take active steps to keep them from being born at all, or to produce a condition of scarcity in order to prevent a drop in price. 

We may orate about competition and free enterprise but among the major industries real competition is practically a thing of the past.

Efforts to prevent concentration seem to be useless as the natural trend under a competitive system is toward merger and consolidation of industries.

There is no doubt that a larger industry highly mechanized can operate with less overhead expense than the small industry. The margin of profit may be small on each unit, but there are so many articles produced that the profit in the aggregate is large for the small number who own the business.

Advertising is one of the important factors in the sale of merchandise. The large concern can advertise. The small industry cannot.

Financing the large concern is easier than for the small business man. Large corporations usually can sell securities at will.

The large concern can borrow from banks to a much better advantage. The Philadelphia Inquirer of January 13, 1940 reported that Reynolds Tobacco Company borrowed $20,- 

000,000 at 1.75%. I have personal knowledge of $100,000 borrowed by one concern at 34 of 1% on condition that the loan should not be paid within one year. Another company recently borrowed $1,500,000 at 12 of 1%. The small concern borrowing from a local bank is obliged to pay 5½ or 6%.

Whether for good or ill, the passing of our industries into the hands of the few has resulted in a profound change which must be reckoned with in our efforts to create an economic system that will provide adequately for all, whether employed or unemployed. 39

CHAPTER VI IMPROVED MEANS OF TRANSPORTATION 

The last century has witnessed a change from the horse and buggy and even the ox team to modern means of transportation by railroad, by automobile and by airplane. 

This change has caused us to become citizens of a much larger community. A trip to the city, a distance of twentyfive miles by horse and buggy, was as much of an event fifty years ago as a trip from New York to San Francisco by airplane today.

Products are moved swiftly from section to section, from zone to zone, from East to West and from North to South, and vice versa. We can move our products hundreds of miles and have them brought back in order to sell them in the community in which they were manufactured, or elsewhere.

Recently there was related to me an instance of a manufacturer of dresses, who cuts his material by machinery, bales it, and ships it to the various islands outside of the United States to have the sewing done. The cost of transportation both ways, together with the cost of labor, is 10c per dress compared with labor cost of 40c at home. 

This is not doing much to increase the purchasing power of the American publie which in last analysis must buy the dresses. 

Merchandise can be transported so easily by truck that large manufacturers reach out and distribute their products to points hundreds of miles from their factories. 

In one instance, in a town of about 5,000 population, 29 producers of baked goods are distributing their products to the residents of this borough. 

Only one local baker has survived and this one must exert himself to the utmost to meet foreign competition. 40

Commuting by train or auto has resulted in suburban development. 

Tourists move about freely.

Improved means of transportation by auto have created a demand for improved highways, for hotels, gasoline stations, tourist camps, lumber, cement, steel and the various other commodities necessary for construction purposes. 

With the improved means of transportation and travel came centralization of authority to a much greater degree. 

Highway construction and maintenance had to be placed under one head, in order to promote efficiency as well as safe and comfortable travel.

With the coming of the railroad and the automobile also came more government regulation. License, highway patrol, state laws, city and borough ordinances regulating traffic. 

There also came into being commissions, such as the Interstate Commerce Commission and the various Public Utility Commissions.

As one group of business men saw that they were losing ground, they accused the other and usually more powerful business men or groups of unfair practices and asked the government for protection. This brought into being a number of bureaus and commissions, which we are inclined to condemn as autocratic and depriving us of our liberties under the American system.

Modern means of transportation have thrown us into contact with so many human beings that we are forced to try to be neighborly whether we like it or not. Being compelled to live with others we learn that they have certain rights which must be respected.

If we want to travel fast and far, we must also be willing to endure the inconvenience of regulations which flow naturally from the satisfaction of those desires. 41

CHAPTER VII IMPROVED MEANS OF COMMUNICATION 

One hundred years ago there was no telephone, no telegraph, no radio, no television. There were few newspapers of any consequence and practically no means of delivering such publications as were issued. 

Whether for good or ill the spreading of news was a slow process.

Today we are almost smothered with news broadcasts, newspapers of all colors and persuasions, tabloids and magazines, informing us of the things that happen immediately after the occurrence, and even sometimes before they happen, and occasionally when they do not happen at all. 

Today the President of the United States can speak to the people of practically the whole nation, yes, even to those of almost the entire world (provided they are willing to listen). 

Improved means of communication have given rise to possibilities of centralization such as were never dreamed of a century or less ago, and probably have made collective action possible to a greater extent than any other single factor or group of factors. No matter where we go we find the radio. The telephone is in common use both for business and entertainment. The rural free delivery carries newspapers and periodicals, letters and parcels to our doors even though we are far removed from the centers of population.

At present no one is really separated from the other unless he voluntarily severs all connections and means of communication, and this he finds exceedingly difficult, especially if those searching for him are tax collectors or high pressure salesmen. 42

CHAPTER VIII IMPROVED EDUCATIONAL FACILITIES 

One hundred years ago our public school system was just being introduced and in many instances was meeting with the most strenuous opposition. In spite of adverse efforts, however, the public school system has been adopted generally, has resulted in improved educational standards and has served as an inducement for higher education. 

Colleges and universities have been endowed and enlarged, furnishing opportunities for a greater number to reap the benefits of college and university training. 

At present the situation seems somewhat paradoxical. 

Young men and women with college degrees are unable to secure employment. The elimination of the small business enterprise has taken away from many young people the opportunity of a business career. They then turn to the professions. These in turn become overcrowded, and for the protection of those already in, it is made so difficult for others to get in, that in many instances after spending possibly ten years in preparation, thousands of young people find themselves kept out by competitive examinations or tenure laws, and without the means of earning a decent livelihood.

Nothing is more pathetic than to see young people who have spent their time, effort and money and for whose education the parents have made sacrifices, often to the extent of mortgaging their homes, breaking stones or digging ditches on a WPA project.

With an improved economic system we can devise a means of not only employing those who have completed courses successfully, but also for those who have been willing to make the effort but can not complete a standardized 43

course on account of physical or mental handicaps, or who have lost in the ever increasing competitive race for position. 

Our educational system takes care of the winner, but it will not be adequate until it can also provide for the loser. 

Much has been said about room at the top but what about those at the bottom, and yet they may be the very persons upon whom those at the top must depend to consume their goods and services. 44

CHAPTER IX SCIENTIFIC ADVANCEMENT 

To discuss the changes resulting from scientific advances thoroughly would require volumes. According to present indications there is no limit to that which the human mind can achieve.

We have overcome most of the obstacles to scientific achievement due to ignorance and superstition.

In the fields of Physics and Chemistry we have leaped forward in the last half century and the results have been even beyond the fondest expectations of those who were called dreamers and visionaries.

Scientists today in describing the maximum and the minimum use such astounding figures that even the most highly trained mind can only accept without understanding them. 

Yet these figures are the basis for calculation in the solution of practical scientific problems. 

They tell us that the Harvard Observatory announces a huge star 40 million times bigger than the earth, 70 times larger than our sun with a surface 100,000 times as bright. 

That the new telescope with a 200 inch disk, which before long will be installed on a mountain top in California, will enable the eye to penetrate space a billion (1,000,000,000) light years (the velocity of light is 186,000 miles per second).

In simpler terms if a candle were placed 41,000 miles (more than 5 times the diameter of the earth) away it would still be visible.

A small particle is described as the electron, 458 of which move freely in the nucleus of an atom. The nucleus is described as being shaped like a whirling foot ball one five trillionth (1/5,000,000,000,000) of an inch long.

In order to produce color the scientist tells us that three hundred ninety-two billions (392,000,000,000) pulsations 45

upon the retina per second are necessary in order to produce red and seven hundred and fifty-seven billions (757,000,- 

000,000) to produce violet.

Such are some of the results of scientific research and calculations underlying the changes which have brought on the power age.

The application of electrical energy has in itself almost created a new world. Light, power and heat from electricity, with all of their numerous applications and variations, open new fields and create phenomena which a century ago would have been considered the result of Divine intervention or the work of the Devil.

The telegraph, telephone, wireless, radio, and the motion picture are the product of only the last few minutes when measured in terms of the time that man has lived upon this earth.

The achievements of medical science have been no less phenomenal. One disease after another has been conquered and we are moving steadily forward along this line, so that the time will come when there will, in all probability, not be a single disease which cannot be reasonably well under the control of medical science. 

Dr. Perrin H. Long, Associate Professor of Medicine at 

Johns Hopkins University, in an address delivered before the New York Herald Tribune Forum concluded as follows: 

"In closing one may say that the outlook for our health is indeed bright. Medical progress has been rapid in the last fifty years, and one can confidently predict that through the application of the sanitary, prophylactic and therapeutic measures now at hand, or on the horizon, we can look forward in the not too far distant future to the control of the majority of infectious diseases." 

The breakup of the atom is a possibility and when this is accomplished it is very likely that the dream of the alchemist, to change the nature of different substances, will be realized.

The University of California has recently (1940) received $1,400,000 for the construction of a new cyclotron or atom smasher. 46

This machine which will weigh 4,900 tons is 20 times heavier and bigger than the instrument now in use, and will produce energies in excess of 100 million volts as compared with the 32 million volts produced by the University's present 60 inch cyclotron. 

Prof. Ernest O. Lawrence, director of the radiation laboratory, points out " that no one knows what new objectives the new cyclotron may light up or what new problems it may produce or solve."

The use of radium and the various rays is of recent achievement, and the future alone can tell what marvellous results are in store for us. Among these rays are the X-rays, violet ray, cosmic ray, and even the death ray is claimed by Dr. Antonio Longoria of Cleveland, Ohio, who vows never to reveal the secret.

A definite plan has been devised by Massachusetts Institute of Technology for research in the solar energy program. 

A house has been constructed with equipment to catch the heat as it falls on the roof. Then the heat will be transmitted into a well insulated water storage tank. Experiments will be made with a tank large enough to hoard an entire winter's supply of heat. 

In discussing the subject of solar energy Dr. Charles G. 

Abbot, Secretary of the Smithsonian Institute, says: "With one-tenth of the sunlight that falls annually on the State of New Mexico alone, a person could furnish the whole United States with heat, light and power for a year. That means a total of ten trillion horse-power hours a year. "Each year man's dream of harnessing the rays of the sun and using their tremendous energy to do the work of the world comes a little closer to realization. A few years ago, an engine which would transform three per cent of the sun's rays into energy was thought fairly efficient. Today, I can convert about fifteen per cent of this energy into mechanical activity. "Tomorrow, I think heat and light from the sun will be caught in mirrors and stored in giant thermos bottles set on house tops. From these storage tanks it will be tapped at 47

will to light and heat homes, to irrigate farm lands, to distil drinking water and run farm machinery."

In the near future science will probably harness the wind as another source of energy. 

Dr. Charles F. Brooks of Harvard University predicts that very soon and probably within a year new style windmills will dot the landscape of New England furnishing commercial electric power over high voltage trunk lines to various individual centers.

In the field of chemistry the achievements have been no less and perhaps even more marvellous than in other scientific fields. While we feel that this branch of science is only in its infancy, yet the capacity of the chemist to analyze and synthesize, to tear apart and put together, to produce inexpensively in artificial form that which nature produced at a very high cost, has been developed to a high degree. 

The change brought about during recent years in the field of industrial chemistry is well described in an address before the Committee of One Hundred of Miami Beach, Florida, by

Dr. C. M. A. Stine, Vice-President, E. I. Dupont de 

Nemours & Company, and appearing in the Dupont Maga zine of May, 1939. Part of this address is as follows: 

"Beyond improvements in manufacture brought about by mechanical ingenuity and the development of new sources of power we had made very little real progress in the art of living from a scientific viewpoint and, like the Stone Age man, were still utilizing in the main what Nature had cast ready-made at our doorsteps." 66 

However, there was a growing light in the skies. Alchemy, called Black Magic in the Middle Ages, had assumed the dignity of an art. And from the art had emerged chemistry, a science that deals with the ultimate composition of things as distinguished from the superficial, mechanical composition of things." "The moment, however, that inventive genius turned to the composition of materials as its starting point, we began to see wood, metal, fiber, rubber and all other natural products not mistakenly as raw materials in themselves, but as compounds of raw materials that are present in super-abundance in the air, the water and the soil." 66 Our real raw materials, we saw, were a comparatively small group of chemical elements, which number in all less than one hundred. Moreover, it was discovered that four elements-carbon, 48

hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen-make up the major portion by far of all things associated with plant and animal life, and coal and petroleum may be included in that classification.” "That discovery was epochal. It made coal, to the chemist, not merely a fuel but an almost limitless source of carbon. The air we breathe and the waters of our streams became inexhaustible reservoirs of oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen. Here, in these three sources, coal, water and air, were the primary building blocks employed in the creation of silk, rubber, wood, camphor, in short most organic things and their by-products." "On the whole, however, chemical synthesis was in its infancy in the United States in 1919 and the chemical industry itself was just awakening to the enormous potentialities of organic research and creation." "More than 200,000 compounds wholly new to man have emerged from this awakened industry and in less than a quarter century! "The utilization of only a few hundred of these compounds has revolutionized the methods and products of more than a score of industries, effected changes in millions of jobs, altered our food, clothing and homes, transformed our daily habits, given a new concept to medical research, and established the United States in the forefront of scientific advance, fortified it against war and saved billions in money." "No longer is it necessary for us to go to South America for nitrates that are indispensable in peace as in war. Chemical plants on home soil extract them from the air. No longer need we look aghast at the prospect of being cut off, in emergency, from the rubber plantations of the Far East. Instead, we can manufacture from coal, water, salt and limestone a product, neoprene, that in many respects is superior to rubber." 

Chemistry is interested primarily in production but not in distribution.

The utilization of a few hundred of these compounds may have "saved billions of dollars." On the other hand we should not fail to notice that Dr. Stine also says that the same discoveries in chemistry which have saved billions of dollars have also "effected changes in millions of jobs, altered our food, clothing and homes" and "transformed our daily habits."

Many of us do not seem to understand that we are producing in almost limitless quantities by scientific methods and by the use of machinery. That the products must be consumed by human beings, and that they can be procured 49

only by having sufficient of whatever medium of exchange may then be in use.

We surely cannot fail to realize that changes of such importance have taken place, as a result of scientific research, and the application of the principles discovered to the production of commodities, that a system of distribution designed generations ago is not adequate to meet the demands of these various transformations. 50

CHAPTER X CONFIDENCE 

We hear it said so often that the only thing necessary to pull us out of the depression is confidence. 

This means that there will again be investments on a large scale, which may be described a little more in detail as a greater demand for goods, first through spending money either for construction of capital goods such as factories and equipment, or the building up of inventories by manufacturing and holding commodities, with the expectation of selling them sometime in the future.

To say that prosperity can be restored by renewed confidence is an easy way out of an argument but not an easy way out of the depression. 

Confidence without cash is just about as effective as faith without works. 

Suppose that at this very moment complete confidence were to be restored and the cash and credit now idle were available for investment. In what would we invest? What do we need?

More steel mills? Not likely when under ordinary conditions, and when not producing war equipment our mills are often operating far below one-half capacity, even as low as twenty per cent.

In railroads? No. With other means of travel and transportation by the use of the auto, truck and airplane we are discontinuing railroads instead of laying new track. There is not much inducement for investing in railroads when we realize that the stock of the New York, New Haven and Hartford railroad has dropped from $279 per share in 1893 to 25c in 1940 and that of the Northern Pacific from $1000 per share in 1901 to 77% in 1940. 51

Shoe factories? No. Our present capacity is more than three times the demand for shoes, that is more than three times more than we have the capacity to buy. 

Automobile factories? No. Even the three largest companies, Ford, Chrysler and General Motors, are very seldom operating to capacity. If we observe the fate of the Auburn 

Automobile company whose stock in 1929 sold at $514 and in 1940 at % or 872¢ per share, we become very dubious about investing in plants and equipment for manufacturing automobiles. 

Airplanes? Yes, with reservations. The demand for airplanes will be for the duration of the war, and that will end sometime if for no other reason than that the nations at war cannot raise the wherewithal to buy the war material. After the war there will be so much capacity for building airplanes that we will not know what to do with it. 

So far as the products now in use are concerned, we have more productive capacity than we need, but what about the new fields?

Dupont announces that in the last 25 years, chemistry has succeeded in producing about 200,000 new products. When these new products go on the market who as a rule controls them? Cellophane, artificial wrapping material displacing paper; rayon and nylon, artificial fabric displacing cotton, wool and silk; neoprene, artificial rubber; fabrikoid, artificial leather; lucite, artificial glass; plastics, displacing wood and metals; dyes and countless other products controlled and produced by Dupont and affiliated companies, and those companies have ample in their treasuries to finance any new project without asking the investing public for any money.

Some one says, air conditioning or television. 

What has happened and is happening every day is that the larger companies have added different lines, leaving other plants idle with plenty of capacity to take on a great deal of additional work without any extra plant capacity. 

As an illustration, General Motors takes on refrigerators, in competition with plants producing refrigerators. The latter 52

loses its business and is ready to take on any new product that may come along, not to enlarge its plant but to keep employed the plant capacity it already has, and which it can not use because some other company has succeeded in taking its business. Then after a company such as General 

Motors has been successful in putting several lines on the market, Dupont comes along and takes over General Motors. 

Very little investigation is necessary to determine that there are numerous buildings formerly used as factories now standing idle, with plant capacity in practically every line far in excess of demand.

It has frequently been suggested that the building of houses on a large scale will relieve the situation, if the houses can be built at a reasonable price. The reason given for not building more at present is the high cost and the high cost it is said is due to high wages on the part of the building trades. If we examine this matter a little more closely, we shall very likely discover that the actual labor cost in creating a building today is less than it was in the past, due to the use of machinery both in factory and in the erection of the building.

On the other hand those engaged in building houses must occupy many of them and they must have income sufficient to pay the price.

We must also bear in mind that it is necessary for practically every family to have an automobile. Radios must be bought as well as shoes, clothing and food. Moving picture shows must be supported or those employed in the industry will be out of employment, and those having money invested will get no dividends. We must chew gum, smoke cigarettes, eat ice cream and candies, use pills and lotions, patronize beauty parlors, in brief we must even now buy an almost endless number of commodities and services in order to keep the wheels turning, so that people will continue to be employed. After buying an almost endless number of other commodities what is left out of the average income for a dwelling house?

Can any one imagine what would be the state of affairs if 53

we were compelled to use the goods turned out by equipment requiring sufficient investment to bring back prosperity? 

We not only couldn't buy it, but we couldn't consume it. 

Commodities would be produced in such quantities that we would literally be crushed under the load.

Confidence in our capacity to consume the commodities produced by more factories will never bring prosperity. Our capacity to consume simply cannot be enlarged to that extent. 

But what about public works and improvements? What are really public improvements are very limited in extent. 

We object to the government interfering with private enterprise. If the government even undertakes such worthy projects as slum clearance and bears only part of the expense it will mean either more borrowing or increased taxation. 

Serious objections are raised to both of these and we are left where we were at the beginning, all dressed up and no place to go. 54

CHAPTER XI WAR AND ITS CAUSES 

What is the relation between the operation of our economic system and war?

In earlier times there was mass production of human beings. The population increased faster than the means of subsistence. It was necessary, therefore, to have a war periodically to kill off the surplus. 

Today mass production of human beings is not so serious. 

It is now mass production of commodities and we have wars designed to destroy property with the killing of human beings only incidental.

This statement may not seem so paradoxical when we consider the amount of material used in constructing the Maginot and Siegfried Lines and other lines of fortifications, the battleships, airplanes, tanks, cruisers, destroyers, airplane carriers, mine layers, submarines, guns of all sizes and descriptions, ammunition, gas masks, clothing for soldiers, autos, trucks, and the vast amount expended for other war material. Since this is not to be used for man's benefit, it must be used for a negative purpose and consequently is created for the purpose of being destroyed. 

We must always bear in mind that the army is a good place to keep the unemployed. There they can be disciplined and kept out of mischief, and for those who are left on the battlefield, it is much easier to place a medal on the breast of a war mother, than to find a job for a discharged soldier.

Modern wars do not begin or end on a certain date. We prepare for war over a long period, and then at a certain time we are ready for the kill. Modern wars start when preparation for war begins and the preparedness program is almost perpetual. 55

The problem of unemployment has been serious for quite a number of years. Most of the war material is produced by labor which otherwise would not be employed. To discontinue the manufacture of war material would result in unemployment for a huge army of people. That means the swelling of relief rolls, a greater demand for WPA and other similar projects, and a program of increased taxes. 

When the point of saturation has been reached in the building of war equipment, something must be done to create a demand for more. Propaganda spreading the poison of fear soon reaches the limit, and without actually consuming the material produced the demand will slacken. 

Open hostilities is the next step with destruction of life and property and untold suffering, the inevitable consequence. 

Modern wars result almost entirely from the failure to properly distribute commodities produced by machinery, or to state it more briefly, modern wars are wars for markets. 

If we drive along the highways the unsightly auto graveyards are evidence of appalling waste. Unless automobiles are consumed rapidly, our factories cannot dispose of the product. So long as the Chrysler factories can produce 5,500 autos and trucks per day, Ford 10,000 and General Motors probably as many or more, there is only one way to market the product, and that is by actually destroying a certain percentage of those produced. One method of doing this is by changing models, so that the owner of the automobile is almost compelled to purchase a new car long before the one owned by him needs to be replaced, with the result that many automobiles which otherwise would be repaired at little expense and kept in good running condition, land in the auto graveyard where a good part of the metal is allowed to corrode, deteriorate and become worthless. 

What is true of automobiles is also true in practically every other line. Styles and models change, so that we are forced to buy new articles long before those that we have are worn out.

When the consumer cannot purchase the commodities produced, we turn to the creation of war material to take up 56

the slack and appeal to the patriotism of the people of different nations to go to war, the result of which is the destruction of property, thereby stimulating the demand for replacements, which in turn creates a demand for labor and more material.

Much is said about the United States getting into European Wars. Almost without exception we are in these wars from the beginning, and sometimes even before actual hostilities break out. Since we are furnishing all of the material to belligerents that they can buy, the only question that remains is, shall we send an army across? 

If we examine into recent trends, we will find that the steel business started on the upgrade about the time that the United States initiated its huge defense program. 

If we investigate in a city of any size, we will find that certain factories are engaged in the production of war material. 

As an illustration, in one city a safe and lock company is now erecting gun carriages, a company which was originally engaged in the construction of automobile bodies is active in the production of different kinds of war equipment, an ice machinery company is now producing refrigerating outfits for submarines and destroyers. A chain company, jack assemblies for the air corps, and a manufacturer of farm machinery, hydraulic powder presses.

We have also been selling on a large scale to Japan. An article written by Dr. Walter H. Judd, ten years Medical 

Missionary to China, gives us an idea of the extent to which we, as a nation, have been responsible for Japanese atrocities after we have furnished the material to assist the Japanese. "We are furnishing a steadily increasing percentage of the essential war materials which Japan herself does not have, and which will enable her to destroy China in violation of the very treaty we persuaded China to accept. In 1937 we supplied 54 per cent of these materials, in 1938, 56 per cent. Since war broke out in 

Europe, Japanese purchases from us have jumped even higher." 66 Over 90 per cent of Japan's scrap iron and steel and copper she gets from the United States. Special steels and alloys, metal working machinery, trucks, lumber, lubricating oils go from our ports every week. Over 90 per cent of Japan's aviation gasoline is American. 57

An embargo on this item alone could practically stop the bombings of open cities which we so piously condemn."

Recently there was put into effect a 30 cents a bushel Government subsidy on 300,000 bushels of wheat ordered from Tokyo for delivery in Shanghai. It will go to feed Japanese soldiers pursuing their war of ruthless conquest. 

It is reported that Canada sold a million bushels of wheat to the Soviet Union. It is likely that these shipments will release Russian grown wheat for the German granaries.

In the United States, while assisting Finland by loans, we allowed an increase in Soviet Russia's purchases during the month of December, 1939, in steel making alloys, manganese, tungsten, chromium, vanadium, as well as copper, tin and rubber. The Department of Commerce reports a total of $3,323,000 more goods sold to Russia in December, 1939, than in the same month of the preceding year. Since the European war started Russia has bought more gasoline from the United States than ever before, about 1,360,000 barrels (57,120,000 gallons). The United States is now a major source of fuel for Soviet tanks, motorized transports and planes which use a low octane product. 

Our exports of oil to Russia have also risen in recent months. 

Here again a "democratic" power is not only strengthening Stalin's war machine, but also helping Stalin to help Hitler. 

The people of the United States are very emphatic in expressing their sentiments against our participation in foreign wars. On the other hand, if we analyze the situation more carefully, we will find that there are a large number who will unquestionably profit by war activities and therefore are willing to at least tolerate war. Men who are employed are anxious to be regularly employed, and if the manufacture of war material is discontinued, many of them will be out of a job. They are desperate to continue working and therefore be assured of an income, and are not concerned about the product of their skill and labor so long as they are guaranteed steady employment and a reasonable return, nor are there any strikes by labor against the manufacture of war material. 58

The man who is interested in stocks is ready to take advantage of the advance in the price of securities. The farmer is relieved when he finds that grains are selling at a higher price. Banks are ready to make loans to business concerns that may come into being as a result of renewed industrial activity. Real estate dealers, storekeepers, saloon keepers, munition makers and practically every person who is required to have an income, is interested in what we generally know as prosperity, even though this improvement has come about as a result of the wars in Europe and Asia. 

A feeling, that is rather general in spite of apparent opposition to war, is well expressed in an editorial that appeared recently (1939) in a leading Chicago daily newspaper. 66 Our material interests lie with the English and the French. . . . 

We have an immediate concern. The farmers of the United States have just harvested a bumper wheat crop. The corn cribs of the middle west still contain millions of bushels of last year's crops. 

Wheat and corn prices are painfully low. Surplus cotton bales are bursting warehouses in the South and a new crop is on the way. 

Cotton is selling at starvation prices. With war cutting off production in Europe, there will be huge armies as well as civilian populations to feed. In our cities are from 10 to 20 million unemployed. 

Everywhere there are idle factories capable of giving employment to the jobless if they are permitted to fill the demands that cessation of industry in warring countries will create. . . . Finally there are the industrialists eager to resume full time production with the profits that would bring."

So long as nations are obliged to struggle for markets, wars will continue. One nation seeking an outlet for its merchandise cannot afford to let the other nation get the advantage. If England must trade, she cannot let Germany take that trade away from her. If Germany must trade, she cannot allow England to displace her. All nations are mechanized and must sell. If there are no markets, the machinery of production must stop. 

England's trade with her colonies is becoming more restricted every day as the industries of these colonies become mechanized. While she does her best to prevent the build- 59

ing of factories in her outlying possessions, this cannot be controlled entirely, as is well illustrated by the refusal of the American colonies as early as 1776 to submit to the trade and industrial restrictions imposed by the mother country. 

These colonies were interested not so much in representation as they were in dollars from their industrialization. 

The result of the sale of merchandise to belligerents will be an increase in the demand for larger acreage, the development of our mines, and other sources of raw material, and when the war ends, a collapse more severe than any that we have experienced.

Practically all wars of the past were intensified by economic demands. Rome needed slaves. The Babylonians, Chaldeans and other nations and empires of antiquity needed food. Then it was a matter of getting food to barely exist. Population increased at a rapid rate, and because of this it was necessary periodically to engage in warfare in order to slaughter the excess population. 

The issue at present is represented to be between Democracy and Totalitarianism. Let us look at the lineup. 

England and France are supposed to be Democracies. 

England tried her best to get the assistance of Russia in order to forge an unbroken circle around Germany. Hitler has denounced Russia most vehemently, but at this time Russia and Germany have joined hands against the Democracies of the West. At the same time the United States, and even Canada, is selling war material and food supplies to Russia, which certainly will be used against the armies of the so-called Democratic nations. Does this make sense?

Just about as much as in the thirty years religious war in 

Europe from 1618 to 1648 between Catholics and Protestants, when Catholic generals often led Protestant armies and Protestant leaders were in command of Catholic armies. 

There is absolutely no reason for war today. So long as human beings were fearful of an insufficient supply of food there was some excuse for stealing and murdering on a large scale by means of war, as it is a law of nature to struggle for existence. We need no longer struggle for survival. Our 60

capacity to produce is sufficient to supply our every need. 

Now, we struggle to destroy our commodities to keep up the price.

There was a time when men fought on account of religious opinions. Today we are not concerned enough about the other fellow's religious views to fight about them. 

We have demonstrated in the United States that we can get along best by allowing every one to worship as he chooses and if he decides not to worship at all, that is his own affair.

One nation today need not take over another for its products. Our greatest problem is to keep out the high pressure salesmen, who have come over and are trying to sell what we do not want or need.

Nations pretend to fight for a supply of oil. 

Chemistry can solve this problem, as the supply of alcohol as a fuel is unlimited.

Modern wars are economic wars. The underlying principle is the destruction of commodities so that more can be produced and thus keep the machinery in operation. The principle is the same whether it is in the change of the model of hats in order that the one in use becomes out of style and must be replaced, or the sinking of a battleship, or the dropping of bombs to destroy cities so that material will be required to replace or rebuild them.

War today is National Insanity and there is only one way to alleviate that condition. Learn to distribute our products so that every one may be economically secure. This, of course, would require the operation of an ideal system. 

While it is well to be idealists, on the other hand it is very necessary for us to realize that we are living in a practical world and that reasonable defense measures are necessary for protection, in the event that the unexpected should happen. 

How then can we best prepare for emergencies? (1) Quit meddling in European affairs. Europe today is only repeating what it has been doing for many centuries, and will probably continue to do for many generations. 61

When the European family quarrel begins, our place is strictly at home, especially since none of the nations now at war can come into court with clean hands. (2) Adopt an economic system which does not require that we sell war material to foreign nations in order to keep our industries operating. We at this time are selling to all of the belligerents; directly to England, France, Russia and 

Japan, and indirectly to Germany through Russia and Italy. (3) Pay special attention to the development of the man power of our nation by keeping it physically fit for our own defense, but not to be used as cannon fodder to further the imperialistic designs of foreign powers. (4) Have sufficient productive equipment to create supplies rapidly when needed. Invest in plants for producing, and instead of turning out a large number of units which soon become obsolete, spend some time and effort on keeping a reasonable amount of military, naval and air equipment up to date, and when the demand arises, then produce to capacity. 

Ford, General Motors and Chrysler can turn out at least 25,000 automobiles and trucks per day. With as much plant capacity as that to produce airplanes, we could deliver such quantities in a short time that any invader would soon realize that America is really for Americans.

The Ford Motor Company has assured us that its plant alone can manufacture 1,000 airplanes a day even without interfering with motor car output and at least 5,000 per day if automobile production is discontinued. 

What is true of airplanes is also true of other war equipment and supplies. The modern industrialist instead of allowing immense inventories of finished products to accumulate and become obsolete, enlarges his plant and waits for orders. Let the same plan be followed in carrying out our defense program. Let us have our productive equipment in first class condition and then wait until there is a real demand, not based upon hysteria. We will soon learn that in almost every instance, that which we fear most does 62

not happen, and if it does happen, we will be ready to cope with the situation. (5) Prevent subversive activities by guaranteeing security to all of our people. Economic insecurity is fertile soil for the growth of Fifth Columns.

CHAPTER XII SUMMARY 

It is evident that change is largely beyond the control of the individual. The life principle seems to be based upon change. If the individual cannot successfully resist and prevent change, he must then learn to adjust himself to it. 

In the early history of man, changes came slowly, then more rapidly, and finally with a burst of speed that has swept us off our feet. The changes have been so rapid that we, for the time being at least, have lost our bearings. 

The greatest change has come in production. So far as actual bodily needs are concerned, these do not change a great deal. If we produce more than we can consume, then in order to continue production we must waste and destroy. 

Our productive system has become mechanized. In order to keep the machinery in operation, the product must be consumed, and under our system we buy and sell. Therefore the consumer must have money with which to buy. So long as consumable goods are scarce, there is a demand for goods and in turn a demand for labor to produce them. Production is the primary factor. Once goods become plentiful, the demand slackens both for goods and labor, and the producer must proceed to create artificial demands. 

When our grandmothers smoked their pipes it was not the result of advertising. They grew their own tobacco, cured it, prepared it, and smoked it. The desire was satisfied, but there was no pressure from the outside insisting that in order to keep young, handsome and streamlined they had to smoke a certain number of pipefuls of tobacco per day. 

Today we can produce more than 15,000,000,000 cigarettes per month. Three men and a machine can produce at least 

1,000,000 per day. The machines stand ready to double this 64

amount, and more, if the demand can be created. Over the radio, on the bill board, in the newspaper, in the magazine, advertisements tell of the virtues of the several brands of cigarettes. 

Cigarettes are used only as an illustration. What is true of cigarettes is true in every industry of any consequence. 

By the use of machinery our merchandise is produced today by a minimum of effort, and in unlimited quantities. 

I say in unlimited quantities as the amount can be increased by merely adding units of equipment. 

The machines produce goods for one purpose only and that is for use by human beings. The cigarette machine cannot smoke; the shoe machine cannot wear shoes; the clothing machine cannot wear clothing; nor can the machines producing autos drive and consume them. 

The machine can produce but it cannot consume. This must be done by human beings. If 100 billion dollars' worth of merchandise is produced, the same amount must be consumed to keep the wheels turning and to keep workmen employed.

Under our system we buy and sell. That means that the consumer must have a certain amount of the medium of exchange to buy the share of goods which he is duty bound to consume in order to keep men employed. 

If there is not sufficient of the medium of exchange in circulation, the consumer cannot get enough, and if he cannot get sufficient, the wheels of industry will cease turning. 

Those who advocate less spending do not seem to realize that only by spending can we keep the machine operating. 

If they said, we cannot raise sufficient funds under our present system it would be more to the point, but to say stop spending is to restrict and perhaps put an end to the business in which they themselves are engaged. 

The answer comes back, but we always were taught to save, be thrifty and don't spend. Precisely so. But that advice was for the time when conditions were different, when we didn't produce more than we actually needed, and did not have to find an outlet for our surplus goods. 65

Can Ford continue to turn out Fords at the rate of 10,000 per day, and Chrysler at the rate of 5,500 automobiles and trucks daily, unless the public keeps on buying automobiles? 

And who buys most of the automobiles? Not those who have large incomes, but often those who can least afford to have an automobile. Then why buy them? (1) Desires are created by seeing others have a car and (2) under present conditions an automobile has become a necessity, particularly since the smaller towns have lost their industries and often it is necessary to travel many miles to work. We now reduce the hours and use most of the extra time travelling to and from work.

Each and every one has become dependent upon the other, and unless someone buys the other cannot sell.

If there is not sufficient of the wherewithal in circulation with which to buy, how shall the consuming public get it? 

We say to a man, "Go out and get a job and when you have a job and have earned money come back and we will sell you the goods." He can't get the job and consequently cannot get the money, and if he does, he cannot earn sufficient to buy the share which he must consume to keep the machine from clogging.

How then can the consumer get sufficient with which to buy if he is unable to earn it?

I can see no other way than to give it to him, not necessarily because we are so generous, but without it he cannot consume his part. If he is unemployed he is not important as a producer, but he remains exceedingly important as a consumer. Formerly production was the primary factor, now the situation is reversed. Consumption has become the primary factor and production has become secondary. 

As we study the history of periods of prosperity and the depressions that followed, we find that prosperity came with the increase in purchasing power, but it came through speculation and the increase in prices, not wages.

The historian writing of the first depression in 1819 says: "Not the banks only but business men of all classes had been mortgaging the future beyond warrant." Of the 66

second in 1837, "In every branch of industry the craze for investment had gone far beyond safe limits"; of the third in 1857, "The third financial panic, like the first and second, was caused by undue speculation "; of the fourth in 1873, "In anticipation of unusual profits, entrepreneurs borrowed heavily and at high rates of interest "; of the fifth in 1884, "The crisis of 1884 was occasioned largely by overinvestment in railroads"; of the sixth in 1893, "advancing prices had induced speculative investments." And like speculation, only on a much larger scale, resulted in the panic of 1907, the decline in 1914 and the most disastrous collapse in 1929.

It should be evident from these facts that purchasing power during the periods preceding the depressions was increased by speculative investment. The demand following investments and new enterprises led to price advancements, which continued to climb until they reached the peak and then the inevitable collapse.

I have not been able to find that at any one time did prosperity come about as a result of employment. The investments created a demand for material and labor, and with that demand created, employment followed, often resulting in a labor shortage and the importation of millions from Europe to do the work, but with the coming of the machine to perform the tasks formerly done by human beings, the labor, used to meet the shortage, has been turned into a surplus and that surplus is now on our hands as the army of unemployed.

Kindly note that I am not writing in defense of any political party or the policies of any group or faction. I am not trying to plead the cause of any ism. I am merely trying to face the facts as I see them. I consider it extremely foolish to close one's eyes to conditions with the hope that by some miracle bad conditions will correct themselves without some one doing something.

To me there is nothing sacred about a political party. 

Those who want to use a party for their own private gain will use the Democratic as well as the Republican party, but 67

I do realize that one, in order to do his part, cannot accomplish much unless he affiliates himself with an organization, which in matters of government must be a political party. 

My aim is to determine causes and, if possible, suggest a remedy that could at least be given a trial. 

The Republicans and conservative Democrats blame the New Deal for conditions as they exist at present. The New 

Dealers among the Democrats in turn remind their opponents of the days from 1929 to 1932 when business was really flat on its back and about 16,000,000 people were out of employment.

The New Deal started to spend some money and conditions improved somewhat, but the remedy did not effect a cure, only a palliative. The patient is still very ill. The remedy being applied at present is War, believe it or not. 

Theoretically all of us are against war. Practically most of us are in favor of it, or at least tolerate it, if it means dollars in our pockets.

Most of us are very emphatic in asserting that we don't want war. On the other hand how many farmers complain about the increase in the price of grain due to the war?

How many strikes have been called because factories have been engaged in building war material? How many Ministers of the Gospel announce that any one who is getting an income from the building of war material shall refrain from placing his coin on the collection plate?

The steel business is on the upgrade, and why? The demand for war material.

Factories on part time before are now on full time. 

A safe and lock plant now is producing gun carriages, an ice machinery company producing refrigerating outfits for destroyers and submarines, a former body works building several different kinds of war material and others building various kinds of equipment for military purposes. No protest at all of this, but keen satisfaction on the part of the public that at last we are pulling out of the depression. 

After the war broke out I watched some men who seemed to be bitterly opposed to war, but who owned steel stock, 68

beam with delight at the advance of the stock market, ready to sell as soon as the price of steel stock reached a high level. 

The feeling of most people toward war is that of the man who sat beside me in a trolley car at the time of our entrance into the war preceding the one now in progress. This man was employed at a military camp at a high wage. He said to me, "I do not want war, but if it did last another six months I would get my house paid."

As a matter of fact, most of us are satisfied to have war so long as it pays and we are at a safe distance from the firing line.

Isn't it terrible that we must depend upon war to keep our factories operating and to keep men employed, but so long as we do not learn to distribute the product of our mechanized system properly, we shall continue to have wars. 

It is reasonable to know that we cannot go on forever building war material without destroying it so that more can be built.

It certainly does seem strange that in this land of plenty, where we have the raw material and the equipment to produce several times more than we can comfortably consume, that many should be in want, and that we must depend upon war to keep our people employed. 

Some steps have been taken to provide security for those who have reached an advanced age, but what has been done is not sufficient. The amount paid will keep people alive, but it will not give them that abundance to which they are entitled, and it will not enable them to consume that which must be consumed by them to keep the wheels of industry turning.

It is hard for us to realize what changes have actually taken place. That the world of today is really not the world of yesterday. Even those of us who have barely passed the half century mark can remember when conditions were entirely different-when long hours of toil were necessary to provide for the necessaries of life and a few luxuries. 

During these years the capacity to produce has been stepped up many times, our means of travel and transporta- 69

tion have changed, our means of communication have shortened space so that within a few minutes one can be in touch with those who are hundreds, possibly thousands of miles away. The radio and the moving picture have given us means of entertainment and information beyond our fondest expectations.

Up to this time, however, we have been unable to adjust ourselves so that we could take advantage of these changes and improvements.

What is to become of the business man who, through no fault of his own, finds that his business has been taken away from him, and with our capacity to produce by machinery or to create artificially, the man in business today does not know whether he will have business tomorrow. 

I have in mind a small factory engaged in the manufacture of cartons. It employed about 40 persons. Those managing the business found it impossible to sell at a profit. 

Upon investigation they found that another concern had installed a machine at a cost of $60,000 that could turn out more cartons by using two employees than the other factory could by using 40. There was only one of two alternatives. 

Buy a machine or quit. They saw it was useless to continue and quit.

How many men have been pushed aside by the installation of machines with absolutely no place to go? They are not needed to produce but are they not needed to consume? 

There are many who say, just let things alone and the depression will end.

I have tried hard to convince myself that this is true, but up to this time I have been unable to conclude that a letalone policy will suffice. It never happened before and how can it happen this time with a much more aggravated condition?

To merely let things alone never cured any depression. 

Prosperity, if we can call it such, did not return until purchasing power was created and this was started by investment on a large scale, and what a field there was for speculaion. Canals, cotton farms, cotton mills, land booms, mines, 70

oil wells, and countless others, but above all railroads and the automobile.

We hear it said frequently, if the Government would keep hands off then there would be investments, but would there? 

Ask that question seriously. In what shall we invest?

Should we build more cigar factories when many are now standing idle? Shall we build more shoe factories when as early as 1931 we could produce 900,000,000 pairs and could sell only 300,000,000? Shall we invest in bakeries when we have bakeries already that can probably produce 5 times more than we can consume? Shall we build more automobile plants when those already built are not operating to capacity? Look about on every hand and in practically every line there is now more plant capacity than we need. 

Or do we want to keep on piling up commodities until there is an automobile for every man, woman and child, and the highways are so crowded that our traffic lanes must be increased to ten or more in number and in the cities half the buildings must be torn down to furnish parking space; so that we would be forced to have a radio in every room in the house including the garage and the bath room; a different pair of shoes for every hour of the day; a different suit of clothes with every change of shoes; chewing gum by the gross and cigarettes by the thousand for each and every person; five times as many potatoes to eat as we have now; many times more hats to wear and a similar increase in every other line.

There is practically no limit to our means of production. 

We can be engulfed with manufactured goods and farm products, but there is a limit to our capacity to consume. 

A noted engineer has stated that our productive capacity can be increased as many as forty times. 

We must call a halt some time and learn to distribute what we have instead of making ourselves believe that we can continue to produce indefinitely without reaching the saturation point.

My personal opinion is that the point of saturation has 71

not been reached in many lines if we can devise a way to distribute, but that is our problem. 

The situation as it appears to me at present is, that we have already reached the point where there is little need for either labor or additional capital. 

Much has been said about restored confidence resulting in large expenditiures for capital goods. Expenditures for capital goods today have an entirely different effect from that of 50 or 75 years ago. When physical expansion depended upon hand labor, it meant that a large number of workmen were employed. Today the buildings are erected by machinery with a minimum of human labor. Huge steam shovels, derricks, hoists, cranes, concrete mixers, and various other pieces of equipment, perform the greater part of the work on the ground. The material that is used in construction is also machine made. The equipment in the plants is machine made, and when the factory is completed it is operated by a minimum of human labor. 

The new Nylon plant erected by Duponts at a cost of $11,000,000 will employ 850 men in its operation. Less than a century ago the expenditure of about $11,000 would employ as many men. Also bear this in mind, that Nylon produced by Dupont with the assistance of 850 workmen will probably displace at least 10 times that number in the different fields whose products such as cotton, wool and silk are displaced by the new synthetic product, Nylon. 

It is reported that in 1940 North American Co., a Utilities system, will expend in construction work $38,500,000; 

Aluminum Co. of America $30,000,000, and Dupont's budget will exceed the 1939 expenditure of $27,700,000, 60% of this to be spent for additional capacity required for Nylon (artificial silk) and Neoprene (artificial rubber). All of this construction work will be done by the use of the latest improved machinery from the production of the raw material to the pouring of the last cubic yard of concrete and the driving of the last rivet, and with a minimum of human labor, and when the work is done, especially by the Dupont Co., the number of men displaced, who are producing com- 72

modities for which Nylon and Neoprene are substituted, will far exceed the men employed in the new factories. 

The Steel Companies propose to spend about $140,000,000 for plant improvement during the year 1940. 

This will result in the unemployment of many thousands of workmen because the latest improved machinery for displacing human labor will be installed.

By the use of the strip steel machines recently adopted by the steel industry 15 men can do the work formerly performed by 100, and a like result will follow the installation of other machines designed to produce more goods with less human labor.

The amount expended when it goes into millions appears large, but this does not mean sources of investment for the general public. The large concerns with their huge surpluses simply dip into their treasuries and the necessary funds are forthcoming. In fact they must do this in order to keep their own idle funds working.

General Motors may be used as an illustration of a large industrial concern with a rather substantial surplus. In 

1938 this was reported to have been $452,000,000 and probably has not decreased any since that time. 

The theory that we can enjoy prosperity by the creation of debts for capital goods is not likely to assist a great deal in curing the chronic economic ills from which we seemed to recover several times, only to suffer a relapse more serious and with more lasting adverse effects than from the preceding attack.

The reader certainly will agree with me that industries of importance are owned by a few persons or corporations. 

Most of the large concerns are already financed and are borrowing very little money, and if they borrow, they get it at a very low rate of interest on account of the large amount of money being out of circulation. R. J. Reynolds Tobacco

Company borrowed recently (January 1940) $20,000,000 at 

1.75%. I have knowledge of two other concerns borrowing on a smaller scale, one $100,000 at 34 of 1% for one year, and another $1,500,000 at 1/2 of 1% for one year. 73

What chance does the little fellow have, who must pay 5, 52 or even 6%, to compete with the big operator who usually does not need to borrow, and when he does, he gets it at an exceedingly low rate?

True, indeed, there are many fields for investment, but not such that private industry will be interested-parks, play grounds, hospitals, schools, elimination of grade crossings, large scale housing-but will private enterprise take hold of these? I fear it will not. Then where are the frontiers in which our idle money can be invested and purchasing power created as was done when the nation was young, and when the individual, if he lost a job, could get another, or if he couldn't get a job he could catch fish or shoot wild pigeons and really get a full meal. 

Investment means debts. The debt of the Nation, private and public, amounts to more than $300,000,000,000. How many of these will be paid? The crash of 1929 is a good example of what can happen in a short time when 74 billions of debts were liquidated within a few years. 

We can never depend upon the creation of debts to stabilize our economic system.

Our Federal Government has never defaulted, and it never needs to, as it can always start the printing presses, but it could go on borrowing until the debt would be so enormous that the entire price structure would collapse. 

If the business of this nation had not gone through bankruptcy at least eight times, the debts by this time would have reached Pike's Peak proportions. Business simply couldn't continue to carry the load, and then the debts were cancelled by repudiation, and if you think that this is not correct, notice in particular the crash of 1929 when security values dropped from 89.6 billions in September of 1929 to 

15.6 in May of 1932, a decline of 74 billions. Add to this the loss in lowered commodity prices and real estate values and it makes the present National debt of $45,000,000,000 look like small change. Add to this all of the losses since this tion started doing business and it would almost require a 74

mathematician with the capacity of an Einstein to make the calculation.

Various attempts have been made to effect a cure for our economic ills. The wage-hour law is an illustration. It is very evident that we need not go on working long hours when machines are installed to do the work, but what is the result of the attempt to regulate wages and hours? A minimum of 30c per hour 42 hours per week, or a weekly wage of $12.60 if working full time, certainly is not sufficient to keep the wheels of industry moving. $12.60 will buy $12.60 worth of commodities when that consumer and his family are duty bound to consume possibly forty dollars' worth. 

To limit the hours without increasing wages merely gives work to more people but does not increase purchasing power in general. To increase the wage is placing the small operator in an unfavorable position, and he is forced to discontinue or go bankrupt, much to the advantage of the operator who has a factory equipped with modern machinery. Net result, therefore, more concentration, fewer small business men, the small operator pushed over into the wage earning class or possibly on relief.

The effect of the wage-hour law upon the cigar industry in this particular community is a good example of the practical operation of the law. Until recently this business. depended upon workers who produced by hand. Then part of the factories were equipped with machines. About 70 out of 100 were displaced. Some of those displaced were advanced in years and could make only a few hundred cigars per day. This income was a mere pittance compared with what they need to purchase their share of consumable goods, but it allowed them to earn a little and the employer could keep going. As is said so often, it is better than nothing, as is relief also, but not so much better. 

If the Government stands ready to pay an adequate income to those out of work, then they will suffer no hardship, but up to this time many of those on relief have been treated as dependents, unfortunates and in many instances almost like criminals if they happened to get a few extra 75

dollars by working and failing to report. 

Recently (1940) there was a proposal in New Jersey (Glover report) to tag as paupers three year reliefers in that state and disfranchise them, with several other suggestions in a similar vein.

Let us bear this in mind. Those on relief are not dependents in the sense of being objects of charity. They are essential as consumers, and the producer must depend upon the unemployed as well as the employed to consume the goods produced by machinery.

The enforcement of a wage-hour law simply means that those employing labor, which is no longer young and alert, must close their factories and the employees are obliged to go on relief or hope for the time when they can draw an old age pension, meager though it may be.

The attitude of manufacturers was well expressed by one employer who said to me, "It is our problem to install enough equipment to produce as much in 42 hours as we did in 55 hours before."

The employer will not employ more labor if he can avoid it, but he will install more machinery so that he can produce the same amount by working 42 hours as he did with the same force when they worked 55 hours. Thus the installation of machinery under our present system will defeat the purpose of any law designed to improve conditions by regulating hours and wages.

I know whereof I speak as I too am an employer of labor and realize that under our competitive system, it is a matter of getting your competitor before he gets you, and in order to meet competition we reduce overhead whenever and wherever possible. At present this is usually done by the installation of more machinery. 

If hours of labor are to be lessened, it is first necessary to increase purchasing power. This will increase the demand for labor, and if all of the unemployed cannot be absorbed, then shorten the hours until each and every one can be called upon to do his share. 

First, give to the consumer sufficient to buy the goods on 76

condition that as soon as there is a demand for labor he will be called upon to do his share, and this is the one part of the plan that will be compulsory. As soon as there is work to be done, the consumer of goods must be at his post and be ready to do his share whether that be four hours, eight hours or more or less.

Let the reader bear this in mind constantly. Prosperity never came through the employment of labor. Purchasing power was not increased sufficiently to start the wheels turning rapidly by paying wages. The pump had to be primed, but with sufficient to keep the water flowing. The difficulty during the past has been, that the pump was primed through speculation and when the water got low instead of putting in more water, a frightened public kept on drawing out, with the result that the water got so low that the pump stopped drawing entirely, and then we sat by and almost famished waiting for another spring freshet. 

One plan proposed, is to give to those over the age of 60 years $200 per month. The formulation of this plan is at least a recognition of the basic fact, that in order to distribute the abundance of commodities produced, it is necessary to have more purchasing power. 

I have no personal objection to trying this plan, as I am quite sure that the system under it would not be more unstable than the one under which we operate at present, and without an experiment we are seldom sure of results. 

I am raising two questions, however, regarding a plan of this type: (1) Why must the individual wait until he has reached a specified age until he is assured of sufficient to support him comfortably, and (2) will the raising of money by a sales, gross income, or transactions tax, not increase prices, with the result that unlimited inflation will follow with the inevitable collapse as always occurred before?

We are told that if the old folks are taken out of production that this will make room for the younger workmen. 

Why an arbitrary age limit when men must step aside?

Isn't it true that some men at 60 or 65 are more able to work than others at 40 or 45? And isn't it also true that 77

most of those above the age of 60 years are out of employment already on account of their age. Why not let men work so long as they are physically able, but see that their income is adequate?

It is said that if the old folks spend their money it will create a demand and the younger people will then be employed. 

If the old folks are not able to work, will they be able to spend as much as is necessary to keep the industrial machine operating?

This matter of allowing the money to trickle down from the top has been tried before. President Herbert Hoover tried it when he caused huge loans to be made by the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, but for some reason it did not trickle very far.

Why not try the plan of putting purchasing power directly into the hands of all of those who consume and see whether it will not move up to the top? We get the best light effect by placing oil at the bottom and letting it move upward through the wick. We have always tried to let the medium of exchange trickle downward. Let us find out what happens if it is given a chance to move upward.

What is always the effect of taxation of products? I stop for gasoline. I read the sign, gas 11¢, tax 5¢. If it weren't for the tax the gasoline would cost 11c instead of 16c. 

Higher prices are paid for cigars, cigarettes and alcoholic beverages on account of the tax. Imported goods are higher because the tariff has added to the price.

If taxation is general, the consumer in order to buy the goods must have a larger income. Higher wages mean higher manufacturing costs and consequently higher prices. 

These in turn mean higher wages with prices always about two jumps ahead of wages, until such time. as the peak is reached and after that the inevitable collapse.

If our system is to be stable, taxation can only be for the purpose of preventing excessive incomes and accumulations in the form of estates.

It is my firm conviction that there cannot be much im- 78

provement in our economic system unless we are willing to make experiments. Do not misunderstand me. I certainly do know that a plan that is to apply to a nation of more than 130,000,000 population cannot be put on and off like a cloak. 

We cannot use a test tube like the chemist in his laboratory. 

The subject is too big for that, but I do propose that we should not insist upon continuing any system simply because it is old. If the system does not work, let's admit it, and then try honestly, unselfishly and without prejudice to devise and adopt a plan that will work under changed conditions.

We frequently boast of our efficiency. When I examine the record and find how often we have virtually gone through bankruptcy, when for example there is scarcely a Railroad Company of any consequence in the United States that has not at some time gone through a receivership or bankruptcy, I am almost convinced that we were so blessed by Nature that we have made progress in spite of our inefficiency and not because of our efficiency. 

When the stock of outstanding companies has dropped from $514 per share in 1929 to 8712¢ in 1940, from $279 in 

1893 to 25¢ in 1940, from $700 in 1916 to 714 in 1932, and from $1000 in 1901 to 7% in 1940, there is something seriously the matter either with the management, or the system under which they are operating. 

A great deal has been said about increased purchasing power, and I have taken the position that there cannot be sufficient created by wages, taxation or by borrowing, or all of them combined. Then, how can it be created? 

Assuming that it is necessary for the head of every family to have an income of $40 per week in order to buy sufficient goods to keep the wheels of industry in motion. A is unemployed, or if he is employed his income may be only $15 or less per week. If unemployed and he must consume $40 worth of goods, then he must get the $40 somewhere. If he has no job and consequently no income, then the $40 must be advanced to him as his part of the medium of exchange required to buy his share of goods. 79

It is evident that there is only one source from which this income can flow and that is the group functioning through its Government. 

If the $40 is issued, what will be the basis for such an issue? The wealth of the nation. The grain, the factories, the roads, the automobiles, and all other commodities and services which may be classed as the Nation's wealth. 

But, says some one, what right has the group through the Government to issue money or the medium of exchange against property owned by individuals as private property. 

In last analysis who owns property, the individual or the group? We as individuals hold it only so long as the group does not need it.

In time of stress, disaster or emergency, are there any questions raised about property rights? If property must be destroyed to stop a fire reaching conflagration proportions, is there any one to ask whether the act is constitutional? In times of invasion by a foreign foe and very often when a Government has imperialistic designs, we are called upon to give not only our property but our splendid boys for cannon fodder as well. If the group wants to take our property by taxation, they do it. It can be taken legally by right of eminent domain and lastly, by that from which there is no appeal, physical force.

To say that title to property is ultimately in the group doesn't mean that the group will take it. We destroy property for war purposes. By increasing the medium of exchange we create more property and therefore the person holding property becomes more secure. 

Once sufficient of the medium of exchange is issued to provide for the wants of those whose incomes are inadequate, then there will be a demand for labor and the unemployed, and even those who are idle voluntarily can be put back to work, each and every one required to do that which is necessary to produce as much as he consumes. 

If some one is aged or incapacitated, he is still important as a consumer of both goods and services and is entitled to his income. 80

If an employee receives through his employment only $15 per week, and he must have $40 to buy his share of goods, then he must get the remainder from some other source, and that source will be the same as the one from which comes the $40 for the unemployed, the aged or infirm. 

This will assist the small business man who cannot pay high wages, and will be a boon to those who reside some distance from the centers of population, where they must be employed if the business man in the smaller towns is forced to discontinue.

But, some one says, when money becomes plentiful prices will advance and uncontrolled inflation will follow. 

Not necessarily. It has always been true that an increase in purchasing power has resulted in increased prices, but that was because prices were the result of production costs and production costs at least partly the result of wages. One follows the other like the cat chasing its tail, always following, but never getting any closer, until it finally falls exhausted.

It is true that we are accustomed to see an increase in purchasing power followed by an increase in prices and in order to avoid this some price regulation may be necessary. 

On the other hand, the plan suggested is vastly different from what we have experienced. When prices depend upon increased purchasing power through speculative inflation, then prices must advance, but if purchasing power is increased without adding to the cost, then there is no necessity for an increase in price and the producer could have no excuse for doing so.

What is there to prevent this medium of exchange from accumulating so that the value would decrease on account of the quantity?

In the first place, not as much would be required as appears at first thought.

A check for $40, or money if we choose to call it that instead, can circulate and move considerably more than $40 worth of merchandise. If a $40 check passes through 10 hands, before it is returned to the place of origin, $400 worth 81

of commodities are exchanged, and if it passes through 20 hands, it causes to move from producer to consumer $800 worth of commodities, and so on, the more hands through which it passes, the more commodities moved. A counterfeit $100 bill, if undetected until it passes through 100 hands, will have moved $10,000 worth of commodities, and yet has no value and was fictitious from the beginning. 

Excessive accumulations are possible and there must be some means of avoiding this. Possibly income and inheritance taxes would suffice as a remedy, with an extension of credit for those in the low income group, which can be cancelled periodically if the debt cannot be paid. 

The currency issued by the Government at present has nothing back of it except the promise by the Government to pay. We are off the gold standard, and even when we were on it, the gold reserve was considerably less than 100% of the currency issued.

Purchasing power is created by banks but not in a sufficient amount, and to depend upon speculative inflation simply means one collapse after another. 

There is no reason why the Government could not issue more of the medium of exchange in order that purchasing power and commodities and services produced should balance. So long as the two are out of balance we cannot have a smoothly operating system. 

So much emphasis has been placed upon foreign markets. 

We are forced to realize that these markets are closing rapidly. Practically all nations now want to sell but not to buy, as they are producing what we have to sell, except when at war. In many instances we furnished the machinery, and if we furnish the machinery, we cannot expect to keep on selling goods. Even nations formerly considered backward, such as China and India, are sending their young men to other countries and they bring back European and American ideas. The extent to which these countries mechanize their industries, to that extent their doors will be closed to trade ith other nations. 82

The creation of artificial products such as nylon to displace silk; neoprene, artificial rubber; and nitrates by synthetic processes, means that we will no longer buy from nations furnishing the natural products. 

We buy large quantities of raw silk from Japan. If nylon takes the place of silk, our trade with Japan will be affected materially. Japan realizes that this is happening and because her trade with other countries is being cut off, is doing what is necessary to get into China in order to find another outlet for its goods. 

Much emphasis has been placed upon the importance of South American trade. Can we expect to trade if we continue to create products such as nitrates, artificial leather and artificial rubber synthetically, and no longer get them or the raw material to produce them, from the several South American and other countries in exchange for our manufactured goods?

Every nation is aiming to become self supporting and can become so if it can learn to distribute its products. 

Every day we are having it impressed upon us that we must depend upon the home market and not upon foreign markets, and after all if we have plenty for all of our own people, why should we worry about foreign markets? 

Whether we like it or not, it will not be long, if it has not happened already, that the home market will be practically the only market, and then we must learn to distribute our products among our own people or suffer the consequences. 

These consequences are, that not only the consumer becomes poverty stricken when he cannot get the merchandise, but the producer, who must depend upon the consumer, has on his hands worthless property because it has ceased to have the capacity to earn. It can only earn when the goods flow freely from the factory or farm.

Stop the flowing stream of commodities and the producer goes into bankruptcy. The consumer on one hand poverty stricken because he has too little, and the producer on the other hand poverty stricken because he has too much. 

Many seem to fear that if goods are distributed so that the 83

amount each one receives will approach an equal share, that everyone will be poor. Those who fear do not know the possibilities of the power age. We have been accustomed to think of wealth in terms of money. It will be necessary to think of wealth in terms of goods and services instead of money.

It discredits our intelligence to have all of the comforts of life within our grasp and yet not be able to have more than a bare existence.

Why do men fear change? This attitude seems to have originated centuries ago. One can understand why ignorant people oppose change, but why should intelligent people do so?

It has been only recently that we have allowed science to have a free hand.

Dr. Servetus was a scientist and John Calvin, the founder of Presbyterianism, allowed him to be burned at the stake. 

Galileo was forced to recant. In his day all science was black magic and the work of his Satanic majesty. 

The Inquisition took its toll of many thousands because they questioned the validity of time-worn tradition. 

Why can we not study the situation with an open mind, preserve that which is applicable and discard that which has become unworkable? 

How thankful we are today that the early scientists risked their lives in unravelling Nature's secrets to give us the benefit of their discoveries.

Do we object to anesthetics when we realize that not so many years ago a major operation meant almost certain death, and that, after a period of agony resulting from the lack of anesthetics and antiseptics; yet those pioneers had to fight every step of the way against prejudice, ignorance and superstition?

With all of the opportunities for securing information at hand, can we not realize that what we now call a depression is more than that? That it is a condition, which should mark the end of the age of scarcity and the beginning of the age of abundance. That the age of scarcity should be a 84

thing of the past and that there is abundance for all, if we allow our goods to flow freely and think not so much of balancing the budget but more about balancing consumption on one hand with production on the other. 

Much is said about balancing the budget while we are doing our best, not intentionally of course, to prevent the budget from being balanced. 

First in the power age, we produce with a minimum of human labor. This means unemployment. The Government is called upon to assist. Result, Government spending for relief. In order to do this the Government must have an income. This means more taxes or borrow money. We do not allow the Government to tax sufficiently, therefore it must borrow and the public debt increases. Balance the budget and what happens? Less for relief, less purchasing power, less for National defense, and National defense means employment of labor for the building of ships, guns, airplanes and other war material. Gradually the web is woven a little tighter. The industries, in order to reduce overhead and compete, install more machines. This increases the relief load. A heavier relief load, more taxes or more borrowed money. 

Someone asks, what did we do in the past? The answer is simple. We went into bankruptcy, cleaned the slate and started from the bottom. 

Why don't we do the same now? Conditions have changed. So little was done by machines in those days that by investing a comparatively small amount those out of work could soon be re-employed. Today Dupont builds a $11,000,000 plant and employs 850 men. Formerly an investment of that size would have employed many thousands. 

Buying capacity had to be created by rising prices and bankruptcy had to follow to relieve the inflation. 

Today so much responsibility has been thrown over on the public by our relief problems, that the Government must now carry the load and the result must be either inflation or bankruptcy. If that inflation is speculative, repudiation is the only alternative. If it is a proper increase in the me- 85

dium of exchange so that production and consumption can be balanced, then repudiation or bankruptcy is unnecessary. 

A remedy that is adequate may be somewhat disturbing for some of our existing institutions, but is it not more advisable to perform an operation than to risk the life of the patient by allowing the disease to run its course? Poor circulation in the human body results in death usually after a prolonged period of suffering. Poor circulation in the social body will have the same effect.

It is rather difficult to visualize conditions in the Roman Empire as being similar to ours, but they were similar to a certain degree.

The Romans brought the barbaric Teutons from the North as slaves. The slaves did the work and the freemen became the unemployed. The Teutonic slaves became more numerous than the Romans. The empire weakened and became an easy prey for the tribes from the North. 

Today our machines take the place of the Roman slave.

Our machines do the work but do not consume even as much as the slaves. The product of the machine must be consumed by human beings; the unemployed have no income and the employed insufficient to buy the goods produced, and there we are again, back to the system under which we must destroy and go bankrupt or manufacture war material to keep the wheels turning.

But suppose in this war business the people refuse to be cannon fodder any longer. Suppose that opposing armies realizing that modern wars are wars for markets, should refuse to attack. Suppose our workmen should say, we will not work on war material, especially when we know that it is going to be sent abroad to murder innocent men, women and children. Suppose we should say there will be no more shipments of scrap iron, steel, copper and gasoline to Japan. 

How soon would the war end? Probably in a very short time.

This may be easy to suppose but it is hard to put into practice. We have become so dependent upon someone else for our means of earning a livelihood that we are not in posi- 86

tion to act. If I quit my job there are perhaps a hundred ready to take my place. So rather than appear foolish, and be numbered among the unemployed I contribute my bit to the slaughter and salve my conscience by saying I hope that some day conditions will be different. 

How then does our system operate? Less employment, less buying power; less buying power, a continued decrease in production and employment. Ultimate result, destitution, misery and want. Starvation in the midst of plenty in a nation boasting of its intelligence, and blessed with a superabundance of Nature's gifts. 87

CHAPTER XIII CONCLUSION 

On this cold January night, with the temperature at some places a few degrees above and at others below zero, we are saddened by the thought that there are families suffering from cold due to lack of fuel. In the coal regions for a number of years the industry engaged in removing coal from old Mother Earth, where the sunshine of prehistoric ages has been stored, has been in distress. Even now a plan has been devised to restrict production in order to maintain the price while thousands of families huddled together in poorly heated homes are wondering whether there is enough in the family purse to purchase a few pounds of coal, which is carried home in buckets or bags or hauled on small sleds or express wagons.

And this is at a time and in a land where it is possible to produce not only in abundance but in superabundance, where with the aid of mechanical devices and solar power, one man is capable of supporting himself and many more besides, an age with the possibility of leisure, education, employment and security for all, including those who are so unfortunate as to be handicapped by Nature and those who have gone over the crest of the hill with their faces turned toward the setting sun.

This is the age of which men dreamed for centuries. The power age. The age when men are beginning to learn that the great forces of Nature which so often have been destructive can be used for man's benefit, if he is willing to obey the laws which have been developed throughout the countless centuries, even before man made his appearance upon the earth.

Today we harness the streams and other forces and gather 88

from them unseen power which can be used either for man's comfort or man's destruction.

With the control of these sources of power, routine tasks that consumed our energy can be performed by mechanical means and we should be left free for the accomplishment of that which can cause the human race to live at peace and to seek all of the finer things of life. 

The possibilities of the power age are beyond the scope of the human imagination. In 1712 one steam power unit did the work of 56 men. By 1871 the 56 had increased to 20,000. 

Today a single unit on a 24-hour basis develops power equivalent to 9,000,000 men, and all of this with practically no human labor. At the present moment the United States has an installed capacity of more than 2 billion 560 million (2,560,000,000) horse power in engines to do work. If these installed engines were operated continuously at capacity, it would require more than 100 times the number of adult workers now living on the earth to equal this output by human labor alone.

No matter where we go today, whether it be in the office, in the factory, or on the farm, we find machines doing the tasks formerly performed by human hands. 

Science has practically freed us from dependence on foreign lands. No longer need we make the long journey round Cape Horn, a distance of at least 5,000 miles, to bring nitrates from Chili. Sixty miles of nitrogen rise vertically above us and the electric current brings fertilizer falling like snow flakes from the point of a carbon needle, extracted solely from the atmosphere. 

Nitrate fleets are discarded, but many of the unemployed crews instead of rejoicing in the abundance of nitrates are in want because science has robbed them of the means of earning a livelihood.

Production of foodstuffs outstrips the growth of population. Vegetables, fruits and grains are raised in abundance. 

Canada today produces 500 million bushels of wheat. We were told in 1898 that 228 million bushels was the limit. 

The possible productivity of British soil has been doubled 89

in the last ten years by science and invention. It is also estimated by good authority that 4,000 men equipped with modern machinery could produce all of the wheat needed by the United States of America. Agrobiologists claim that 

500,000 persons on a tract of land the size of Kansas could provide all the foodstuffs necessary for the people living in the United States.

The art of mass production has been mastered, but we have not yet learned the art of mass distribution and mass consumption.

The machine which should be man's greatest benefactor has often been a curse instead. Scientist and machine stand ready to give us every conceivable thing we need, food, clothing, shelter, leisure and security. The gift which should enrich often impoverishes instead. New inventions are hidden lest they interefere with established interests. 

Only a few years ago science had produced more than we could sell, while thousands of families had not the wherewithal to keep them in health and comfort. To correct this condition we sought scarcity in the midst of plenty. Half a million sheep were burned in Chili, six million dairy cattle and two million pigs destroyed in the United States, twenty million bags of Brazilian coffee dumped into the Pacific Ocean, almost a small mountain of oranges destroyed in California, milk was poured down drains, thousands of acres of growing grain plowed under, boat loads of vegetables dumped into the ocean off the Jersey Coast, and a ship load of Spanish oranges shoveled into the Irish Sea, while the ships and empty boats that had carried the oranges and vegetables steamed into their respective harbors amongst children for whom these articles cast into the ocean were an unattainable luxury.

As if to destroy the evidence of the age of plenty we have now turned to restriction. Orders have gone forth to India, Ceylon and the Dutch East Indies to restrict tea by 121 million pounds, a quantity which would have filled every English larder with 15 pounds of an essential commodity. 

In Pennsylvania the output of anthracite coal is restricted in 90

order to maintain prices. Production of agricultural products is limited by the United States government. 

As a result of these restrictions and many others, we are led to believe that we are poor while machines are idle, while unemployed miners' children shiver from lack of fuel, tailors go threadbare, producers of food stuffs go back to their miserable quarters hungry, and while the unemployed are looked upon as objects of charity and forced to exist upon an inadequate Government dole, after they have exhausted the cherished earnings of a lifetime, which had been laid aside with the fond hope of educating their children or using it during the twilight of life, after years of patient and unceasing toil.

Is it reasonable that in this age of possibilities, this age of science and superabundance, that we should fail to grasp the opportunity and allow civilization to perish because we refuse to adjust ourselves to changing conditions? 

When we study the history of the human race we are almost forced to the conclusion that it has often tried to commit suicide, but failed to accomplish more than to retard its progress for perhaps thousands of years. 

It may be impossible for the human race to destroy itself, but with the forces of Nature as never before within man's control, used for his destruction, results can not be anything but most devastating. Modern wars are the best illustrations.

The two great social diseases, poverty and war are still with us.

America is at the cross roads. Two paths lie open before us, one leading to peace, harmony, good will, comfort and security in the midst of plenty; the other to war, discord, destruction, scarcity, disease and untold suffering. 

A grave responsibility rests upon our shoulders. For the sake of this generation and of millions yet unborn, let us not make the wrong choice. 91
