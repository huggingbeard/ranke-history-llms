Foreword

California timberland owners and operators have been given the opportunity to regulate their woods activities so as to insure a future source of timber for the people of the State and the continuance of the forest industry. To accomplish these ends the State 

Legislature, in 1945, declared that regulations were needed and wisely decided that the industry itself could best determine what practical actions should be taken to leave the land in a productive condition after logging and to protect present and future forest crops from destruction by fire, insects and disease.

The Redwood Forest District (see map, page 1) 

is one of four forest districts outlined in the Forest 

Practice Act, Chapter 85, Statutes of 1945, as amended in 1947. The forest practice rules for this district have been prepared by a committee of forest land owners and operators, have received the voted approval of more than two-thirds the private timberland ownership of the district, and were given the force of law by approval of the California State Board of Forestry on 

February 13, 1947.

The type of future the California forest industry is to enjoy is largely dependent upon the success of this program. Realizing this the forest practice committee in each of the forest districts spent several months in careful preparation of the rules. Final success of the program depends upon the operators and the men in the woods. The State Forester is charged 

Iwith the administration of the Forest Practice Act. 

The State Division of Forestry will cooperate with operators who desire suggestions as to how they might best fit their operations to the rules.

DEWITT NELSON State Forester

Article 6. Definitions 

Operator-as used in these rules, shall mean any person, copartnership, firm, corporation or company, or a recognized agent or representative thereof engaged in logging of timber or harvesting any forest products for commercial purposes. 

Diameter-as used in these rules shall mean the average diameter of a tree, outside the bark at a point 4½ feet above average ground level unless otherwise stated. 

Seed tree-as used in these rules shall mean a coniferous tree with a sound butt, full crown and unbroken top, bearing or capable of bearing a good crop of seed. 

Old growth-Stands of essentially undisturbed virgin timber over 100 years of age.

Young growth-Stands of thrifty timber under 100 years of age.

Forest Practice Act 

Besides setting up the machinery for the preparation, approval and enforcement of rules of forest practice for the four forest districts, the Forest Practice Act also provides for amendments to the rules, the submission of alternate plans, and the registration of all timber operators engaged in the cutting and removal of timber or other forest products. 

Amendments will become law after public hearings, and a vote registering the approval of two-thirds of the forest land ownership, and finally, the approval of the State Board of Forestry. They may be initiated by a petition signed by 50 percent of the private timber and cut-over land ownership. 

Alternate forest management plans may be submitted by forest land owners to the district forest practice committee. Upon receipt of the approval of the committee and the State Board of Forestry, the alternate plan will have the force of law on the property to which it applies. 

Registration of forest operators with the State Forester is required annually. The fee for registration is $1 per year.

Forest Practice Rules—Continued Article 5. Forest Insect and Disease Protection Practices 

915. Every operator and timberland owner shall provide reasonable protection against forest insects and disease. To meet the minimum requirements under this provision, he shall:

A. Assist the State in determining the location of insect and disease outbreaks by reporting any such outbreaks to the State Forester.

B. Cooperate with other landowners and public agencies towards control of insect and disease infestations as authorized by state law. 

END OF FOREST PRACTICE RULES Insects and Diseases in Redwood Forest District 

There are no serious tree-killing insect pests or diseases which attack the coast redwood, therefore protection against insects and diseases for this tree species is not a problem. Other coniferous trees, namely spruce and fir, are attacked sometimes by bark beetles and defoliating insects but these insects seldom reach epidemic proportions to require measures for control. Reports of any outbreaks of infestation should be made immediately to the State Forester who will investigate and recommend measures for control.

State Cooperation in Forest Insect Control 

The State Forester may enter into cooperative agreements with private landowners and other public or private agencies for the eradication of insect infestations. (Pub. Res. C. Secs. 4455 and 4459.) [24]
