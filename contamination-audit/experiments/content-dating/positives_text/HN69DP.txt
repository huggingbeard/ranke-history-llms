FOREWORD 

There is so much to write about Torringford that the choice of material for a short book is very difficult. While there are still many things which I do not and probably never will know about the early days, my available material needs to be boiled down and condensed and much must be omitted from this little book. 

I have been deeply impressed while studying the old records by the courage and accomplishments of the early settlers in providing educational and religious advantages for their children and their children's children.

This year of our Lord 1944, the 200th anniversary of the coming of Torringford's first settler and the 175th anniversary of the settlement of Father Mills, seems an appropriate time for Torringford to be written up.

I owe much to the help of a great many people, some of them no longer living, and I now acknowledge my indebtedness. 

My father and mother always encouraged and helped me in my study of local history and to them this little book is dedicated. 

ELIZABETH. B. GAYLORD 

Elizabeth Blaylord

CHAPTER I Early Settlers 

The name of Torringford dates from the year 1763 (Torring for the township of Torrington and -ford for the township of New Hartford) and implies that there are parts of two towns included. This makes a thorough knowledge of its history more difficult. 

The eastern part of the town of Torrington was called East Torrington until 1763. I do not know what the western part of the town of New Hartford was called, but the people living there were much closer to the people in East Torrington than to the people living on 

Town Hill in New Hartford or farther east.

Torrington was a Windsor town while New Hartford was a Hartford town. We do not need to worry that our land was stolen from the Indians, because both of our towns were a small portion. of the large tract of land purchased in 1720 by the towns of Windsor and Hartford from the Indian proprietors and owners, Weromaug, Weraroquom, Alias Curllow, Nepatoo, Ahanjean, Mawehew, AwBund, Tawhooks, Paconopeet, Takahound, Alias John, Worenough and Wossomaug.

Those on the General Tax List of Windsor for 1720 were taxed enough to raise 52 pounds to pay Windsor's share of this purchase of land from the Indians, with the understanding that they would receive some of the land when it should be divided. The total 1720 tax list of the 136 Windsor taxpayers who in 1732 became Proprietors of Torrington was 6431 pounds, 9 shillings and 5 pence. Their share of the 52 pound payment was probably less than 20 pounds, and for this small sum of money they later received in the three divisions of land and the Mast Swamp divisions more than 18,000 acres of land in Torrington.

The survey and laying out of Torrington was begun in 1733 with Lieut. Roger Newbury of Windsor as surveyor. The town was laid out in eleven tiers, with broad north and south highways between, each tier approximately half a mile from east to west. For some reason the surveyors did not lay out long continuous east and west highways, most of them running through only one of the tiers of land. 

The town of New Hartford was laid out in tiers three quarters of a mile wide instead of half a mile as in Torrington with north and south highways between the tiers. On the boundary between the two towns there is a highway, part of it in Torrington and part in New Hartford. [5]

The western part of Torrington was settled sooner than the eastern part. In October 1740 the General Assembly gave the inhabitants of Torrington (all on the west side) town privileges with authority to "choose their own town officers and order their own prudential affairs as other towns by law are enabled to do". 

The First Church of Christ in Torrington was organized October 

21, 1741 at Mr. John Cook's on Highland Avenue with Rev. Nathaniel

Roberts as minister. Mr. Roberts was hired by the town at town meetings and paid by the town until 1758 when the First Ecclesiastical 

Society took over. 

Mr. Abraham Dibble, the first settler of East Torrington, came in 

1744 and settled on the east side of what is now called Torringford Street, a little north of the present Harwinton Road. He was first mentioned in town records at the town meeting of January 14, 1745. 

Benjamin Bissell, second settler of East Torrington, came in 1745.

He soon owned 164 acres of land along the east side of Torringford Street from just north of the Capel house to the east and west highway on which Mr. Albrecht lives. He was mentioned in the town meeting record of December 9, 1745. 

The third settler was John Birge who came in 1746. He probably lived on the hill where Mr. Emanuel Richard lives now. Nehemiah Gaylord also came in 1746.

In 1748 there were seven settlers in East Torrington, Abraham 

Dibell, Benjamin Bissell, Nehemiah Gaylord, Elijah Gaylord, Gideon 

Loomis, John Birg, Thomas Dibell. Elijah Gaylord lived just south of the highway to the Torringford Cemetery and soon bought considerable land north of that Highway. Thomas Dibell lived south

Gideon Loomis lived on the west side of Torringford Street near the south end. Nehemiah lived where the Brasels live now. 

At this time the General Assembly had sent a committee to Torrington to select the exact site for the first meeting house on the west side of the town. This location was very inconvenient for the settlers of East Torrington on account of the long distance and the lack of any good east and west highway between the two sides of the town. 

On November 15, 1748 these seven men of East Torrington signed an agreement with twenty-six settlers on the "Westerly Side of the Mast Swamp and the Red Mountain". The East Torrington men agreed that in times to come they would never intermeddle about the building and finishing of the First Meeting House, and the West Torrington men agreed to acquit those on the east side of the town from paying any part of the building and finishing of the meeting house, the men of East Torrington paying only their proportional part with the whole town towards the support of the ministry so long as the town should remain in one ecclesiastical society. 

In 1757 the number of settlers had increased by at least the following fourteen people: Jonathan Kelsey, Jonathan Gillett, Joshua 

Austin, Shubael Griswold, Benjamin Matthews, Nathan Kelsey, David 

Birge, Ebenezer Winchell, Charles Mather, Aaron Yale, John Burr, Jane Loomis, Samuel Dorwin, Ephraim Dorwin.

Jonathan Kelsey lived on the west side of Torringford Street and his house is still standing, the oldest house now standing on this side of the town. In 1766 he deeded the north half of the house to his son Jonathan Jr., who had helped him build the house. After the death of both father and son, the house was sold in 1792 to Timothy Loomis, and stayed in the Loomis family until it was bought by Tullius Hayden in 1849. For many years it has been called the Huxford house. Nathan Kelsey's house was a little north on land he bought from his father Jonathan. 

Jonathan Gillett owned land on the west side of Torringford

Street beginning a few rods south of the Burnell place and running north to West Pearl Street. He deeded the south fourteen acres to 

Nehemiah Gaylord. He died about 1760, and the remainder of his land was split up, most of it going to David Soper. His widow Mary died in Torringford May 19, 1782. 

Shubael Griswold owned 119 acres south from the State Road (he deeded land for the road from Torringford Street to West Street by way of Dibble Street). In 1754 he built his colonial house (called a tavern) on the site of Edson Davis' fruit house. There was a wigwam of friendly Indians just a short distance south of this house. [7]

In 1755 Joshua Austin bought the seventy acre tract between Shubael Griswold and Nehemiah Gaylord and probably built the old colonial house the foundation of which is just south of the Brasel house. In 1762 Joshua sold to John Strong Jr. of Windsor.

At this time Abraham Dibble, first settler on the east side of town had deeded his farm near the south end of Torringford Street to Ebenezer Winchell and had bought land north of Nathan Kelsey, the Old South Road to Albany cutting the northeast corner of it. In his house just north of Nathan Kelsey's Abraham died in November 1760 and his only son, Abraham Jr., 20 years old, died the same month.

Benjamin Matthews was a heavy land owner here in the early days. His house was probably a little way north of Abraham Dibble and a little south of John Burr's house.

John Burr had built a large colonial house (tavern) at the sharp turn in the old South Road very near the present Singer bungalow. 

Mr. Burr owned a large amount of land to the north of his house and elsewhere.

John Burr bought the land where his house stood from Ephraim and Samuel Dorwin. Samuel also owned over one hundred acres on the east side of Torringford Street, later the Hayden farm. He had a house about where the ell at the south end of the Shields house stands. Ephraim Dorwin lived on the east side of Torringford Street near the south end on the hill north of Lead Mine Brooks' southerly crossing of the street. Both Dorwin brothers moved away to Lanesboro, Mass. early in the 1760's.

David Birge settled on the west side of Torringford Street somewhat south of his brother John. He died in 1764 leaving his wife, Elizabeth Allen of Windsor with three small children. Her second husband was probably a Catlin of Harwinton, and she is buried in the Torringford Cemetery beside Shubael Griswold as his relict. 

Charles Mather lived on the Many place until 1769 when he sold to his brother Zacheriah, who lived there until 1794 when he sold to 

Dr. Samuel Woodward. Charles Mather, who married Ruth, daughter of Deacon Jonathan Kelsey, moved into the town of New Hartford and lived on the east side of Torringford Street. 

Aaron Yale lived in a small house at the corner of Burr Hill Road and Torringford West Street. When the part of the Old South Road to Albany from the Shields place on Torringford Street to the Norfolk line was surveyed in November 1761, Aaron had a mill some distance up Sawmill Brook at the southerly end of Observation Mountain (Burr Mountain). 

I cannot place Jane Loomis. [8]

CHAPTER II Ecclesiastical Affairs 

To our early settlers an opportunity for attending a place of worship and providing schooling for their children seemed very important.

At town meetings it was voted that Mr. Nathaniel Roberts should preach six Sabbaths a year on the East Side of the Town in 1754 and 1755. For the year 1756 it was voted that Mr. Roberts should preach on the East Side in proportion to the tax list and for 1757 that for four months the East Side settlers need not pay Mr. Roberts but might use the money to hire an orthodox minister to preach among them. 

In May 1757 eighteen men and one woman living on the East Side of the town presented a petition to the General Assembly in which they say that they are from five to seven miles from the place of worship, and are obliged to attend church, some in New Hartford and some in Harwinton: "that our number is so increased, being about twenty families, we think it our duty, (as it will be much for our ease) to have preaching among ourselves. We therefore pray your honors would grant us the liberty to have preaching among ourselves such part of the year as we may be able." They also pray to be exempt from supporting worship at Torrington Church "since we cannot take the benefit thereof, and especially since the burden of supporting the present ministry will be easy without our help.” 

This petition was granted by the General Assembly and I consider this the real beginning of the East Side Society. [ 10 ]

The inhabitants of the East Side of Torrington held their first recorded meeting October 27, 1757 with Deacon Jonathan Kelsey as 

Moderator. Nehemiah Gaylord was chosen Clerk and Joshua Austin treasurer, and Jonathan Kelsey, Exekiel and Benjamin Bissell a committee to agree with a minister of the Gospel. It was voted to raise ten pounds to support the Gospel. 

At a meeting of the Inhabitants of the East Side of Torrington on December 25, 1758 a committee of three was appointed "to apply to the Assembly to see if we can get the west part of New Hartford and the East part of Torrington set off to be a Society.” 

At a Town Meeting in Torrington April 23, 1759 it was “voted that the East Side of the Town should have four tier and a half from the East Side of the Said Town in order for a Society." 

In May 1759, the people of the East Side asked the General Assembly to incorporate a society to include four and a half tiers in Torrington and the western tier of lots in New Hartford; or, if that may not be granted, that the people in said western tier may be exempt from supporting in New Hartford, that they may support in Torringford. They made this petition "although at present we are scarcely able to settle a minister or support the gospel, as we should be glad to do; yet since our circumstances are singular, and it would be much for the encouragement of settling and improving the land among us, and so of increasing our ability to the above said purpose, if we were made a distinct society." This petition was denied by both houses of the Assembly.

In October 1759 East Torrington presented another petition signed by twenty men, stating that there were then in East Torrington twenty-two families and one hundred and sixty-six persons; that they were of small estate, and that their children were not large enough to afford much help, and they ask that they may be exempt from "public charges" in order that they may maintain worship among themselves. 

This petition was denied by both houses of the Assembly. 

At a meeting of the Inhabitants of the East Side of Torrington February 4, 1760 it was "voted by the Society to pay to Mr. Joshua 

Austin fifteen pounds money when he shall get the Society Exempt from paying country Taxes." These country taxes were probably imposed because a part of the Old South Road to Albany, a government or colony road, was laid out in 1759 from the New Hartford town line to Torringford Street (just south of the Shields house). 

In May 1760, nineteen men of East Torrington petitioned to be exempt from paying country taxes, that they may be able to support the gospel among them, since they can not do both. The petition covers two foolscap sheets and is urgent and eloquent. They plead that [11]

-- if they go abroad to attend public worship, they have to start so early, and do so much work in order to do it, that it does not comport with the right keeping of the day. They speak of being but twenty-one families and most of us but new beginners on new rough farms. They mention the expense "for making public highways fit for travel and the great cost we are and must be at for schooling our children being blessed with large families of small children." They pleaded for their young and numerous children who can not be taken out of the place to meeting and for the aged people, "who think it hard to be denied the means of grace now in the last stages of their lives." This petition was granted during the pleasure of the Assembly. we

On December 19, 1760 the Inhabitants of the East Side of Torrington "voted to begin to hire Preaching the first Sabbath in April next and to continue to the last Sabbath in November." On March 4, 1761 it was "voted to Raise forty six pounds thirteen shillings and four pence money to hire preaching this year."

December 3, 1761 the Inhabitants of the East Side of Torrington "voted Joshua Austin and Ebenezer Miller a Committy for to go to town meeting at New Hartford to get the west tier of New Hartford voted off to us to make a Society." Later in December 1761, the town of New Hartford "voted our consent, or to manifest our minds that we are willing, to have four miles of the south end of the west tier of lots in New Hartford annexed, or set off, to the east part or society of Torrington, to pay their ministerial charge there." 

March 15, 1762 the Inhabitants of the East Side of Torrington "voted that we will send to the Assembly next May to be Incorporated into a Society and to get a party of New Hartford Enexed to us.” In 

May 1762 the lower house of the Assembly granted this petition but the upper house refused it. The early settlers petitioned again in 

October 1762, that the proposed society be incorporated, and that the New Hartford part be exempt from country rates, and from ministerial and school charges in New Hartford, in order that the proposed society may build a house of worship and settle a minister. This petition, also the one made in the spring of 1763 was rejected. 

In September 1763 the petition for incorporation was granted by the General Assembly and the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society was incorporated in October 1763.

The act of incorporation reads as follows: "Upon the memorial of . 

the inhabitants of the east part of the township of Torrington, in the county of Litchfield, praying to be made a distinct ecclesiastical society, with all the powers and privileges of other societies in this colony; also that the west tier of lots in the township of New Hart- [12]

ford beginning at the south bounds of said New Hartford, and to extend north four miles, be annexed to said society; and also that the said inhabitants now living, or that shall hereafter live on said west tier, be exempted from paying country rates, &c as per memorial on file- Resolved by this Assembly, That the inhabitants living on the east part of the township of said Torrington, viz: to begin at the east bounds of said Torrington and to extend west four tiers and a half of lots; and also that the west tier of lots in the township of New Hartford, from the south boundary of said New Hartford to extend north four miles, be, and they are hereby made and constituted, one distinct ecclesiastical society, with all the privileges and powers that other ecclesiastical societies in this colony are vested with, and shall be known and called by the name of Torringford; and also that the inhabitants now living, or that shall hereafter live on said west tier of lots in said New Hartford, shall be exempted from paying colony taxes during the pleasure of this Assembly."

In May, 1786 the Assembly on petition added one half of the second tier of lots in New Hartford. As thus constituted, the Torrington side of the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society is about two and one quarter miles wide and six miles long, and the New Hartford Side one and one-eighth miles wide and four miles long, including about seventeen square miles in the entire Society. 

The Torrington Ecclesiastical Society handled schooling until 1795 when the Torringford School Society took over. The Ecclesiastical Society held its annual meetings in December, while the School Society held its meetings in October. 

The last meeting of the Torringford School Society, recorded in the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society book, was held on the second 

Monday of October 1821.

CHAPTER III 

Ministers and Deacons

The first minister to preach on the East Side of Torrington was 

Rev. Nathaniel Roberts, who was hired by the Town of Torrington in 

1741 and who was paid by the Town until 1758. After that time he was paid by the First Ecclesiastical Society on the west side of town. By vote of the town, Rev. Mr. Roberts preached in East Torrington six

Sabbaths in 1754 and in 1755, and a few Sabbaths in 1756. From that time until the settlement of a pastor it is probable that preaching was maintained at least part of the year, although the names of some of the preachers are not recorded.

In March, 1761 the people of East Torrington voted to raise money to pay Mr. Heaton for preaching. In March 1763 they voted to employ "Mr. Gould." This was Rev. Ebenezer Gould who was then living on Torringford Street, where Mr. Shields lives now. 

In September, 1764 the people of Torringford voted to continue to employ “Mr. Ebenezer Davenport." They gave him a probationary call in June 1765 and in July following, a call to settle, but he declined. [14]

The people made further attempts to secure Mr. Davenport's services in May 1766 and in August 1766 renewed their call, offering him a larger settlement and salary. This call was again declined. 

The Ecclesiastical Society voted in March 1768 to apply to the Association of the county to grant some preaching for the year. In 

September 1768 it was voted to apply to Mr. Judson and Mr. Tread- 

Iway as candidates.

The next candidate was Mr. Samuel John Mills, who was licensed by the Association of Litchfield County February 7, 1766. He was examined by the Association September 20, 1768 and commended as a suitable person to be settled over the church in Torringford and probably began to preach here about that time. In February 1769 the people gave him a probationary call, and in March, a call to settle. 

They offered him a settlement of two hundred pounds, to be paid in three years in annual installments, a salary of fifty-five pounds, to be increased five pounds yearly until it reached seventy pounds, to be paid one-half in money and the other half in wheat, rye and Indian corn at the market price, and his fire-wood. Mr. Mills accepted the call and the Torringford Society appointed Lieut. Shubael Griswold, Elijah Gaylord, Jabez Gillett, and John Strong tavern-keepers for, the ordination.

On Tuesday, June 27, the Consociation of Litchfield County met at the house of Mr. Nehemiah Gaylord and examined the candidate. 

The examination was unanimously sustained. On Wednesday, June 

28, 1769, Mr. Mills was ordained to the work of the pastorate in Torringford. Torringford made a very favorable impression on Mr. Mills, who is reported to have said, "Here let me live, and here let ine die!"

CHAPTER IV Torringford Meeting Houses 

The facts about the first meeting house are taken largely from the Torringford Centennial Book and I can vouch for their accuracy. I wrote the account of the present building in 1939. 

For many years, the settlers worshiped in some private dwelling. 

In March, 1763, it was voted, "that Nehemiah Gaylord shall have twelve pence a Sabbath for the use of his house to meet in." This house stood where the Brasel house now stands and bore on the latch of the front door the initials "N.G.” and the date “1761”. In December 

1763 the settlers voted to make some preparations for building a sanctuary; and to raise thirty pounds, to be paid in boards and shingles, within a year, for this purpose. In December, 1764, they voted that the house be forty-eight feet by thirty-eight, and appointed a committee to pitch a stake for a location which would accommodate the whole society. It is related that one of the brethren, at a prayer meeting, referring to the location of the stake in his prayer said, “O 

Lord, Thou knowest I think it ought to be put in Jabez Gillett's turnip patch". In June, 1766, they voted to proceed to build a place for worship. In April, 1768, they agreed to raise the house as they could conveniently.

In September, 1768, they voted that the annual meeting of the society be held in the church. In October, 1768, they presented a petition to the General Assembly, showing that the territory of the society embraces about ten thousand acres of land, of which about seven thousand belongs to non-residents; that they have erected a church, which is covered, has doors, a floor, convenient benches, and glass windows for the lower story; that they wish to settle a minister as soon as they can; and that the list of the present inhabitants is only two thousand and eight hundred pounds, and they pray that the Assembly would grant a tax of three pence an acre for three years on all the land in the society. to purchase a settlement for the first settling [17]

minister, and, if any surplus remain, toward completing the church. 

A tax of two pence an acre for three years was granted. The parish then contained forty-eight families and about three hundred and fifty persons. This house stood on the highway on the west side of the road nearly opposite the house now occupied by Mr. Frederick Weingart. 

In December, 1773, they voted Jabez Gillett ten shillings for the ensuing year for sweeping the church and keeping it locked. In 

December, 1783, a committee was appointed to consider and decide whether the church stands in the right place; and, if it does not, to pitch a stake where it ought to stand. At the same meeting, it was voted to raise means to “procure materials to do off the inside of the meeting house."

In April, 1784, the society's committee were instructed not to call. out the committee concerning the location of the house until further notice; and in December, 1784, they rescinded the vote about doing off the inside, and appointed another committee to see where the house should stand. The north part of Torringford Street had been settled and the meeting house was no longer in the center. 

In January, 1785, a tax was laid, payable by the first of June, to finish the house. In July, 1785, the committee of the society were directed to "proceed to finish the joiner work, the inside of the meeting house, and shingle the roof, and, if there be money voted sufficient, plaster the inside." In September, 1785, it was voted not to plaster that fall; and also, "that the meeting house committee provide materials for plastering the meeting house, to be ready against next spring, if there be money voted sufficient."

In August, 1787, they "voted to have the meeting house plastered, by the great, or job, take it as it now stands, and plaster it off complete by the first day of November next; to be set up at vendue and struck off to him that shall bid the lowest." In March, 1788, a committee was appointed to settle with David Soper for plastering the church.

In December, 1788, a seating committee was appointed, and this appointment was made annually while they continued to worship in that house. In December, 1790, a committee was appointed to settle the matter respecting the plastering of the church. The house was painted in 1794. It had neither bell nor steeple. In October, 1799, they voted sixty dollars to under-pin the house, "provided that subscriptions can be procured sufficient to finish the under-pinning and step-stones." In December, 1817, a two and a half cent tax was laid to raise about three hundred and fifty dollars, to repair and paint the church. [18]

In December, 1818, it was voted that the committee supply the stove with wood the ensuing year at the expense of the society; and this probably marks the time when they began to warm the church. 

In December, 1820, it was voted to shingle the house the next season. 

In December, 1826, the committee of the society were dircted to inquire into the conduct of young people who cut and damage the pews, and to prosecute for such offenses. In December, 1828, liberty was given to alter the front gallery for the singers. In December, 1835, the thanks of the society were voted unanimously to Uriel Tuttle for the use of his organ for the time past. The Tuttle organ was put in the church about 1831. Thanks were also voted "to Charles B. Smith and Frederick Phelps, for their services-playing on the organ previous to this time, and for their future services for the same." 

THE SECOND MEETING HOUSE BACKGROUND 

Father Mills, who had been Torringford's pastor since June 29, 1769, died May 11, 1833. Rev. Epaphras Goodman, who had been his colleague since 1821, then became settled pastor. 

Without the genial presence of Father Mills in the community, things did not run so smoothly. In 1835 some dissatisfaction arose and Mr. Goodman asked to be discharged from his duties in Torringford.

At a meeting of the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society held December 7, 1835, it was voted by a large majority not to grant Mr. 

Goodman's request.

A special meeting of the Ecclesiastical Society was held December 14, 1835 to consider building a new meeting house. The first meeting house built in 1768 was only 67 years old. What, then, was the need of a new building and a new location? 

When the first meeting house was built, it did not have a good foundation, but it was 'underpinned' at a later date. The outside of the building was not attractive. The inside woodwork was beautifully carved and there was a gallery at one end, but many Torringford people wanted a modern style building. 

The old building needed extensive repairs. This first meeting house stood entirely in the highway and such location had become a disadvantage. When the development of the north end of Torringford Street, the Greenwoods section, the center of population had shifted northward and the old meeting house was no longer in the center of the community. [19]

The Torrington part of the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society comprised nine twenty-seconds of the entire township, or, expressed in old style, four and a half eleventh parts, extending from Harwinton on the south to the township of Winchester on the north, and from the east boundary of Torrington westward a little more than two and one-fourth miles. The New Hartford part of the Society extended northward four miles from Harwinton, and eastward about one and one-fourth miles from the west line of New Hartford.

DISCUSSION BEGINS 

At the special meeting December 14, 1835, it was voted to build a new meeting house. A committee of nine was appointed to recommend a location. The members of the committee were Uriel Tuttle, J. B. Phelps, Horace Gillett, Thaddeus Griswold, Barzilia Hudson, Harvey Watson, Nathaniel Smith, Ebenezer Rood and Thomas Miller.

The meeting was then adjourned to the 'Academy' one week from date. (The seven later adjournments also were held at the Academy.) 

This, by the way, was Torringford's famous Academy, started by Rev. Epaphras Goodman about 1823 and which flourished for upwards of ten years. It was a two-story brick building on the east side of 

Torringford Street a little north of the old meeting house. 

Monday evening, December 21, 1835. The committee of nine reported that its members could reach no agreement on a location. 

The meeting then repealed the former vote to build a new meeting house and voted to repair the old house. A committee of three, Thaddeus Griswold, Horace Gillett and Uriel Tuttle, was appointed to find out the expense of repairing and moving the old house. 

Monday, January 25, 1836. The committee of three reported that the old house might be moved on either side of the road, roof altered and put in repair with a steeple and the inside finished “in modern style" for $1500. This report was laid on the table and the meeting adjourned until Thursday evening.

PASTOR DISMISSED 

Thursday, January 28, 1836, the Rev. Epaphras Goodman presented an urgent request to be discharged from his ministerial duties in 

Torringford after the first of April, sooner if desired. The vote to grant his request was unanimous, one of the few unanimous votes of the Society on record.

Mr. Goodman had been in Torringford less than fifteen years, about twelve years as acting pastor and colleague to the beloved [20]

Father Mills and less than four years as settled pastor. He left no record of his work here.

Tuesday, March 8, the report of Mr. Cowles and Mr. Norton was not accepted, the vote being a tie, 19 in favor, 19 opposed. It was then voted to build a house near the old one. 

The site recommended a few rods north of the Woodward barns, on land now owned by Mr. Thomas Dunne, was a good dry location, but too far north to please the people at the south end of Torringford. 

The other site on the east side of the street, where the old Elijah Gaylord house stood then and where the Ruwet house is now, would have been the ideal location, with the old highway south of it, but Thaddeus and Margaret Griswold wished to keep it a dwelling house site. 

MORE DISCUSSION 

March 22, 1836, voted to appoint a committee of six to recommend a place for the meeting house and report to the next meeting. 

Horace Gillett, Luther Birge, Thomas Watson, Daniel G. Humphrey,

Jeremiah Spencer were the committee (only 5 names are recorded.) 'Motioned that if the committee cannot agree they are to call on the 

7th man.'

March 29, 1836, the committee reported that they could not agree and the meeting was dissolved. 

And so ended on March 29 the special meeting of December 14, [ 22 ]

1835, which had been adjourned eight times. Although all wanted a new location somewhere on the main street, they could not agree on any spot.

The meetings had served as social gatherings at the Academy for the men in the evenings or afternoons during the winter, and the discussions had provided inexpensive entertainment. 

On April 1, 1836, Torringford was without a pastor and looking forward to hearing candidates. No progress had been made toward a new meeting house or a new location for the old building. 

ANOTHER ATTEMPT 

At the annual meeting of Torringford Ecclesiastical Society held December 7, 1836, it was 'voted that the Society's committee be instructed to call a Special Society meeting for the purpose of giving the Rev. Mr. Bronson a call to settle as pastor of the Society also to take measures for building a meeting house if found necessary.' 

The special meeting on December 14th postponed action in regard to Rev. Mr. Bronson for two weeks. A committee of four, Horace 

Gillett, Thomas Watson, jr., Uriel Tuttle and Cicero Hayden, was appointed to inform and wait on a Locating Committee.' Adjourned for two weeks. 

December 28, 1836. Action about giving Rev. Mr. Bronson a call was postponed to a future meeting, and the meeting was dissolved. 

Mr. Frederick Wolcott and Mr. Isaac Kellogg, the locating com mittee, with instructions to have particular reference to those who have and are likely hereafter to support the Society, met Horace Gillett, Thomas Watson, jr., Uriel Tuttle and Cicero Hayden at the house of Thaddius Griswold on January 10, 1837. Mr. Wolcott and Mr. Kellogg examined documents presented to them and also the available sites for a meeting house. That day and for two days afterward they examined various roads leading from the different parts of Torringford to the center.

On January 12th after carefully studying all the schedules, plans, statements and documents exhibited to them and hearing the Witnesses offered for examination and the observations and arguments of the local committee and "of other Gentlemen members of Society" in relation to the various proposed sites, they reported that in their opinion the peace and permanent welfare of said Society would be promoted by locating the meeting house on the west side of Main Street in Torringford on land which was a part of the garden south of the dwelling house of Dr. E. D. Hudson, "the North East corner of said meeting house to be placed at a stake and stones in the line of the dwelling [ 23 ]

house owned by the said Hudson and the Rev. Mr. Goodman, about ten feet South of the present centre of said garden."

On February 1, 1837 the motion of Henry Colt to reject the report of the Locating Committee was carried by a vote of 39 to 28, and the meeting was dissolved.

One point in favor of this suggested location was the highway on the south side as well as one on the east side. The land, however, was too wet to make a good building site. 

There were several reasons why the feeling against this location was so strong. Since the death of Father Mills, Rev. Mr. Goodman and Dr. E. D. Hudson had stirred up considerable hubbub in Torringford. Mr. Goodman condemned as an evil the Americanization Society founded in 1816 through the efforts of Samuel J. Mills, jr. This aroused some resentment in Torringford against Mr. Goodman.

In 1833, Dr. Hudson bought the house which the people of Torringford had built for Father Mills in 1823 (the present parsonage). 

Dr. Hudson added a large two-story ell at the south of the house. (This was later moved and used as a separate house). 

Rev. Mr. Goodman owned the house next south, on the south corner of West Pearl Street. Mr. Goodman and Dr. Hudson opened a Family Boarding School for Boys, taking their pupils into their homes to live. At the very first the instruction may have been combined somewhat with instruction at the Torringford Academy established by Mr. Goodman in 1822 or 1823, but soon the new school detracted from the success of the Academy. The Torringford people had raised the money to erect the Academy building and many resented Mr. 

Goodman's withdrawal from the enterprise.

Dr. Hudson had become an ardent abolitionist and was trying to promote anti-slavery activity in Torringford while many felt that such activities at that time did more harm than good. 

For the account of the anti-slavery doings of January 1837 we are indebted to Rev. Samuel Orcutt, and we trust that the good old clergyman didn't exaggerate the story. 

Many prominent people in Litchfield County were invited to meet in Wolcottville in January, 1837 to organize a county Anti-Slavery Society. Those who made arrangements for the meeting were refused the use of every church and hall in the village and the only available place for the gathering was a large barn. Although it was an intensely cold day, the floor, scaffolds, haymow and stables of the barn were filled with people.

An Anti-Slavery Society was organized with two Torringford men among its officers, Uriel Tuttle one of the vice-presidents and Dr. [24]

Hudson treasurer. Meanwhile a furious mob, partly from Wolcottville and partly from other places, was gathering in the village. This mob paraded the streets, then gathered around the barn and by deafening shouts, the blowing of horns and ringing the alarm of fire by the bell of the Congregational Church broke up the meeting. 

Some of the Torringford people at the convention offered the use of their old meeting house, and the meeting was adjourned to Torringford and continued in session two days. When those in convention left the barn and started in sleighs for Torringford, there was great shouting, thumping of pans and kettles and furious ringing of the church bells. The mob tried to overturn the sleigh of Deacon Ebenezer Rood of Torringford West Street and to frighten his horses. In a voice of defiance he shouted to them: "Rattle your pans, hoot and toot, ring your bells, you pesky fools, if it does you any good," then put his horses on the run and got clear from the gang. 

This was probably the most exciting meeting ever held in the old meeting house with the exception of the few town meetings held there during the Revolution and of the Sunday service which was interrupted by Father Mills to read the names of sixteen men who were drafted from the congregation to march in Capt. Jabez Gillett's Company.

Many Torringford people sincerely disapproved of the anti-slavery meeting and Dr. Hudson's part in it did not increase his popularity in the community.

The recommendation of Dr. Hudson's garden as a meeting house site in the same month as this eventful gathering aroused the members of the Ecclesiastical Society to action. It is not surprising that they voted down the location of the meeting house between the two dormitories of the Family Boarding School, one owned by Mr. Goodman and the other by Dr. Hudson, and within a stone's throw of both.

Much has been told about the activities of the Devil on the west side of the town. Somehow he got across the Great Swamp into 

Torringford. Here are the alleged remarks of Father Mills from the pulpit of the old meeting house at a singing lecture held there: "I have come into this meeting house a great many times, and I saw that the devil was here. I wished to begin service, but I did not like to introduce the worship of God while the devil was in the people. I took up the Psalm book and read; but I could see him skipping about from pew to pew in the galleries; but the instant the chorister got up and blew the pitch pipe, he quit, and all was sobriety and decorum among the young people and children."

History does not record that His Satanic Majesty moved from the old to the new meeting house, but he seemed to be there frisking around about 1850, when one pastor delighted in excommunicating many church members.

THE NEW HOUSE OF WORSHIP 

In 1839 the outside of the meeting house looked much as it does today. Then, there was a long stone platform on the south side where people alighted from their horse-drawn vehicles, and the front steps were stone and wood instead of concrete. 

The present belfry is not the original one, for that blew down [ 29 ]

in and another was built. Again in the early seventies the top of the belfry was blown down and in falling damaged the south roof. 

Inside, there have been more changes during the century. In 

1839 there was a gallery for the singers above the front hall and two stairways leading to it. This gallery was closed up in the seventies (it has been whispered that the devil disturbed the young people as in the gallery of the old church) and the present choir box was installed. 

Originally, there were 56 pews in addition to the spacious stove pews. The two side aisles were the same as now. The south section then, the same as now, had eleven pews, the double center section had fourteen south-opening pews separated by a partition from fourteen north-opening pews, and there were and still are eleven pews in the north section.

The rear pew in the double center section was later taken out to widen the cross aisle at the back of the church and placed in the front hall. There were never any slave pews as in the old meeting house.

Originally, there were three Amen pews where the choir box is now, and on the opposite side of the pulpit three more Amens. 

The side walls were white plaster and there was a gracefully arched high white ceiling.

Until the seventies there was no chandelier. In early days when lights were needed, people probably carried their own candles and whale oil lamps to the meeting house. 

About 1874 Mr. Robbins Battell gave the tower clock and the present church bell.

In 1892, through the efforts of Mr. C. H. Scott, a fake minister but a good preacher and worker, the chapel, which was built in 1849 as an academy on the opposite side of the road, was moved to its present location and attached to the church. Three or four horsesheds were removed to make room for it. 

Later when repairs were necessary, the present side walls and ceiling were put in. Still later, Mrs. Ellen Battell Stoeckel had the church repaired and painted and electric lights and a furnace installed. In 1929 she gave the Hymnals now in use. 

CONCLUSION 

In 1839 or 1840 the old meeting house was sold in pieces, and some pieces of its carved woodwork still exist. 

In 1905 Deacon Chester Hopkins Barber, who as a boy had at- [ 30 ]

tended services there, selected a native stone and had it placed on the site of the old building. The inscription reads: 

Site of First 

Torringford

Meeting House 

Built 1768

Mr. Barber completed this work on June 29, 1905, the 136th anniversary of the settlement of Father Mills. 

Great honor is due the sturdy pioneers through whose efforts Torringford's first meeting house was built in difficult colonial days. 

We of the present day can learn much also from the courage and faithfulness of all the builders and preservers of Torringford's second meeting house. Long may it stand to serve the community! 

CHAPTER V The Torringford Academy 

All my life the story of the Torringford Academy has intrigued me. 

In January, 1943, I assembled all the scraps of information I had and wrote up the story. Since then I have found additional information in Torrington Registers of the year 1886 which almost make it seem as if two students at the Academy, Stanley Griswold and Giles L. Gaylord, came back for a brief moment from realms of glory with information I wanted.

The early settlers of Torringford always provided a good education for their children and were willing to be taxed quite heavily for schooling. About 1806-1808 Nathaniel B. Gaylord had a "Select

School" of grammar grade, about which little information is available. 

It may have been this select school which, according to family tradition, was held in the cellar of my house, then occupied by its builder 

Joseph Gaylord, Nathaniel's uncle.

In 1821 Torringford's pastor, Rev. Samuel J. Mills, asked for a colleague and Rev. Epaphras Goodman came to Torringford to supply the pulpit. In 1822 he was installed as Father Mills' colleague. 

He appears to have been a young man with progressive ideas of education. Soon after his installation he leased a large room, which had been used for other purposes, fitted it up and opened a select school for advanced pupils, both boys and girls. In a Torrington Register in November, 1886, my grandfather, Giles L. Gaylord, said this school was at the Battell place, now owned by Mrs. C. B. Loomis. 

There was great enthusiasm in Torringford over Mr. Goodman's school and in 1823 the people clubbed together and erected a build- [ 31 ]

ing known as the Torringford Academy building. It stood on the east side of Torringford Street a little north of the site of Torringford's first meeting house, a very short distance north of Mr. Frederick 

Weingart's barn. When the ground isn't covered with tall grass or snow, the outline of the foundation of the building can be plainly seen directly opposite Mr. Erdman Schoen's driveway. It appears that the Academy building stood entirely in the wide highway which adjoined the land of the estate of Jabez Gillett. 

I have in my possession an old account book kept by my great grandfather Elijah Gaylord beginning in 1822. On the next to the last page is the Academy account dated, 1823. The Academy owed 

Elijah $37.66 for bord lumber and candles. He owed the Academy $11.00 for his own subscription and two others, $12.00 for money he had paid to Mr. Hudson, and $4.68 for Schooling for Est and Giles, a total of $27.68. From this account book it appears that my grandfather Giles and his older sister Esther were pupils at the opening of the Academy.

I think that the Academy probably was built by the Torringford men themselves, some subscribing money, some furnishing materials and others giving their labor.

Although I have no positive proof, I think the activities of the Torringford Academy closed for all time at the end of 1835. Some friction had developed between the church members and Rev. Epaphras Goodman. At Mr. Goodman's request at the end of 1835 he was dismissed as pastor April 1, 1836. He had been in Torringford about fifteen years and had given good service to the church and had certainly done remarkable work in connection with the Academy. 

Unfortunately all the records of the Torringford Church were lost in 1823 when the house of Rev. Samuel J. Mills was burned. After that time a record must have been kept by Mr. Goodman. However, [ 40 ]

when Mr. Goodman went away he left no record at all of his ministry or of the activities of the Torringford Academy.

The Torringford Ecclesiastical Society records show that the Academy was not built nor owned by the Society. In these records the Academy is first mentioned in 1831 when the annual meeting of the Society was held there on the first Wednesday in December. On 

December 5, 1832 the Ecclesiastical Society voted to furnish wood for singing meetings and so forth for the Academy. There is no further mention of the Academy until December, 1835 when the Society began holding meetings there to discuss repairing the old meeting house or building a new one. Until 1838 practically all Ecclesiastical Society meetings were held in the Academy. 

The Rev. Epaphras Goodman was Superintendent of the Academy from 1823 to 1835. It seems probable that he himself chose the trustees and engaged the preceptor and preceptress or assistant. It would be interesting to know the salaries paid these instructors and the amount of tuition charged the pupils but no such information is available.

I have the impression that when the people built the Academy, they thought of it only as a place where their own young people could get a good education. I imagine they were surprised at the popularity of the school and at the number of pupils from out of town. It might have been a longer lived school if the building had been owned by the Ecclesiastical Society. From what happened at the Academy in 1839 it does not appear that the people did quite as good a job of construction and maintenance as they usually did with their own homes.

In the late thirties the anti-slavery discussion rocked Torringford. 

Miss Abbey Kelley, a young and educated Quakeress of superior talent and good character came to Connecticut to take part in the public discussion of slavery. Dr. E. D. Hudson was then the general agent for the Connecticut Anti-Slavery Society. He welcomed Miss Kelley to the home of his wife Martha Hudson in Torringford and secured audiences for her in Torringford and other places in nearby towns. This happened when Rev. Herman Vaill, last pastor at the old meeting house and first pastor at the present meeting house was here. 

I quote the story from Orcutt's History of Torrington: "From many pulpits in Litchfield County Miss Kelley was proclaimed as 'that woman Jezebel who calleth herself a prophetess to teach and seduce my servants'. The watchman of Torringford (Mr. Vaill, although Orcutt does not give his name) uttered a cry of distress and requested the women and their lords to meet him at the Academy, [ 41 ]

to receive his testimony and instructions concerning the sphere of woman. The assemblage was large; the women filled one side of the room, and the men the other facing them. The minister presided, and after solemn preliminaries, and the reading of St. Paul's epistle, adapted to the occasion, he discoursed vehemently upon the duties of woman, her proper sphere; and the unwomanly and unwarrantable work of woman as a public teacher; or to address promiscuous audiences and thus depart from the good old days of orthodoxy. When he had barely closed his address, the decayed timbers in the deep cellar of the Academy which sustained the floor, suddenly gave way on the woman's side of the house, and the entire floor, and all the women were precipitated into the cellar, in one general mass of tangled confusion, the whole accompanied by screams, groans and cries; one woman exclaiming “O Lord, forgive us for having attended such a wicked meeting". The men, after the dum-astonishment had passed away, hastened from on high to drag out their wives, sisters, daughters and mothers, with bruised limbs, torn garments and dissatisfied countenances; and hastened to their homes, glad to have escaped without encountering any worse sphere of action, though this was not exactly satisfactory."

Toward the end of the year 1839 Rev. Herman Vaill was dismissed as pastor and I find no more references to this Academy or its use until 1849 when another Academy was built. 

In the Torringford Centennial Book published in 1869 there is a statement by Rev. W. H. Moore, pastor here in 1849: The Academy in which Mr. Goodman taught was erected in 1829 and stood a few rods north of the old church on the other side of the street. After being unoccupied for several years, it was moved and rebuilt in 1849 as an academy and conference house and stands opposite the church. 

Orcutt in his History states that the Academy had a deep cellar and was a two-story brick building. Dr. E. D. Hudson, a student there in its early days and a trustee in 1835 calls it a brick building. 

There was an article in the Torrington Register July 24, 1886 about the Academy, from which I quote: "In 1823 according to the records in Mr. Griswold's possession, the first academy was built. It was of brick from the Hayden brick-yard, was considerably larger than the present one, and stood south from this. It flourished for many years, having within Mr. Griswold's remembrance as many as eighty pupils at a time. The first teacher was Epaphras Goodman, with his sister, Mrs. Faxon as assistant. In 1849 the building was taken down, the bricks used elsewhere and the present academy was built of wood [42]

near the church." Mr. Stanley Griswold who furnished this information was a student at the first academy.

And so the truth of the matter seems to be that the first academy building was a two-story brick building, made of brick from the Hayden brick-yard, the building was taken down in 1849 and the brick used elsewhere and the second academy building (present chapel) was built of wood in 1849. It is possible that some of the interior woodwork in the first academy was used in the present building.

For some years the Academy was used for special meetings of the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society while the annual meetings of the Society were usually held in the meeting house. 

At least as early as the late sixties and during the early seventies [44]

there was an Academy school for advanced pupils held in the Academy building. Both my father and my mother attended this school and always remembered it very pleasantly. Two of the teachers of whom they often spoke were Mr. Edson W. Davis, father of Edson G. Davis and my cousin Mrs. Mary J. Bronson, mother of Mrs. E. P. Jones of Winsted.

By 1886 the building was no longer called the Academy but was referred to as the chapel. I remember attending mid-week meetings there while it was on the west side of the street opposite the church. 

At a meeting of the Torringford Ecclesiastical Society held in September, 1892 it was voted that the Society's Committee be instructed to engage Rev. C. H. Scott to become acting pastor. A committee of three, A. M. Birge, E. B. Birge and Hiram Cable, was appointed to take such action as they might deem advisable in regard to moving the Chapel to the east side of the street. 

This fake minister, Mr. Scott, came to Torringford saying that his wife would come later when she finished her canning in the middle west. He boarded with the Hydes who lived in the Ruwett house. 

Mr. Scott was full of energy and made things hum in Torringford and he certainly was an eloquent preacher. He borrowed a stereopticon owned by Dr. Forbes, Superintendent of Torrington schools, and he gave many stereopticon lectures. Much money was raised and the parsonage was fixed up in the way he said his wife would wish. He encouraged the men in the community and during his short stay here the chapel was moved to its present location. 

Mr. Scott kept giving different reasons why his wife was delayed in coming to Torringford. Suspicion was growing here that he was not a licensed minister. Finally a man came to visit him and stayed a few days. One morning there was no sign of Mr. Scott and his friend but there was a ladder up to his window. He had hired a team from the north end of New Hartford to take him and his friend to a train. He borrowed fifty dollars from a New Hartford minister to speed them on their way. Part of the borrowed stereopticon disappeared with him. The eloquent and energetic fake, Mr. Scott, was never seen again in this vicinity. On the whole, I think he did more good than harm in Torringford, because he got the parsonage fixed up and was largely responsible for getting the chapel moved. 

And now we have the chapel, part of which may have come from the Academy built in 1823, in its present location right next to the Church.

In these days when pupils above the fourth grade are transported to schools in Torrington it is well for us to remember the days when [ 45 ]

Torringford gave her young people the opportunity to study Algebra, Geometry, Surveying, Chemistry, Psychology, Greek, Latin and French right here, and when students from away came to our hill top to share the opportunity.

Torringford Street

In colonial days Torringford Street was referred to in the old records as "the middle street", while the north and south street half a mile west was called "the west street" and the street to the east "the east street".

In the old days Torringford was fortunate to have two stores. 

William Battell came to Torringford in 1784 and bought the house now owned by Mrs. Loomis. He soon bought a small piece of land along the highway south of his house and built a store there. Almost anything imaginable seems to have been sold there. In 1806 Nathaniel 

Smith came here from Milford and worked for a time at the Battell store. About 1808 William Battell turned his store over to his sons, [ 52 ]

Joseph of Norfolk and William Junior of Torringford. William

Jr. continued to run the store until about 1832. 

In 1809 Nathaniel Smith started his own store on the corner where the Torringford school house is now. April 1, 1813 he was appointed Postmaster and kept the postoffice in his store. My father described the store building to me as he remembered it. It was about 

20 feet by 24 feet, 20 feet along the State Road and 24 feet along Torringford Street. There were counters and shelves along the east and west sides. An ell part and shed ran out to the east attached to the main store at its north end. Codfish, molasses, oil, etc. were kept in the ell. The building had a gambrel roof. The east counters and shelves held groceries while the west section had all kinds of dry goods and hardware, etc. The post office boxes were at the northerly part of the west side of the store. 

The people of Torringford sold such articles as cheese and butter to both the Battell and Smith stores. My great grandfather sold them large quantities of cheese and butter and transported such articles to Hartford or to Avon (New Haven Northampton Canal) for both stores. 

Nathaniel Smith was postmaster until his death in 1854. Rufus

About 1893 Mr. Herman Humiston who lived in the present Hewitt house was postmaster for a short time. In 1894 the postmastership went to the Davis family in the brick house. Then the Torringford men got together and built a two-room post office building at the southwest corner of the State Road and Torringford Street. Torrington Register, October 2, 1886 

East Side Pioneers

Long before this village had a human inhabitant, but instead was a virgin forest of sky-scraping pines, with here and there a grove of hemlocks or beeches, the brow of the eastern hill, extending from the Harwinton line across by way of the Bissell place and up to West street on the border of Torringford was dotted with houses where lived the sturdy families of the original proprietors. The view from points along the line, of our valley seen in its entire length, with tier upon tier of hills rising westward and southward, must have caused in the breast of the first discoverer something such a glow as Daniel Boone felt when enraptured with his first view of a Kentucky scene. 

Few, very few of the descendants of these families are with us. 

Many have gone to other regions, and of others even the children are not left among the living. But one family struck its roots so deep and wide spread that its members are still "on earth" and from one we learn who once dwelt in this territory. Winthrop Cook lives on the road laid out in the original settling of the town, or as soon as the Torrington and Torringford proprietors needed to have communication, nearly a half century before the turnpike was built in 1800. At first it did not run as direct as now, and a gully shows the route of the first few years. 

Mr. Cook's great grandfather, John, came from Windsor to “Cook

Hill" on the west side, and was an early town clerk and was the one whose name appears so many times in the records as a respected member of the early legislature. His son, a second John built a log house shortly above the present dwelling of Winthrop, which he built later, or more than a century ago. It was at this same time, that many [55]

houses were built in this section, which is the subject of this article, and of whose proprietors we shall speak. 

The third John and his brother Luther (father of Winthrop) built in 1816 the saw mill now at the head of Main Street. Then there was plenty of work for it to do, plenty of fine trees for native lumber. 

The mill did a great deal of cutting of whitewood (tulip trees) for the Thomaston clock works, and for wagon work. These tulip trees, now only here and there, were then abundant. They made a pretty sight when in blossom. One in particular near Mr. Cook's, Gen. Abernathy used to come over every year to take a look at when in bloom, it being very large. 

John Brown and Winthrop Cook were great friends, the former making his home with Mr. Cook when here on his visits from Akron and North Elba, Ohio. He used to buy stock of Mr. Cook and pay him fancy prices. He spent as many as twenty nights in the house of the latter, and has written him letters, some of which he has. Brown went from here to Kansas in response to a long letter from his son, received here.

In the early days people were very social and Mr. Cook's neighbors used to visit nearly every evening. His place was then considered by itself, and was in the forest. The view from the front of the house, now of a scene thickly dotted with homes, more and more each year, was in the boyhood of our informant that of nearly unbroken woods. Mr. Cook's father died thirty-one years ago.

Next above the Cook place, less than a half mile to the north was the Elihu Barber homestead. It was built by a man named Avery who sold it to Mr. Barber, who is still living at an advanced age in Newfield. He owned an immense tract of land, the mountains along the northern end of this valley, and hundreds of acres on the east side, also carried on a large dairy. A little farther up, near the Durand corner, was the schoolhouse with its sixty scholars, and from that point along West Street at short intervals were the houses of substantial farmers. Only four houses remain of them all. There were three 

Austins, all of whom went to Ohio in the exodus that took so many of our citizens to the 'New Connecticut." Torrington Register, November 20, 1886 Indoor Work 

Our sketches so far have given but the briefest hint of the interior life of our families at the beginning of the century, and the woman's work of that time. This article will help to meet that need. We visited 

Monday in Winsted Mrs. Roderick Bissell, who, with the exception of a very few late years, has spent her four score and five years in Torringford. Mrs. Bissell is a daughter of that house of Gaylord written of last week, born in 1801, grafted in 1824 into the Bissell tree. The name of Bissell is one of the most frequent recurring among the Torringford settlers, yet by removal and death scarce a representative remaining in the town at the present day.

Ezekiel Bissell came from Suffield in 1757 and settled about three fourths of a mile above the church. He has four sons, Ebenezer, Eliphaz, Ezekiel and Hezekiah, and when they came of age he parceled out the land among them. Roderick was the son of the second 

Ezekiel and lived on the next hill north of the James Woodward farm. 

Mrs. Bissell says she has heard her grandmother tell of her fears of the bears, the forests were so heavy around them. 

Previous to eighty years ago in these sections away from the few cities, the tableware was of the plainest. Wooden trenchers were used and in the better-to-do families pewter. The pewter was scoured to look bright as silver. A piece in the Bissell family was made in England and has the coat-of-arms of the family in that country. There was some crockery ware kept for choice occasions. It had been the custom to put the meat in the trencher cut into mouthfuls with vegetables on the same dish, from which each helped himself, there being no plates. A young man of one of the best families in [59]

Kent, came home from the city and objected to eating this way. His mother told him not to feel above the good old customs. 

Sugar was a scarce article, even maple sugar was not very abundant. The imported sugar all came by the loaf, of 10 or 12 pounds, and a loaf was made to last a long time. Sweet apple cider was boiled down and used for pudding. The lights were "dips" which the women of the house made. After spinning the wicks from tow they were dipped repeatedly in melted tallow until the right size,-thirty dozen being a day's work, and enough to last a half year. 

Cloth Making 

Besides the household duties and the care of milk and making butter and cheese the mothers of that day spun and wove their cloth. 

We give the method. After the flax was raised and cut, it was "broken" by pounding and whipped with long wooden knives to get out the "shives", then hatcheled by drawing between spike points, said spikes or nails being driven through a board to their heads. The flax was drawn both ways, separating the tow which was of two qualities, fine and coarse. The flax was then left 18 to 24 inches long. The men did the breaking out-of-doors and sometimes helped at the hatcheling in the evenings. The ladies next spun it on the flax wheel, reeled into skeins and twisted into bunches of 20 knots each. The next process was to boil in water and ashes in a kettle for an hour to make it soft and smooth, then rinse in the brook. Then the thread was ready for the hand loom which everybody had, the flax making the warp and the tow the filling. The cloth was woven a yard wide and in strips 30 to 40 yards long which were cut up into 5-yard lengths. 

These were whitened with weak lye and by putting in the sun for several days. Then wasn't the housewife proud of the cloth, clean, white, durable, and often very fine? Number 40 was the finest, 32 very common. It was made unbleached for men's summer wear. 

Calico then was 75 cents a yard, and the possessor of a calico dress was esteemed fortunate. There were some women who went out spinning. They were paid 75 cents a week, and were expected to do a 'stint' of 80 knots of warp, or 40 knots of filling a day. If they did more it was overwork. Mr. Gaylord (Elijah) saw the old flaxmill in Mast Swamp, and went home and constructed one to work by horse power (Mrs. Bissell thinks the one here was worked by water power). 

For the farmer's winter clothes, his wife took the wool and carded it with hand cards, spun it, then it was sent to the fulling mill. 

When machine carded wool first came about there was a great prejudice against its durability. In those days the ladies used to have [60]

their spinning around as now they do their fancy work. Once Doctor 

Charles Woodward called upon Miss Gaylord after her father had built his machine, and disliking her keeping at her flax, asked if the tow made by the new machine would burn. Suiting the action to the word, he dropped a live coal upon it, and the discomfited maiden saw her cherished tow disappear in flame.

Mrs. Bissell says with spirit, that she thinks the people took as much comfort in the times of her girlhood as now. Then health and strength were the rule, and people were linked together by strong ties. It means more for our aged informant to say this than some, for she lives in the midst of the business life and the comfort of modern life, and moreover retains undimmed faculties. It is interesting to know that she taught school in the brown schoolhouse on Main Street in this village over sixty-two years ago. She lived with her husband fifty years and nine months without a single death in the family, either among the grandchildren, or those who married into the family a remarkable record. Mr. Bissell died in 1875 at the age of 79. The Register issued the week of his death speaks of the great value of his citizenship in the town. He was known as Squire Bissell and being well read in law was much consulted by his neighbors.

CHAPTER IX 

A CHRONICLE OF A BATTLE WITH THE WICKET BALL 

In which it is Shown how the Hill Men did greatly Prevail against the 

Workers in Brass.

In the valley of the Naugatuck there was a hamlet called Wolcottville, and much brass was made therein. And its artificers of brass were very skillful, and they did also handle the ball with a wicket with great power.

And it came to pass about this time that they did utter loud boastings, saying, "Who can withstand us in a battle wherein a ball with a wicket is used?”

Now to the eastward from their village in the hill country, was a place called Torringford. And the men thereof did formerly handle the wicket ball, but latterly they had not done so, for they were elderly and infirm. 

Then spake the workers in brass to the hill men, saying "Go to, now, and take unto yourselves men from the town, and outside of our great mill wherein brass is made, and we will give you battle with the ball."

And the men of the hill did hearken unto them and did accept their challenge, for though not boastful, they were brave men and not afraid. 

Then did they select Wilbur, surnamed Birge, for a captain to lead them. He was a worthy man and good to look upon, and very valiant withal, having often defeated the enemies of his country in the great war. He was also filled with wisdom, having been taught in the temple of knowledge which is near to the Lord's house upon the hill top. 

And straightaway he did agree with the chief of the brass men that the battle should be upon the field known as the Fair Grounds, which was upon the borders of the village, wherein dwelt the men of brass. 

And the hour was fixed, even the ninth hour of the last day of the week.

And when the day arrived, many did go down to the village of brass from the hill country. And amongst them was Stanley, surnamed

Griswold, who hath much land and fine herds, and he selleth stamps to the people and sendeth their letters even to the uttermost parts. 

And Tine also went, he who hath gotten great gain by trading in cattle; and Ed Birge, who was a lawmaker aforetime; and Hubert, who is called the Gaylord, because he is just gay enough and not too much so; he doth roll the ball swiftly; Elijah, who worketh upon the highways, was also of the number; and Crit Colt and George Colt, who doth greatly delight to handle the ball with the wicket — all these and others did go forth to the battle. 

[64]

As hath been said many of these men had become aged and their heads were covered with gray hairs. 

But most of the workers in brass were young, and they did glory greatly in their strength. And when they beheld the old men they looked upon them in derision.

And Epaphroditus (commonly called Paphro) did laugh greatly, and did joke the old men from the hill, saying "Lettest now thy servant get ladders on which thou canst climb down from thy chariots, for thou art too feeble to descend alone." (For the hill men did ride in their chariots for the distance was great, and the hills were grievious.) 

And the brass boys did offer to get spectacles wherewith the old men could see the ball as it should go through the air, and much more chafing did they do. 

But the men from the hills did hold their peace, for they were men of understanding and of knowledge, many of them having been pedagogues in the schools.

And gatekeepers were appointed, who did collect tribute from the people who did go in to witness the battle, from every man a dime. 

And about the tenth hour in the morning of the last day of the week the battle did commence. And from the beginning, the hill men did punish the men of brass.

Howbeit, the latter were not dismayed, but did pluck up courage, saying, “the old men will soon be tired out, and then can we beat them."

But behold, as the battle waxed hot, the hill men did knock the ball with exceeding great power, until the brass men were astonished and greatly dismayed.

And when the men of brass did try to knock the ball with power, straightway the men from the hill did prevent them and did give them the "grand bounce", and they did vanish from the field, especially he who is called Paphro did disappear like a beautiful dream. 

Now there lived in the hill country a man who is called James, the Woodward. And aforetime he did handle the wicket ball mightily but latterly his infirmities had constrained him, for he was elderly, and besides he was somewhat broken with much labor, for he had labored exceedingly and builded himself buildings upon an high place. 

Now when James the Woodward did smell the battle afar off, he did say to his son, "Fasten the steed to the chariot wherein we do carry milk, and we will go down and see the battle." And they went down, and it came to pass when they arrived there that Wilbur the captain saith to James, "Join theyself to us and help us to overthrow the men of brass." [65]

But James did object on account of his infirmities. 

But Wilbur, the captain, did entreat him and finally did prevail, for this Wilbur hath a tongue that moveth easily, and he doth always prevail with any man or woman with whom he talketh. 

And when the time of James did come to go upon the war path and to knock the ball at the brass men, they did open their mouths to laugh at him, and verily he was a curious sight to behold, for he had lamed one leg badly so that he hobbled and could hardly walk, and he had wrapped about his leg his outside garment called a duster, and the sleeves and ends thereof did dangle in the air as he limped upon his leg.

But as soon as the wicket ball did hit against his limbs, his bones they did seem to become more limber. 

And he did strike the ball with much force, and did send it to the right hand and to the left and to the rear. 

And the brass men could in nowise prevail against him.

Then did Epaphroditus, who is one of the mighty men of brass, roll the ball with great power, and he did perspire profusely and did talk wildly about mill dams and all kinds of dams. But James kept on striking the ball.

Then was Epaphroditus the more fierce, and he did cry out in a loud voice, "Ye men of brass, be valiant now and watchful and straightway we will take the pelt of this James from the hill.” 

Still James kept on striking the ball and his enemies did in nowise take his pelt. Verily it was all they could do to take care of themselves, for James did knock the ball swiftly, and it did hit them on the back and in the stomach and against their shins and did nearly break them asunder, for the spirit of the olden times was upon him, and he did gird up his loins as well as his leg. 

And the brass men did run to and fro upon the field. And they gave thanks that James was not lame in BOTH legs for then he would assuredly have knocked them to pieces.

And he who is called Paphro did cease from loud speaking and from boasting, verily he was dumb and closed his mouth, even tightly as doth the clam upon the seashore. 

Nevertheless, when James did arrive at his home at night he was exceedingly lame and in much pain, insomuch that he did cast himself upon his bed and was unable to go out to milk his kine. 

And the next day which was the Sabbath he was greatly troubled to walk to the Lord's House, but he was constrained to go because he was chief singer in the choir. 

And about the third hour of the afternoon the battle did cease. [66]

Then arose the judge who was a just man and learned in the laws of wicket and he did proclaim in a loud voice that the hill men and their helpers had gotten nearly twice as many scalps or tallies as had the workers in brass.

But the hill men did not deride the brass men because they were considerate and full of compassion.

And the hill men did mount upon their chariots and did return to their homes, each with a smile upon his countenance. 

And the brass men also sought their abodes, and they also did try to smile, but it was only a ghostly, deathly counterfeit. 

And the brass men did appear to have some distress inwardly, even as doth the small boy who hath eaten many green apples, which do not move aright but go “wobbly, wobbly" in his stomach. Thus did the men of brass appear to feel. 

These things were seen by the scribe, and he knoweth them to be true, therefore doth he write them. 

Torringford, Sept. 7th, 1880 H.A.C.

This was written by Henry A. Curtis (son of Truman A. Curtis and 

Lana Woodward, and great grandson of Dr. Samuel Woodward. 

CHAPTER X 

Courtship & Marriage of William L. Durand and Mary Ellen Miner.

Now it came to pass in the first year of the reign of Benjamin, whose surname is Harrison, that a certain man of good repute in the hill country in which he dwelt, called by the name of "William the Silent", reasoned from experience that it "was not good that a man should be alone", and said to himself "Go to now, I will seek me the fairest and best in the land in which I dwell, to be unto me a helpmeet."

And he search diligently. Now there dwelt in the same land the daughter of Darius, the king, who pleased him mightily, being fair and of excellent repute, by all the people of the land wherein she dwelt, for good works. Moreover she presides at the high sounding organ in the synagogue, on the Sabbath day and at sundry other times and occasions. 

Now "William the Silent" reasoned within himself, "How can I obtain the hand of the king's daughter? This will I do; on the third day of the week at eventide will I go to the king's house, peradventure I may please the king's daughter. [67]

Now the scribe liveth on the highway leading to the king's, and he beareth witness and his witness is true, that "William the Silent" was faithful in his attentions to the king's daughter, making haste thereto for many months.

Now the days were short and evenings cold, and rain was abundant in the land, and “William the Silent" reasoned within himself again saying, “it is not good for me to live alone longer" and he hastened to the king's daughter and said, "Wilt thou be my wedded wife?" 

And the king's daughter wilted.

And it came to pass that the governor of the land in which they dwelt proclaimed a feast day, even the feast of Thanksgiving, on the 28th day of the eleventh month. And it was proclaimed that "William the Silent" and the king's daughter would celebrate the feast by their marriage, and it pleased the king and the queen, and they made a great feast and bade their kinsfolks to the marriage and they came, even all their kindred who dwelt in the land. 

And the high priest, whose name is Robinson, was summoned to the marriage, and he married "William the Silent" to the king's daughter.

Now the feast which the king and queen had prepared in honor of the marriage of their daughter was very abundant, and of such quality as becometh the king's table. And the scribe and his family shared in the abundance of the feast with thanks: and the people of the land rejoice that the king's daughter and the king's son-in-law will continue to dwell in the land as aforetime. 

This account of the marriage was written by Mr. James G. 

Woodward, grandson of Torringford's old doctor, Samuel Woodward.

He bought the old Ezekiel Bissell place and for a time lived in Ezekiel's colonial house. Later he built the house now owned by Mr. Charles 

Drumm. My father, as a boy, attended the raising of this house. Mr.

Woodward's article was printed in the Torrington Register dated 

December 13, 1889.

Mr. William Durand was the grandson of Hezekiah Durand, Torringford's casket maker. Hezekiah and his brother Samuel settled on 

Torringford Street for a time, and then Hezekiah bought property on West Street. His son William continued to live in the old house where the present Grosclaude house is. Shortly after the marriage of William's son, William L. Durand, the present house was built. 

Mary Ellen Miner was daughter of Darius Miner who came from Cornwall. Both "Deacon Bill" and his wife did a great deal for the Torringford Church. The organ in the church today and the organ in the chapel were gifts from Mrs. Durand. [68]

Mr. Durand willed much of his money to the Connecticut Missionary Society to be used for the Torringford Church during its continued existence.

CHAPTER XI 

E.B.G.

The Famous Three 

Father Mills 

Samuel John Mills was born in Kent, Conn., May 17, 1743. He was graduated from Yale in 1764, then studied theology for two years with his pastor. At the age of 26 he was ordained to the pastorate at 

Torringford June 28, 1769. From the start Mr. Mills liked Torringford and Torringford liked him. On his first arrival he is reported to have said, 'Here let me live, and here let me die. In November, 1771 he married Esther Robbins of Canaan.

Father Mills was tall and well proportioned, and his finest appearance outside the pulpit was on horseback. The boys and girls genuinely admired his graceful and dignified appearance on his horse. 

He was a unique and brilliant preacher speaking without notes. Children, including Harriet Beecher Stowe, liked his preaching, always listening for an interesting story. Father Mills loved the boys and girls and won their affection, and when they grew up their affection for him never diminished.

During the Revolution the people found it difficult to pay Mr. 

Mills his salary and he released them from part of the payment. Many people in the old days dissented from the Torringford Church, some saying they were Baptists, or Churchmen, or Strict Congregationalists. Even the dissenter, however, thoroughly respected Father 

Mills as a man.

Mrs. Mills was a great help to her husband in his work. Her death, December 30, 1810, was a great loss to her family and to the community.

In 1818 Father Mills had two bereavements. In February a 

Hawaiian lad, who had been like a son in the Mills family, Henry Obookiah, died at Cornwall, Conn. In the summer of 1818 Father 

Mills received word of the death of his youngest son, Samuel, jr., on 

June 16th on his return trip from a trip to Africa. He continued to preach until 1821 when he asked for a colleague. In 1823 his house was destroyed by fire and the people of Torringford built the present parsonage and presented it to Father Mills. 

The inscription in the Torringford Cemetery reads "Sacred to the [ 69 ]

memory of Saml J. Mills, first pastor of the church in Torringford, who died May 11, 1833 Ae90ys. He was ordained June 28, 1769, officiated without a colleague 52 ys. & continued in the pastoral office until his death." 

Samuel J. Mills, Jr.

Samuel J. Mills, youngest child of Father Mills, was born in 

Torringford, April 21, 1783. He received his early education in Torringford and then went to Morris Academy, South Farms, Litchfield for a year.

In April, 1806 Samuel entered Williams College and in August of that year he was one of the five pupils at the famous Haystack Prayer Meeting. It was proposed at this meeting to send missionaries to 

Asia and Mills spoke the words which have encircled the globe, “We can do it if we will."

In 1808 Mills and four others organized a secret society, 'The Brethren', the purpose of which was to send missions to the heathen. 

After his graduation from Williams in 1809 Mills went to Yale to become a graduate student and to arouse interest in missions. Here he met Henry Obookiah and brought him home to Torringford. Part of 

1810 Mills spent at Andover with other members of "The Brethren'.

Through his efforts the American Board of Foreign Missions was established that year.

In 1812 five of Mills' friends, Brethren, were sent out as foreign missionaries. That year the Missionary Societies of Massachusetts and Connecticut sent Samuel Mills and John F. Schernerhorn "West" as home missionaries to get information about the needs of the country. 

They went through New York, Ohio, Kentucky, Indiana and Tennessee.

The 1000 miles from Nashville to Natchez they traveled on board 

General Andrew Jackson's ship. From Natchez to New Orleans they went by flatboat. Mills was gone from Torringford one year and three days, traveled nearly three thousand miles through difficult country. He published reports of the conditions and the needs of the people West of the Alleghany Mountains, which were read and discussed in Europe as well as in America. 

In 1814 Mr. Mills made a second trip through the south and west, organizing Bible societies and distributing Bibles and in January and February, 1815 he acted as chaplain in New Orleans for soldiers from Kentucky and Tennessee. After he returned he heard that some of his friends, Brethren, were to be ordained as foreign missionaries. Mr. 

Mills was ordained as minister the same day at Newburyport, June 21, 1815.

Mr. Mills was largely responsible for the organization of the Na- [70]

tional Bible Society and the United Foreign Missionary Society in 1816. He spent the summer and part of the autumn in New York 

City doing home missionary work and distributing Bibles. In October, 1816 he visited his father in Torringford.

In 1817 Mr. Mills helped organize the American Colonization 

Society and offered his services to go to Africa to select a suitable place where Negroes from this country could make a settlement. He had been familiar with the work of Paul Cuffee, an outstanding Negro of Massachusetts who at his own expense had made two voyages to the British settlement of Sierra Leone on the western coast of Africa, taking with him on the second trip forty Negroes to begin a settlement. In the summer of 1817 Mr. Mills was appointed agent to go to Africa and he chose Mr. Ebenezer Burgess to go with him.

Paul Cuffee had returned from Africa a very sick man and Mr. Mills traveled more than one hundred miles to Westport, Mass. to be with him in his last sickness and to hear about his work in Sierra 

Leone. Mr. Cuffee died Sept. 7, 1817. 

Mr. Mills and Mr. Burgess left America Nov. 16, 1817 and embarked from England for Sierra Leone and vicinity February 3, 1818. 

Mills did a very thorough job of investigating conditions in Sierra Leone and in what is now Liberia, holding palavers with the kings and chiefs. Mr. Mills left a very valuable diary of his work on the coast of Africa. He finished his work and embarked for the United 

States May 22, 1818 on the Brig. Success.

On June 5th Mr. Mills became ill with a cold and fever and continued to grow worse until the afternoon of June 16 when he breathed his last. That night at sunset his body was committed to the ocean.

Less than a week before his death, Mr. Mills said to his companion, Mr. Burgess, “I have now transcribed the brief journal of my visit to the coast of Africa and turned my face toward home. If it please God that I may arrive safely as I may reasonably hope, I think that I shall take Obookiah and go to the Sandwich Islands, and there 

I shall end my life.'

As a result of this trip to Africa, the republic of Liberia was established. Samuel Mills, jr., lived only thirty-five years but in that brief time accomplished great things for the good of the world. 

Henry Obookiah

Henry Opukahaia (known in this country as Obookiah) was born in 1792 in Owhyhee in the Sandwich Islands, now Hawaii. He saw his father, mother and little brother killed in a war of succession. For a [71]

time he lived with an uncle, a priest, who planned to make a priest of him. 

Henry Obookiah

Capt. Brintnall of New Haven, master of a ship, offered to bring 

Henry to America and after a very long voyage they landed at New York in 1809. Capt. Brintnall took Henry and his countryman, Thomas 

Hoopoo, home with him to New Haven. Both Henry and Thomas began to study with students at Yale College. Henry was living at 

Dr. Dwight's and studying with young Dwight when he met Samuel 

J. Mills, jr., of Torringford.

In Henry's own words, "During this time Mr. S. J. M. wished me to go home with him; he says he has a good father, mother, brother and sister. This requesting was very pleasing to me - so that I consented. I then left New Haven and went home with Mr. M. I lived with the family in the year 1810. These people were the most judicious and kindest people. I was treated by them in the most affectionate [72]

manner. It seemed to me as my own home. It was. And I have made my home there frequently. While I was with them I continued my study in spelling, reading, and writing to Mr. J.F.M. a brother of Mr. M. Here I learned some sort of farming business, cutting wood, pulling flax, mowing, etc. - only to look at the others and learn from them."

Toward the close of the year 1810 Samuel Mills, jr., took Henry to Andover Seminary to be educated by the students and then for a time sent him to Bradford Academy.

Before Samuel Mills started on his first missionary tour in 1812 he made arrangements for Henry to spend several months in Hollis, New Hampshire. In the fall of 1812 Henry returned to Andover and in the spring of 1813 went home to the home of Father Mills in Torringford where he spent the summer. At this time Henry said, “When 

I at home at Torringford - out in the field I can't help think about Heaven. I go in a meadow - work at the hay - my hands - but my thughts no there - In Heaven - all time - then I very happy." 

In the fall of 1813 James Morris of Litchfield invited Henry to spend the winter in his family and attend his public grammar school. 

Here Henry began to study English grammar, geography and arithmetic. In the spring of 1814 he returned to the home of Father Mills in Torringford and spent the summer, working part of the time on the farm. Henry continues the story: "At this time Mr. M. wished me to go and live with the Rev. Mr. Harvey of Goshen. This was pleasing to me, and I went to live with Mr. H. and studied geography and mathematics. And a part of the time was trying to translate a few verses of hte Scriptures into my own language; and making a kind of spelling-book; taking the English alphabet, and giving different names and different sounds - (for this language was not written language) I spent some time in making a kind of spelling-book, dictionary, grametc." mar,

In speaking of his desire to join the Torringford Church, Henry said, "I therefore went and conversed with my good friends and father M. concerning my case. All the matter seemed to him well. He wished me to come over on the next Sabbath and attend my examination. I stayed at Goshen until the approaching of the Sabbath which was appointed and then went over to Torringford. I thought while 

I was traveling that I was going home to New Jerusalem - to the welcome gate. As I walked along I repeated these words 'Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire besides thee! I was received into the church of Christ at Torringford on the ninth day of April in the year 1815. The following is [73]

the text which the Rev. Mr. M. preached from: 'I will bring the blind by a path that they knew not; I will lead them in paths that they have not known'". 

In October, 1815 Henry left Goshen and went to live in the family of Rev. Mr. Prentice of Canaan. Toward the end of the year 1816 

Henry went to Amherst to accompany the Rev. Mr. Perkins, an agent of the Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions on a tour through that section of the country to solicit donations for the benefit of the 

Foreign Mission School at South Farms, Litchfield. Obookiah's pres ence greatly increased the success of the tour and contributions were very liberal. 

In April, 1817 Obookiah returned from Amherst to South Farms and remained there with his countrymen until the first of May when the Foreign Mission School was moved to Cornwall, Conn. He spent the remainder of 1817 at the School in Cornwall, preparing himself to go back to Owhyhee as a missionary and translating the entire book of Genesis into his own language.

Early in 1818 Henry became seriously ill and had to give up his studies. Everything possible was done for him but he continued to grow worse. The day before his death he talked with his countrymen, reminding them of God's goodness to them and urging them to love God. On the evening of February 16 Father Mills visited him and prayed with him. On February 17 Henry Obookiah peacefully fell asleep with a beautiful smile on his face.

The funeral sermon was preached by Rev. Lyman Beecher of 

Litchfield, who urged the churches to take inspiration from the life of Henry Obookiah. In 1820 the first missionaries from this country sailed for Obookiah's homeland. 

The tombstone in the Cornwall Cemetery reads:

In memory of Henry Obookiah, a native of Owhyhee, His arrival in this country gave rise to the Foreign mission school, of which he was a worthy member.

He was once an Idolator, and was designed for a Pagan Priest; 

But by the grace of God, and by the prayers and instructions of pious friends, he became a Christian.

He was eminent for piety and missionary Zeal, When almost prepared to return to his native Isle to preach the Gospel, God took him to himself. In his last sickness he wept and prayed for Owhyhee but was submissive. He died without fear, with a heavenly smile on his countenance and glory in his soul. 

Feb. 17, 1818, aged 26 [74]

A monument was erected at Napoopoo, south Kona at the Kealakekua Bay facing Captain Cook's Monument across the bay:

Henry Opukahaia 

Born in Kau 1792

Resided at Napoopoo 1797-1808 

Lived in New England Until His Death 

At Cornwall, Conn. In 1818.

The First American Board Mission to Hawaii in 1820.
