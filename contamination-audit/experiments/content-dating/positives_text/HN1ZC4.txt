PREFACE 

The material in this text has been selected and arranged to meet the ordinary mathematical needs of the trades. The book, as a whole, is designed to give the student, who has had some training over all or part of the material herein, an opportunity to review the fundamental processes involved in practical calculations. It also can be used by one who has never become thoroughly grounded in fundamentals by means of formal classroom work. 

The authors have been guided by the principle that the same fundamental mathematical truths underlie the calculations required in all of the trades and that these truths must be grasped before their applications to specific trades can be efficiently made. They also believe that, to be successful, a man cannot confine himself to any one trade exclusively, but that he must have some knowledge of the trades that are related to his.

No attempt has been made to treat all the problems of any trade. Nevertheless, applications to the various trades are pointed out in such a manner that the student, with the mathematics in the text as a basis, can recognize the methods of applying his knowledge to the problems of his particular trade. 

Moreover, recognizing the needs of the student in business and in the social life of the community, the authors have endeavored to enrich the material in this text by including a number of problems of business, and of social significance. 

The authors are indebted for valuable information, suggestions and criticisms, to the members of the faculty in the various shops at Carnegie Institute of Technology and also to

The difference between 10 and 5 is 5. 

The difference between 60°F. and 10°F. is 50°F. (Fahrenheit). 

The difference between 100 ohms and 50 ohms is 50 ohms (unit of resistance).

The difference between 200 ft. and 96 ft. is 104 feet.

ART. 25. PROFITS AND LOSSES 

Profit is the excess of the price received over the price paid for goods sold.

Loss is the excess of the price paid over the price received for goods sold.

ART. 27. INTEREST 

Borrowing and loaning money for temporary use is today a world-wide practice. Various rates (per cent) are charged for the use of money and many varied conditions and circumstances are involved in the making and in the settlement of loans. 

Consequently in this discussion no attempt is made to give the student a thorough knowledge of the various methods employed. 

Instead, the discussion will be limited to the use of the simple and more common problems involving the calculation of interest. 

A few fundamental definitions relating to the subject of interest are here given:

Interest is a payment for the use of money, or is the money paid by one person (or by a group of persons) for the use of some other person's (or group of persons) money. 

The principal is the sum of money borrowed for temporary use.

The amount is the principal plus the interest. 

The rate of interest is the rate per cent (for a year) charged by the person (or by the group of persons) for the use of their money.

Simple interest is the interest computed on the original principal.

Compound interest is the interest that accrues (is added) when that of each year is added to the principal of a loan and the interest for each following year is computed on the whole as a new principal.

1% for 1 month.

ART. 30. COMPOUND INTEREST 

Many times, when a person places a sum of money in a savings account in a bank or in some other type of investment where he desires to leave it for some time, the interest accrues from interest date to interest date. When the depositor or investor does not desire to draw the interest, it is added to the principal and then the bank pays interest on this increased amount as a new principal. The successive continuation of this process is called compounding interest.

CHAPTER VI RATIO AND PROPORTION ART. 34. DEFINITIONS AND PRINCIPLES 

The ratio of two numbers is the quotient of the first divided by the second.

It may be either indicated or expressed as a single number. 

Thus the ratio of 12 to 3 is 12 ÷ 3 and may be written 12 = 4 The indicated equality of two equal ratios is called a proportion. Thus 12 is a proportion. It is a true proportion because each ratio is equal to 4 · It is quite customary to write the ratio above as 12:3 and the proportion as 12:3 8:2. However the form indicated above is preferable as it clearly shows the real meaning of a ratio or a proportion.

The first term of a ratio is called the antecedent, the second term the consequent.

In a proportion the first and last terms are called extremes; the second and third terms are called means. 

It is a known fact that the product of the extremes equals the product of the means in a true proportion. This is another way of saying that, if two fractions are equal, the product of the numerator of the first by the denominator of the second is equal to the product of the denominator of the first by the numerator of the second.
