CHAPTER I. 

THE CHRISTIAN RELIGION 

Word of God and the Bible. The Religion of Sidney 

Lanier, Edgar Allan Poe, and others Poets; also of Theologians, and Scientists Do you wish your children taught things that are not true or the truth? Our young people are worthy of the best explanation of the Christian Religion. Are they getting it? Some teach their children to believe religion because the Church teaches it; others because the Bible teaches it-neither because of the essential reasonableness of religion which is that God reveals himself to all men or to none.

A person 15 years old can understand this book if he will forget while he is reading it the trash and absurd things that may have been taught him. We teach that the Church and Bible are sources of authority in religion in the sense that the Church is a witness of religion in the present and the Bible of religion in the past; but to believe religion solely because the Church and Bible teach it makes a weak and mentally lazy religion that fails us when we need it most as it is doing today.

The Christian religion demands the best thought we are capable of because the time comes in every thoughtful person's life-be he Roman Catholic, Protestant, Jew or Gentile-when the religion which lasts and is worthwhile is based on our ability to see things as they are; to know the certainties of religion; such certainties as "we know we are the sons of God," "we know we have passed from death to life," which is the heart of the Christian Religion. 

Will you not then let me tell you why I believe the 3

the Christian religion, which I shall do briefly by describing what nine men-for whom I shall never cease to thank God-the Apostles John, Paul, and Peter; 

Sidney Lanier, Dr. Dubose, and Edgar Allen Poe; Prof.

LeConte, Shelley and Keats-have taught me. 

You know who the Apostles John and Paul and

Peter are; John taught me what the word of God is; 

Paul that God is All in All. Peter that Christ not only preached His gospel on earth but in hell. Dr. Dubose, the authur of The Ecumenical Councils and other books, taught theology in the University of the South at Sewanee, Tenn.; Prof LeConte, a scientist and philosopher, the author of Christianity and Evolution and other books, taught science in the University of California. The other four are poets: Poe, Shelley, Keats, and 

Sidney Lanier. England produced two of these, Shelley and Keats; the South the other four: Poe, Sidney Lanier, Dr. DuBose, and Prof. LeConte, who are the greatest religious thinkers the South has produced. 

They all have the same religion but to understand it, it is necessary to coin a new word-Theopanismwhich is St. Paul's highest religious insight-"God is 

All in All" (I Cor. 15:26-28). Theos means God and

Pan means All. Theopanism therefore means God is 

All in All. 

There is no escaping the conclusion, that "since man is made in the image of God then God is in the image of man", so that to know the one is to know the other, which is the soul of Christianity. Theopanism teaches that because God is in All, He is in you and me or nowhere at all. This is why we know God, and why how to know God is to know thyself.

We know we are finite but we also know that the infinite is in us and that we have the power to think his thoughts-otherwise man could never have even the idea of infinity. If there were no love in you, you would have no idea of love; no God in you, you would have no idea of infinity. There is no other basis for re-

ligion than God in man, and the incarnation of God as man. "God in Man" is the Old Testament religion; “Go incarnate as Man" is the New Testament religion. 

The Christian religion is: "I believe in God, who is my Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth and all things visible and invisable." Cut out the words in black and you have the Jewish religion which is: "I believe in God almighty, maker of heaven and earth and all things visible and invisible." They believe in 

God as creator but not as our Father which is blasphemy to them. Therefore when Jesus claimed to be the Son of God, they cried blasphemy, because they thought it impossible for any man to be a Son of God It is necessary to know the fundamental difference between the Jewish and Christian religions. When a Jew believes that God is his Father he ceases to be a Jew and becomes a Christian. 

Theopanism is the central idea of Poe's Swan Song, Eureka, published in 1848. Poe was the first man in 

America to accept Theopanism in all its fullness. Dr. 

DuBose, one of the greatest theologians in ancient or modern times, tells us what it means in "The Ecumenical Councils", published in 1896, in which he explains what Jesus Christ, the Word of God made flesh, means. 

He says: "Jesus Christ is the eternal mind, will and activity of God revealed in all things, everywhere one and the same. He is the truth of the atom, law, life, the soul, of human and divine reason, the world, man, and God. If he is God he is also man and nature, he is the unity of God with nature and man." This is Theopanism; not Pantheism.

We idealize the best in us and call it God, we dramatize the worst in us and call it the devil. All the God and devil in the universe are in every man, as taught in 

Gal. 5:18-23: "But if ye be led of the Spirit (that is

God), ye are not under the law. Now the works of the flesh (which is St. Paul's devil) are manifest which are these:

"Adultery, fornication, uncleanness, lasciviousness, idolatry, witchcraft, hatred, variance, emulations, wrath, strife, seditious, heresies; envying, murders, drunkenness, revellings and such like. They who do such things shall not inherit the kingdom of God. "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such as these there is no law." 

St. Paul teaches us that the unrestrained appetites of our flesh is our devil and makes our hell; the conquest of them makes our heaven, as Sidney Lanier teaches in the strongest poem he ever wrote, "How Love Looked for Hell."

Let us now turn to Sidney Lanier, the one hundredth anniversary of whose birth will be in 1942. His life has been an inspiration to many thousands of people. His poetry has endeared him to these for all time. 

His religion, which at the present is scarcely known or appreciated by the general public, is baptized with the dew of immortality. He was born in an age which believed that the Bible is the Word of God and our only sure knowledge of God. This was satisfactory to those who are content with "second hand" religion. 

But such souls as Sidney Lanier believe that if God does not reveal Himself to them He does not to anybody.

So there grew in him a repugnance to the sectarianism of the churches. He says: "I fled in tears from men's ungodly quarrel about God. I fled in tears to the woods, and laid me down upon the earth. Then somewhat like the beating of many hearts there came up to me out of the ground; and I looked and my cheek lay close to a violet. Then my heart took courage, and I said: 'I know that thou art the word of my God, dear violet: 

And Oh, the ladder is not long that to my heaven leads.

Measure what space a violet stands above the ground; 'Tis no further climbing than my soul and angels have to do than that." "Dear violet, I know that thou are the word of

God" is not sentimentalism nor Wordsworthian love of nature to Sidney Lanier as some think. In a violet, in man and in the universe he finds God, and not in the Bible except, insofar as it describes the God Sidney Lanier finds in the world of nature and in his own soul, as in the "Marsh Poems", and "How Love Looked for Hell".

Religion is man's reaction to the universe in which he lives. Whatever that may be is our religion. We wake up in a universe of life, matter, and mind. We look around and ask, "How did I come here?" It seems to me that the only satisfactory answer is: "I am an incarnation of the universe"; or as Tennyson says in Ulysses. "I am a part of all that I have met;

Yet all experience is an arch wherethro' Gleams that untravell'd world, whose margin fades 

Forever and forever when I move."

The reaction of Sidney Lanier, a poet, to the universe is that God is the Poet whose poem is the universe, his hymns the music of the spheres, and his songs the hearts and souls of men. To him poetry, religion, and music is the harp of God a thrill with the vibrations of life which create the universe. He closes his wonderful poem, "The Symphony", with "Music is love in search of a word." The sum of John's Gospel is God is love. Therefore to Sidney Lanier religion is the poetry of the universe, the song, the heart-beat, the music of God in the song of birds, in inspired poets' souls, and in the ecstacy of lovers' hearts-which is the 

Word of God made flesh.

To understand the religion of Sidney Lanier, it is necessary to know what he means by the Word of God. 

To Sidney Lanier the universe as a whole and in all its parts is the Word of God. If a person were to say to him, "I do not believe that the Bible is the Word of 

God," Sidney Lanier would say, "I do not believe but know that the Bible is not the Word of God. How did you ever get the idea that it was? Certainly not from the Bible, for it never says anywhere that it,

the Bible, is the Word of God."

The Bible is a book of human words, and because so its writers never call it the Word of God. Turning to the first chapter of John's Gospel, we read: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." This is what the Bible means by the Word of God. It is not the Bible. "All things were made by the Word, and that which hath been made was life in the Word." This is not the Bible. "And the Word was made flesh." This is not the Bible. “And dwelt among us full of grace and truth.” This is not the Bible. It is Jesus Christ.

What the Word of God means is also explained in detail in the first chapter of Genesis, which teaches that light, life, the world, and man is the Word of God, because what God says in his Word. John's Gospel teaches the same thing as Genesis I does, which is: the 

Word of God is the life of all things, is the light of all men, is the flesh of you and me and Jesus. 

However before we can understand what the

Word of God means, we must learn the difference between the Word of God and the word of man. Man thinks his thoughts with his mind. But his thought in his mind is invisible and unknown to any other than himself. He communicates his thoughts to others by means of words. He can do this by a gesture, an uttered sound, or a written symbol. You are on one side of a street and wish a man on the other side to cross over to you. You can do this by gesturing to him to come over; or use the uttered sounds, “Come over here"; or write these words on a piece of paper and send it over to him. 

Now note that "thought" to become "word" must become "material form", something that can be seen,

heard or felt. In this way you make your invisible thought or idea visible so that it can pass from your mind to another mind and become known to him. A human word therefore is man's thought plus the form which expresses his thought. God's Word is his thought plus the form which expresses his thought. 

We are now ready to understand the difference between the Word of God and the word of man. When 

I say “tree” it produces in your mind a mental picture of a tree, but when God says "tree' there is a tree growing in the forest which is the cause of the mental picture in our minds. The Word of God is a thing, the word of man is the name of a thing. The Word of God-his thought made visible-is a flower, a star, man, the universe. It is God talking to man. The Bible is man's mental picture of the Word of God expressed in human words.

Nowhere in the Bible is this more beautifully expressed than in the 19thPsalm, in which the Psalmist describes the Word of God in the world of nature and in our souls, which is how God revealed Himself to the Psalmist and how He reveals Himself to you and me. No truer nor sublimer poetry is found anywhere in the literature of religion than in the 19th Psalm which first describes the Word of God in nature. 

PSALM XIX (1) Word of God in Nature 

"The heavens declare the glory of God, and the firmament showeth his handiwork. 

Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night showeth knowledge.

There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard.

Their line is gone through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world.

In them hath he set a tabernacle for the sun, which is as a bridegroom coming out of his chamber, and rejoiceth as a strong man to run his race. 

His going forth is from one end of the heaven, and

his circuit unto the ends of it; and there is nothing hid from the heat thereof."

Notice the Psalmist calls all forms of nature the Word of God. Human words are the names we give to the Words of God. 

(2) Word of God in Our Souls 

"The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul; the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple.

The statutes of the Lord are right, rejoicing the heart; the commandment of the Lord is pure, enlightening the eyes.

The fear of the Lord is clean, enduring forever; the judgments of the Lord are truth and righteous altogether. 

More to be desired are they than gold, yea, than much gold; sweeter also than honey in the honeycomb. 

Moreover by them is thy servant warned; and in the keeping of them there is great reward."

This is the Word of God we see in our souls. (3) Man's Reaction to the Word of God "Who can understand his errors? Cleanse thou me from my secret faults. 

Keep back thy servant also from presumptious sins; let them not have dominion over me: then shall I be upright, and I shall be innocent from much transgression. 

Let the words of my mouth, and the meditation of my heart, be acceptable in thy sight, O Lord, my strength and my redeemer."

This Psalm teaches us that not the Bible, but the subject of the Bible, is the Word of God its writers see and you and I see in the world of nature and in our souls. Four important conclusions are derived from knowing what the word of God means. It unseals the 

Bible and makes it a new book. It teaches us how we know the Bible is true.

The first conclusion is that, since the Word of God is the universe speaking to man, God reveals Himself to all men. The second conclusion is that if the writers of the Bible make mistakes by not perfectly describing the Word of God, we can correct these mistakes, since we see the same Word of God they saw and described. The third conclusion is that man's know- 10

ledge of the Word of God is a continuously progressive thing, so that we ought to know more about the Word of God than the writers of the Bible, because we have their knowledge plus what we are continually discovering. The fourth conclusion is that our universities, whether they are studying the Bible or not, are studying nothing else but the Word of God, and can study nothing else, because the Word of God is flower-starman-the universe.

We are continually discovering new laws of matter and life, which is the Word of God. Commodore 

Maury, the Pathfinder of the Seas, said: "When I discover a law of nature I see a thought of God and tremble". He knew the meaning of the Word of God. 

He was a poet; as Sidney Lanier was when he said, "I know that thou art the Word of my God, dear violet;" as John was when he said, "Jesus is the Word of God made flesh;" as Coleridge was when he wrote: "So then, believe that every bird that sings, And every flower that stars the elastic sod,

And every thought the happy summer brings To the pure spirit, is a word of God." 

To Sidney Lanier God is not an abstraction, and is more than invisible spirit; He is seen spirit known in everything we see, hear, and feel as John teaches in his first Epistle: "That which we have seen and heard and handled of the Word of Life declare we unto you that your joy may be full." Sidney Lanier gives us his Creed in "A Florida Sunday":

This is the religion of which Sidney Lanier is the high priest. He is not a theist or pantheist, but a theopanist. For he concludes his poem with: "Thou Father, without logic, tellest me 

How this divine denial true may be, -How All's in each, yet every one of all 

Maintains his Self complete and several.”

One can be a theist without believing in any incarnation of God at all, as Mohammedans and Jews; or believe, as most Christians do, that the incarnation of God is limited to Jesus. A theopanist believes that the Word of God is more or less incarnate in all men, but denies the pantheistic teaching that man is absorbed in deity as the rivers are in the ocean. 

Theopanism teaches God is incarnate in man, not that man is absorbed in God; that man is the terminus ad quem of God-His Word made flesh as man. It is God and man; it is God in and as man; forever separate and distinct, yet one: Father and Son in the unity of one 

Spirit of the universe who is eternal Father, Mother and Child.

This places Sidney Lanier in the front rank of poets. While the South produced him there is nothing sectional in his poetry except his scenery. His outlook is national and his sympathies are universal. He is the conscience of America as Tennyson is of England. 

He will live because he is the high priest of man's highest social and spiritual ideals, as in "The Symphony", and the end of our search for truth found and known as God incarnate in ourselves as in "A Florida Sunday".

When man finds God in himself it dethrones all other gods except the one God of the universe who is All in 

All. Religion to Shelley, Keats, Poe and Sidney Lanier in none other than I am the incarnation of the universe. 

This is not atheism. It is more than theism. It is theopanism. It is the Christian Religion. 

To Sidney Lanier and Poe theology is not static but progressive. "Knowledge of nature," says Poe, "adds 12

to our knowledge of God, and Macaulay's assertion that theology is not a progressive science is declared to be false and misleading". Poe continues, "That we know no more, today, of the nature of deity, of his purposes, and thus of man himself, than we did even a dozen years ago, is a proposition disgracefully absurd." "Twenty years ago", says Poe, writing in 1844, "credulity was the characteristic trait of the mob, incredulity the distinctive feature of the philosophic: now the case is conversed. The wise are wisely averse from disbelief. To be skeptical is no longer evidence either of information or wit." "Indeed", says Poe, "to our mind the only irrefutable argument in support of the soul's immortalityor rather, the only conclusive proof of man's alternate dissolution and rejuvanescense ad infinitum-is to be found in analogies deduced from the modern established theory of the nebular cosmogony which demonstrates that all existing bodies in the universe are formed of nebular matter, a rare ethereal medium,* pervading space; shows the mode and laws of formation, and proves that all things are in a perpetual state of progress; that nothing in nature is perfected." 

Poe's definition of poetry is derived from his be lief that nothing in nature is perfected. CHAPTER II. 

THE GOOD GOD 

Theopanism, "God is All in All," gives us a new conception of what the good God means. God is a good 

God and everything he has created is good "very good" as the first chapter of Genesis teaches-then how explain the universe filled with pain, suffering, and death? Does God create a world of suffering and death? Everything that is born of flesh dies, and everything that lives suffers. Has God so created the universe that this must be so, or is it caused by violation of the laws of life on the part of living creatures, or is it caused by the intrusion of a malignant spirit who hates God and wishes to destroy his creation? It depends upon how we answer these questions whether we honor or dishonor God; or even continue to believe in God at all.

The first answer to this problem in the Bible denies that God created pain and death. The world in the beginning was all good; that is, free from pain, suffering, and death-which is what most people mean by "good." It is what is called paradise, the Garden of Eden. 

According to the "snake and tree myths," the first sinner was a serpent who seduced man to disobey the commands of God by stealing the wisdom of God, "knowledge of good and evil," and giving it to man.

Man, however, was not cast out of the Garden of Eden because of what he did but to prevent him from doing something which he had not yet done but was about to do. "And the Lord said, Behold the man is become as one of us, to know good and evil; and now lest he put forth his hand, and take also of the tree of life, and eat and live forver; therefore the Lord God sent him forth from the Garden of Eden, to till the 18

ground from whence he was taken."

In passing let us note that the serpent did not lie to man. Every word he spake was the truth. Everything he promised came to pass, but something happened which he did not foresee. This serpent God was not actuated by evil motives. He was trying to help man on the road to progress—trying to lift them from their low estate-so low that they did not know "good from evil" to the wisdom of the gods.

Nor did the serpent intend to stop here. He was just on the point of taking the next step of conferring immortality upon man by urging him "to eat of the tree of life and live forever," when all his plans were suddenly thwarted by a jealous God who will not permit men to "poach on his own preserves" and become immortal by eating food which confers immortality upon the gods. This is another form of the Greek Titan myth of Prometheus. 

The Myth of Prometheus: The Titans ruled the earth before the Gods of Olympus overthrew them and imprisoned them in the earth. One, however, escaped- 

Prometheus, who pitied the low estate of "miserable men" of whom the Olympic Gods were "unmindful" in their celestial power and happiness.

So Prometheus began to help men by teaching them the arts of agriculture and by stealing the "fire of Zeus" upon which the progress of man depends. This angered Zeus so much that, to punish Prometheus, he was chained to the rocks of Caucasus, while the eagles ate out his vitals by day but were renewed every night.

Substitute Jehovah for Zeus and the Snake God for Prometheus, and you at once see that the Genesis story is another form of the Titan myth of Prometheus. 

It is astonishing how long a religious myth, especially if it is constructed with the consummate literary art of the third chapter of Genesis continues to live and teach religious error which holds men down to low ethical levels which teach untrue and unworthy 19.

conceptions of God. The myth lasts as long as it meets the needs of those for whom it was first made, and can be outgrown only as men rise to a morality higher than the morality of the God of the myth.

But finally a wondrful man by the name of Moses was born who taught a nobler idea of God which is contained in the Ten Commandments, although even in them elements remain which every truly moral man today has outgrown, so that such a man is better than the God Moses worshipped-a jealous, cruel, and revengeful God. 

The Ten Commandments teach that if the parent commits a crime worthy of death, all the family is killed down to, and including, the grandchildren. The final answer of the Old Testament to the problem of suffering and death is given by the prophet Isaiah. This is as far as the Old Testament carries us, teaching that evil is not the creation of a devil nor enters the world through the sin of man. How reconcile pain with the character of God without sacrificing his power is our problem. God could be the God we see in nature and not necessarily be a good God. Yes, a God of wisdom, power, and majesty but not necessarily a good God because he could do all this simply for the pleasurable exercises of his power as a great but ambitious and thoroughly selfish man does. In a word, "God created man for his own glory," as we have been taught. Shame on such dogma!

A good God creates for the glory of his creation and the good of his children. If he cannot do this without subjecting his children to pain, suffering and death, and then is willing to endure all the agony of his creation and share all the anguish of his children, as a good woman does who understands this and yet is willing to become a mother, such a God is without doubt a good God—at least, as good as a good woman is. 

A good, all-wise, and all-powerful God will not create a universe of pain and suffering unless this is for the highest good of his creation; and then, if necessary to perfect mankind, he will endure all the agony of it by becoming incarnate as man. This makes God more than omnipotent, omniscient, and omnipresent. 

It makes him a good God! It makes God an all-powerful God who uses pain and death to accomplish his purpose of love in order that he may make his children all that he is, which they cannot be without suffering and death.

CHAPTER IV. 

MEANING OF HELL IN THE BIBLE 

If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable.-I Cor. 15:19.

Hell, as used by the ordinary person, means something entirely different from what the word does as used in Bible and Creed of Christendom. For the popular conception which people have imported into the word "hell" utterly confuses the scriptural meaning of that word. We read the Bible and come across the word "hell", never stopping to consider what that word means, taking it for granted that, of course, everybody knows what the word "hell" means.

In fact, there is not one person in a hundred who knows what the word "hell" means as defined in a good English dictionary. And until one has taken the trouble to find out this simple thing, is it not reasonable to believe that his opinion as to the meaning of the word "hell" is worthless?

The Greeks, Romans and Hebrews believed that the world was hollow and this hollow they called "hell", the abode of the dead-both good and bad. The Hebrew word translated hell in the English Bible is "sheol".

Its definition in Gesensius' Hebrew Lexicon is: “Its etymologically means a cavity, a hollow, subterranean place; just as the German word Holle, hell means a hollow, a cavern ; where dwell the spirits of the dead; the underworld. In Sheol are both the good and the wicked after death."

The words that mean "hell" in the New Testament, "Thayer's Greek Lexicon of the New Testament", are: 

1. Hades. "It etymologically means, not to be seen, the unseen place. It means the same as the Hebrew 

Sheol; the common receptacle of disembodied spirits: both the good and the bad." 35

2. Gehenna. "The name of a valley on the south and east of Jerusalem which was so called from the cries of little children who were thrown into the arms of Moloch, an idol having the form of a bull. The Jews so abhorred the place that, after the horrible sacrifices had been abolished by King Josiah (2 Kings 23:10), they cast into it not only all manner of refuse, but even the dead bodies of animals, and of unburied criminals who had been executed. And, since fires were always needed to consume the dead bodies, so that the air might not become tainted by their putrefaction, it came to pass that the place was called 'Gehenna of fire.' And then this name was transferred to that place in Hades where the wicked after death will suffer punishment." 

Do not forget that fire is never used in the Bible as the agent of divine punishment but always of purification! 

3. Tartarus. "The name of a subterranean region doleful and dark, regarded by the ancient Greeks as the abode of the wicked dead, where they suffer punishment for their wicked deeds: it answers to the Gehenna of the Jews." 

4. Paradise. "It etymologically means a park, an enclosure, shady and wellwatered, in which animals are kept for the hunt. That part of Hades which was thought by the later Jews to be he abode of the pious until the resurrection."

The meaning of hell as used in the Bible means the condition of man at death. It describes his condition from death to the resurrection.

Why the Apostle Judas 

Jesus was a good judge af character. Why then did he choose Peter and Judas as two of his apostles 42

the one a liar and coward, the other a thief and traitor?

He knew when he chose them that one would desert and deny him in his hour ofsupreme trial, and the betrayal of the other would cause his death. 

The usual answers are not the real answers to the question. The real answer is that Jesus came to save all men-cowards, liars, thieves, traitors as well as all other people; and that he could not save Peter without letting him deny him and Judas without letting him betray him. First of all then he chose these two men to save them, and then use them afterwards to save other men of their class and type.

Whatever one may think of the fate and characters of these two men and the reason Jesus chose them as two of his apostles, all can readily see that they have been of use in saving other men from committing the sins of which they were guilty. 

The cowardice of Peter's denial of his Lord has saved unnumbered hosts through all the ages since, and caused them to choose the martyr's fate rather than follow in the cowardly footsteps of Peter. As there is something of the traitor in most people, the fate of Judas has saved millions through the ages since from being traitors, when they think of the horror of the consequences of the traitorship of Judas. Judas has saved more men from being traitors than all the rest of the apostles combined.

It is a mistake to think that Judas belongs to the class of hardened criminals. When he realized the fearful consequences of his act, and his powerlessness to undo it, in his self-loathing, despair, and bitter remorse he found life no longer worth living. Though he repented he went out and hanged himself. Here we usually dismiss Judas, consigning him to the unending torments of the traitor's hell.

HELL GOD'S PRISON OF REFORMATION 

We have had fathers of our flesh which corrected us, and we gave them reverence; shall we not much rather be in subjection unto the Father of spirits, and live? For they varily for a few days chastened us after their own pleasure; but He for our profit that we might be partakers of His holiness. Heb. 12: 9, 10.

Many people think that everlasting and eternal mean the same. In the Bible three adjectives are used with clear and distinct meaning. The word temporal describes the life of nature below man. It means that which has a beginning and an end. The word everlasting means that which has a beginning but no end. It describes the life of the soul of man. The word eternal means that which has no beginning and no end. 

It is the adjective which describes God and his attributes. Eternal punishment means divine punishment. 

The Bible uses the word eternal and not everlasting to describe the punishment of sinning man. In 

1880, the translation of the English Bible was revised by the best scholarship of the world, and in those passages which refer to the punishment of man, they agreed that the proper translation of the Greek word aionian is eternal and not everlasting. So that the verdict of the best scholarship of the world, as represented in the Revised Version of the New Testament, is that nowhere is the word everlasting used to describe the punishment of man; and this is why I use the word eternal.

Eternal means that which never had a beginning and will never have an end, as when did 2 plus 2 begin to make 4? Has it not always been so, and will it not always be so. So this truth of mathematics is an 45

eternal truth. In like manner, there never was a time and there never will be a time when the sinning person, as long as he sins, will not be punished. Scripture describes this punishment as that "fire prepared for the devil and his angels," always ready for one who chooses to become a devil by sinning-the condition of entrance. 

Sin gets one into eternal punshment, and repentance gets one out of eternal punishment. This punishment, always ready and existing for every creature that sins, is called in the Scripture "eternal punishment," and it cannot be called by any other name. But the word eternal is also used to describe kind of like. 

Eternal life means divine life, revealed to us incarnate in Jesus, so that although He lived here upon the earth for only a few years in the flesh, He lived an eternal life because a divine life. The word eternal is used to describe divine kind of life.

Take, for instance, these passages which have perplexed all thoughtful readers: "Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer; and ye know no muderer hath eternal life abiding in him. And I give unto them eternal life and they shall never perish. These shall go away into eternal punishment and these into eternal life."*

Substitute the word divine and see how these passages at once become perfectly plain and beautifully luminous: "Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer; and ye know that no murderer hath divine life abiding in him," because there is no murderous quality of life in 

God. "And I give unto them divine life and they shall never perish," not unless God perish. "These shall go away into divine punishment and these into divine life," because of their sinfulness or sinlessness.

As a matter of fact, the Scripture teach in scores of passages that only a divine sinless life is everlasting, so that the problem of making us partakers of eternal life is the problem of bleaching our sin-stained souls. whiter than snow. Eternal, or divine punishment, is * John 3:15; John 10:28; Matt. 25:46. 45a

thus seen to be a necessary part of the salvation of every sinning person; for divine punishment is divine purification, exceedingly painful as the sinful soul is purified in the fires of God's wrath of love, which must last as long as sin lasts, and can only come to an end for us when sin has come to an end in us. 

In this light, the Scripture admonition, "They that sow to the flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption, but they that sow to the spirit life everlasting," comes with tenfold power! Not to scare children, but to solemnize men and women living for the world, the flesh, and the devil, showing them the hell that they, not God, are inevitably creating for themselves. These admonitions of Scripture verily and indeed become guideposts unto our feet into the way of life-forsaking the sinful desires of the flesh, using the things of this world and not abusing them, so that our entrance into the next world will be a resurrection into higher life, and not a "resurection unto damnation" in Dives' flames of his desecrated phychic life.

For what we have thought, loved, and done, makes the resurrection of the "I ams" that we are to-day, and the condition in which we enter the life between life and resurrection. Whether we live a life of sinful misery or spiritual happiness, here or elsewhere, is no arbitrary thing imposed upon us from without. We ourselves make ourselves miserable or happy, as we obey or disobey the spirit-laws of life infolded in our being. 

If we draw from the inexhaustible fountain of the life of God in us, the tide of life ever rises higher and higher in us, enabling us to pass unscathed through the fiery furnaces which the world and the flesh build around us. When our souls let go their hold on God then our troubles begin. Then it is in the power of persons and forces to make us so wretched and miserable that many times we will wish that we never had been born.

This misery, wretchedness, and breakdown of our 45b

lives will be a threefold wreckage of body, mind, and spirit. For when man yields to his appetites and lusts, he will build into himself a body which will become the source of untold shame, misery, and degration. The next effect of turning away from God is to darken the sources of mental life in us. The mind will become a fountain of misery; our thoughts becoming corrupta veritable hell of punishment. The last effect will be to plunge our spiritual nature into its abyss of misery. 

Conscience will arise with its thousand whips of remorse, as we writhe in agony and torture, all combined making a hell that beggars Dante's and Milton's!

This threefold effect is visited upon every prodigal son, who leaves his Father's house, and wanders into the far country of self-sufficiency and sin. The body, the mind, the spirit,-misused, abused, desecrated-become the sources of that retributive justice which constitutes the fires of hell, which rage and roar and flame in our being-primarily not outside us, but inside us, as a foul bird makes a foul nest.

Jesus, when asked where was the kingdom of heaven, said, within us. When asked where God was, said, within us. So when we obey these heavenly powers, the source of heavenly life in us, we grow in wisdom and stature and in favor with God, until at last we become the living temple of the living God, and the universe becomes heaven to us. On the other hand, another career is open to man, the opposite of this. If we choose to disobey God, speaking in us in the authoritative voice of conscience, the life of spirit, mind, and body beats with feebler pulsation; and, as our spiritual vitality lowers, sin, disease, and suffering of the whole man creep in; at last wallowing in the mire of loathsome materialism, filthy mentalism, foul spiritism, and the universe becomes hell to us. Thus one man creates for himself hell out of the same materials with which another man creates heaven!

I see all this taking place here in this world every 45c

day. I pass along the streets and look into one face and see every appetite, passion, and desire diverted and desecratd from the divine functions for which God created and intended them to be used. I look into that person's eyes and see that person's body has become a roaring hell of unrestrained, outraged, desecrated appetites and passions. That man will not have to die to get into hell. He has made himself a hell unto himself.

I pass on and look into the face of another person which has become symbolical and sacramental of all things pure. In that face I see evidences of a victorious struggle which has been fought and won in which the body has become the willing agent, the passive machine, and the ever mobile and perfect expression of the spirit of holiness within. 

I have been trying to make plain that God arbitrarily sends no man to heaven or hell; that they are the two poles of the biological laws of the universe; that heaven is attained by our spirits cooperating with the Spirit of God, until we can say God lives in us; hat hell is attained by lack of cooperation between our spirits and the Spirit of God, continued so far until the person who began in the innocence of childhood can say, Hell lives in me.

I also understand that the Scripture teaches that this process of character building, which is begun in the flesh, goes on in the unseen world; that as the "tree falls so it lies"; that is, we begin our life in the unseen world in the same character with which we leave this world.

We are taught by many good people that the act of flesh-death makes a miraculous change in our characters and in God's attitude towards us; repentance before death being accepted, afterwards availing naught; repentance one minute before flesh-death sends a man immediatly to heaven; repentence one minute after flesh-death sends a man forever to hell. The gross 45d

stupidity of this at once empties the whole transaction of any moral meaning whatsoever.

You are doubtless familiar with such teaching as this: one person from childhood has lived a life of blessing to himself and others with whom he has come in contact. He dies, and it is said of him that he has gone to heaven. Another person lives a life of covetousness, lying, cheating, and perhaps murder; but five minutes before he dies he repents, and it is said of him that he has gone to heaven also.

I do not deny the genuineness of death-bed repentance, nor its effectiveness to make the lot of such a person a better one upon his entrance into the "unseen world" than one who dies in willful sin. But there are two things which I cannot tell that person: (1) that immediately after death he or any other person will go to heaven, for Jesus did not enter until after He rose the third day, John 20:17; (2) that his lot upon entrance into the next world will be the same as that of the person who has lived a life of righteousness in this world. "As a man sows so shall he reap;" upon entrance into the next world, "we shall be judged according to the deeds done in the body;" these are the plain matterof-fact statements of Scripture, persistently reiterated. 

To teach a person that by repentance just before death he can escape the consequences of a sinful life, and immediately thereafter take his place by the side of those who have lived saintly lives, is the grossest of all immoral teaching!

It is born of the superstition of the savage and the ethics of the barbarian. It cuts up by the roots and destroys our conception that God is a righteous Judge. 

It utterly confuses the clearest revelation of Scripture, and the sanest moral sense of mankind, uttering in a single voice, "They who have sinned little shall be beaten with few stripes, they who have sinned much with many stripes."

Jesus, Luke 23:39-43: 

And one of the malefactors which were hanged sailed on him, saying, if thou be Christ, save thyself and us. 

But the other answering rebuked him, saying, dost not thou fear God, seeing thou art in the same condemnation?

And we indeed justly; for we receive the rewards of our deeds; but this man hath done nothing amiss. And he said unto Jesus, Lord remember me when Thou comest into thy kingdom. And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, Today shalt thou be with me in paradise.

The second occurrence of the word is in cennection with the experience of St. Paul recorded in 2 Cor. 

And i' knew a man (whether is the body, or out of the body, I cannot tell, God knoweth). Now that he was caught up into Paradise, and heard unspeakable words which it is unlawful for a man to utter.

The third time the word is used in the wonderfully imaginative, poetic, and symbolic book of Revelation 

22:7: 

To him that overcometh will I give to eat of the tree of life which is in the midst of the Paradise of God. 

The Bible clearly teaches that man exists in three separate and distinct conditions of existence corresponding to his birth, death, and resurrection. If this had always been clearly seen and all its necessary deductions accepted by all Christian teachers, it would have saved us from falling into some grievous errors, as for instance two which I will mention. 

It has been taught that life in the flesh is a proba- 46

tion, which means that one's final destiny is fixed by the life lived in the flesh in this world; for some this may be true. Others have taught that this life is an education for heaven; for some this may be true. The reason, however, why our life in the flesh is neither a probation nor an education, in the sense which it has been used in theology, is because the great majority die either in infancy or in childhood. How is it possible for this life to be either an education or a probation for such as these, who constitute the large majority of mankind? In this world there is neither time for education nor probation for such as these, but only a beginning to be, so that this world for all is God's seed-bed for the propagation of souls. 

The moment these facts of life are clearly faced, we see that whatever education and probation of any kind the overwhelming majority of mankind may get is not attained in this world. Not only is this true for those who die in early years, but the education of the seemingly most favored of the favored few is only begun in this world to be carried on to completion in the life between death and the resurrection, called Paradise. 

The Bible teachs that whether in birth, or in death, or in the resurrection, man is never bodiless, but always "clothed upon." These bodies which birth, death, and the resurrection give us are called flesh-body, psychicalbody (soul-body) and spiritual-body. 

Flesh, Psychic, and Spiritual Bodies

Man's body is first of all a flesh body which he shares with the animal creation. By means of his five senses he has communion with the material universe. 

But within his flesh body he has another body. It is his thought body, his mind body, in which soul, psyche, functions. Psyche is the thinker, the mind; its body is the words with which it expresses its thought. 

This body is made in the brain of man in Broca's Convolution. It is there man makes his images, ideas, his thoughts, words, and ideals-his soul body. It transcends his flesh body. It is the functioning of interstella 47

ether in the brain as it functions in the radio.

It is made of indestructible matter. 

It only remains to be added that the word Paradise means the place or condition, or both, of the good between death and the resurrection. In this classic passage. Phil. 1:23-24. St. Paul gives his estimate of the relative value of this world and Paradise for soulgrowth, to him and to us, in these words: 

I am in a strait betwixt two, having the desire to depart and be with Christ, which is far better; yet to abide in the flesh is more expedient for you. 

The Great Apostle of the Gentiles standing upon the confines of this world and touching the borders of Paradise; this world in the background, that in the forefront; this world soon to be a memory, that for future achievement; the cup of this world's experiences of joy and sorrow drained to the last drop, that of Paradise brimming with joy and bliss soon to be raised to his lips-thus the battle-scarred veteran of Christ stands, being in "a strait betwixt two"; having the desire to "depart and be with Christ" but with this ruling passion strong in death exclaims, "yet to abide in the flesh is more needful for you." These are the words which will always express the feelings in our hearts, when they are divided between those whom we have "loved and lost awhile, and those loved ones who remain to sight." 

We are like St. Paul, in a strait betwixt two, feeling that it is better to depart and be with them, but for the sake of others it is more needful to abide in the flesh.

There came a time in the experience of St. Paul, when he found his affections divided between this world and Paradise, with the larger share of them gone on before. There came a time when this world seemed to be growing the unreal world, as he found the center of the gravity of his life shifting towards Paradise. And this experience of St. Paul is the normal one in the life of the mature Christian, the experience which All 

Saints' Day commemorates; the day of divided affec- 48

tions and mingled sorrow and joy, which marks the ending of one work and the beginning of one still greater; the feelings which come over us whn the scenes of this world close around us, and the glories of the next dawn upon us. 

Why Needful for Him to Abide in the Flesh

The reason is not to be found in anything which the world had to give St. Paul. He had sat at the feet of 

Gamaliel the Jew, and Plato the Greek, but in sitting at the feet of another, he had become greaer that these. 

He had exhausted Jewish and Gentile learning, and in the realm of the world's knowledge he was a past graduate. Born a citizen of the Roman Empire, he had enjoyed all the privileges of the highest political possibilities of the then world. In matters physical, he had known the joys of childhood, youth, manhood, and old age; and had been "in perils oft". He had exhausted all that the world can give of intellectual, social, political, and physical life; and, as the world's past graduate, he stands before us the world's master, and, as such, the world no longer has anything to give to him!

Looking into Paradise he saw that there, not here, lay the scene of his future development, and, so far, as he was personally concerned, it was better for him to depart. But when he turned and looked back upon the world, he saw that it was better for him to abide in the flesh, for he had so much to give to the world. So St. 

Paul is seen to be one of those rare souls whom God sends into the world from time to time who make the "great renunciation," which the soul makes when it has earned the right and title to enter higher spheres of life, but turns back and says, "That can wait. I will not enter that higher life and selfishly enjoy its beatitudes, but prepare others to share them with me. 

And so, we too, following the example of St. Paul afar off, can make the great renunciation. Doth learning open her ample page before us? We can cease to be learners and become teachers, and experience some- 49

thing of its being more blessed to give than to receive, by dwelling in a world of scholars we have made, instead of living a scholar in a world of ignorance. Doth wealth surround us with its luxuries? We can share that wealth with others, and learn, by making glad their lives, that it is more blessed to give than to receive. 

Here is the reason God permits His aged saints to linger in this world. It is that they may impart their vivid faith in things unseen to others, enabling them to see and realize that "the things which are seen are temporal, but the things that are unseen are eternal." 

Graduates of life in this world, while yet your feet linger upon the shore on this side, and for a little while are post-graduates waiting for the favoring wind, a most benevolent and divine work is yours. It is to turn back and help others over the rough places your own feet have trod!

Why Rest, Joy and Felicity in Paradise 

But it is a blessed thing to know, that, however much this world has to give us, there is a better world into which we enter upon leaving this; and, if we ask why it is so much better than this, the answer is because it is a world of "rest, joy, and felicity" to those who have not abused their opportunities in this world. And if we would inquire more particularly how it is a world of rest, we have but to fasten our attention for a while upon this world, and note the cause of our activity and work, and what part of our nature tires, and the answer is forthcoming.

Everyone who comes into this world continues in it under the condition that "in the sweat of thy brow shalt thou eat bread all the days of thy life." For the activities of this world are born of the needs and necessities of flesh-and-blood beings. Look at yonder field of waving grain and snowy cotton, yonder flocks and herds grazing upon a thousand hills, and why are men busy tending the one and cultivating the other? To feed 50

and clothe the body. Why do men bowel in the earth and dive in the deep sea? Why are the rivers spanned, the mountains tunneled, and old ocean diked? Because the needs of the body demand it. Why is yonder ship taking the wave like a thing of life, and yonder locomotive speeding like a thing of light? Because the needs of the body have said go-and they have gone. This is the world of flesh and blood, and the soul begins its education by working under the conditions of the fleshbody. In the activities of the camp, the court, the forum -the factory, the shop, the field-these are the conditions of our development and training in this world; but in Paradise the activities of this world cease, because that part of our nature, in behalf of whose needs the activities of this world have their origin, is dead. 

The flesh-body is left behind and the person rests. "Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord for they do rest from their labors."

If this is the world of partings, that will be the world of meetings; if this is the world in which the ties of families and kindred souls are sunderd, that will be the world of joy and felicity in which they will be reunited. There in our pscychic-bodies we will have the joy and felicity of entering into sweeter fellowship and tenderer relations with those whom we have loved and lost awhile. "She is not dead-the child of our affection,- 

But gone unto that school, Where she no longer needs our poor protection, And Christ Himself doth rule. "Day after day we think what she is doing, In those bright realms of air; 

Year after year her tender steps pursuing, Beholds her grown more fair. "Not as a child shall we again behold her;

For when with rapture wild, In our embraces we again infold her, She will not be a child; 51

"But a fair maiden in her Father's mansion, Clothed with celestial grace;

And beautiful with all the soul's expansion, Shall we behold her face."

The Person is Perfected in Paradise 

But if we would know what will constitute the supreme joy and felicity of Paradise, we will find it in the reason St. Paul gives-"to be with Christ." And the result of this more intimate fellowship with Christ will hasten the development and education of our souls ending in perfection. This will be seen if we compare the soul's entrance into Paradise with its condition upon its departure. We know that just before the soul's entrance into Paradise, the body which corresponded to its development and was its fitting garment and objective self, was an incarnate psychic-body; but the body corresponding to the soul's development and therefore its fitting body upon its exit from Paradise, will be a glorified spiritualbody. In the words of St. Paul, "it is sown in weakness, it is raised to power; it is sown in corruption, it is raised in incorruption; it is sown a psychical body, it is raised a spiritual body."

If it be asked, then, whence this marvelous change in the two bodies of the same soul, the reasonable answer and the efficient cause is given by saying, that when the soul left the earth, that body which fitted it and registered its growth was a psychic-body under limitations of flesh; but owing to its marvelous growth during its intimate fellowship with Christ in Paradise, that body which will be its fitting garment in the resurrection, and fully register its age-long growth, must transcend flesh and blood, and be a glorified resurrected spiritual-body under limitations of celestial matter. "For of the soule the bodie form doth take; 

For soule is form and doth the bodie make." 

Body is always the objective manifestations of spirit, working through mind, and conditioned by two 52

factors: (1) growth of soul, (2) kind of material in which it is functioning. There are celestial bodies and terrestrial bodies made out of celestial matter and terrestrial matter; but the essence all matter is one substance, the transmutation wrought by spirit,, the wondrous alchemist, as heat on the physical plane changes solid to liquid and liquid to gas. The difference between the body of a grain of sand and an archangel is the kind and degree of life incarnate in each; for "that which hath been made was life in Him" who descended from heaven as spirit, incarnated Himself as flesh-body, descended to hell in His psychical-body, and ascended to heaven in His spiritual-body.

St. Paul uses every metaphor, analogy, and simile upon earth and in heaven to teach us that soul shapes the body, and that for every degree of development of soul there is a corresponding change wrought in the body, crowning it with additional glory. We have seen with our own eyes the transforming and glorifying effects upon the body that a transformed and glorified soul hath wrought. The sensual, overreaching, and selfish soul degrades and stamps its foul seal upon the body-bloated and bloodshot eyes, cunning and beastliness written in every line of the face, with the lineaments of the hog, the fox, the eagle, the hyena-the outward stamp of the foul and degraded soul within.

But let that person be spiritually regenerated, and what a transformation is wrought outwardly, corresponding to the inward change. The eyes clear up, and the sensual expression goes, as the beastly body is transformed by the new life coming down from heaven. 

If we will go to the family album, and notice the likeness of ourselves taken at different ages, we will see in the photograph of the baby that the face is smooth, innocent, with no trace of care, and without character chiseled in the face-only happy and most blessed childhood. The next photograph will reveal boyhood merging into manhood. The childish face has gone, because the 53

child soul has grown into the youthful, boyish soul light-hearted, full of enthusiasms, high hopes, and ambitions. The other photograph reveals the face of a thoughtful man, with the cares, anxieties, and dignities of life resting upon him, and hints of skies which have been lightning-rent and thunder-riven. What has changthe face of the happy child into that of the hopeful boy, and the face of the hopeful boy into the thoughtful man, but the growing and expanding soul? Childhood-boyhood-manhood-mark the progressive evolutions of the growing and expanding soul, ever shaping to itself a body corresponding to its inward life. 

In this manner we see how the soul shapes the body, and makes it correspond to, and objectify, its thouht development. And because the soul goes on growing in Paradise throughout an aeonic series of years, "because to him that overcometh will I give to eat of the tree of life which is in the midst of the Paradise of God," the soul will finally attain such power that it cannot be holden of death, and will as naturally come forth from the realm of Hades as the strong man breeaks the bars of his prison and comes forth. 

Thus our life in Paradise explains, as nothing else ean, both the life in this world and the life of heaven to come. In Paradise man stands in the middle of his pilgrim journey, and looks back to this world, and sees the beginning; and looks forward and sees the end, and is satisfied. Since God must begin His creative process somewhere, man looks to this world and sees that it is the world of beginning; the world of creation; God's seed-bed, in which the soul of man is created and launched upon its pilgrimmage of psychical life. God made man's body out of the dust of the ground, and breathed into him the spirit of life, and he became then and there a soul.

No Life in Vain 

In the light of this it is clearly seen that no life in this world has been in vain; for consider the purpose 54

of this world sin stained. It is God's seed-bed for the propagation of souls. And seeds are planted in seedbeds to be transplanted sooner or later. If the seed-bed has propagated the plant its purpose is served, and the plant is to be transplanted to the fields where its growth is to be finished. So man's life here at best is but a few years, all leaving this world incomplete; for no life that is sin-stained can be completed here,-and so, more or less of the growth of the soul must be done in Paradise. Since this world is for us but the world of beginning, we can readily see how Paradise is the vastly more important of the two worlds, so far as our growth is concerned. 

First, on account of the duration of time spent in each. In this world life may be for only a minute, a day, a month; counting ourselves most blessed, if we have lived our three-score years and ten. But, as regards duration, how different is life in Paradise. Here a few years at best, there age upon age; for the world of 

Paradise began for this planet with the death of Abel and will last-well, as long as souls are created and are dying on this planet; when that creative process will end it would take a brave man to say. Secondly, it is the more important world for us because the soul will be more closely united with Christ, and the environment for soul growth will be doubly favorable, both on account of aeonic series of unbroken years of soul development, and the purer and more powerful spiritual atmosphere emanating from the presence of Christ. Therefore "I wage not any feud with death 

For changes wrought on form or face; 

I know transplanted human worth

Will bloo m to profit otherwhere." 

Paradise Robs Death Of Its Terrors 

And, lastly, Paradise robs death of its terrors by showing that the angel of death and the angel of life are not two angels, but the same angel. The angel that 55

takes our loved ones from us in this world we call the angel of death-the black-robed angel. On this side we are weeping around the form that once contained our loved one. This is the side of the picture which we see, but the other side how different!

This same angel, which in the darkness of earth we call the black-robed angel, as it wings its way into the light af Paradise becomes the white-robed angel of birth and life. And on that side there is rejoicing, because a soul is born in Paradise. So the angel of death and the angel of life are the same angel, every death being a birth, and every birth being a death; for death to this world is birth into Paradise, and death to Paradise is birth into heaven. 

Therefore we can triumphantly exclaim with St. 

Paul, "O death, where is thy sting?" and exultingly proclaim, Thou are everlastingly swallowed up in the ever-increasing greater gift of life, until man's long pilgrimmage is ended, having returned whence he came, forever at home with God in heaven, enriched with all the experience the good and evil of the universe can give, the fullness of the stature of Christ-Jesus at last wrought out in us.
