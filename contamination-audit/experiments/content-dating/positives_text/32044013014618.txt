INTRODUCTION 

Ten years before L. Frank Baum wrote The Wizard of 

Oz, of which more than nine million copies have been sold, he was trying desperately to keep the sheriff from attaching his weekly newspaper in Aberdeen, South Dakota. As editor of the Aberdeen Saturday Pioneer from 1888 to 1891, Baum made fun of townspeople, vigorously attacked rival editors, and ridiculed officeholders. He discontinued the newspaper in March 1891 because, he said, the sheriff wanted it more than he did.

From Aberdeen Baum moved his family to Chicago, where he successively worked on the Post, sold crockery, and edited a periodical for window decorators. In 1897 he wrote his first book, Mother Goose in Prose; it was also the first book illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Two years later Baum wrote Father Goose: His Book and William W. Denslow illus trated it, after which they bought the plates and within three months sold sixty thousand copies. 3

4 The Wizard of Oz was an instant success when it appeared in 1900. The next year two vaudeville actors Fred 

Stone and Dave Montgomery-were skyrocketed to stardom in a stage version of the book. In 1939 a motion picture version of it was shown in theaters throughout the world. 

From 1900 to the time of his death in 1919, Baum wrote scores of books. He had a penchant for pen names, writing twenty-four books under the name of Edith Van Dyne and several others as Floyd Akers and Susan Metcalfe. Two novels were published over the nom de plume of Schuyler 

Staunton. His fourteen "Oz" books-such as Emerald City of Oz, The Tik Tok Man of Oz, and the Woggle Bug Bookwere the most popular and they still have tremendous circulations.

While workers of the South Dakota Writers' Project were looking through early newspaper files in Aberdeen, scanning the pages for material to be used in a booklet about the city, they became intrigued by a column called "Our Landlady" that appeared in the Aberdeen Saturday Pioneer from

January 1890 to February 1891. Soon the researchers, typists, and editors were as engrossed in reading the serialized column as they had been years before in reading about the cowardly lion or the magical monarch of Mo. In "Our Landlady," through the medium of a fictitious boarding house proprietor and her boarders, Baum first showed evidence of his flair for fantasy. He described talking motion pictures as long ago as 1891 and predicted all kinds of electrical devices that have not yet been invented.

We have tried to select the most interesting columns for publication. To make this delightful satire of a Midwestern boom town more amusing, we have identified characters, places, and situations in the margins.

SHE INSISTS ON HER BOARDERS KEEPING LENT 

"I suppose," said the landlady, furtively eyeing an inkstain on the carpet and smoothing the ample wrinkles out of her ample gown with her ample hands, "I suppose, gentlemen, as you're all good 'Piskipalians.'

The doctor colored, and answered, "I frequently attend that church, and yes, I may say that I am an Episcopalian." "Ever sense that Juniper sociable!" remarked the landlady, sarcastically. "I myself feel strongly drawn to that excellent-would you call it religion? or sect? or-" "Call it the Guilded Clique!" chuckled the landlady, to the Colonel's no small confusion. "And Tom-" "I was brought up in the tenets of the church," replied that languid young man. "I don't know what the tenets were, but I was brought up in 'em." "Then they was probably red flannels an' diapers," answered the landlady, absent-mindedly, while Tom turned to the photograph of Susan B. to enable him to regain his selfpossession. For poor Susan always has possessed herself. "Therefore," says the landlady, with a smile of satisfaction, "you are are all 'Piskiples. Of course you'll keep lent."

The boarders looked at each other in surprise and uneasiness. "I think I shall deny myself something," remarked the 

Colonel; "I shall either smoke nickel cigars instead of imported ones or take to a pipe. I haven't decided which."

"And I," said the doctor, cheerfully, "shall economize on horse feed. My mare has really had too liberal an allowance of oats lately. What shall you do, Tom?" and they all looked curiously at the dyed-in-the-wool Episcopalian. "Oh, there is one course of denial which I always follow," says this interesting youth. "I deny myself postage stamps and write to all my friends on postals. It's inconvenient, ye know, but the lenten season must be duly observed."

The landlady smiled an Act III, Scene III smile, for the climax was approaching and led them without a word to the dinner table. "Mrs. Bilkins," said the Colonel, when all were seated, "I am a little rushed today, as I have a client awaiting my return to renew a note. Please fetch on the dinner." "The dinner," replied the landlady, trying to repress a fiendish look of triumph, "is on. This is Ash We'nesday. 

Most landladies who has 'Piskiple boarders has nothin' but ashes fer to eat today, but I ain't that sort. Good 'Piskiples, as 'tends the Guild socials so r'glar, mustn't be starved, altho' they should be incouraged in them tenements o' the church as Mr. Tom were brought up in. So I've got some nice mush and milk for you, an' if your conscience don't prick you,-fall to an' eat hearty!"

The boarders were conquered. They turned their hollow eyes and mouths and pink suffused brows upon the mush, and naught save the rattle of the spoons against the bowls broke the ominous silence which was the only thing that had reigned in Aberdeen since winter set in. "I once knew a woman," remarked the Colonel at last, spitefully, "so mean that she put holes in her fried-cakes to economize." "Did she die a horrible death?" asked Tom. "She did." 

The landlady was unmoved. "And an old woman with whom I boarded chopped her 15

hash so fine that she had to press the atmosphere over the platter to keep it from floating in the air." "That was in Lent," beamed the landlady, good naturedly. 

Here the doctor distinguished himself. "One good thing about this season," said he, "is that boarding house keepers never ask for any money, because they know it's Lent!"

Mrs. Bilkins turned pale, and left the room abruptly, while the other boarders made the best of their meagre fare and started for town in a brighter mood. 

The landlady looked after them through a crack in the kitchen door. "It'll be a heap o' savin' just now, this Lent business; but I'm afeared," with a sigh that came from the darns on the heels of her socks, "I'm afeared they'll more 'n make it up at Yeaster!" 16

SHE MAKES A TERRIBLE MISTAKE AND GETS JILTED 

Our landlady sat in the kitchen peeling some genuine green apples for pies, and as she dexteriously separated the outer cuticle with the old razor that was the only visible relic of the late Mr. Bilkins, her thoughts wandered into figuring the cost of each apple and how much would go into a piece of pie and whether she could really afford to feed her boarders on such a luxury at the present rate of board. And then, glancing at the razor, she thought of the late Mr. B. and sighed as she remembered her lonely condition, till womanlike widow woman-like, we mean she begun wondering when the fairy prince would come to her and awaken her into new life with a lover's kiss, saying "Sairy Ann, loved one, you shall never keep boarders more while your Alphonso lives to work his fingers to the bone to dress you in silks and satins!" The razor lay idle upon her knee now, and our landlady wiped her eyes and nose on her apron and gazed dreamily out of the window. 

"Madam!" 

It was the Colonel's voice that aroused her. He had come to see if he couldn't get his dinner a little earlier than usual as he was going out of town for a day, but seeing how startled she was, he controlled the natural severity of his voice, and coming to her side he added "My dear Mrs. 

Bilkins, I have a proposal to make to you." 

Undoubtedly her previous train of thought had slightly turned our landlady's head.

A light of rapture shone in her eyes; the pan of apples

flew right over the Colonel's head the razor just skipping his cheek and a large apple paring wound itself around his nose and buried its point in his right eye, while to cap the climax to his surprise Mrs. Bilkins threw herself with a loud scream into his arms.

The Colonel had been through many wars, but this was a coup-de-main for which he was wholly unprepared. 

He gave a groan and sat down abruptly upon the floor, Mrs. Bilkins falling upon his lap and retaining presence of mind enough to pin down both his arms in a warm and hearty embrace, while she stuck the knob of hair she wore on top of her head plumb into the Colonel's open mouth.

For a moment nothing could be heard but the babble of Mrs. Bilkins' endearing words, for the Colonel was struggling for breath and buried under the weight of a good 300 pounds.

Then the door suddenly flew open, and Tom and the doctor stood upon the threshold.

For one instant they stood horror stricken, and then they wickedly burst into a roar of laughter, while the one eye the Colonel could see out of glared angrily over the top of Mrs. 

Bilkins' head, and the landlady, impervious to interruption, murmured: "My sweet, sweet Colonel-I've a prayed for this hour to come an' I knew all the time as you loved me as I love you an' no knife can cut our love in-two, my own lovey dovey-" "Excuse us," said the doctor, in his gentle voice, "We were unaware that a love scene was going on here, and finding no dinner ready, we-" 

The Colonel made a superhuman effort and rolled Mrs.

Bilkins from his lap. Then he sprang to his feet, pulled his dripping hat out of the water barrel, and with a cry of rage that sounded like a cuss-word, tore out of the back door and disappeared.

Tom attempted to assist Mrs. Bilkins to her feet, but

she pushed him aside and, rising unaided, she cried: "There, you've found out our little love affair almost as soon as we found it out ourselves! I don't care for my part, but the Colonel seems a little riled." "He does, indeed," said Tom. "May we congratulate you?" asked the doctor. "To be sure ye may!" answered the landlady, holding her breath to see if she couldn't blush, "now set right down here, an' I'll git dinner an' tell ye all about it at the same time. Ye see this here Lenten season is just the time for loveaffairs. There ain't no parties, an' the boys an' gals just keep company, an' after the season o' Lent is gone there allus comes a season o' keeps, when marryin' an' givin' in marriage is in order. In Aberdeen a gal ain't in style if she ain't going ter be married after Yeaster, or ain't got a diamond ring ter show, anyhow. I can count more'n twenty couples as is parin' off, an' the Colonel an' I jest thought as we'd be in style. He hain't give me no ring yet, but," she added reflectively, "he ain't had a chance." "I think he has gone for one now," said Tom. "Well, anyhow," said the landlady, "you can't keep no secrets in a boardin' house, so it's just as well you found it out. The Colonel ain't young, ner handsome, ner rich, an' he owes me nine weeks board, but love is blind, they say, an' he's a man, anyhow, and 'll pertect me if he knows what's good fer himself! But there dinner's ready. Excuse my flustration, gentlewen, fer a perposal allus knocks a woman silly - even when she expects it!" 

The two boarders ate their meal in dismal silence. They were full of awe and horror, and there was an air of mystery about the affair which puzzled them. 

As they finished, a messenger boy strolled lazily in with a note for Mrs. Bilkins. "Please read it, doctor," she said, excitedly, "it's from the Kernul, but I'm that flustrated I couldn't find my glasses in a week!" 19

"My dear madam'," read the doctor," I simply wished to propose to you that you give me a lunch before dinner so I could catch the train, as I am obliged to be out of town for a day. But as it is, I have gone without it, so don't trouble yourself'."

A hushed silence pervaded the room, during which the doctor was afraid to look at the landlady, and Tom was afraid to look at the doctor.

At length the former remarked quietly as he fitted his napkin into its pewter ring: "There's many a slip 'twixt the cup and the lip." "An' there's as good fish in the sea as was ever pulled out'n it," replied the landlady bitterly. "For my part," said Tom to the doctor, "if the Colonel ever tries to put on any more airs, I shall just call him 'the landlord'!" "Men," replied Mrs. Bilkins, as she walked into the kitchen and kicked the cat for eating the soup, "Men is all deceivers, an' allus was. I won't git a husband not jest now but I'll get them nine weeks' board if it takes a leg!" 20
