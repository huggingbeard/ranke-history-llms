SOUTHEAST ASIA Area of Challenge, Change, and Progress INTRODUCTION 

In many parts of the world where developments ive traditionally been measured in terms of nturies, the past 20 years have been a period of eathtaking change. Nowhere is this more true an in Southeast Asia. Here a vast area- >reading over more than a million and a half quare miles of land inhabited by about 190 milɔn people has suddenly undergone a sharp hange in the pattern of its relationship to the st of the world. From the isolation imposed y geography and a colonial pattern of control, ost of these lands and peoples were for the first me thrust into the midst of international polics mainly as a result of national movements hich had led them to independence. Their rapid olitical growth may also be attributed to adances in technology, which have shrunk global istances in time and space.

The problems imposed by this sudden transforlation are staggering in scope. These countries re fired with a zeal for self-government but are imited in the traditions of representative govrnment, politically experienced populations, or ne skilled administrators essential to the attainient of this goal. They are rich in resources but oor in the capital or technology needed to exploit hem. They are dedicated to the preservation of heir ardently cherished independence, but they ften lack the military and economic means to uarantee it.

From any point of view the struggles of these ountries today are dramatic. Their efforts to dapt the social patterns of their countries to the omplexities of modern life are an epic in themelves. Their concomitant struggles to establish lew forms of government, to develop their econonies, and to raise their standards of living are monumental by any standards. But perhaps the most striking, and certainly from their own point of view the most emotional, is their battle to retain independence in the face of continuous Communist pressures.

Each of them has gone about this task in its own way, depending on its history and experiences, its geographical position, and its conception of the dangers which it faces. Some governments, for example, have concluded that their independence is best served by neutrality in the face of the world's great ideological division.

Others are convinced that the threat to their independence, embodied in the subversions and aggressions of international communism, can best be met by a collective defense alliance. 

These latter nations-Pakistan, Thailand, and the Philippine Republic-have joined with the 

United States, the United Kingdom, France,

Australia, and New Zealand to form an alliance for their mutual protection. Each of these powers recognized that the extension of Communist control into Viet-Nam exposed the remainder of 

Southeast Asia to danger. All were aware that a threat to the security of this area constituted a threat to the security of the entire free world. 

They joined forces in recognition of their need for united action to halt Communist aggression in the far Pacific:- 

At Manila in September 1954 these eight powers took action under the charter of the United Nations, demonstrating their will and their ability to reach accord and to create unity for security and peace. This was the first conference, as Secretary of State John Foster Dulles then pointed out, "... where representative nations of Asia and of the West sat down together to work out a program of mutual security." They formed an alliance, the credentials of which are the South- 948788 1

east Asia Collective Defense Treaty (Manila Pact) and the Pacific Charter. 

The Manila Pact is a treaty for defense against both open armed attack and internal subversion. 

The Pacific Charter, to quote Mr. Dulles, 

". . . dedicates all the signatories to uphold the principles of self-determination, self-government, and independence for all countries whose peoples desire it and are able to undertake its responsibilities." 

Since the formation of the Southeast Asia

Treaty Organization (SEATO) in 1954, much has been done to develop understanding and friendship among the peoples of the member nations through cooperative programs of mutual economic aid, technical assistance, and cultural exchange. 

While developing these positive plans for peaceful progress, the SEATO countries are increasing their capacity to deter would-be aggressors by building up an efficient collective defense system, which is providing a shield for the protection of the entire area. In this way the energies of the peoples of Southeast Asia are being released of positive and constructive work toward the comm welfare of these nations.

World War II underscored the importance of 

Southeast Asia both as a gateway to the Orient ind as a source of strategic raw materials and agricultural products. Lying in the path of 

Japanese expansion, Indochina passed from French to Japanese control shortly after the fall of France. By the spring of 1942 the remaining countries of the area were under Japanese occupation. Until the end of the war in 1945, the gateway was a barrier and a source of supply inaccessible to all nations except Japan. 

Not long after the withdrawal of the Japanese from the area, it became apparent that Southeast 

Asia lay in the path of yet another type of expansion, that of world communism. Ever since 

World War II the Communist strategy of infil tration to exploit existing nationalist revolutionary movements or to foment them where they do not exist has complicated-and in some instances retarded-the transition of Southeast Asian countries from colonial status to independence. 

The successful advance of communism through 

China and into northern Viet-Nam threatens the remaining Southeast Asian countries with a more deadly form of colonialism than any the world has ever known. Extension of Communist control over the remainder of Viet-Nam, Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, and Burma, which together form 

Asia's rice bowl, would place most of the independent Asian nations in mortal danger. India,

Ceylon, Japan, and Malaya, which are dependent upon that rice bowl for life itself, would have access to it only on Communist terms. 

No Southeast Asian country is militarily strong enough to withstand armed Communist aggression without help. If these governments are to repel armed invasion, they must have outside military aid. Furthermore, the newly independent countries all face problems on raising their standards of living high enough to insulate their people completely against Communist subversion. If they are to prove to their peoples that free institutions can give them more solid satisfactions than communism's promised "pie in the sky," they must have economic and technical assistance from other non-Communist countries.

The United States has supported the Southeast Asian countries in their emerging independence and is ready to help them to preserve their freedom. Most of these newly independent countries of Southeast Asia know that the United States is a friend and has no imperialistic designs on them or on any other countries in the world. 

THE LAND AND ITS PEOPLES 

Although it sprawls across a vast area more than 3,000 miles from east to west and more than 

2,000 from north to south, Southeast Asia has less than half the actual land mass of the United States. Its population is about 15 million greater.

The configuration of the land has created barriers not only between countries but also between communities. 

The area abounds in rugged, trackless mountains rich in virgin forests and scarcely tapped minerals. There are dense jungles, fever-infested swamps, fertile inland plateaus, turbulent rivers, and fertile river deltas and coastal plains. In the monsoon belt wet and dry seasons alternate with regularity; outside it the lowlands are hot and humid the year round. Temperature variations increase with altitude, and altitudes range from sea level to heights of more than 18,000 feet in the north where the Burma-China border becomes indistinct.

The region's lush fertility makes land clearance a never-ending labor. The work of years was ob literated by World War II. Bridges, railways, roads, and inland waterways suffered wholesale destruction, and once-cultivated fields reverted to jungle. Economic disruption was serious throughout the entire area.

The peoples are disparate in racial background, religion, language, and customs. They have known about every form of rule which may be exercised by a tribal chieftain, a rajah, a sultan, a king, a dictator, or a democratically elected parliament.

Some 3,000 years ago the area began to receive waves of immigrants who were to effect great changes in the life and thinking of whatever land they reached. The Chinese were first, coming usually in the spirit of trade and conquest. A later wave consisted of Hindu immigrants, who came from India in the first and second centuries A.D. and brought with them higher forms of religion, philosophy, and art and an advanced social organization. Much later, traders from the Arabian Sea arrived at Indonesian ports, bringing the Islamic religion as well as commercial cargo. 

Eventually Islam was carried to Malaya and the southern Philippines.

In the 16th century the Europeans began to reach the area-Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch,

British, and French-in that order. Their merchant companies busied themselves with developing natural resources for the good of the mother country. Their missionaries came to teach the 

Christian faith to receptive elements of the population. And in due time, European racial strains were added to the existing complex. 

European colonialism and the impetus it g to commerce drew more Chinese and Indians rien the area. Both groups-the Chinese in partic lar became small businessmen, middlemen, an moneylenders. As they prospered, many becam landlords, bankers, and plantation operators. T day more than 10 million Chinese live in the are The vast majority of them still consider themselve Chinese regardless of their place of birth. In the sparsely settled inland frontier regions, steep mountains and turbulent rivers separate hill peoples from one another well as from their countrymen in the densely pop ulated lowlands. This separation of communities has preserved intact hundreds of dialects and h increased the difficulty of achieving cooperation among the peoples.

Despite diversity of origins, languages, and re ligions, the peoples have had certain experiences in common. Throughout the area the standard of living is measured within a rice economy. Vil lage societies have persisted for centuries. Aver age life expectancy is low and infant mortality high, with malaria, dysentery, and tuberculosis taking a heavy toll.

Barring some exchange of raw materials fo manufactured goods from the West and fro some Asian countries, the populations have bee largely self-sufficient, with little need of trade other dealings with one another. Every country within the area has a past history of tribal of interracial warfare and periodic invasions, prin cipally from the north-from China. None unaware that pressure for southward expansion continues in the giant country to the north.

Southeast Asia is the world's largest exporter of natural rubber, copra, quinine, kapok, rice, teak, pepper, tapioca flour, and tin. It produces in some quantity sugar, tea, coffee, tobacco, sisal, tropical fruits, essential oils, spices, natural resins and gums, oil, iron ore, and bauxite. Burma has some oil, but the only large resources discovered as yet are in Indonesia and British North Borneo. Currently the area produces rice for its own needs and a surplus of almost 4 million tons for export; its rubber plantations provide the world with about 90 percent of its crude rubber; its mines provide about 60 percent of the world's tin; and it produces some 84 percent of the world's copra and coconut oil. ing and farming methods sharply limit production in most countries of the area. Currently the yield of agriculture, which accounts for more than 70 percent of the total production, is only a fraction of what it might be. As yet Southeast

Asia has scarcely tapped its vast natural resources. 

Southeast Asia supplies about 5 percent of all

U.S. imports and buys approximately 5 percent of all U.S. exports. Although much of the area's trade is with other Asian countries, about a fourth of it is with the United States and another fourth is with Europe.

The heaviest producers of rubber and tin have been most seriously affected when there is a drop in prices, and they have tended to hold the United 

States responsible. Indonesia and Malaya de pend on rubber for nearly 35 percent and 46 percent, respectively, of their foreign exchange earnings. Rubber exports are important also to Viet- 

Nam and Cambodia. Sales of tin provide 8 percent of Malaya's foreign exchange, 4 percent of 

Thailand's, and 12 percent of Indonesia's. The rise or fall of a few cents in the prices of these raw materials makes a difference of millions of dollars to the producing countries. 

Rubber, which sold for as high as 88 cents a pound in 1951, dropped in 1953 to 20 cents; since then it has risen gradually, and at the close of 

1954 the market was relatively firm, with the price hovering around 36 cents per pound. It dropped sharply again in 1957 but began a gradual, steady recovery in the first part of 1958. 

Well into 1959, the price was just above 30 cents per pound. Over the same period the price of tin has fluctuated even more violently, particularly as the result of Soviet tin disposals in 1958. 

The specter of low prices for these vital exports, plus the low productivity of the area, constitutes 10

one of the major economic problems of Southeast

Asia. 

Throughout the area, per capita income appears to be growing, but very slowly, and in several countries it has failed to reach pre-World War II levels. This situation slows political and economic progress at the same time that it makes economic development the principal objective of most government programs. A complex of circumstances has contributed to the slow rate of increase in per capita income. Outstanding among them are destruction wrought by World War II and subsequent guerrilla fighting, reluctance of the West to reinvest capital, lack of savings for domestic investment, scarcity of management skills, and lack of skilled labor. 

In Southeast Asia, as in other parts of the world, serious deterioration in economic conditions offers opportunity for Communist sub sion and consequent political instability.

Southeast Asian countries are receiving this as sistance from various agencies including the United Nations, the Colombo Plan, and the Inter national Bank for Reconstruction and Develop ment. They also receive aid from the United 

States, from Japan through the settlement of reparation claims, and in some cases also from the Sino-Soviet bloc. Most of the countries need more assistance than they are currently receiving This critical area has fabulous potential wealth in its natural resources of fertile soil, mineral de posits, and manpower; but now, and for some time to come, it must have help if it is to learn how to make the most of them.

The island world of Indonesia, formerly known as the Netherlands East Indies, makes up the argest and most heavily populated of the Southast Asian countries. Its total land mass is more han double the size of Texas and is a vast archipelago stretching south and east of the Malay

Approxinately 2.5 million Christians and 3 million Chiese Taoists, Buddhists, or Confucianists enjoy he freedom of religion provided for religious miorities in Indonesia's Constitution, but the largst segment of the population-over 90 percents Muslim.

Second in size of the three states which formerly composed French Indochina, Laos is more than 89,000 square miles in area-somewhat larger than Idaho—and has an estimated population of 2 mil-

lion, chiefly of the Thai race. It is located in the heart of Southeast Asia, wedged in between Communist north Viet-Nam and the Republic of Viet- Nam on the east, Communist China on the north, Burma on the northwest, Thailand on the west, and Cambodia on the south.

Laos is underdeveloped, some of the non-Lao mountain tribes being completely primitive. 

Throughout the country agriculture is conducted about as it was 2,000 years ago. Industrial enterprises are confined to a few transport undertakings, one mining operation, and some sawmilling and small-scale local manufacturing. Laos must, therefore, import almost all manufactured products necessary to its economy.

One of the major economic difficulties is the high cost of transportation of goods to Laos and the poor internal communications and distribution facilities. Recent extensions of the Thai railway and installation of a ferry system across the Mekong River near the capital, Vientiane, have facilitated the use of the port of Bangkok in place of the longer and still more costly previous route from Saïgon. Internal transport routes include over 1,000 miles of roads, badly damaged by the war, and the Mekong River.

On July 19, 1949, as a result of a treaty between France and Laos, the latter became an independent state. Final powers were transferred on December 29, 1954. The country is a constitutional monarchy with a King who is popular among his people and a Crown Prince who is a devoted patriot. Under the December 1956 revision of the 

Constitution originally promulgated by the King in May 1947, the hitherto exclusively male franchise was extended to adult women. Less disrupted by Communist aggression in the early days of its independence than its neighbor Viet-Nam,

Laos has consequently known a smoother transition from colonial status to independence. 

The prospects for Laos have clearly improved in the past year as a result of a considerable effort. on the part of the patriotic leaders of the country, an effort that would not have been possible without the material support of the U.S. mutual security program. 

However, renewed Communist insurgency, which has brought world attention to Laos, has created serious difficulties for the country's co tinuing progress. Malaya 

Malaya is a long, thin finger of land forming the southernmost part of the Southeast Asian peninsula. Smaller than Florida, and with 80 per cent of its terrain dense jungle, Malaya is more highly developed than most of its neighbors. is rich in strategic rubber and tin, has a higher standard of living than any other Southeast Asian country, boasts the comparatively high literacy rate of 40 percent, and has more trained administrators than any other country in this area. 

The geographic term Malaya is loosely used and refers jointly to the Federation of Malaya and the State of Singapore, although the two territories are entirely separate political entities. The 

Federation of Malaya became a sovereign nation within the Commonwealth on August 31, 1957, with its capital at Kuala Lumpur. It comprises 

11 states, including the 9 Malay States and the former Straits Settlements of Penang and Ma lacca. The Constitution of the Federation provides for a parliamentary democracy with a strong central government within the framework of an elective monarchy. It also provides for the adop tion of a similar parliamentary system in each of the 11 states comprising the Federation, with the 10

ultans acting as constitutional rulers in the May States and appointed governors serving in this apacity in Penang and Malacca.

Chinese are a mixture of Confucianists, Taoists, Buddhists, and Christians. The Indians are prelominantly Hindu, and the Europeans and Euraians predominantly Christian. Malaya's strategic location has made it important to the plans of aggressors in that part of the world. It was used by the Japanese in 1942 as a steppingstone to Indonesia, and it is not by accident that one of the most ruthless and persistent armed Communist terrorist offensives since the end of World War II took place in Malaya. 

Since mid-1948 Communist guerrillas have waged war against the forces of law and order in Malaya with the expressed objective of establishing a Communist state. Operating from the great jungles and from clandestine cells, the guerrillas seriously disrupted the life and economy of the country and for a long time were successful in thwarting the efforts of the military and police to eradicate them. Within recent years, however, new military measures and civil programs directed against the Communist guerrillas have virtually eliminated Communist-inspired incidents throughout most of the country. Malaya's achievement of independence has already deprived the Communists of one of their principal ideological weapons in carrying on an increasingly hopeless struggle against the lawful government of the Federation. 11

By Southeast Asian standards, Thailand's pop lation of 23 million is remarkably homogeneous.

Although defense and economic development fforts have strained Thailand's budget, its forign exchange position and long-term economic prospects are good. By Western criteria the

Since 1932 Thailand has been a constitutional monarchy and changes in government have come about principally through coups d'état. The mountainous central are known as the High Plateau is sparsely inhabited by primitive tribal people, of whom there are several different groups.

Potentially Viet-Nam is rich. Its people an often rated the most energetic and aggressive of Southeast Asia, and it is one of the world's larg est rice producing countries. Other important agricultural products include rubber, corn, copra tobacco, and sugar. Rubber is the most important foreign exchange earner, and it currently exceeds prewar production. Viet-Nam has not yet achieved its prewar level of rice exports because of dislocation of production and increased dome tic consumption, but production is rising as new rice lands are brought under cultivation. A few other agricultural products are exported in small quantities, and a number of additional items are grown for local consumption.

Viet-Nam also has resources of iron, chrome ores, phosphate, zinc, coal, and other minerals as well as valuable forests and fishing waters. The mineral resources have been found mainly in the north.

Under the terms of the Geneva cease-fire agree ment in 1954, Viet-Nam was temporarily parti tioned for military regrouping purposes into two parts at approximately the 17th parallel. The agreement provided that everyone in the country should be allowed to decide freely in which zone he wished to live, and as a result about 800,000 people fled their homes in the north to seek refuge from communism in the free south. Slightly less than half of the area of Viet-Nam and slightly more than half its population are still under Communist control in the north.
