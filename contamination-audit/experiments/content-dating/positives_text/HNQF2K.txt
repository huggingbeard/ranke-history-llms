SUGGESTIONS FOR LEADERS 

1. PURPOSE. This material is intended for use in adult or young people's church school classes, young people's society programs, Schools in Christian Living, Temperance Sunday programs, or leadership-education courses. It will also be of help in the preparation of talks and sermons dealing with the liquor problem. The authors have attempted to combine the findings of careful scientific investigation with the idealism of the Christian religion. 

2. HOW TO USE. "An Old Fight in a New Arena" contains material for at least seven sessions. Each chapter is divided into four parts.

I. WORSHIP. The worship can be used when the group is able to conduct a worship service in addition to the study period. It should be prepared carefully, and conducted by someone other than the person presenting the study. When stories are included, they will be more effective if told rather than read. Some suggestions for prayer are included. It is hoped that the worship leader will amplify this part of the program; and make use of prayer from individuals in the group as well as his own prayers. 

II. STUDY. This material can be presented by one person, or it may be divided among several, each preparing one of the subdivisions. For the most part, it should not be read, but given from memory or from notes. 

III. DISCUSSION. While nine or ten questions for discussion are listed in each session, the leader will probably be wise not to use more than the three or four which seem particularly relevant and suggestive. A blackboard will be useful to the leader in listing the answers of the group. 

Discussion should be spontaneous, and it should be closed if there is a tendency to lag. 

. A brief devotional service is suggested, so that the session may end with the spirit in which it began, that of worship. [7]

3. ACTION. A list of suggested activities is included on pages 

93 and 94. It is hoped that groups using this unit will develop a sense of "mission," and seek practical ways by which they can be effective in improving social conditions in the local community and in the nation.

An Old, Old Struggle II. STUDY 

"Upon no one subject is the industry of man kept more constantly on the alert than upon the making of wine," complains Pliny, the ancient Roman naturalist. Writing in the first century, Pliny classified [10]

fifty kinds of "generous" (high-spirited) wines, thirty-eight varieties of foreign wines, seven types of salted wines and eighteen different sweet wines. 

Long before Pliny's time, the prophets of Israel had cried out against the drunkenness of ancient civilization. They also deplored the drinking which often prevailed among their own kings and priests. 

Noah's is the first case of drunkenness recorded in the Bible. "Noah began to be a tiller of the soil and he planted a vineyard; And he drank the wine and was drunken" (Gen. 9:20, 21). From Genesis to Revelation may be found such forceful indictments of strong drink as the following: "The drunkard and glutton shall come to poverty." "Look thou not upon the wine when it is red. . . . . At the last it biteth like a serpent and stingeth like an adder." "Let us walk honestly, not in rioting and drunkenness." Thus biblical writers associated drinking with the vices of ancient civilization. 

The pagan world often gave Israel "horrible examples" of drunkenness. Antiochus Ephiphanes, king of Syria in the second century before Christ, cruel oppressor of Israel, sometimes appeared at midday "flushed with wine, tossing money by handfuls into the street." Moehlman describes vividly the orgies of this thirsty potentate. "When celebrating his conquest over Egypt in the grove of Daphne, the festivities went on for a month. The chief city fountain sometimes ran with wine. Antiochus himself was up and down among the banqueters, sitting, standing, declaiming, drinking or bandying jest with the professional mummers."¹

In the ancient world many people abandoned their lives to alcoholic indulgence. Some leaders in ancient kingdoms observed this appalling loss and adopted personal abstinence. Thus also the temperance tradition got its start in antiquity. 

1 "When All Drank and Thereafter," Conrad H. Moehlman, Alcohol 

Information Committee, p. 3. [11]

Alcohol Comes into Its Own 

In spite of the lurid stories of drunkenness told by ancient writers, we now realize that the people of biblical times did not know what real drinking was. Since the process of distilling liquor and of fortifying wine was not discovered until about the eighth century A.D., the revelers of old were limited to the use of natural fermented beverages of not more than 14 per cent alcohol, by volume. 

Two interesting facts are revealed by this bit of historical information: 

1) We know that the ancients experienced a menacing drink problem though they were acquainted only with light wines and other naturally fermented beverages. 

2) History and literature reveal intensified attitudes toward alcohol as distilled liquors supplemented naturally fermented brews. 

Those who came to love their fortified wines and hard liquor claimed these would cure everything and anything. Some early leaders who saw the effects of this new and excessive use of alcohol set the stage for the crusade against John Barleycorn.

Drinks which now came to have an alcoholic content of 40 to 50 per cent were seen also to have greater potency in the destruction of health and in the production of crime and other social consequences. 

Strange as it may seem, a typical attitude came to be that of loving liquor and at the same time knowing the terrible results of its continued use. 

Professor Moehlman illustrates this tendency by citing a medieval toast: 

Op. cit., p. 3.

In the public house to die 

Is my resolution. 

Let wine to my lips be nigh,

At life's dissolution. 

That will make the angels cry, With glad elocution. 

Grant this toper, God on high, Grace and absolution.2 [ 12 ]

Students at medieval universities are said to have drunk healths to prisoners, captives, all Christians, the living, the dead, those traveling by land and by sea, the king, the Pope, and half a dozen other persons, fortunate and unfortunate. Nobility, peasantry, scholars, merchants and clergy were among those who tarried long at the cup in those days, and yet from each of these groups arose crusaders to warn against the hazards and handicaps of drink. "Firewater" and Gin Condemned

The desire for personal safety and security is well illustrated by two groups of liquor reforms. Recognizing the murderous consequence of encouraging the use of "firewater" among the savage Indian tribes of New France-now the Great Lakes region -Louis XIV of France in 1681 issued a decree prohibiting the sale of intoxicants in these parts. With but few exceptions the governing authorities dealing with Indians have followed this policy down to the present day. Prohibition against selling liquor to the Indians was a Federal Government regulation covering all Indian reservations. Western states sanctioned this policy because they feared to do otherwise. 

Similar vigilance was followed to discourage heavy drinking among the Negroes during the reconstruction period in the South. A new danger was abroad. Alcohol inflamed the freed slaves' sense of reckless liberty. Under slavery, drunkenness was met with sharp discipline by most slaveholders.

Now that the slaves had been freed, some of the same factors that operated in the development of the Ku Klux Klan also gave an impetus to the temperance movement in the South, particularly in rural areas, where women were afraid of violence from gin-drinking Negroes. In the interests of selfprotection, the White South deemed the passing of prohibition legislation a necessity. A hang-over from this condition was felt during national prohi- [13]

bition when in Southern states, which had gone dry to keep the bottle away from the colored population, officials flouted attempts at enforcement as far as the "respectable and well-to-do" white classes were concerned.

These reforms, prompted by fear of the scalping Indian crazed by firewater, and the razor-wielding Negro with his cheap but potent grade of gin, were prompted by a type of fear which we are again observing at work. The scene has now shifted to the drinking motorist, who, because of his considerable contribution to the ghastly motor death-rate, is already inspiring terror in the hearts of many who voted to establish the re-legalized liquor traffic. 

Again, the urge of self-preservation may take the field for drastic action. Along with these facts, let us also keep in mind that temperance reform has had deep roots in the South, among both white and colored populations, and that some Indian reservations claim to have the oldest temperance societies in the United States.

A Century of Progress 

Beginning with George Washington's administration, the Federal Government exercised the regulatory power of a high tax on alcoholic beverages. 

This was met with the Whiskey Rebellion in western Pennsylvania, which had to be quelled by militia. 

Step by step, town, county, state and federal authorities battled liquor interests for nearly a century and a half.

Millions of citizens were recruited in mass temperance movements of the early nineteenth century. 

The Washingtonian Society, formed in 1840, set out to reform every drunkard in the land. It pioneered for ten years. By that time Maine had established a state prohibition law. At the outbreak of the Civil 

War thirteen states were dry by state prohibition. 

Pressure for increased revenue both for the Federal Government and the Confederacy brought about the [14]

repeal of nearly all these early state prohibition laws. Following the Civil War, the influx of foreign population, increased organization of the liquor industry, and the post-war moral sag, greatly retarded temperance and prohibition activity. During the fifty years from 1870 to 1920, however, the crusade against the saloon marched steadily forward with increasing momentum. Legislation providing compulsory alcohol education in public schools, urged by the W.C.T.U., was enacted in all but two states. 

Local option, state prohibition, war-time prohibition, and finally, national prohibition rewarded the efforts of the temperance movement led by the Anti- Saloon League as "the church in action against the saloon.'

The Prohibition Era 

Thirteen years of national prohibition, 1920-1933, is the next chapter in the story of the American struggle against alcoholism. It is still difficult to discuss this era, because of the strong prejudices which were fanned to a white heat during the presidential campaign of 1932.

Certain conclusions, however, may now be ventured upon with which there will be general agreement: 

1. The prohibition ideal passed from the hands of its friends into the hands of its enemies, when it became a part of the supreme law of the land and was dependent upon the enforcement of local, state and federal authorities. The amazing thing is that it was enforced as conscientiously as it was. 

2. The traditional friends of prohibition looked upon the adoption of the Eighteenth Amendment as a final and complete victory. From that time forward they believed they could be free to concentrate on other social and economic problems. Temperance education was permitted to sag, woefully. Perhaps the removal of the saloon made this inevitable. 

There had been tremendous gain for temperance. [15]

3. But there had also been great loss to a vast alcohol industry, leaders of which had argued that the government should have bought up all their capital investment before putting them out of business. For the entire thirteen years, these men poured millions of dollars into the fight to reopen their breweries and distilleries. They made every effort to convince America that prohibition was a failure. Aligned with these interests were the politicians who had always relied on the patronage of the liquor constituency. 

4. Although much exaggerated, the relation of the illicit liquor traffic to criminal groups was used to the utmost by the "wet press" and by the "Crusaders" to disparage prohibition. 

5. The grossly extravagant charges that prohibition was debauching youth were based on reports that fast social and sport sets were increasingly patronizing swank speak-easies and toting hip flasks. 

6. It should be remembered that all official and intensive surveys of the Prohibition era, a wet survey included, show a marked decrease in the consumption of beverage alcohol. One of the most extended analyses indicated that with generous allowance for border and seaboard smuggling, and for bootleg operations, America was measuring up to only one-third of its preprohibition drink record. 

7. Then came the depression. The reinstatement of the alcohol industry, with all its allied trades, would put men to work! The government could collect vast new revenue and ease down on income tax rates! Under the stress of nation-wide panic, these campaign slogans were distorted out of all proportion to their significance.

So one might go on and on enumerating the issues involved in the rise and fall of national prohibition. 

Historians will be compelled to re-evaluate these issues as they record for posterity the story of the twentieth century, third decade. [16]

What Have We? 

It is needless to devote much space here to the discussion of conditions under "Repeal." Later discussions in this series will serve to analyze present conditions and trends. Three facts, however, may point the way toward intelligent discussion of the present situation: 

1. The whole nation is becoming alarmed over the drunken driver-and the drinking driver-as an increasing menace to public safety. The state governments are again groping and experimenting to discover effective systems for controlling the liquor traffic. Nearly as many plans are now in operation as there are states. This is inevitably making for confusion and inefficiency, while the Federal Government assumes a disinterested role, aside from collecting its share of the revenue and of suppressing bootleg operations at the rate of 300 raids per week. 

2. That the American people are responding to the high-powered appeals of the liquor traffic is shown by United States Internal Revenue figures, which indicate that from 1933 through 1937 America consumed at least twelve billion dollars' worth of alcoholic beverages. The annual drink bill mounted steadily from Repeal until it attained the peak of $3,530,000,000 in 1937. It is comforting to note that this figure dropped back to approximately three billion dollars during 1938. Even at this rate, however, the average family drink bill must have been in excess of seventy-five dollars for the year. 

Nearly $5,000 was being spent for drink in the 

United States every minute. 

3. Temperance and prohibition work in many areas is experiencing a new awakening and meeting with marked success. Hundreds of towns and counties are again dry by local option. Other groups are making progress with efforts to attain more rigid control legislation. [17]

An Old Fight Enters a New Phase 

What is to be the strategy in facing the liquor problem of the future? It is doubtful if anyone can answer this question dogmatically. Shall we educate? Shall we seek to take the profit out of the liquor business? Shall we employ more drastic methods of control? Shall we retrace the steps of history, working for local option, state and national prohibition? Or shall we simply do nothing until conditions become so bad that prohibition automatically comes back on the flood tide of public indignation? For the next ten years the alcohol problem in American life is sure to offer opportunity for interesting exploration. As in the past, the church will be challenged, time and again, to champion the Christian solution as opposed to vicious exploitation.

When the chamberlain returned, he had in his company four of the strongest, most handsome young men to be found in all Jerusalem. The king greeted them kindly and assigned to them comfortable rooms in the palace. He then gave orders that they should be provided with good clothing and the best of food. It happened that, according to Mosaic law, pork was not considered proper meat for their people to eat. It disturbed the Jewish boys greatly when they saw that pork was set before them at table. What should they do? They did not wish to offend the king, and yet they did not wish to offend the God whom they loved. Finally one of them, Daniel by name, went quietly to the master chamberlain. "If it please you, sir," he said courteously, "we beg that we be not compelled to eat the king's meat and drink his wine. Let us eat only vegetables and drink water and we shall be glad and grateful to the king.' 

This worried the master chamberlain. He had become fond of these four young men. In a troubled voice, the official replied, "I fear the lord, my king. 

What would he do if I allowed you to go without proper food? If your faces grow thin and starvedlooking, he will come after my head to pay for my negligence." 

But Daniel only laughed and said, "Fear not, but just try us ten days. Give us vegetables to eat and water to drink. Then examine us and see if we have lost in flesh and color, and if we look worse than those who use the king's food and wine."

Unwillingly, the chamberlain consented to this. 

As the days passed, he kept an anxious eye on his young wards. But to his vast amazement, he observed that the Jewish boys actually seemed to be growing plumper and rosier than any of the boys who ate the king's food. After that, the chamberlain allowed them to eat what they wished, and they grew and prospered in body and mind. [21]

II. STUDY Choosing One's Health 

"Now if those youngsters don't stay healthy, it's their own fault," says the efficient superintendent of schools, who sees to it that physiology and hygiene are taught, including a proper emphasis upon temperance instruction and narcotics information. 

He also has appointed a school doctor, school nurses, and a staff of gymnasium teachers. These are but a few of the steps toward giving the oncoming generation a thorough-going education in health. Hence, we scarcely can blame our superintendent for saying, "Stay healthy or it's your own fault"; although, of course, he may have to reckon with heredity and economic factors.

The emphasis of the past thirty years on health education and preventive medicine has been a primary factor in stretching out life expectancy. People have known the truth and the truth has made them free from many of the pitfalls of ill health. In the light of this modern knowledge, good health or poor health becomes largely a matter of choice. If we eat proper foods-follow the practice of asking for milk as our mealtime beverage; if we devote reasonable time to recreation, rest and sleep, we have from medical science and the insurance companies a promise of reward in terms of health, happiness and long life. On the contrary, the characteristic lack of sleep, and the recreation associated with patronage of the typical Repeal tavern, coupled with the mistake of using alcoholic drinks as if they were wholesome foods and beverages, inevitably undermine such assurances. In part, at least, good health becomes a matter of one's own choosing. [22]

The person who deliberately sets out to increase the likelihood of enjoying good health will be certain to recognize alcohol as an obstacle to that goal. 

In addressing the American Public Health Association, Dr. Haven Emerson-who is a nationally accepted authority on public health-said: "Lack of information is largely responsible for the use of alcoholic beverages, as if they were necessary or in any way useful to a healthy man or woman.' 

Your Health Must Be Good 

The importance of being healthy is a cardinal principle in industry and business. Though not necessarily expressed in so many words, the employment agent and personnel director let it be known that only persons in good health "need apply." The sequel to this proposition is the very limited patience exhibited toward the man or woman who gets sick or develops a physical weakness on the job. Indeed, your health must be good and stay good, or you will face the kind of destitution that ends with the unemployable section of the Relief Roll. 

He was with the company for fifteen years, and is now age forty-too old for employment in many companies. "Discharged in poor health" is next to having a prison record, as far as some firms are concerned. But he had signed a twenty-year contract to buy a home. This he must now give up, having lost his down payment and having paid more every month than could properly be charged off to rental. Perchance he gave a chattel mortgage on his furniture, to tide the family over until he could regain his health and find re-employment. All this brings him to a terrifying dead-end street. 

The hazard imposed on health by alcoholic indulgence, more than any other single factor, tends to hasten and intensify such trends of misfortune as have just been suggested. This is the reason many industrialists and social workers are joining with the school men in warning against the subtle conse- [23]

quences of drink. The railroader's "Rule G," to the effect that no employee is to imbibe on duty (and that the frequenting of drinking places off duty may constitute sufficient cause for discharge), is being revived by many industries for the protection of labor as well as for increased safety and efficiency. 

This policy has been adopted by the air lines and we venture the prediction that it will rapidly become a general rule.

Digestion, Respiration and Circulation Handicapped 

During the wave of temperance education in schools, churches and factories prior to the World War, the chronic alcoholic was vividly described as a horrible example suffering from ulcers of the stomach, "hobnail liver,” kidney disorder and heart trouble. All of these woeful conditions were truly enough the characteristic consequences which overtook the poor fellow who was about to stagger into the drunkard's grave. And the habitual heavy drinker must still contemplate such end-results. Government statistics reveal that three to four thousand persons in the United States die each year from acute or chronic alcoholism. Everyone knows that this figure falls far short of telling the whole story. 

So often alcoholism is considered a contributory cause, and not the primary cause of death-or out of respect for the family the cause of death is listed as "heart failure." We may be well assured that the government record at this point is extremely conservative. Accepting this figure, however, we must face the depressing fact that some drunkards forfeit their lives to alcoholism every day. In nearly all of these extreme cases, death closes a career of miserable health following upon the abuse of the digestive system and other vital organs.

It is doubtful if this parading of the "horrible example" has ever been a very effective method of temperance education. The drinker may hear of the calamitous misfortune and death of an alcoholic; [24]

his probable response will be, "That'll never happen to me. I know when to stop. I belong to the class of people who drink like ladies and gentlemen." 

Another unfortunate response to this "horrible example" type of temperance education comes particularly from youthful drinkers whose reaction might be characteristically phrased, "Well, what if

I do wind up in the Keeley Cure forty years from now, with the d. t.'s and cirrhosis of the liver-I'll have a gay, old time before I get there." Yet another reaction will come from young people who will say, "Exaggeration!" when the alleged physical effects of strong drink are pictured. Their observation of people they know does not seem to bear out the predictions of the reformer. Wet propaganda is always stressing the beauty of "moderation."

The new trend in temperance education, which developed primarily during the Repeal period, has not dealt so much with the "horrible example" as with the myriad of complications, handicaps and fatalities that arise from the moderate, occasional use of alcohol. We find the effects of alcohol on the nervous system to be of far greater significance to modern temperance education than its effects on the stomach. 

The Nervous System 

One might term alcohol the worst "gate crasher" with which the body has to contend. Unlike normal foods and beverages which undergo an intricate series of digestive processes, alcohol passes into the blood stream unaltered. When especially strong alcoholic drinks are taken, we are told by scientists, a small part of the alcohol may enter the blood stream even through the walls of the mouth and esophagus. Whereas the stomach is ordinarily a sort of mixing-bowl, preparing food for later absorption into the blood, some of the alcohol will penetrate the stomach lining and thus be absorbed by the blood stream. [ 25 ]

Once the alcohol gets into the blood stream it passes rapidly into the muscular and nerve tissues. 

It also loses no time in reaching the fluid of the spinal column-which gives it immediate access to the brain. This explains the speed with which alcoholic drinks show interference with normal mental processes. So complete is alcohol's penetration of the whole human organism that there is not a single function of the body, we are told, that is not affected by alcohol's presence. This results in a slowing down and a depressing of the vital organs, and a retarding of muscular activity-because beverage alcohol acts upon the nervous system as a narcotic and depressant. That the heart beats faster is no indication that the alcohol has stimulated it to better action, but rather that the delicate control holding the heartbeat to its normal tempo has been tampered with by the irritating influence of alcohol. 

That alcoholic indulgence in this machine age is bad for the nervous system may be illustrated by three of its most significant and invariable results: (1) It lengthens reaction time. (2) It handicaps muscular co-ordination and control. (3) It renders the drinker irresponsible.

In an age when so many important actions and decisions must be arrived at and executed in a split second, the prolonging of reaction time by even a fifth or two-fifths of a second may mean the difference of life and death. Athletic coaches know how alcohol prolongs reaction time, thus handicapping the runner in getting off at the shot of the gun. A far more distressing application of this principle is found in the coroners' records, from coast to coast, as drinkers, slowed down by alcohol, squander those precious split seconds and "embrace sudden death"; or what may be worse, live in remorse for having maimed or killed another.

But it is not merely the person who drinks a great deal who encounters that tragic aftermath of having done and said things he would never have risked when sober and normal. He was silly, undignified, and perhaps said hurtful things. Having imbibed more, he became stupid, possibly brutal. At a still later moment, when conscience and memory were thoroughly paralyzed, he did the thing that got him into front-page news-filling to overflowing his cup of sorrow, perhaps for a lifetime. That this can and does frequently happen is inescapable to anyone who reads the newspapers.

So we must urge that it is not only what alcohol does to the stomach, the liver and the heart, but more especially what it does to the nervous system and to the brain that is of chief concern today. "You [ 28 ]

can get along with a wooden leg, but you can't get along with a wooden head," declared Dr. Charles

H. Mayo in addressing a convention of Boy Scouts on the alcohol problem.

It's the Brain That Counts 

The old adage, "No smoke without fire," is not as invariable as this, When alcohol is in the stomach it is also in the blood and the brain. As long as it remains in the blood (and it is principally eliminated by the slow process of oxidation), it continues to feed into all the organs, including the most precious and delicate one of all, the brain. 

We gain some insight as to the way alcohol attacks the brain structurally when we learn that the whole nervous system, including the brain, is protected by a sheaf of fatty film or covering, and that alcohol dissolves fat. When such damage occurs, ill health or even death will follow. This inescapable fact is basic to the explanation of why from 7 to 25 per cent of insanity, as recorded in the various state hospitals, is chalked up to alcoholism. 99 "It's the brain that counts." So, from a physiological standpoint, it deserves to be kept in good health; not by trying to discover a "brain food," but by keeping it free from the poisoning effect of alcohol.

Alcohol Stalks the Sickroom 

The full significance of alcohol's handicap in the struggle against disease is brought to us most forcibly by the following statement by Dr. Haven Emerson:

Alcohol is a depressant, habit-forming narcotic drug. 

Alcohol is a protoplasmic poison. Alcohol is drunk to get the drug effect, and whenever it is so taken in whatever amount, it exerts to some degree its depressant and narcotic effects. Alcohol causes disease: alcohol psychoses, multiple neuritis, gastritis and alcoholic cirrhosis of the liver. Alcohol causes deaths from acute and chronic poisoning. Alcohol reduces resistance to infection. Alcohol diminishes likeli- [ 29 ]

hood of recovery from acute infections such as pneumonia. 

Alcohol increases liability to accidents and delays recovery from injuries and infections.

The fact that many hospitals no longer lay in stocks of beverage alcohol and that the medical profession generally has declared that liquor is outmoded as a medicine, suggests the progress that has been made in driving John Barleycorn from the sickroom. Dr. Bogen believes that during those rough and ready days on the frontier, when alcohol was prescribed as the universal cure-all, more persons died of alcohol than from the snake bites for which the liquor had been administered. Patent medicines containing as high as 50 per cent alcohol often served to ensnare unsuspecting victims whose last ills became ten times as acute as their first. 

Summing up the relation of alcohol to disease, we may conclude, (1) that alcohol addiction actually brings on certain types of disease; (2) it makes many illnesses more serious and prolonged; (3) it induces set-backs or recurrences of certain diseases; and (4) it can have no place in a sound program of disease prevention. Mary Lewis Reed, R.N., in her booklet, The Physiological and Psychological Effects of Alcohol and Their Social Consequences, quotes Dr. Haven Emerson as saying, "The discontinuance of the use of alcohol will mark a greater advance in public health protection than anything else since the application of our knowledge of the bacterial origin of disease."

A classic illustration of how drinking can darken the intelligence is found in Emile Zola's story, "Drink." Coupeau, a zinc-worker, and his wife, Gervaise, were living happily together, until one fateful day when Coupeau fell from a roof and was laid up for three months. Lying idle so long, he lost the habit of work and wasted his time and money in drinking shops. This was his start on the down road. After he was able to resume work, he con- [34]

tinued his heavy drinking and frequently returned home in a drunken state, at which times he smashed furniture and abused his wife and children. 

As time went on, Coupeau became a confirmed drunkard, leaving his wife to support the family by taking in washings. He did work occasionally, but as Zola puts it,

He certainly made a present of his labor to the government; for Gervaise never saw his money. She no longer looked in his hands when he returned home on pay days; he arrived swinging his arms, pockets empty. At first he made excuses. . . . After a little he no longer troubled himself to give any explanations. The money evaporated, that was all.

Yes, it was their fault if they descended lower and lower every season. But that is the sort of thing one never tells one's self, especially when one is down in the gutter. They accused their bad fortune; they pretended their fate was against them. Their home had become a little hell by this time. They bickered away at one another the whole day....

The saddest thing was that they had opened the cage of affection; the better feelings had all taken flight, like so many canaries. The loving warmth of father, mother and child, when united and wrapped up in each other, deserted them, and left them shivering, each in his or her own corner. 

Coupeau went from bad to worse. He was not sober once in six months. Then he fell ill and had to go to the asylum; but when he came out repaired, he would pull himself to bits again and need another mending. In three years he went seven times to the asylum in this fashion, until he died in the extremities of delirium. 

The fate of Coupeau's wife, Gervaise, was not better. She had resorted to drinking with her husband, and sank lower and lower. She no longer worked; she no longer ate; she slept on filth. As soon as she had a few pennies she drank and fought. 

Her landlord finally decided to turn her out of her room, but allowed her to occupy a hole under the staircase. While she lay there on some old straw, her teeth chattered, her stomach was empty, and her bones were frozen. She was becoming idiotic. [ 35 ]

Death was to take her little by little, bit by bit, dragging her thus to the end through the accursed existence she had made for herself. Dissoluteness took her life, just as it had killed her husband. 

II. STUDY 

Many elements in modern life handicap the quest for happiness by threatening mental stability. One of these certainly is beverage alcohol. A conservative estimate indicates that alcoholism is directly responsible for 7 per cent of all insanity, to say nothing of the much larger percentage of cases in which alcohol is a contributing factor.

In the development of this session we shall observe the steps by which alcohol affects psychological maladjustments long before the drinker reaches the acute stages referred to as insanity. We shall see how alcohol tends to undermine mental efficiency much the same way that it jeopards physical health. "I Can Take It or Leave It"

Men of all times have gloried in the fact that they were not ruled by instinct but by reason. In spite of tradition and custom, they have evidenced traits of individuality to the extent of selecting a thousand varieties of food and almost as many modes of dress. 

For economic reasons the Chinese learned to live on rice in seasons of famine, subsisting on pathetically small portions, and again in times of plenty they exhibited gluttonous capacity for this simple food and its accompanying vegetable delicacies. Similarly, their Japanese neighbors adapted their famine and feasting capacities to the diet of fish. Necessity taught them they could take their food-or very nearly-let it alone.

It is here that man has been overtaken by a most serious fallacy, as he has tried to apply this freedom of choice to alcoholic beverages. The prevalence today of the personal attitude, "I can take it or [36]

leave it," "I can drink in moderation, like a gentleman," betrays that we have learned little from the experience of the race. Perhaps a better explanation is that we have failed to discriminate between things over which we can justifiably claim control and those that potentially control us. 

Alcohol is a habit-forming narcotic drug. Its use almost inevitably results in the development of a progressive appetite for it. The basic explanation for this is the fact that alcoholic drinks are taken primarily for the sensations they produce. To heighten the sensation the drinker consumes more and more, and after continuous use, develops a sort of tolerance for such drinks, the alcohol penetrating his blood stream somewhat more slowly, whereupon he must drink a larger quantity to derive the desired sensation. Such factors as these explain why the man who exercises discrete self-discipline in matters of food, dress and social conduct may prove to be a dissolute weakling if he forms the drink habit. The Japanese have a proverb for it: "First the man takes the drink; next the drink takes the drink; then the drink takes the man."

Psychologists tell us that within every group of persons who set out to drink with moderation a certain predictable percentage will be unable to resist the habit-forming appeal of alcohol. Scientists point out that many people are so constituted nervously that drinking cannot but lead to excess, and consequently to serious personal and community problems. They also insist that no one can predict which members of any given "moderate-drinking" group will become the "problem drinkers." The late William A. White, M.D., who treated many sufferers from alcoholism when he was superintendent of St. Elizabeth's Hospital, Washington, D. C., once estimated that out of ten persons who develop a liking for alcoholic drinks, only seven will be able to "take it or leave it." Three will become addicted, and unable to break the habit. The late Dr. W. J. [ 37 ]

Mayo confirmed this observation, and added that while three out of every ten drinkers would become addicts, it is not possible in advance to predict which of the ten. If this is an accurate estimate of the "problem drinker" hazard, three out of ten will go all the way along the path that leads to the disintegration of character; and we may be assured that the other seven will stack the odds against their better selves. 

Judgment, Emotion and Sensation Attacked "I start at the top!" is the clever description offered by Dr. Emil Bogen as to the characteristic manner in which alcohol attacks the mental equipment. That apex of man's mental endowment for which he has waited longest, and in the achievement of which he has worked hardest, is always the first to be destroyed when alcohol comes into the nerve structure. Judgment-that attribute of our mental activity which differentiates us from the rest of the animal kingdom and makes us closely akin to God-falters, plays strange tricks on us, and subtly fades out, as the anesthetic qualities of alcohol come into play. During the process of this "fading out" the drinker is usually found to talk with abandon, lacking the restraint of good taste and of self-criticism. At the outset he will begin to say things that place him in an undignified light among sober companions and he may find himself making remarks that offend.

From this earliest stage, interference with our higher functions passes quickly into the area where emotional controls are affected. The man who is normally soft-spoken, quiet mannered, and peaceloving, may react by exhibiting just the opposite traits. In extreme cases the drinker will display brutality to members of the family, or commit vicious crimes for which he may be obliged to spend a lifetime of remorse. [ 38 ]

It is alcohol's tendency to unstabilize emotional control among moderate as well as heavy drinkers that is of keenest concern. A typical case occurred in a manufacturing community where a thirty-fiveyear-old male employee decided one day to go to his favorite drinking place. According to his own testimony in court, he remembered having been at this tavern until about 1.00 P.M., after which everything went blank. What he did not remember was that at 2.00 o'clock he coaxed a little girl into his car, drove to an isolated area of a park, and was about to molest his victim when he was overtaken by police. The whole city was furious over this incident. When the court showed leniency, some dared to suggest openly that justice would have been served better by "direct action." Probation officials were entirely convinced that this man had become the victim of temporary insanity, during which moral judgment and memory were blotted out. It was argued that he had always been a faithful employee and a truly devoted husband and father. 

Right or wrong, the man was given a second chance, and has lived a useful and orderly life since that harrowing experience. Many variations of this story could be told, as evidenced by sordid news accounts of crime and drinking.

In the more extreme cases of mental interference by alcohol-when the casual explanation is, "I didn't know what I was doing"-the influence of drinking has not only cancelled judgment and sent emotional controls into confusion, but has drugged the important functions of sensation. The psychologist explains that the drunkard is inclined to shout because he does not hear normally. The vision of the drunken driver is affected seriously in terms of shortened range, narrowed scope, inability to distinguish color, and even that treacherous abnormality known as "seeing double." It is this interference with sensation that explains the clumsiness and the staggering characteristic of intoxicated per- [ 39 ]

sons. The two-way circuit, from the sensory nerves to the brain and from the brain to the muscles, is slowed down or even completely blocked. 

Once again we must remind ourselves that the extent of this interference is a matter of degree, depending upon the amount of alcohol consumed. 

Miss Bertha Rachel Palmer, Director of Temperance

Education for the National W.C.T.U., has explained the progression thus: "Little alcohol, little harm; more alcohol, more harm; much alcohol, much harm."

Personality Takes a Beating 

Alcohol tends, first of all, to attack the mental and nervous system, but a close sequel to this is the way it interferes with character, personality and spiritual values. Doctor Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, and the much-discussed dual personalities in real life, suggest the radical alteration that so often transforms the drinker. The devoted husband and father may become grouchy and brutal; the cultured man or woman boisterous, silly or indecent. The ambitious person may change to one who fritters away all his time. A little drinking frequently alters one's attitude toward associates and environment. Is it fanatical to say that such a change is never for the better?

One of the most unfortunate, if not disastrous, ways in which alcohol handicaps personality is when it is taken in order to escape reality. Since alcohol is first and always a narcotic drug, its most common use is for the depressant effect. The false sense of freedom from worry and obligation, and the stupor in which the drinker is transported into the land of fancy, are all too often "the kick" that is really sought.

A man or woman is confronted with a difficult set of circumstances. At least four courses present themselves: to try to do something about it; to worry and fret; to do nothing and see if things will [ 40 ]

not work themselves out; or, to "get drunk." The rescue missions consume much of their time trying to reclaim those who have welcomed drink as "the easy way out." It is appalling that the use of alcohol as an avenue of escape from reality should have attained such popularity. The movies, magazines and current fiction are prone to justify this practice of running away from life via the wine cellar, the beer tavern and the taproom.

Alcohol as escape is the antithesis to courageous living. It is the complete opposite to those motives which led Jesus to endure the cross. He came to redeem human personality, the most precious thing in the world, which even in his day had taken many a beating at the hands of King Alcohol. 

Psychology of the "Morning After"

The splitting headache, and the indescribable feeling so characteristic of that "morning-after hangover" are never considered pleasureable experiences from the physical standpoint. When drinking is resorted to as a means of escaping reality, the mental phase of the "hangover" is apt to be more distressing than all its physical discomfort. What dejection ofttimes overtakes the drinker who awakes to find his woes intensified by his attempt to run away from them!

It is not difficult to imagine the remorse of a factory worker who responded to the announcement of a lay-off by going on a three-day "bender," and was later informed that he could have been transferred to another department had he not been drunk when the telephone rang. He answered the 'phone in his drunken stupor and hurled insults at the foreman, thus converting his lay-off into definite unemployment. Most unfortunate of all was the privation inflicted upon his family. His "morning after" will serve to haunt him for a lifetime, and may even endanger his mental health, because things have broken badly for him since that experience. [ 41 ]

A similar case was reported recently in a metropolitan newspaper. A young man came home drunk several nights in succession. His explanation for drinking was that he had lost his job. Day by day he became increasingly irritable and violent at being aroused from his morning sleep-although his mother did not try to awaken him until nine or ten o'clock. One morning, amidst his raving at being disturbed, he shouted, "Mother, if you wake me up in the morning I'll kill myself." And he did just that; on being called, later in the morning, he walked through the house and out into a field, shotgun in hand. A single shot announced that death had struck.

Once more we must remind ourselves that not all who drink are going to experience the hangover in such destructive form. Nevertheless, in our candid study of the alcohol problem we must recognize its progressive handicapping of mental attitudes.

Employ the kind of temperance education that will counteract the notion that drinking is thrilling or glamorous. Information is the best antidote for curiosity.

Suggested answer: Are there not many subtle dangers attending the use of alcohol which do not occur with other forms of relaxation? Entering into a parlor game, or any of the popular sports, for relaxation, one plays until fatigued, or until one has derived the desired satisfaction. Lack of confidence in being able to exercise "quitting control" is the hazard involved in drinking for relaxation. 

Forgetting for the moment the habit-forming treachery of alcohol, drinking is a costly indulgence.

Call the Psychiatrist

Lest we draw the criticism of being fanatical in our analysis at this point, it may be well again to remind ourselves that drinking and its consequences are largely a matter of degree. Not everyone who drinks will encounter the mental disturbances that [ 46 ]

lead to the psychopathic hospital. However, this admission must not be thought as in any sense condoning moderate drinking. We have yet to find anyone who has been benefited by the use of alcoholic drinks, and from the psychological as well as physical standpoint there are many hazards that lurk in the pathway of even the most serious-minded moderationist. On the other hand, we cannot do justice to this study without recognizing that the end result of drinking, in thousands of cases every year, is insanity. In New York State alcohol is blamed for 

7 per cent of all insanity, and is conceded to be a contributing cause in a much larger percentage of the cases admitted to the state hospitals.¹ 

To get the total picture of mental maladjustment arising from alcoholism, one also must consider the millions of drunkards who repeatedly come before the police courts of the large cities. Typical of this condition is Rochester, New York, where 35 per cent of all arrests are for public intoxication, thus totaling over 2,500 arrests annually for this one offense.2 The public-spirited citizens in Rochester, as those in a score of other metropolitan areas, are frankly alarmed over the trend. In effect, they are saying, "Let's call the doctor and get at the root of this malady." Remembering the sanitariums and the Keeley Cures of a generation ago, people at first thought of this large-scale drunkenness as a physical sickness. The insistence upon bringing medical science into the job of reclaiming the habitual drunkard was in the nature of a vigorous protest against merely punishing the drunkard along with hardened criminals.

Bad as they may seem, drinking conditions would be infinitely worse but for the pervasive emphasis on mental health-a crusade uniting the forces of education, religion, home life and industry. Sound minds, sound bodies; sound bodies, sound minds-is good Americanism. [ 48 ]

II. STUDY Early America-the Story of Rugged Families 

American history has exhibited to the world a new conception of family life. The struggle of the pioneer families to surmount indescribable obstacles, the acquisition of progressively higher standards of living, the emancipation of womanhood, and the triumph of public education are important chapters in the story of the American family. It would seem appropriate to laud "rugged familyism" rather than "rugged individualism" as the clue to the greatness and stability of our national heritage. 

The family is now generally regarded as of fundamental importance, the basic unit of society. Most of our institutions are designed to supplement the desires and needs of the family group. The philosophy persists that by conserving the noblest features [51]

in family life the protection and enrichment of the individual are best served. One can readily understand why society in our day has denied that the drink question is "purely a personal issue."

The Drink Problem Strikes Home 

In modern family life, the drink problem continues to present acute difficulties. In many families where relationships between certain members have not been satisfactory, the introduction of drinking habits tends to blast the group into complete disintegration. Not a few ardent crusaders against drink got their impetus as a result of a calamitous experience in their own family, or through observing other family upheavals. Newspapers reflect in vivid fashion the critical family situations which may arise from alcoholism. Watch the headlines: 

"Man shoots wife who refused drink money." "Child, age four, dies after drinking half pint of whisky left on table by uncle." "Son shoots mother-both had been drinking.” "Boy, fourteen, kills father after booze attack." "Widow sues state in beer poison death."

Such headlines could be augmented from the columns of nearly every community newspaper. Harrowing stories of death and destruction do not tell the whole truth about alcohol as a family problem. 

In thousands of homes where there is drinking, things seem to go about as usual, except for such lesser difficulties as the following:

The financial struggle is made more difficult. 

Too large a share of the weekly pay envelope may be spent for liquor before it comes home. 

So much time is spent in the tavern with drinking companions that home life is neglected.

In some instances drinking undermines health to the extent that it becomes a serious family problem. 

Disagreements may arise over multiple standards which are established, to the effect that the father [ 52 ]

may drink as he chooses, while mother is to have no more than an occasional cocktail, and the young people are not to drink at all. 

Parents set out to teach their children to drink "like ladies and gentlemen," completely forgetting the habit-forming characteristic of alcohol as a narcotic poison and drug.

These supposedly minor problems of drinking in the home often burst forth into major perplexities. 

The best that can be said is that the security, the culture and the happiness of the family group are subjected to a much greater strain when its members imbibe.

The following humorous plan ludicrously illus trates the need for safeguarding the home against the hazards of drink:

SUBMARINE ATTACK ON BOOZE2 

For the married man who cannot get along without drinks, the following is suggested as a means of freedom from bondage to saloons:

Start a saloon in your own home. Be the only customer (you'll have no license to pay). Go to your wife and give her two dollars to buy a gallon of whisky, and remember there are sixty-nine drinks in a gallon. Buy your drinks from no one but your wife, and by the time that the first gallon is gone she will have eight dollars to put into the bank and two dollars to start business again. 

Should you live ten years and continue to buy booze from her, and then die with snakes in your boots, she will have money enough to bury you decently, educate your children, buy a house and lot, and marry a decent man, and quit thinking about you. 

On or Off the Premises

The above ingenious scheme brings us face to face with the dilemma which has perplexed students of the liquor problem for more than a century. If a man is determined to drink, is it better for that drinking to occur in the home, or outside the home? 

Is it best for it to take place within the atmosphere

[53]

of the family, where it is subjected to the controls and restrictions of the family group, or for the drinker to betake himself to the saloon, grill or tavern, and thus spare the family his convivial presence? These two opposing points of view are somewhat represented by the Canadian and the American philosophies of dispensing alcoholic drinks. In the main, the Canadian system of liquor control has encouraged drinking in the home by utilizing such methods as government liquor stores, the refusing to license saloons, and the restricting of purchases. 

It should be remembered that the several provinces have many variations from this general plan, and also that the doctrine that it is best to do one's drinking in the home is constantly under fire in Canada, both from those who would welcome the return of the saloon and by those who see the unfortunate results of home drinking.

By and large the United States again encourages saloon drinking, though, of course, the outlets are now called inns, taverns, taprooms, cocktail lounges -anything but saloons. In many of the wet states the licensed drinking places have taken on all the serious abuses of the loudly condemned pre-Prohibition saloon. The following characteristics of the "grill," or "taproom" saloon, are cited as being particularly injurious to home life: 

The two o'clock closing hour, with all-night operation permitted on special occasions. 

The "ganging up" of patrons into "jam sessions" and the forming of cliques dominated by criminals and other questionable characters. 

The very frequent introduction of gambling and immorality into the regular operation of "low class" drinking places.

The sponsoring of cheap and vulgar entertainment.

The renewed eagerness of drinkery proprietors to "be of service" in cashing pay checks. [54]

This is by no means an exhaustive list of the complaints arising from millions of American homes which must again bear the brunt of liquor abuses. 

Perhaps two of the basic faults to be found with the theory that it is best to do one's drinking away from home are (1) the unfortunate associations, and (2) the indifference to home life and family obligations.

Similarly, we might summarize the hazards of home drinking as follows: (1) The tendency to encourage young people and all adults of the family to join in the drinking, thus spreading the direct handicaps of alcohol to all members of the family group. (2) The intensifying of the family economic problem by absorbing food money, especially in times of depression. The habit-forming nature of alcohol takes unfair advantage of milk when a choice is required. (3) A factor of paramount importance to the family is the effect of alcohol upon womanhood and motherhood.

Science is positive in its verdict that the vigor of the offspring is endangered where drunkenness prevails among the parents. Wholesomeness of family life is never blasted so completely as when the mother becomes an advanced alcoholic. It can hardly be denied that extensive home drinking breeds just such problematic conditions as have been enumerated. With these facts before him, the prospective drinker should have no difficulty in finding the right answer to this multiple choice: 

Shall I do my drinking at the bar of the taproom? 

In the home? Not at all?

The Family Drink Bill 

We have already stated that when money is spent for liquor, serious family and community problems may result. How much did your family spend for alcoholic drinks last year? Taking the three billion dollars spent for drink in the United States during 1938 and dividing it among all the families of this [55]

country, we discover that the average household drink bill was about seventy-five dollars. Maybe your family did not use its seventy-five dollars' worth; there were millions of families that did not. 

This means that among the families that do go in for drinking, the cost must be considerably higher than seventy-five dollars. The extensive advertising campaign of the liquor interests, through the papers, magazines and other advertising media that reach the American home, is aimed toward progressively increasing the family consumption of alcoholic beverages. Figure it any way you will, the inescapable conclusion is that money spent for alcohol cannot at the same time build homes, provide food, clothing, education and the finer things of life. Three billion dollars for liquor—a major share of this coming from underprivileged homescan but forebode evil results in terms of economic, social and crime problems. The cost to the drinker is only the first cost. Sooner or later the drink bill levies on the family, the community, industry and our government.

Stacking the Liabilities 

"Theirs was such a happy family until." In such manner begin the tragic stories of a countless number of family disasters. Reading these disaster reports in the daily papers induces a pessimistic mood. There is the joyous little home from which one or perhaps both parents are snatched as the result of a motor crash. In a less spectacular account, misfortune comes to haunt the family because the husband-father loses his job. In another, the police knock at the door and take a supporting member of the family away to await trial and imprisonment, the victim of momentary circumstances conditioned by drink.

A myriad of contributing factors may enter into these misfortune-dealing experiences, but in analyzing the major causes it will surprise the honest in- [56]

quirer to observe in what a high percentage of cases drinking is at least a part of the cause. This much is true: after dividing all the factors into two classifications, "preventable" and "unpreventable," the use of liquor stands high in the list of the "preventable" causes of misfortune, accident and crime. 

Social Drinking 

Among hundreds-perhaps thousands of families in nearly every industrial community are to be found folkways and practices that reflect the customs of European ancestors. For example, the daily use of beer or wine is looked upon as a matter of course. It is because of this background of social, traditional drinking that public school teachers often encounter a reaction to their efforts in complying with state laws providing for teaching alcohol education. Tony or Fred or Tom is persuaded by scientific knowledge that alcohol is one of the things that can do him no good and possibly much harm, and that it is something he should avoid. But then there is the family. The mother is offended and the father indignant-even to the point of going to the school and "telling that teacher a thing or two."

The brighter side of this picture is reflected by the fact that among drinking families in this country, the past fifty years have seen a gradual trend toward the conviction that alcoholic drinks are harmful. Hence the usual adjustment of this conflict between the school and home is that the children may be strongly and permanently influenced by their temperance instruction, but are prompted by respect and politeness not to impugn the motives or the intelligence of their drinking parents. Still other home situations have been brought to light where children and young people have led other members of the family to the discovery and acceptance of the scientific attitude toward alcohol. The quiet example of children is also potent. [57]

Home Remains the Bulwark for Temperance 

The community must look to the public schools, churches and other character-building agencies for organized leadership and formal instruction in connection with the new temperance crusade. The modern trend of liquor abuses and the resultant public reaction will quicken the tempo of this movement. 

A most encouraging sign of temperance progress has been the enthusiasm with which the best religious and educational talent of the nation have directed their energies and skills toward the formulation of new temperance materials and teaching techniques. In full recognition of this increase of temperance interest, we must build the new scientific temperance crusade upon the principle that the home is the fundamental bulwark of temperance, and that we must look to it for the best assurances of a sober nation-particularly at a time when the government washes its hands of responsibility!

II. STUDY Home Town Is Worried 

In 1932 thousands of home towns all over the United States were the scenes of heated argument over "National Prohibition versus Repeal." While in nearly all of the metropolitan areas sentiment seemed predominantly for Repeal, public opinion on this issue was much more evenly divided in the towns and smaller cities. Conservative analysts point out that Home Town acquiesced in the swing [61]

away from Prohibition under the influence of these three factors: the political-economic unrest, engendered by the depression; the nation-wide publicity campaign magnifying and distorting the shortcomings of Prohibition; and the glib assurance that 

Repeal would correct all these abuses. The saloon "would never return." "Crime would diminish because of increased respect for law." "Youth would cease drinking as the glamour of speakeasy patronage disappeared." The liquor industry was to operate under the strictest controls regarding such matters as exploitation through advertising and participation in politics. The utmost protection was promised to dry areas and dry communities. In effect, Home Town seems to have reacted by saying something like this, "All right; if John Barleycorn has learned to be a nice fellow, as you say he is, we'll put him on his honor and give him one more chance."

The years following 1932 and 1933 have found 

Home Town groaning with disillusionment, as various items in the repealist's pledge have been violated. In fact, the whole pledge was straightway forgotten by the Crusaders who disbanded soon after Repeal. Here again one is reminded of the "forgetfulness" so characteristic of alcoholic indulgence. But Home Town could not forget. She was obliged to go on living with these broken promises. The rising tide of resentment against rampant abuses is obvious to any observer traveling from town to town. Newspapers, magazines, and even the journals of the liquor industry, reveal that Home 

Town is worried over its "modern old-time" alcohol problem.

What to Do with the Problem Drinker "Dickey" Fitzgerald, a notorious character in 

Rochester, New York, boasted on August 3, 1938, [64]

that since 1915 he had served 173 sentences in the Monroe County Penitentiary, all of them for public intoxication. "Dickey" has many close runners-up for his alleged "supremacy." The age-old problem of the "village drunkard" again constitutes one of the serious aspects of the present drink evil. 

The city of Rochester, just referred to, presents a typical pattern in its struggle to cope with public drunkenness. It has a population of about 328,000, and licenses approximately five hundred drinking establishments. In many respects this community exhibits a marked degree of temperance-mindedness. Its leading newspapers and radio stations reject liquor advertising. Its schools display phenomenal interest in temperance education and give enthusiastic support to the community's strongly organized temperance movement. With such a background it is startling to discover that the police are obliged to make more than twenty-five hundred arrests a year, on the charges, "public intoxication" and "driving while intoxicated"-one drunkenness arrest for every 120 persons; except that due allowance must be made for repeaters like "Dickey" Fitzgerald. This one classification of arrests supplied the local penitentiary with half its population. 

Official reports from Los Angeles, California, a city representative of our largest metropolitan areas and in which considerable temperance sentiment has been manifested, reveal one drunkenness arrest for every thirty of its population. During the fiscal year 1936-1937, 64 per cent of all the 76,878 arrests were for intoxication. City officials estimated that at least one-third of the time and expense of the police department was absorbed in caring for "drunks." These officials were irked by the fact that for every dollar they received from liquor licenses they had to spend eight dollars for the care of "drunks" and the regulation of the liquor traffic. [65]

So we feel justified in our conclusion that Home Town and Big Town alike are worried over their new crop of chronic and criminal alcoholics. 

Jails Are Not Good Enough 

Judges and heads of penal institutions complain loudly over the futility of cluttering up our jails and prisons with alcoholics. Their objections are made on the following observations: First, they believe that the drunkard or the "problem drinker" is a sick man-and in need of specialized treatment -rather than a criminal, in the common sense of the word. These judges and sociologists therefore urge that although the old-timers may be past redemption, society certainly owes better treatment to the young alcoholics. There is a movement in the New York State Magistrates' Association to persuade the legislature of that state to establish an experimental camp for the reclamation of problem drinkers. Such an institution would resemble the C.C.C. camps, providing the "patient" with a normal amount of out-door work, recreation and adequate medical treatment. It would seek to develop a new outlook on life. The opponents of this experiment argue that it is too ambitious, that the cure would not last at least not while alcoholic drinks are accessible. To this the friends of the idea respond, that the government which sanctions and benefits financially from the liquor traffic is obligated to care for the unfortunate end-results of the system; that the maintenance of large jails and prisons is far more costly than work camps; and that if men like "Dickey" Fitzgerald can be reclaimed from spending a lifetime "in and out of jail," a worth-while economy is effected.

The student of the alcohol problem will recognize that reclaiming habitual drunkards is only one step toward the achieving of a sober nation. From 

1840 to 1850, a vigorous movement known as the 

Washingtonians set out to reform all drunkards. [ 66 ]

Historians relate that this campaign finally failed, since for every drinker who reformed, the saloon captured ten new ones. Although attempts at reform will be attended by discouragement, Home Town seems in a mood to give even the village drunkard a chance for a better life. Christian idealism must sanction such an attitude. 

Ten Nights in a Taproom 

T. S. Arthur's famous play, "Ten Nights in a

Bar Room," was one of the best-known melodramas of the nineteenth century. It served to focus attention upon the typical abuses that had brought the saloon into utter disrepute. In more recent years this heavy drama of the woes of drunkenness has usually been presented in a farcical mood because of its quaintness of manners, speech and dress. So, the old "tear-jerker" is now perpetrated as a roaring comedy. It may be enlightening to analyze some of its complaints against the old saloon and see if any of them might be recognizable were we to produce an up-to-date version entitled "Ten Nights in a Taproom. 

1. Simon Slade's saloon, "The Sickle and Sheaf," served up all manner of alcoholic drinks to every comer, young and old, poverty stricken and rich, drunkard and moderate drinker, criminal and public official.

Today we have laws against serving minors and drunkards. Some degree of care is exercised against serving small children, but in many communities it is not difficult for 'teen-age young people to buy drinks. As for refusing drinks to drunkards, that restraint is customarily applied when the customer is without funds or too intoxicated to put his hand in his pocket. 

2. Slade's barroom was the scene of unfortunate associations.

Today when character-building agencies are concerned about the constructive use of leisure time [67]

and the development of wholesome group associations, they feel deep apprehension over the efforts of grill operators to develop "chummy clubs" and "jam sessions." Our problem is not helped by the prevalence of women and girls now seen on both sides of the bar. 

3. Simon Slade's saloon had its accessories. The room upstairs was the scene of gambling, and of a resultant murder.

Today it is the room upstairs, the room in the rear, or the room next door, that is of concern to those who see modern drinking establishments exhibiting the characteristics of the old-time saloon. 

The intimate alliance of liquor, gambling and prostitution is to be seen in every section of the country where the new saloon enjoys legal status. Result: an accelerated mania for gambling, and nation-wide concern over the prevalence of venereal disease following upon increased prostitution. 

According to statistics furnished by the United 

States Public Health Service, 140,000 persons die each year because of syphilis and syphilitic heart disease. Ten million people in this country now have or have had this disease. One-tenth of all insanity and one-seventh of all blindness are attributed to it. It brings death to 25,000 babies annually. 

Ray Lyman Wilbur, president of Stanford University and chairman of the American Social Hygiene Association, said recently: "There is a direct relationship between the increasing use of liquor and the amount of gonorrhea and syphilis in the 

United States." Bascom Johnson, another official of the Association, declares: "Since repeal of federal and state prohibition laws, the old alliance between prostitution and the saloon has been renewed with enthusiasm on both sides.”

The dimensions of America's drink problem are indicated by the fact that Big Town and Home Town are now supporting nearly a half million licensed establishments dispensing alcoholic drinks. Thus [68]

the nation is provided with one drinking place for every 283 persons, including men, women and children and account must be taken of large areas in which such establishments are still forbidden by law. 

Advertising Makes Home Town Liquor-Conscious 

"If a man wants a drink he should be able to get it without breaking the law." Such was the prevailing philosophy of the man in the street who voted for Repeal. Campaigners made the most of this sentimentalism, and waxed bold in their promises of removing the profit motive, of protecting dry territory against invasion of liquor, and of discouraging advertising exploitation. The new liquor traffic would be content to serve an already existing market and would refrain from widespread efforts to stimulate new appetite for its wares. The prohibition bootleg problem had been exaggerated to such extent that the rosy prospect of capturing this illicit trade may have been responsible for the industry's willingness to soft pedal its intensive advertising campaign until the public might have time to forget such promises. Even so, it is estimated that more than ninety-five million dollars was spent for beverage alcohol advertising and propaganda from 

1934 to 1938.

The dissemination of liquor ads, through nearly every known medium, suggests that we have now forgotten the campaign promises about sparing the American home from this class of advertising. The brewers and distillers are willing to pay premium prices for newspaper ads, the most brilliant magazine spreads, and have eagerly acquired tens of thousands of bill-boards, to extoll their products. Their determination to ultilize effective advertising media is most apparent in those centers where advertising channels have been refused them. Rochester, New

York, the home of three breweries and a distillery, illustrates this tendency. The leading newspapers and radio stations have refused liquor ads. Smart- [ 69 ]

ing under what they consider unfair discrimination, the liquor industry set out to capture the bill-board space. Novelty advertising stunts were employed. 

For a short time the street cars carried large liquor ads on their exteriors, until the public, through a petition campaign, secured the discontinuance of this practice.

It should be mentioned that within the beverage alcohol industry is found an active minority sentiment against the continuance of widespread advertising activity. This minority is fearful of the consequences; especially as they observe public_uprisings, such as the one against bill-boards in Evanston, Illinois, and the experience with street car placards in Rochester. 

However ambitious the advertising aims of the liquor traffic, there is one cause for encouragement. 

Many choice advertising channels have remained closed to beverage alcohol ads. These include several important magazines. Five hundred fifty-two daily newspapers refuse hard liquor ads, and 178 newspapers refuse beer ads as well as hard liquor and wine ads. A list of these papers was printed in the Editor and Publisher, December 18, 1937, with the note that "Almost universally the publishers rejecting liquor advertising report 'no regrets.'

Some also indicated that it was "good business to refuse such advertising." During the first days of the Repeal era, the N. W. Ayer and Son advertising agency, a nationally prominent firm with headquarters at Philadelphia, demonstrated a genuinely idealistic attitude toward this problem by terminating a lucrative contract with the manufacturers of a leading brand of ginger ale when it became known that this company's advertising would tend to increase the consumption of alcoholic beverages.

Similarly, a great number of radio stations have refused time to the beverage alcohol industry. With the exception of a program which gave beer a lusty boost by lauding a certain metal container, the [70]

public has been shown consideration by the major broadcasting systems. In 1939 the National Broadcasting Company went on record as refusing to release broadcasts advertising alcoholic beverages.

It is probable that sooner or later the Federal Government will control liquor advertising, especially in consideration of the rights of "dry" territory. The Culkin-Capper Bill, designed to accomplish this objective through congressional enactment, has been gaining increased support both in Washington and throughout the nation. It is more important for the present that Christian citizens take a firm stand, individually and as groups, to discourage every form of liquor advertising, and to patronize those advertising media that do not accept beverage ads or propaganda.

Home Town Revolts 

In spite of the amazing patience and longsuffering of the American public, there are evidences that Home Town is gradually acquiring a will to revolt against the current abuses of the liquor traffic. This determination is taking expression in a variety of activities including the following: 

Local option campaigns. 

Drives to obtain earlier closing hours, Sunday closing, and to secure rigid enforcement of existing restrictions against drunken driving.

The encouraging of judges and magistrates who take courageous stands on the drink issue.

Campaigns against various forms of beverage alcohol advertising. 

Citizens' movements to encourage patronage of those stores and restaurants that refrain from selling alcoholic drinks.

Surveys to determine the relationship of saloons to establishments bidding for the spending of leisure time and recreational money.

The formation of societies to pursue continuous study of the problem, with a view to employing [71]

whatever strategy gives promise of improving conditions.

It is encouraging to observe that during the Repeal era local option has proved effective in more than five thousand political units, including a large proportion of townships and a few counties. But we must keep in mind that local option is far from being the only strategy to be used against the encroachments of the liquor traffic. A costly mistake has been the tendency to dismiss further interest in the problem the moment a local option contest gained its objective. Moreover, there are many states and metropolitan areas in which local option is not legally permissible, or where public sentiment and political pressure make it useless to attempt this strategy. Here again we must urge that Christian citizens in such areas assume an aggressive rather than a defeatist attitude. The cause of civic righteousness has seldom been served by sitting placidly on the sidelines, hoping that conditions would become so intolerably bad that they of themselves would cause a reaction. The price of Christian social reform is work and self-sacrifice.

II. STUDY 

From one camp comes the cry, "We've got to keep liquor out of politics"; from another, "You'd better [75]

start by getting politics out of liquor." These issues have arisen in one form or another to haunt every generation from the time when George Washington marched the militia into western Pennsylvania to quell the Whisky Rebellion. The accomplishing of either of these objectives appears extremely remote in the present situation. Throwing the major responsibility for liquor control back to the fortyeight states gave little hope of our ever being able to divorce liquor from politics. The Federal Government retained but three prerogatives: (1) to collect her lion's share of the revenue (about seventeen cents on the dollar); (2) to see that she gets all that is coming to her by warring on bootleggers; (3) to protect the states in whatever status they might vote to give the traffic in alcoholic beverages. Thus, deducting the four prohibition states (permitting the sale of beer only), we find the nation floundering with forty-four varieties of attempted liquor control. 

Varieties of Uncontrol

Even the skill of the proverbial "Philadelphia lawyer" would be taxed were he to attempt a complete analysis of the hundreds of liquor laws and rulings that have been put into operation since Repeal. No two states have exactly the same provisions; it is safe to say that no state has exactly the same liquor laws two years in succession. For example, the New York Legislature is presented twenty to thirty proposed changes in the law every session. A few of these usually are enacted. Further alterations are effected as the result of court decisions, and by the liberal discretionary powers of the Liquor Authority, responsible only to the governor.

Impertinent as it may have seemed to refer to American efforts of liquor control as "Varieties of Uncontrol," we now find that the sponsors of all these methods are generally disillusioned as to results. Those who worked hardest to achieve state monopoly or state ownership of retail liquor stores admit that it is the privately owned and licensed beer parlor and liquor restaurant that is giving most trouble. A survey of 3,088 of Chicago's taverns visited between 7:00 P.M. and 5:00 A.M. revealed that 2,122 of them violated one or more laws, by permitting such conditions as the following: improper lighting, obstructed view, sale to intoxicated persons, gambling, violation of closing hours, sale to minors, faulty sanitation, indecent dancing, employment of minors, employment of hostesses, and prostitution. These conditions were discovered by the Chicago Recreation Committee and Northwest ern University. The observers listed an average of three violations per tavern.

The Government Tries to Collect 

It was pointed out a few years ago that a probable reason why a few extremely wealthy families were active for Repeal was their hope of tax relief. 

Ironically, the tax burden on large fortunes has steadily and materially increased. 

The collecting of the federal revenue on liquor is not Uncle Sam's easiest chore, as signified by the fact that the Revenue Department now employs two thousand more agents than during Prohibition.

Commented the United States News, published in [78]

Washington: "With Repeal it was hoped the bootlegger would go; but Uncle Sam's battle to put him out of business continues on an enlarged scale with increased Federal forces thrown into the fray." 

Various estimates place the illicit alcohol sold today at from one-fourth to one-half of the total amount consumed. The absurdity which confronts the government is this: to realize large revenue, a high tax must be levied against liquor, but the collecting of a high tax involves the government in increased enforcement costs.

Thus again the government tries to collect. 99 There is another standpoint from which to consider the government's beverage revenue “bonanza.' 

One must figure in the costs, not only of collecting this revenue and of suppressing bootlegging, but also the direct and indirect costs to society. Government units (local, state and national) have a stake in these costs. If the mounting drink bill of the nation increases dependency and poverty, welfare costs are affected. If the nearly half a million drinking places stimulate crime and other antisocial conditions, enforcement and correctional costs are heightened. If drink results in increased accident mortality, then industry, insurance companies and the government must share the colossal burden imposed. It is quite certain that government in the future will pay even more dearly for the socioeconomic damages of drink. The end-results of extensive drinking will take a cumulative toll. 

The Way Out 

What is to be the new political strategy of those opposed to the manufacture, sale and use of beverage alcohol? At the present writing the answer to this question seems to be uncertain. This point may be illustrated by referring to two periods of history. Those who recall the temperance crusade of 1910-1919 will remember how positive was the feeling that Prohibition, state and national, was the right and inevitable way out. Those who still hold that point of view seem generally lacking in the assurance and depth of conviction that prevailed in those days. We seem to stand in the same position as did the friends of temperance who lived during the reconstruction period following the war between the states. Thirteen states had been dry prior to the [80]

系 ​war, but under pressure of the need for all available revenue with which to prosecute the conflict, nearly all had gone wet. For three decades the temperance cause in America resembled the Israelites wandering in the wilderness-a wilderness of despair and confusion. Once it emerged from that wilderness, the temperance movement rapidly developed the most effective campaign against liquor the world had ever seen. Again, we seem to be tramping through the wilderness of confusion and uncertainty. The way out is not clearly marked. Certain paths are being charted. Whether or not they lead into the promised land of a healthy, happy nation, only time can tell.

Local option is one of the broadest and most commonly used paths. During a period of little more 

1 than four years, 7,000 local option contests have been held by township, city and county units of government. Approximately 5,000 of these have resulted favorably for the dry cause. This is the more remarkable in view of the apparent lethargy and despair that gripped the "drys" following Repeal. Moreover, since twelve states and the District of Columbia do not permit local option elections, these 5,000 victories occurred in only thirty-two states (deducting the four that have legalized only beer).

The limitations of local option, however, are apparent in the present situation. Success seems to be hopeless in large cities, or even in towns adjacent to metropolitan areas. The greatest foe of local option seems to be the "almighty tax dollar." In

New York State, the average town stands to lose about $3,000 annually in liquor revenue if it votes dry; and the weight of this loss is usually exaggerated by the wets in appealing for opposition votes.

It may well be that local option points the best trail through the wilderness, but it will need to surmount many of its current barriers and greatly broaden its scope before it becomes the high road out. [81]

Other criss-crossing paths through the wilderness are posted "Government Retail Monopoly," "Rigid

Control and Limitation of Alcoholic Beverage Man ufacturers,' "The Ontario System," "Finland's 

Method," "Higher Taxation," "Education for Moderation," "Education for Total Abstinence," "National Enabling Act"-whereby Congress may control, restrict, or prohibit the liquor traffic. The trouble with each of these suggestions is that even when it functions perfectly, other segments of the liquor problem remain unsolved.

Although it can hardly be looked upon as the ultimate solution, temperance education must be promoted with improved technique and heightened enthusiasm. As to the controversy over whether education should urge moderation, or total abstinence, many educators are suggesting that children, young people and adults be given all the scientific facts concerning the effects of alcohol upon the individual and society, with confidence that in the light of such facts individual choice and conduct will be on the side of avoiding alcohol altogether. 

Temperance education must be considered a beacon light in the present wilderness.

The Need for a National Policy 

Wanted: A national policy relative to the liquor problem! It seems strange enough that in a day when the Federal Government concerns itself unhesitatingly with almost every conceivable phase of life affecting citizens and local units of government, it refuses to enunciate any correctional policy toward the liquor issue. One might say that its avowed policy is to have no policy-beyond leaving the whole matter to the several states, and collecting the revenue.

Aside from abrogating national prohibition, the 

Twenty-first Amendment (Section 2) hinted at com mitting the Federal Government to the policy of protecting dry territory against the exploits of the [82]

new alcoholic beverage interests. The government has thus far dodged this measure of responsibility by insisting it has no jurisdiction in protecting the dry portion of a partially wet state. This attitude has been stretched even to apply to the four traditionally dry states which prohibit everything but beer.

Those who look to the Federal Government for positive remedial policies in the future will expect a minimum program to include: 

1. Drastic restriction of all beverage alcohol advertising projected through interstate channels. 

2. Energetic enforcement of the constitutional guarantee of protection to dry territory. 

3. Rigid control of alcoholic beverage manufacture and distribution engaged in interstate commerce. 

4. Increased congressional interest in the liquor problem, with appropriate inquiries into the liquor traffic and its results, and the consequent passage of laws aimed at the correction of abuses. 

5. The development of an ultimate policy that would give to the government many of the advantageous powers enjoyed during Prohibition, although seeking to avoid the weaknesses of "the noble experiment.' 99

The Mind of Christ II. STUDY 

We come now to that part of our study where we must face the problem of the Christian's attitude toward drinking. This is a perplexing question since good people differ as to the answer. There are some for whom nothing short of total abstinence would be truly Christian; others who hold that it is permissible for the Christian to drink in moderation, if he knows "when to stop." There is probably no one who would claim that it is Christian to drink until one reaches the last stages of intoxication, or, as the moderationists would term it, "to excess. It may be said that no one deliberately sets out to become a drunkard. Even drunkards despise themselves for having fallen into that condition. But those who drink to the limit are invariably recruited from the ranks of the moderate drinkers who claim they can "take it or leave it." 

There is no problem as to whether it is Christian to get drunk. Our question is, whether or not a 

Christian should drink at all. How are we to decide? 99 In Charles M. Sheldon's famous book, In His 

Steps, a group of people agreed to live by the motto, "What would Jesus do?" Is not this a question that all Christians must always ask?

As his followers it is our business to discover the mind of Christ. Once having determined what we think the mind of Christ to be, there can be no alternative. In some areas it might be difficult to determine the mind of Christ, but as to the liquor problem, the evidence is relatively clear. Is it Christian to drink a little? Well, what would Jesus do?

Temple or Beer Parlor 

At a previous session, we studied the effects of alcohol on the drinker. We discovered that even small quantities of alcohol injure the nervous sys- [87]

tem, damage the stomach, impair the brain, weaken endurance, increase susceptibility to disease, and shorten the life span. These facts are scarcely debatable, for they come from the impartial observation of the best medical and scientific authorities. "What of it?" someone will say. "My body is my own, and I can do with it as I please. If I want to injure it by drinking, that's my business."

The Christian cannot talk that way. He believes that his body belongs to God, and that his use of it is a trust, a stewardship. The body must be kept with care so that it may serve the highest purposes. 

Paul states the Christian position for us, "Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the 

Spirit of God dwelleth in you?" (1 Cor. 3: 16.)

It is unthinkable that Jesus would have gained any kind of pleasure in ways that would harm body or mind. Our body was meant to be a temple and not a beer parlor. Here is the conclusion of a group of Christian young people who issued this statement recently: "We believe that the use of alcoholic beverages is a detriment mentally, physically and morally to the individual, and thus to society, and contrary to Christian principles and teachings." 

My Brother's Keeper "Am I my brother's keeper?" is the question that 

Cain asked when he had killed Abel. He rather assumes that the answer is, "No, I have no responsibility for my brother's welfare." This can be the attitude of a pagan, but never of the Christian. 

Here the mind of Christ is made clear: I am to love my neighbor as myself. I am to bear his burdens.

When he is hungry, I am to give him food. When he is sick, or in jail, I am to visit him. When he is bruised by life, I am to bring healing. And I must be careful not to put anything in his way that will cause him to stumble.

Paul states the Christian position when he says that "It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink [88]

wine, nor anything whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak" (Rom. 14:21). "Wherefore, if meat make my brother to offend, I will eat no flesh while the world standeth, lest I make my brother to offend" (1 Cor. 8:13). In other words, if my drinking makes life more difficult for my family, I will not drink. If my drinking endangers the safety of my neighbor, as I drive my car on the highway, I will not drink. If my drinking influences my friends to form the alcohol habit, I will drink no alcohol while the earth stands, lest I make my brother fall. Right-Wrong-Best? 

But here is someone who says, "I can't see that it's wrong for a person to drink a glass of beer occasionally, as long as he doesn't drink enough to injure himself, and as long as he drinks in private." 

The discriminating person will recognize at once that this is a hypothetical case. In the first place, it has been pointed out that even small amounts of alcohol affect the human mechanism. In the second place, the drinker seldom stops with an infinitesimal amount. He drinks for the alcoholic reaction, or as he would say, "to get a kick out of it." If one beer has enough alcohol to give him the kick, he may stop, or he may continue for the purpose of experiencing a greater kick. But if a single beer fails to give him satisfaction, then one drink calls for another and another, until the kick arrives. In the third place, it is seldom that a person drinks alone. 

Drinking loves company, and you are not as likely to find the drinker in a corner alone with his bottle, as you are to find him drinking with his friends. 

We talk about right and wrong and we like to catalogue various kinds of behavior; to say, "Now this is right," "That is wrong." Jesus didn't talk like that. He didn't hand down a thumb-nail guide, but rather a few great principles by which we should live. One of these principles is that the [89]

Christian must always make the highest possible choice. It is not just a matter of doing something that is right. In making a decision, there may be many alternatives that I can choose, and no one of them may necessarily be wrong in itself. But if I am a Christian, I must weigh these alternatives carefully, and then choose the one that seems to accomplish the greatest good. The good in our lives is often the enemy of the best. If I am a Christian, it is not enough for me to ask, "Is this right, or wrong?" but "Is this the highest right, the best possible choice I can make?" Jesus said, "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect."

When Christians face the problem of moderate drinking, it is not enough to ask, "Is moderate drinking right, or wrong?" but rather, "Does it accomplish the highest good that can come from this expenditure of time, money and energy? Is it right-wrong-or best?"

May Christians Be Cowards? 

Life seems to be one problem after another. Many of them are small; we can solve them with little effort; but some are so baffling and crushing that they well nigh break our spirit. Presumably, God could have arranged it so that we might live without having problems; but he didn't. Well for us that it is so, for it is thus that our characters grow. 

A psychologist was testing the comparative intelligence of Norway rats and domestic white rats.

He discovered that the Norway rats have a much higher intelligence quotient. This was to be expected. The Norway rats have to exert considerable effort and ingenuity to secure their food and keep alive. The white rats are tame, live in cages, and are fed the food they need. The Norway rats face more difficult problems and develop greater intelligence. [90]

In a previous study it was pointed out that many people drink as an escape mechanism, to avoid the problems of life. It may be the problem of avoiding family irritations; or the drabness of routine factory work or the nervous exhaustion of a factory "speed-up"; or the problem of a sense of inferiority; or the loss of investments or of one's job; whatever the difficulty, when a man resorts to drink he is not solving his problems-but only running away from them. His act is not a courageous facing of reality, but a cowardly escape. It eventually produces an indifference to responsibility and a deterioration of character.

When a Christian faces the problems of life, he has at his command certain resources which help him meet these challenges victoriously. James Gordon Gilkey tells the story of a boy who was being chased by a bull. In front of him was a high fence, behind him the bull. The boy gave one leap and cleared the fence in safety. Several days later he went back to the same field, when the bull wasn't around, and again tried to jump the fence. He couldn't. When the boy had faced the crisis, an extra power came to him which he did not ordinarily possess. And when the Christian faces the crises of life, he has at his command unusual powers of assurance and spiritual strength. To use these powers is the way to higher character. The drinker runs away from his problems, and becomes morally flabby; the Christian meets his problems courageously, and experiences victorious Christian living.

Alcohol in Building a Christian World 

The Christian must be not only good, but good for something. His must be an aggressive goodness. 

Jesus calls us to follow him and work with him in building the kingdom of God on earth. For those who accept his invitation, it means the sacrificial giving of mind, heart and soul to the tasks of the Kingdom. 

In keeping with this challenge, Christians are band- [91]

ing together in a movement to give first place in their lives to the building of a Christian world. This is no small undertaking; it demands all the resources of mind and strength, an utter consecration, and courageous sacrifice. If our cause is to succeed, we must give it all we have. Where does alcohol fit into this pattern?

SUGGESTED ACTIVITIES¹ 

We have talked about the liquor problem, is there anything we can do about it? 

For Individual Action: 

1. Practice total abstinence. 

2. Assume a Christian attitude toward those who use alcoholic beverages. 

3. Patronize as far as possible—and make known to others-establishments that do not sell alcoholic beverages. 

4. Give support by letter, telegram or personal interview, to individuals and movements opposing the liquor interests. 

5. Support legislation that will restrict the liquor traffic. 

6. Answer misrepresentations of the real facts, as they appear in newspapers and magazines. 

7. Protest against liquor advertising in newspapers, magazines, radio programs, road displays and billboards. 

8. Recognize and support those newspapers and magazines that refuse liquor advertising. 

For Group Action: 

1. Make and publicize a survey of the local community. Such a survey should reveal the number of drinking places, the nature of their surroundings, and the extent to which ordinances are being obeyed.
