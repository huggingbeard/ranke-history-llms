INTRODUCTION 

Fabricated parts are those industrial goods, manufactured from raw materials, which are definite units in themselves. As such they are incorporated without physical alteration into products sold to the consuming public.

The peculiar province of the present study is the advertising of fabricated parts in consumer media. 

Perhaps the classic example of such advertising is that of Timken roller bearings. It is familiar to everyone who reads the popular magazines. 

Some very natural questions arise as to its value to the advertiser. For example, what can a manufacturer hope to accomplish by consumer advertising where the consumer has no voice in the engineering problem of selection of fabricated units incorporated in the product he buys? Does the consumer have a voice in the problem and should it be heeded by the manufacturer of the product, or should such selection be based upon engineering tests? Is consumer advertising designed primarily to reach the consumer, or is it used for its influence directly upon manufacturers of the finished product? Is there perhaps a composite of appeals reaching various groups and resulting in direct and indirect benefit to the manufacturer of the part advertised? These and other problems appear when various aspects of consumer advertising are considered.

In spite of the interesting problems presented by consumer advertising of fabricated parts, no comprehensive study of the practice has previously appeared. General works on advertising, marketing, or merchandising frequently, though not invariably, recognize the existence of such advertising but do not analyze it to any extent. 

It should be noted that the advertising of certain materials, particularly metals, is closely analogous to the type considered here. To the extent that the products are similar in character, the same principles hold, but in the stricter sense the conclusions reached herein apply only to fabricated parts. 

Opportunity is taken here to acknowledge a debt of gratitude to those who have made material contribution to the conduct of this study. The Curtis 

Publishing Company rendered a helpful service in furnishing information whereby the advertising record compiled by the writer was checked for accuracy. Printers' Ink also cooperated in granting permission to use data concerning total advertising space employed in the Saturday Evening Post during the period studied. Finally, without the cooperation of the individual advertisers who kindly furnished information regarding their experience, conclusions could not have been stated with any degree of finality. Valuable aid was also given by many others who cannot be mentioned here.

Care has been taken by the writer not to express any personal opinion concerning the quality of the products discussed herein. [ 6 ]

The Problem 

The manufacturer of a fabricated part is faced with the problem of securing distribution of his product at a price that will yield a profit. Several methods are open to him. He may concentrate his efforts in making a product of standard quality at the lowest possible cost, go direct to the manufacturer of the finished goods in which the part is designed to be incorporated, and rely upon price in making sales. If his product adds an apparent consumer appeal to the finished goods, he may concentrate his efforts upon the retailer. Once the retailer is convinced that the part will help to sell the finished product, his influence upon the manufacturer of the finished goods may result in sales of the part. 

Finally, the manufacturer may make his appeal exclusively to the consumer. Should the consumer be led to insist upon a particular part, an effective demand for it will result. The ramifications of these methods and of the means employed are extensive and vary with the characteristics of the part and of the finished product.

But it is possible that consumer advertising may influence not only the consumer but the retailer or manufacturer as well. A Harvard case study presents an analysis of the strategy of consumer advertising of a fabricated part.¹ It is pointed out, first, that where consumer appeal is sufficiently strong, inquiries from the consumer will eventually react

upon the manufacturer of the finished product. Second, where only consumer recognition is secured, the manufacturer may desire to utilize the possible selling point by incorporating the advertised part in his product. Third, although there is no effect upon the consumer, the retailer, wishing to benefit from the advertising, suggests to the manufacturer the possibility of increasing sales by incorporation of the part in the finished product. Thus there would appear to be some justification for the belief that consumer advertising of fabricated parts has a definite and useful place in the marketing of such products. 

It is therefore desirable to set forth under what circumstances and subject to what limitations the policy may be pursued with profit. More specifically, some value may accrue from an attempt to determine the qualifying effects upon the profitableness of such advertising exerted by the following: physical characteristics of the part, extent to which it loses its identity, quality of the part, possible adaptability to other industrial fields, scope of operations of the manufacturer of the part, possibility of replacement, scope of the market for the part, stage of development of the industry, tendency toward stability or change in the industry. 

Plan of Analysis 

The basic material for the present study was drawn from two principal sources. First, a representative sample of the advertising to be studied was selected, and a tabulation by products, covering [ 8 ]

a period of seven and one half years, was made. 

Second, the particular advertisers concerned were requested to furnish information as to their motives in using such advertising and to express opinions regarding its effectiveness. The products were then grouped according to their significant characteristics and analyzed separately with particular reference to the advertising employed. In the analysis constant reference was had to the opinion of recognized marketing authorities as well as to available information concerning the methods and experience of other concerns.

A number of periodicals were surveyed in selecting a sample of advertising to be made the basis for the study. The relative scarcity of the type of advertising under consideration in all consumer periodicals except the Saturday Evening Post precluded the advisability of including data from other media in the tabulation. National advertising by radio is of course consumer advertising similar in character to that which appears in the Saturday Evening Post. 

It is employed, as well, by manufacturers of fabricated parts, e.g., the Perfect Circle Company. The difficulties of studying such advertising, however, when no published records are available, prevent its inclusion here. But it is believed that the conclusions reached with regard to consumer advertising of fabricated parts may be applied to radio advertising as well as to magazine advertising.

ADVERTISING OF FABRICATED PARTS- A COMPARISON 

The period covered by the present study, 1927 to 1933, is peculiarly appropriate for an examination of advertising practices under widely different business conditions. Business was generally prosperous up to 1929 after which a decline occurred. The purpose of the present section is to observe the effect of the broad economic changes upon national magazine advertising in general and upon the advertising of fabricated parts.

In order to measure relative quantitative changes in general advertising and in the advertising of fabricated parts two groups of data were compiled. A consideration of the character of advertising expenditures and of the benefits derived may shed some light upon the apparent tendency toward greater variation present in the latter class of advertising.

When an expenditure is made for advertising, a benefit to the advertiser is expected. The benefit may be immediate or deferred. When a merchant advertises a special sale in the local newspaper, he expects an immediate return. When a manufacturer advertises his product in a periodical of national circulation, he expects the benefit to accrue at a somewhat later date and to cover a longer period of time. 

If he advertises a specific product which is on the retailer's shelf, the lapse of time will be less than if he seeks to introduce and distribute a new and unknown product. Likewise, if he advertises a product for which the demand is indirect and which must go through an intervening stage of manufacture, the lapse of time will be greater. Once set in motion, however, the circular flow suggested will have some momentum. If the advertising or motive force is shut off temporarily, the advertiser will continue to receive benefits for a time. In periods of business inactivity, therefore, when reduced incomes necessitate reduced expenditures, advertising may be the first to suffer. That is to say, past advertising may have been in the nature of laying away for a rainy day and when that day arrives the benefits are sought to be reaped. Such considerations appear to apply particularly to advertising designed to create [15]

institutional prestige to which class the advertising of fabricated parts seems to belong.5

PRODUCTS WHICH ENJOY NO SIGNIFICANT DIRECT MARKET 

The products considered in the present section enjoy no direct market largely because of a single characteristic which is that they are not readily interchangeable or replaceable with other or similar parts. It is not meant to imply that in all cases replacement or interchange would be impossible, but that it does not readily take place and it is not often done or considered desirable. This situation is probably due to several factors, not the least of which is the inherent nature of the items with respect to the finished product, which necessitates that they become an integral part of the whole. Take, for example, an ignition system incorporated in the mechanical structure of an automobile. It becomes so vital a part of the whole that it would hardly be desirable for the consumer to replace it with another system even though the new system were definitely superior. The manufacturer, accordingly, makes no provision for replacement. If we contrast a complete ignition system with a storage battery which is readily replaceable, another distinction appears. The products considered in this section are such that their life corresponds roughly to the life of the finished goods and thus a replacement market, except for repair parts, does not exist. 

Since certain fabricated parts have and can have [18]

no direct consumer market, insofar as sales result from consumer advertising they presumably come about in an indirect manner. In other words, it might be said that the consumer who is influenced by advertising of such parts must be first a prospect for the complete product and must be influenced to the extent that the incorporation of the part will be a factor in his selection before sales of the part may result. Copeland asserts that little possibility exists for influencing the consumer to this extent because in the final analysis the maker of the finished product must assume complete responsibility for it. There is, accordingly, reason to doubt the value of further assumption of responsibility through advertising by the manufacturer of fabricated parts. But the employment of consumer advertising of such parts encourages the belief that the manufacturer benefits by it. It will be well to examine the nature of the possible benefit. 

Cheltham Bold informs us that every product has a "primary" market and a "secondary" market, the latter consisting of persons who by some change in status may become prospective purchasers.' Thus, consumer advertising may be building influence for the future. When a man becomes a prospective purchaser of an automobile, he has ordinarily long since formed a judgment as to which car or group of cars he prefers. Advertising plays a large part in

It seems evident, however, that the relative significance of the advertised part as compared with the final product is of considerable importance. The greater or the more vital the portion of the finished goods represented by the part, the more influence it would be expected to exert upon the consumer's choice. For example, if we assume the same degree of consumer preference for each, the make of body used would certainly have a greater conditioning effect upon the choice of an automobile than the type or brand of spokes found in the wheels. It follows, as well, that an advertised part of considerable relative significance, capable of greatly influencing the consumer's choice, would find a more ready sale to manufacturers of finished goods than one of less importance. It is reasonable to suppose, therefore, [ 20 ]

that a fabricated part of greater relative significance could expect a greater increase in sales as a result of consumer advertising.

Insofar as consumer advertising of fabricated parts is designed primarily to reach consumers, its efforts must come about largely through the channels suggested above. It may not be necessary, however, for such advertising actually to influence the consumer in order that a benefit be derived. Many of the advertisers consulted rely upon it to reach industrial purchasers directly. Although it is frequently considered impossible to introduce goodwill into sales of industrial goods (which are sold primarily on the basis of rational motives), advertising may be deemed successful if it acquaints operating executives with the qualities of such goods." 

If the manufacturer of fabricated parts, in addition to acquainting potential purchasers with the qualities of his product, is able to create a favorable opinion of the company, his position is much improved. It is, of course, more desirable for many reasons to buy from a concern whose standing seems unquestioned. A. Products Identifiable by Inspection. 

There are two fairly obvious reasons for drawing a distinction between parts apparent to the eye after becoming a part of the final product and those which are not thus apparent. In the first place, it is clear that if advertising is to be carried on for the purpose of influencing the consumer, some means must be provided by which he may recognize the advertised article when he sees it. The trade-mark is the customary means by which the consumer is able to recognize an advertised article.11 But if the part advertised is hidden in the finished product, the

trade-mark is not in evidence and identification cannot readily take place. With respect to advertising, accordingly, parts which are concealed in the finished goods are placed on a different footing from those which are identifiable by inspection. 

The second reason for distinguishing between parts apparent to the eye after incorporation in the final product and those which are not thus apparent, relates to the possible consumer appeals which may be made. Quite evidently a hidden part cannot be made the subject of appeal to the desire for style, beauty, luxury, or any of those desires having to do fundamentally with the outward aspects of the finished product. A hidden part, therefore, which does not contribute to efficiency, dependability, economy, or other enhancement of physical as contrasted with psychic satisfaction in use, does not lend itself readily to advertising to the consumer.

The reasons for segregating those parts designated as identifiable by inspection having been briefly stated, the advertising experience of individual products falling within the group will be discussed. 

Auto-Lite Equipment.-The Electric Auto-Lite 

Company manufactures complete ignition systems for automobiles. Although an ignition system is largely hidden, it still may be identified by observation and there is no difficulty in the way of advertising on this score. The means of identification consists of the trade name which appears on ammeters and other visible instruments, and on such parts as [ 23 ]

generators and distributors. Such products, however, being strictly mechanical in nature, lend themselves solely to the more rational appeals previously mentioned. 

The advertising of Auto-Lite in the Saturday

The central feature of the advertising was a cut of the starter-button with the driver's foot being applied. Dependability and universality of use were stressed through the medium of such expressions as, "Tested," "A Million Times a Day," and "Leadership." Hercules Engines.-Hercules truck engines, because of their relative significance, would appear to lend themselves well to consumer advertising. It is true that the replacement market is probably negligible and that the extent of replacement would not be subject to increase by advertising. But the significance of the product is sufficient that preference for it might constitute a very effective selective buying motive. The record of advertising, however, would lead to a different conclusion. But seven pages were utilized in all and advertising appeared only in 1930. The theme of the copy centered around heavy-duty use and resistance to abuse. The uses were listed in each advertisement and consisted of oil field trucking, agricultural trucking, industrial work, and the operation of snow plows, snow loaders, sprinklers, sweepers, scrapers, rollers, and other road machinery.

The use of advertising was perhaps not extensive enough to constitute a fair test, but its failure, if such it was, may be readily understood. The general public has no interest in the selection of such equipment as that listed. The circulation of general periodicals, therefore, is exceedingly thin so far as the market for the equipment is concerned. Those who must be reached consist primarily of road contractors, state, county, and municipal highway and street departments, and a few others. They may be reached much more effectively by trade publications. Hence the abandonment of the program was probably wise. [ 27 ]

Torrington Spokes. - Spokes for automobile wheels are markedly insignificant as compared with the complete product. It is essential that wheels give satisfactory service, of course, yet little complaint arises with respect to spokes. There appears, therefore, little justification for the belief that they can be profitably advertised to the public. Torrington spokes are said by the maker to possess desirable characteristics which consist of finer and more uniform adjustment due to the use of a "nipple" and increased tensile strength, hardness, and flexibility of steel due to "swaging." But while the product may have desirable qualities and can be identified in the finished goods, it does not possess the significant essential to a vital advertising appeal. The situation was evidently sensed at an early date for only two pages of advertising appeared, one in 1929 and one in 1930, an amount insufficient to create either manufacturer or consumer influence.

It may be well to digress here for a moment to discuss a general principle suggested by this product. Torrington manufactured spokes attached to [ 30 ]

the "drop-center" rim by means of a "nipple." Engineers favored spokes electrically welded to the rim, except for the higher priced cars, no doubt because of the greater production economy possible. The disposition of manufacturers to employ welded spokes and the lack of concern on the part of consumers offered obstacles which advertising by Torrington could have overcome only with difficulty. 

It is evident that a product whose market depends upon a condition running directly counter to a general tendency prevailing in a certain trade cannot hope to meet with much success in advertising. It is difficult to say just how far advertising of a particular product can go in altering such a tendency. In the case of style goods, Cherrington believes the manufacturer is not entirely helpless.18 But on the other hand, the Hartley Company,¹ a manufacturer of steel umbrella frames advertised to the public, found its market entirely dissipated by the change in style which dictated the use of frames of different materials. Advertising was not adequate to combat the trend successfully. Usually a wiser course is to study the trend carefully and, where at all possible, to alter the product or branch out into another line. 20

Bunting Bearings. - Bunting phosphor bronze bushing bearings enter into the manufacture of a wide variety of articles. The particular objectives sought to be accomplished by advertising are expressed as follows by the advertising agent for the company: 

1. To establish an obvious and undisputed national leadership in a specific field; 

2. To reach numerous uncharted prospects and countless individuals who influence specifications of materials; [35]

3. To create a national consciousness of the existence of, product of, and policies of, the company among all who in any way come into contact with the company's products. 

The breadth of the market provides perhaps the only reason for believing that consumer advertising would be profitable. Although broad, the market is quite thin in that potential customers are limited to manufacturers of machinery of one kind or another. 

While the latter is true of all the products being discussed in the present section, the market for Bunting bearings is broader than that for most of the other products. It is not limited to a single industry as is the market for those products entering only into automobile construction. 

The distinguishing characteristic of Bunting advertising is that it was openly addressed to manufacturers almost exclusively. For example there was the query, "Do you build a machine?" The emphasis upon quality was brought out by such phrases as: "The Invisible Difference," "The Heart of a Good Machine," "For Extraordinary Requirements." Great stress was laid upon the expert bearing knowledge made available to manufacturers through the Bunting consultation service. The strategy of the Bunting program is evident from the stated purpose. The secret of its success lies in the breath of the market. In the case of other products, such as ignition systems or slide fasteners, there was an evident attempt in the advertising to give the fabricated part some definite appeal in the finished product. In order to create the appeal the consumer had to be addressed directly while the manufacturer was apparently of secondary consideration. His good-will could be expected to grow out of the advertising as used if it were to be created at all.

Although the market is thin, it can probably best be covered completely by a periodical of general circulation. The advertising agent for the Bunting company is firmly convinced of the superiority of consumer advertising for such products as Bunting bearings. To reach substantially all those whose influence is essential would require the use of a vast number of trade papers. By a medium of wide general circulation the same message can be conveyed at much less expense. A further advantage of such a medium was suggested by some of the advertisers consulted.

PRODUCTS FIRST SOLD AS ACCESSORIES OR REPLACEMENTS 

The products now to be considered possess potentialities which may result (or have already resulted) in their shifting over into the first category-products which enjoy no direct market. Such products are usually marketed first as accessories or replacements for standard equipment. 

There are two instances in which the indirect market may be invaded by means of previous sale as accessories. The first occurs when the particular industry which it is hoped will adopt the part is in a state of evolution. An excellent example is afforded by the radio industry. In its early or experimental stage, parts were advertised and marketed direct to the users who assembled them. Later, when simple sets were sold, accessory equipment was sold direct for rebuilding or augmenting the assembled sets. The market thus became familiar with the manufacturers who were innovators in the field and parts made by them carried a sales appeal in the later completely assembled sets. 

The second instance is exemplified by the automobile industry at the present time in which the "novelty" and "expansion" stages have been left behind.27 Here in many cases it is necessary for a

But such items as auto radios, which have now reached the stage of optional factory installation, must continue to be sold as accessories until general consumer demand requires their universal installation. The concerns whose names are familiar to the public as makers of superior accessory radios will later have a competitive advantage in selling the manufacturers. 

In some cases a market for replacements exists, even after the developmental stages of an industry have been passed, because the life of certain parts is not coextensive with the life of the finished goods. 

When, on the other hand, an accessory item is so improved that it continues in service for the useful life of the unit in which it is incorporated, the replacement market will disappear.

Where a considerable direct market exists, relative significance of the part is of less importance than where the market is wholly indirect. PRODUCTS WHICH ENJOY BOTH DIRECT AND INDIRECT MARKETS 

In the final group we shall consider those goods which have a market consisting of industrial users but which in the very nature of things will continue to be sold direct in considerable volume. The direct sales may conceivably be the more important and insofar as this is true the products should be considered as any other consumer's goods. An example is afforded by rubber heels. It is inconceivable that shoes will become so cheap or heels so sturdy that no replacement market for heels will exist. If this be true, then consumer advertising for rubber heels is on substantially the same footing as that for any similar article with no industrial market. 

So far as the indirect demand is concerned, it is necessary that the part be identifiable in some way after its incorporation in the finished product. By such identification, consumer recognition becomes a factor in the sale. Since the products discussed here are comparatively insignificant, probably selective buying motives cannot be brought to bear but consumer influence may strengthen buying motives already in existence. However, if selective buying motives come about through customer preference, the possibility of replacement of the part would render them somewhat inoperative so far as a particular finished product is concerned. To illustrate, suppose [57]

the customer would refuse to buy a car unless it were equipped with a certain make of spark plug. There would be no incentive for a manufacturer to equip his cars exclusively with that plug. Those having a special preference may be served in any given instance by installation of the preferred part at the sales room. That is to say, if a particular part is to be the deciding factor, the approval of the customer may be secured by the installation of the part he desires. 

Thus an otherwise only partly acceptable product may be made wholly acceptable.

The important characteristic of the present group lies in the fact of a continuing direct market. Advertising may thus continue to be profitable without regard to the indirect market. But the market for original equipment, if extensively served, may enable the manufacturer to pay fixed costs and the direct or replacement market may become increasingly profitable.

A. C. Products.-The advertising of A. C. Pro ducts in the Saturday Evening Post during the period studied was not heavy in volume but it was continuous. It appeared to have the primary objective of keeping the "family" name continually before the public to aid in the introduction of new products. As new products were brought out they were featured, while in each advertisement the entire line was listed with small cuts of the products. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION 

Since most of the goods considered in the present study relate to the automobile industry, it may have occurred to the reader that the conclusions which are reached would not be generally applicable. Regarding the advertising of fabricated parts Copeland says: "Although it seems to have been successful, particularly in the automotive industry, during the period of technical improvement and rapid growth, it is not yet certain that it has a permanent place as a merchandising method for the manufacturer of most types of fabricating materials and parts. 1933 On the other hand, one of the most consistent and successful users of such advertising-Talon-is related to an entirely different industry. If slide fasteners and bearings are compared, however, it is seen that they have important characteristics in common. 

The two products are adaptable to a wide variety of uses. Moreover, the markets for the finished goods which incorporate the two products are extensive. 

Thus it seems evident that generalizations are more properly to be drawn with respect to the characteristics of the parts and their markets than with respect to industrial divisions. It is true, however, that industries which are in a state of constant change offer greater rewards to the advertiser of fabricated parts than those which are relatively more stable. 

33. M. T. Copeland, Principles of Merchandising, p. 148. [ 62 ]

If the results of this study were to be summed up in a single statement, it would be somewhat as follows: The profitableness of consumer advertising of a fabricated part depends upon the peculiar characteristics of the part, its market, and the market for the finished product in which the part is to be incorporated. A valid judgment as to profitableness in any given case must be predicated, accordingly, upon a careful analysis of those characteristics. In making such analysis, general principles must be relied upon to guide the judgment, and an attempt is therefore made to state the principles which apply. 

It should be evident that the more significant the part with respect to the finished product, the more profitable is its advertising likely to be. Comparison of the advertising of Body by Fisher with that of Auto-Lite equipment serves to bring out this principle. While the ignition system of an automobile is important, it is hardly conceivable that advertising could so popularize a particular system as to make it the important factor in the buyer's choice. On the other hand, Fisher bodies with their numerous features may be of some importance in leading purchasers to select General Motors cars. 

But it is not, in fact, necessary that such selective buying motives be established in order that advertising of a fabricated part may be profitable. An excellent example is offered by Timken bearings whose advertising, according to the advertising manager of the company, has continued with profit for twenty [63]

years. In this case consumer recognition is believed to give an additional appeal to cars so equipped. 

Thus Timken presumably presents manufacturers and dealers with a selling point which may be utilized to advantage, reacting favorably upon sales of the bearings. It is, of course, essential that the advertised part have the necessary quality in order to retain its appeal.

Although identification of the part in the finished product is necessary if it is to be advertised for the purpose of influencing consumers, a hidden part may overcome inherent disadvantages. Resorting again to bearings for illustration, advertising may carry a listing of the finished products in which the part is incorporated. When the manufacturer of a finished product is convinced of the appeal possessed by the part, identification may be accomplished by mention of the part in his advertising or catalogs. On the other hand, hidden parts such as bronze bearings, which lack a strong consumer appeal, may be profitably advertised although there is no attempt to provide for identification. In such cases the object is to reach the large number of those who influence machinery specifications and who can best be reached by consumer advertising. Provision for identification is unnecessary as well when an established part, such as the Bendix drive, is advertised without the intent to make sales but merely to pave the way for the introduction of new products.

Regarding concealed parts it should be noted, [64]

moreover, that consumer appeals are limited to those of a rational character. But the limitation is not a disadvantage since such goods are free from style factors and the uncertainty due to the whims of fashion. Thus a more stable advertising policy is possible, which is highly desirable for fabricated parts since returns are delayed and cover a greater period of time. As a further consequence of the fact that benefits are not immediate, consumer advertising is looked upon by the concerns consulted as being of doubtful value during extended periods of business depression when expenditures must be curtailed.

It has been sufficiently emphasized that by means of direct sales as an accessory, a part may be introduced and finally come to be regarded as necessary standard equipment. So long as a direct market exists, whether the part concerned be an accessory or a replacement item (such as piston rings), increased consumer sales may justify the advertising expenditure. Manufacturer and dealer influence will be incidental but valuable in reaching the indirect market. Consumer advertising may continue to be profitable in maintaining the prestige of the part after the direct market has disappeared.

As illustrated by A. C. Products, the family idea may be used in advertising fabricated parts. The wider the range of products included in the group, the greater the possibility for profit. The family idea is also useful in introducing new products to consum- [65]

ers, dealers, and manufacturers. If the concern in question sells to consumers both directly and indirectly, there may be an interaction between the two types of market and both be stimulated by consumer advertising.

Aside from its more direct and measurable benefits, probably the most important benefit of consumer advertising is its contribution to institutional prestige which facilitates sales at every point in the distributive process from manufacturer to consumer. Therefore, in spite of the waste circulation and the difficulty of checking results, it was highly indorsed by most of those advertisers who offered their opinions of the writer. The benefit appears to accrue in direct proportion to the advertising expenditure, regardless of the character of the part. The ultimate profit to be derived from such prestige, however, depends in large degree upon the potentialities of the market for the part and the market for finished products in which it may be incorporated. The principle involved may be illustrated by comparing bearings with spokes as a subject for consumer advertising. Although equal advertising effort might be expected to produce equal institutional prestige, the market for bearings, as well as that for articles incorporating bearings, is so wide that advertising of them is obviously the more profitable.

Several factors which may impose definite limitations upon the value of consumer advertising of fabricated parts should be mentioned finally. As exemp- [66]

lified in the case of the advertising employed by manufacturers of shock absorbers using the friction principle as opposed to the hydraulic principle, advertising can scarcely combat successfully a trend in the industry. In fact, it is much wiser for manufacturers to anticipate and follow such trends whenever possible. Again, as exemplified by Prentice fasteners, advertising which lacks aggressiveness cannot hope to overcome the advantage of a competitor already established in the field. Or, as in the case of Hercules engines, where the market for the equipment which uses the part is too largely industrial, trade publications are the more effective medium. Lastly, as suggested by the experience of Empire bolts, nuts, and rivets, low unit value and lack of a vital advertising appeal because of lack of significance impose serious obstacles to profitable consumer advertising.

It appears, therefore, that when its proper limitations are observed, consumer advertising has a definite and useful place in the distribution of fabricated parts. In spite of its usefulness, however, such advertising cannot be made to bear the entire burden and in all cases should be supplemented by trade and business paper advertising. Thus, consumer advertising produces its greatest returns when used in conjunction with a comprehensive industrial advertising program. [ 67 ]
