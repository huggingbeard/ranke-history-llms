INTRODUCTION How TO USE THIS GUIDE 

This leader's guide has been prepared with the hope that it will offer a variety of suggestions to leaders of groups who are interested in learning more about our Latin American neighbors. It contains three parts: 

I. Suggestions for Discussion Groups II. Suggestions for Program Meetings III. Suggestions for Worship 

Generally speaking, those leaders desiring to conduct a study course in which members of the group participate in a creative manner will want to give particular attention to the materials contained in Part I. These suggestions are based upon W. Stanley Rycroft's book, On This Founda tion: The Evangelical Witness in Latin America. “The 

Outlook Pamphlets on Latin America" are used as collateral reading and study material.¹

Those who find that their time is limited and that class discussions are not possible may draw upon Part II for help in building a series of programs. 

Regardless of what procedure may be followed in conducting a study on Latin America, the leader will want to guide the group in worship. Part III contains prayers, meditations, and titles of appropriate hymns.

CONDUCTING A STUDY GROUP 

Regardless of how the leader may carry on a study of Latin America, he should keep in mind the fact that he must have as many members of the group participating as possible. This participation may be of many sorts. The suggestions that follow are based upon the principle that we learn through experience. That is, we learn through doing, through reading with a specific purpose, through creative discussion, through interviews, through personal contacts, and through the many experiences of everyday life. 

A good leader will, therefore, seek to provide a variety of opportunities for each member of the group to grow in his knowledge and understanding of Latin American peoples and in Christian attitudes toward them. 

It is suggested that the "Commission Plan" be used as a general working procedure in conducting this study. Briefly, the Commission Plan consists in the appointment of certain committees from the group who study specific phases of the subject and bring in reports at specified times. These reports are discussed by the entire group. It is usually best to have a separate committee for each general topic on which a report is desired.

If the size of the group makes it impossible to have a committee for each topic, a committee may be appointed to

INTRODUCTION 

report at each session and the committee in turn may apportion responsibility for reports on particular aspects of the study to individuals on the committee. It is sometimes preferable, in small groups, to appoint individuals to make these reports rather than committees; but, in this case, they should be chosen not later than the first session and their topics assigned at that time. 

The committees should be appointed at the first meeting of the group, if not earlier. It is advisable that the chairmen of these committees be selected well in advance of the first meeting. This gives time to arrive at a clear understanding of the particular work to be assigned the several committees, to secure source materials, and to confer with each other regarding the relationship that may exist between their respective topics. If it is not feasible to select the chairmen ahead of time, the leader should secure basic materials and have them ready for distribution to the committees at the first meeting.

The leader should read this pamphlet through before appointing the committee chairmen and should have clearly in mind what each committee is to do. As soon as possible after their appointment the leader should confer with the committee chairmen concerning the nature of their reports and the date when they may be expected.

Each committee should meet and distribute its work among the members at the earliest possible moment. A committee will need to meet at one other time to consider and formulate the report. A report may consist of one statement presented by the chairman or secretary of the committee, or it may consist of several brief reports. In any case, a report should be open for discussion by the whole study group. 

No hard and fast rules can be laid down for the discussion of reports. It is presupposed that the leader will be familiar with creative group discussion methods and technique.

PART I: SUGGESTIONS FOR DISCUSSION GROUPS Session 1 DESTINY OF THE AMERICAS: MANY LANDS AND MANY PURPOSE PEOPLES 

To learn to know Latin Americans as brothers, heirs of our own Father, God.

ATTITUDES 

There are two difficulties often faced by discussion group leaders that must be avoided or overcome in the very beginning: 

1. Many groups take the attitude: "Let the leader do it." As they look at the leader their eyes say, “We dare you to entertain us." Much of this can be avoided by giving the proper publicity, both oral and published, to the course; by the appointment of committees or individual investigators and the assignment of their respective topics at the beginning of the first session; and by keeping the discussions within the limits of the group's own experience. 

2. Other groups, influenced by school or club experiences, think of a study such as this as a discussion of history or economics. This attitude may be overcome by leading the group, in a spirit of worship, into the realization that the Christ has identified himself with these our brothers "south of the border." We are trying sympathetically to enter into the life situations of the Father's children of whom Jesus said, "Inasmuch as you did it unto . . . them, you did it unto me."

DEVELOPMENT 

This entire series of discussions of our fellow-Americans "south of the border" may be undertaken as an imaginary journey down the Pan American Highway from the Texas border to Santiago, Chile, then around through Argentina and Brazil. This method will be particularly useful in gaining a rapid geographical and historical knowledge of Latin America. It is, therefore, proposed as the procedure in the first two sessions. Instead of a trip down the Highway, some groups may prefer to take an imaginary trip by air, steamship, and rail. All subsequent sessions should be planned in the light of these first two.

I. In order that this imaginary journey may be realistic and interesting it is necessary to have maps. Several should be on the walls of the room. Not to have them is to invite failure for the whole program. It is especially necessary to have a map that shows the whole of Latin America. One showing the general topography is useful in this first session; and if the entire Western Hemisphere is shown on this map, it is much better. A wall-size enlargement of the colored map in Dr. Rycroft's book is available for twenty-five cents from denominational literature headquarters. There should be maps of the various regions of Latin America. A pictorial map in color, 22½ x 29½, entitled "The Americas," showing the United States and all of Latin America, may be obtained free from Kelly, Nason, Inc., 247 Park Avenue, New York,

N. Y. The leader should procure this map and a selection of travel folders before the meeting of the group and distribute them to the proper committees or reporters at the first session or before.

Emphasize the things that unite us as well as those that separate the two Americas. 

If any member of the group has known Latin Americans, the discussion may be made more realistic by having this member tell briefly about them. He may tell about their general appearance-height, color, dress; their personal habits-food, recreation, manner of greeting friends, calmness or excitability under unusual circumstances; their spiritual qualities-loyalty, sincerity, philosophy of life. Let him tell how they differ from his neighbors. If no member of the group has known Latin Americans, illustrations may be given from reading. This will bring out the fact that we are not all alike even within a given race. Remember in all this that stories and interpretations of persons are far better than general descriptions.

PURPOSE 

To understand how our Latin American friends came to be what they are.

DEVELOPMENT 

Along the Highway into the heart of Latin America we have noticed many conditions of economics, race, culture, and religion that are quite unfamiliar to us. Now we go back to ask "Why?" This answer will best be given by a committee that has been subdivided into some five sections or by five separate committees, who should report to the group at this session. The assignments to the committees, made not later than the first session of the group, can be arranged either geographically or historically. 

I. If the assignments to the committees (or individual investigators) are according to geographical areas, the route of the Pan American Highway should again be taken, as follows: (1) Mexico; (2) Middle America (Central Amer ica and the Caribbean area); (3) the west coast or Andean region; (4) the River Plate area; (5) Brazil. Sections from the regional "Outlook" pamphlets will be helpful in this discussion, but Rycroft will furnish most of the material essential to the discussion. See also Part II, Programs 3 to 7.

II. III. In either case the discussion may be grouped around certain historical figures who represent the contributions of that area or period. This adds human interest and is good, provided we do not lose from view the humbler people of that day. Thus the story of the Indians might be grouped around Montezuma II and Atahualpa; that of the Iberians around Cortés, Pizarro, Solís, and Cabral; that of the colonial period around Bartolomé de las Casas, Antonio Vieira, Luis de Velasco (father and son), and the Brazilian bandeirantes; that of the revolutionary period around Bolívar, San Martín, O'Higgins, and Hidalgo; that of the modern period around 

Pedro II, Sarmiento, and Juárez. These same names may be regrouped and used to represent the regional development of the discussion.

In any discussion of the characteristics of Latin American peoples today, this chapter of Dr. Rycroft's must have a large place. It is a masterful summary of the whole subject.

DISCUSSION GROUPS PURPOSE 

To discover the place of the Evangelical church, its opportunities and problems in present-day Latin America.

DEVELOPMENT 

As we moved down the Pan American Highway we noticed the ruins of many old temples used before the days of Columbus; also many beautiful old Roman Catholic churches and perhaps a few Evangelical churches. We turn now to see the place of these Evangelical groups among our 

Latin American friends. If the suggestions made in the first session have been observed, there are either three committees (or sub-committees) or three individual reporters now ready with special reports for this session. 

I. The first report deals with statistics. First, the figures for the denomination represented by the discussion group should be given. They should include the number of communicants, number of people directly influenced by church schools or other organizations, number of missionaries and national church workers, as well as educational and social enterprises carried on by the denomination. The "Outlook" pamphlets may be consulted for general statistics regarding the Evangelical churches and communities in the several countries. Along the whole stretch of our trip down the Pan American Highway and in every country we saw that Latin America is largely agrarian and the system of land tenure is still feudal. This is what Dr. Rycroft means by the latifundio system. His explanation of the system is most helpful.

THIRD SPEAKER: What of Languages and Culture? 

This speaker should emphasize the fact that, although Spanish and Portuguese are the "official" languages of the Latin American countries, there are still millions of Indians who either do not speak these languages at all, notably in 

Mexico, Bolivia, Peru, and Ecuador, or who speak their native Indian language from preference though they can speak the official language when the occasion demands. The term 

Indo-American better describes these people.

The indigenous cultures of the Indians of Latin America were modified and in some instances almost destroyed by the Spanish and Portuguese invasions. The conquerors brought with them their language and culture. The enslavement or conquering of the Indians resulted in the destruction or retarding of the Indian cultures.

In a series of studies on Latin America, it is fitting that the themes for worship have to do with the reality of the Living Christ and the meaning of the Cross in experience. 

In lands where for so many decades Jesus has been known as "Pobre Cristo" (Poor Christ) and where the Cross has been for so many only a magic symbol, thousands are now coming to know the Living Christ and the Cross is becoming a transforming reality. If, in brief moments of meditation, we can sense what Jesus Christ is now coming to mean to our Latin American neighbors, it is certain that by this he will become more real to us.

I. The Living Christ HYMNS: "Son of My Soul" or "The King of Love My 

Shepherd Is."

SCRIPTURE: Ephesians 3:14-21; Galatians 2:20. 

THOUGHT FOR MEDITATION: "Christ dwells in our hearts." (Ephesians 3:17.) 

Goodspeed translates this verse: "Let Christ in his love make his home in your hearts." Christ conquered death. The grave could not hold him, no church nor cathedral can now contain him. The real dwelling place of Christ is in the hearts of believers. The greatest proof of his existence is the evidence of his presence in the life of his followers. 

On the highest mountain overlooking the city of Rio there is a magnificent statue of Christ with arms outstretched so that the sun casts the shadow of a cross on the mountain below it. 

The statue was completed in 1932. Floodlights on the mountain at the base of the statue make it look at night like an angel suspended in air. It is the pride of Brazilians. 55

Thousands of people think Christ can be found only at shrines. 

A sad picture given by Dr. Webster E. Browning in his book entitled New Days in Latin America illustrates this: 

"One of the most pathetic sights at Esquipulas was to witness the departure of the different groups. A group of Indians will march from the temple steps through the long, narrow streets and up the steep ascent at the end, chanting as they go a low, mournful plaint.

Christ behind at Esquipulas, never perhaps to see him again. Yes!

That is the end of their pilgrimage. A long weary march on foot, a short glimpse of a black wooden image, a few moments of excitement from the stimulus of a crowd engaged in a common act of devotion, and then an empty, aching heart to keep them company on the long journey home, aye! and an empty heart for all the days to come." 1

Aspiration among Andean Peaks

By Dr. Walter J. Montaño, formerly a Roman Catholic priest, who, after leaving his monastery in his search for truth, became a Protestant. For several years he has been head of the Evangelical Alliance of Lima. 

We thank thee, O Heavenly Father, for having given us the privilege of living in this country with its lovely valleys, its expansive rivers, and its immense forests. We thank thee for the wealth of its soil and for the treasures thou hast buried in its depths. 

However, forgive us, we beseech thee, for not having elevated our ideals to the height of our Andean peaks, for not widening our vision like our Amazonian rivers, for not having explored our possibilities for spiritual betterment as we explore our mines for gold and silver, and for not having allowed thee to transform our lives as our craftsmen transform the wood of our forests into objects of use and beauty. Amen.
