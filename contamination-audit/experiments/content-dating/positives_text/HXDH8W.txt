JANEKSITY INTRODUCTORY CHAPTER THE CALM 

IT WAS the beginning of summer, 1914. I was a pupil of the eighth grade of the Board school in the small country town, B, which is situated somewhere in Central Germany on the highway that connects two old and well-known cities, namely, Heidelberg, in the state of Baden, with its historic castle and world-known universities, and Wuerzburg, in the former kingdom of Bavaria.

The biggest, or rather, the only worry I had then was, what was I going to be when I grew up, or to do after leaving the Board school? Should I learn a trade, and what kind, in one of the little shops, of which there were some of most every handicraft; maybe start as an office boy, or what? Of course I would have liked to go to some high school and get a broader education than the average boy of a country town like ours. But 

I was not as fortunate as some of my classmates, who were already off in a nearby city, to acquire knowledge above that supplied in the Board school. 

It must be said to the credit of the German school system, we received a fairly good education at that. 

After eight years of grammar school, one was obliged to go four hours a week for a period of two years to the Fortbildungschule, Finishing school. Among the subjects taught there were: some bookkeeping, writing of business letters, instructions for the different [ 9 ]

trades and the like. The trouble of it was, one had to pay for everything, which was not so easy for many a family-father, and mine was one of them. He, like most of the people there, was only a small farmer, who was glad to be able to feed and clothe his family, and no deficit when the year was over. But there was a deficit more than once, all depending how the crops turned out on those few pieces of land that my father owned.

I was the youngest in the family, and had to use the books my elder sisters and brothers had used before. When there was a leaf or two missing here and there, "Well, that's too bad," my father would say, or "That can't be helped, you will grow up without them." 

And my crying or protesting didn't help any either. 

So you see that there wasn't any money to send father's little boy to high school, to enable him to become something above the ordinary. This, from early youth, was my ambition. I wanted to go out in the world and become a great man, or what they would call here, a self-made man.

For this the average boy of Germany-or any European country, for that matter, I believe-has not as good an opportunity as most every American boy has. 

Sometimes it makes me feel that some American girls and boys do not know or realize how fortunate they are in having all the opportunities the American schools and public libraries offer them. Here in these great 

United States, the poor girl and boy with talent and will has about the same chance to get a good, broad education as the rich ones. While in Germany or [10]

THE CALM 

Europe one has to have money for that. I often wished to have been born in America, but I was not to give my parents any order in this matter. However, I am very glad, indeed, to be able to state that I am a citizen of these great United States of America, and I shall try with all in my power, to be a good, respectable citizen, so that I may be worthy of my new home-land, which I have learned to love.

As circumstances were, I tried to make the best of them.

From one of our schoolmasters, who was quite a patriot, we learned that, if a boy would serve in the army for a certain number of years, take up some studies for which one would get time off, one could get a permanent government position with old-age pension, maybe in a post or railroad office. All the railroads in

Germany then were under the ownership of the government. Yes, one could even become a postmaster, like the one our town had, who had served twelve years in the army. "Twelve years soldier," I thought. "Gee! that is a long time to stand and walk at stiff attention every time one meets a superior. But then to become a postmaster, have a permanent position, that wouldn't be so bad. Yes, that would be fine, that would be something I always wanted to be. Then too, I should rather work a little harder while I am young, and get it more easy as, age was coming on. After I finish with the 

Board school, I shall learn some trade, maybe tailor and barber." [11]

Most of the tailors also did barbering as a sideline, and in the army these "mechanics" were needed. "When I get old enough I shall volunteer. Perhaps after awhile I could get into some of the army shops, take up the necessary studies, rise to the rank of a sergeant or lieutenant, then become a postmaster." 

My father thought, "Better don't climb up so high, so you won't fall so hard." He was always a little pessimistic. But to me it was a wonderful plan, and the future looked bright. What a disappointment I had to experience! 

As I mentioned before, most of the people in our town were farmers. Some of them were well-to-do, such as the owners of the flour mills, of which there were four, and two sawmills. To me it seemed peculiar that those mill owners also possessed the most land, that is, they owned more acres than the average farmer. There were a few other well-off farmers, that had a team of horses to do their work with, but most of them were more or less like my father, who was fortunate enough to call about half a dozen heads of cattle his own. Then there were the different mechanics with their small shops, carpenters, cabinetmakers, blacksmiths, cartwrights, tailors, shoemakers and most every handicraft.

Some of these mechanics had the trade as a side occupation, others had a small piece of land on which to raise wheat or potatoes for their own use. Everybody, up to the stationmaster, doctor and parson, had a vegetable garden.

There was a machine factory with sawmill that em- [12]

THE CALM 

ployed about sixty men, a four-story brickkiln, and in the surrounding hills were four quarries which kept quite a number of men busy. You see we had a little industry, and to keep that running it takes money. 

So of course we also had a bank, "The People's Bank." 

Only many did not have much to do with it. In the various country stores one could buy all the necessities for the household and tools for the shop and farm. 

The storehouse of the "Farmers Association" was located close to the railway station. We also had an apothecary. Too many times I sat in his store, waiting for the filling of the prescription for my mother. 

The county hospital was standing up on the slope of a hill, looking down into the valley with its soft, green meadows, that lay to both sides of the stream, which flowed through town.

From this stream the mills got their power to drive the machinery.

Then of course there were the different inns and beer gardens with names such as, "Gasthaus zum gruenen Baum," "Wirtschaft zum Ross" and the like.

Not to find signs like these, coming into a town or village around there, is something impossible, even if the village consists of only about half a dozen houses. 

Those inns seemed to be a necessity. They were the only places for a little amusement, especially during winter and the rainy season; and where the men spent most of the long winter evenings playing cards, or had a cool, well-earned drink after a hard day's work during the warm summer.

Our town considered herself lucky and historic, in [13]

having one of those antique buildings one finds in Europe, and which remind us of rulers and times of long, long ago. It was an old castle and, unlike most of its kind, was built down on the valley or level ground.

The knights of the olden times usually built their homes on a point that was hard to reach by their enemies, but easy to fight against an attack. Wherever they settled themselves, they picked the most secure place to establish their home. So most of those yokerulers built their castle up some steep hill. 

As the hills around our town were not suitable, they built on the level. One side of a hill may be very good and easy to defend, but the knights wanted to be safe from all angles. A solution of this problem they had, by building on level ground wherever it seemed best for them. Around, about a hundred feet away from the main building, the castle itself, they built a high stone wall. The only passage was a drawbridge. 

In time of danger this was pulled up, and the revolting peasants or the soldiers of another enemy knight were unable to get at their prey. 

The large courtyard was closed in by the barns and storage buildings. All around these, including the castle itself, was another moat. On two sides this moat and the stone wall were still there at my time. 

In time of war, this canal and the space between the castle and the stone wall were filled with water, to serve as a blockade. Fighting then had to be done by hand-to-hand mostly. Rifles and cannon did not shoot as far and were not as disastrous as in the World War. [14]

THE CALM 

The lookout tower, likewise built of stone, stood about five hundred yards off. It was connected with the castle by means of a subterranean passage. 

It was in this old castle that I received my schooling, for it served our town as schoolhouse. The gymnastic exercises we had on the first floor of the main storage building and on the courtyard. All the boys had to attend the gymnastics during the summer period. If these exercises were somewhat of a preliminary training for military service, they certainly helped much for the development of muscles and discipline.

The castle yard also did good service as a playground for the children during recess, and as banqueting place for civic and patriotic celebrations. 

Amidst all the foregoing stood the church, with its tower reaching far above all the other buildings, as though to admonish the people below of some higher might, some invisible force, that guides the sun, the moon and the stars. An early forenoon sun was smiling down on Mother Earth. Today no toiling workers were seen in the fields. No smoke went up in the air from the two tall chimneys of the brickkiln and the machine factory. In town it was very quiet and peaceful, and the streets were exceptionally clean. Also here was nobody to be seen, except here and there some small children, playing. One not knowing what day of the week it was, but a little familiar with the life and doings of a coun- [15]

try town, could have easily guessed that it was Sunday and people were at church. 

Soon the stillness was broken by the ringing of a bell and shortly afterwards by the people coming out of church. If you looked at them you didn't need to be told that the great majority were just a lot of simple but happy small-town folks. Standing around in small groups, the older men and women greeted one another, while the girls chattered with their boy friends and held council, through which of the neighboring woods they should promenade in the afternoon, or to which of the nearby villages to go and have a good time, to use an American expression, "make whoopee," or in finer language, if you rather please, to enjoy themselves with song and dance. School children mingled among them.

This scene didn't last very long for the women folks had to be going home to prepare dinner, and most of the younger men went into the inns to have their "Fruehschoppen," morning draught. I don't know if they took these as an appetizer or for the thirst. Some ever seemed to have the latter. But they were broadshouldered, sturdy, healthy looking he-men, such as are needed on the farm, in the sawmill, quarry and brickyard.

Among all the towners was a young couple whose happiness and contentment did not seem to be shadowed by the least of a dark cloud, for they had a broad smile and cheerful greetings for everybody they knew. They too went into one of the inns that was close to church. The lady conversed with her good [16]

THE CALM 

acquaintances, while the man had a glass of beer and a talk with the innkeeper. When the man was paying the latter, he said a few words to the servant that was washing glasses behind the bar. The servant disappeared to the rear. The man escorted the lady out through the front door.

Outside, the servant brought a horse from the stable and put it to a carriage that stood in front of the inn. 

Gallantly the man helped his fair escort into the vehicle, tipped the servant, took the reins, and off they went a perfectly happy looking couple.

They were Mr. and Mrs. Hofer, owners of the 

Burghof (castle-farm), otherwise known as Hofer's 

Anna and Tony. Their house and farm buildings stood on the outskirts of town, and so they drove in their carriage to church on Sundays. Tony and Anna had been married for about three years, and Tony loved his wife with the tenderness of a good husband. 

They were blessed materially, but greater was their joy when the first baby, a girl, arrived about two years before. Now it was Tony's wish that he might have a son, to be the heir of the Burghof. 

As they drove along towards their home through the bright summer day, Tony lost in these thoughts,

Anna put her head close to his and whispered something-something that is a sweet secret for a wife to tell her husband. Tony tenderly put his arm around her waist and said very hopefully: "I hope it will be a boy." 

On arriving at their place, their little girl, Irma, came running towards them. Anna fondly picked her [17]

up in her arms. Joerg, their old servant, unharnessed the horse and put it in the stable. The younger servant, Albert, was feeding the cows and poultry, among which a peacock showed its sparkling colors in the bright sunshine. In the house the maid was setting the table for dinner.

On the farm they were not very busy right then, as the hay was already in and too early for the other crops. An atmosphere of peace and calmness lay over town, everybody seemed to be as contented as the faithful watchdog that was lying in its kennel, snoring carelessly. [18]

CHAPTER II WAR! 

THE WHOLE town was on its legs; everybody seemed to be as busy as a bee. The barns, the streets, the fields were alive with working people. It was harvest time. 

In the shops and factories they made their weekly cleanup and got ready for quitting time, for it was Saturday afternoon. Others cleaned their section of the street as usually before Sunday. Inside of town each one had to clean half of the street in front of his property. I was doing this for a neighbor, for which every time I received a dime. While sweeping the street, I was dreaming of tomorrow, Sunday, when we kids would have a few hours of real play. True, we just had school vacation then, but we children in the country didn't get vacation for rest or play, which a vacation is really for; but mostly to assist our folks in bringing in the crops. In fact, that time I thought, this was the reason why we were given school vacation at all. So one can imagine that we always looked forward to a Sunday, when we could have some play and pleasure.

All at once I was awakened out of my daydream by the sound of a trumpet. At first I thought it was the call for the fire brigade. Could there be a fire somewhere? Now, when everything is so dry and the water low. Then, no, that was a different sound. The sounds came closer, and already some people, who were [ 19 ]

coming from the direction the trumpet was blowing, spread the news to all sides with horrified looks on their faces, crying, "It's war! It's war! State of war is declared!"

The servant of the town council with the trumpeter came close now, and read his message from a sheet of paper. "By order of his Majesty the Kaiser, the country is declared in a state of war. First day of mobilization is the second of August, 1914."

Usually the council servant had only a handbell with which to call people's attention, when he brought the council news around town. But of course for something so very important as this, a trumpeter was engaged, to make more sure everybody would hear it, and none of the reserve men miss his day, when each one had to report for duty.

What a shock this was for most of the people! 

Germany had enjoyed peace for over forty years, and the Kaiser was called the "Peace-Kaiser." But now war was declared, surely he must have good reasons for it, or was forced to do so. In just a moment of time, the hearts of mothers, wives and sweethearts were full of sorrow and woe; for now they were to give their sons, their husbands, their beloveds into the war-battle, to work with guns and rifles, instead of bringing in the crops, which promised a good harvest this year. 

Would they come back, or stay out on the battlefield, to be buried there? And, if they did come back, how would they? Maybe disfigured, blind, missing limbs, crippled for life. [ 20 ]

WAR!

Of course most of the men were different, especially the younger ones. What enthusiasm and fighting spirit did they have! Men are like that. That is, they were then.

In the first excitement I did not know what to do, to cry hurrah with some other young fellows, or be sad, because the news just received might spoil the hoped-for Sunday play. I also looked at soldier's life as something different than doing a little training and maneuvering and some studying, to become a postmaster. At any rate, my ambition to serve twelve years in the army was a trifle cooled off right there, and I took the consolation that none of my folks would yet have to go and fight. The war would have to last quite a long time before they would take my eldest brother; very likely it would be over long before this was necessary.

I was scarcely finished with these thoughts and still sweeping, when I heard distant singing that came from the hills where the quarries were. I dropped my broom, ran into the house, up to the attic and leaned out of a small dormer window, from where one could see the street that went up the hill to the quarries. At other times I had been leaning out of the attic window, to watch colored fireworks up on the hill, in connection with some town festivities. But what I saw now had some real characters behind it. Up at the quarries they had heard the good news by telephone, and so the men there marched home all together, in lines, a long group, and singing like they were soldiers already. 

Surely one can easily guess what they sang: "Es [21]

braust ein Ruf wie Donnerhall," "The Watch at the 

Rhine," of course. And couldn't they sing! What voices those stone-breakers had, just as big and strong as their muscles! No matter what political affiliation one had, if monarchist or socialist, democrat or centrist, they all marched now side by side, singing and thinking the same thing, of keeping watch at the Rhine, and thereby defending and protecting their homes. Yes, it was vividly inspiring, to see those men marching down the hill, into town, up to the town hall, led by their foreman, a big husky fellow who was a young reserve officer and so one of the first to leave. 

Two guards were stationed at the town hall, where they had to do night-shift in case some special should call or orders come in. Most everybody seemed to be suffering with an exciting and enthusiastic fever. 

Sunday morning, the second of August, passed like most any other Sunday morning. Only a few men of our town had to leave the first day of mobilization, and they slipped out to the station quietly, as not many knew about their leaving. In the afternoon, however, warlike activities took place.

When a country is in war, one of the duties at home is to watch for spies and catch them. Germany, as well as other countries, then was full of them, at least according to talk, and most everybody with a suspicious face, or whose actions seemed to be, was believed a spy, although he might be as innocent as his Majesty himself. Of course spies should be caught, for a spy may do more good for his country, or bad to the other, than [22]

WAR! a whole army may be able to do. For, what good are the best plans and orders of a general, if these plans are secretly told to the other side and thereby it is enabled to set a trap?

The telephone lines to our town were busy as never before. Messages went back and forth about all kinds of spies that were to go through our town, and should be stopped for search and identification.

It was in the afternoon. Among men, some of us boys were standing around the town center by the town hall, watching the guards there with their rifles and bayonets, and eagerly awaiting some news or message that each one would like to be the first in spreading around town. In the two nearby inns things were very lively. All kinds of patriotic and soldier songs were heard from them.

Finally one of the gendarmes and another man came hastily out of the town hall. One could see, he must have some important news. The gendarme came up to the men and said: "There is an automobile with spies coming from H-. We have to stop them; come along, boys."

The men followed the gendarme, who was hastening towards the west end of town, where the supposed spies were to come in. Some of the men went along 

Main Street, to be sure not to miss the spies, while others took a shortcut, so they might be able to see them first. One of the bigger boys ran into the inn to get more help. "Hey, come on, you fellows, and do some real work! 

Spies are coming," he bellowed at the men inside. [ 23 ]

But his report was completely drowned by the voices of the men, who had just started another toastsong. Some other method would have to be used to make those fellows listen. Next moment the boy stood up on the table among the beer glasses, crying out his message again. Now that made them pay attention. Even if they wouldn't hear him, they couldn't help seeing him. Most of the men jumped right up and ran out, following their boy leader; never mind about paying the check, catching of spies was more important now. However, three of the younger men were so deep in their song and beer, they forgot all about their patriotism, and kept right on singing and drinking.

To be sure of bringing the spies' machine to a stop, it would be best to blockade the street, one of the men suggested. With hay wagons and carts this could be done quickly. As it was Sunday these things didn't stand about on the street, like usually on week-days. 

Right where the spies were awaited was a sawmill. So everybody took a hand, and in a minute there was a pile of logs and lumber lying across the street that would have made it warm for a tank to push out of its way.

The gendarme went a little ahead to give the stop signal. If the spies wouldn't obey him, they sure would that pile of logs, and there was no getting around it, either, without ruining their machine. Some boys on bicycles rode farther ahead, to watch and listen for the approach of the spies, as the street wound up another hillock. [24]

WAR!

A short while passed, when one of the boys came tearing down at full speed. He could barely stop his bicycle, so as not to smash it on that pile of logs. Almost out of breath he told us that he got a little behind the others, to be the first one to come down, and the spies must be coming now, for he had heard the sound of a motor car.

The excitement among the men was growing. Now they could do their first act in fighting the enemy. 

Ten . . . fifteen minutes passed. Nothing showed up, except the rest of the boys on their bikes came back. They hadn't seen or heard an automobile yet. 

The men began to grow anxious. Surely the spies ought to be coming now. It should not have taken them any longer, from the town it had been reported they were seen, to our place. Did they get wise, leave the highway, taking a detour through the neighboring villages?

Soon a messenger from the town hall came and said, that a call had just come in. The spies were stopped in the town ahead of ours; everything was all right they were no spies at all. After the street was cleared again, the men went their ways, some of them who would have liked to lay their hands on these spies, a trifle disappointed.

Fifteen minutes later an automobile, with army officers and a guard armed with a rifle, stopped at the town center. Looking at the road guide, then pointing towards the east, one of the officers asked: "This way to W—?” [25]

"Yes, sir," said the gendarme, who came running up and gave the salute.

Thus our town had its first meeting with "spies." 

At both ends of town were now posted guards, who had to stop every automobile that would pass. There were a few that didn't show any interest or enthusiasm at all. One of them was Joerg, the old servant on the Burghof. While the above was going on, Joerg was sitting in one of the inns, having his glass of beer as usual on Sunday afternoon. A few other elderly men sat around, among whom was a staunch blacksmith who had served in the war of 1870. Joerg wasn't in his good humor, like he used to be. He was thinking about the departure of the master, Tony, and his wife bearing a child. "I always have had my troubles and plenty of hard work, but so far the world seemed pretty good to me; but now I don't know what to think of it. Have they gone mad, or what? Just think, right now, when there is so much to do out on the field, to bring in the crops, our best men are taken away, to fight and be killed." 

Joerg was complaining in his simplicity. "Well," said the old veteran, "they're just doing their duty, and besides, this war won't last very long. 

In five, six weeks our troops will be in Paris, then they'll return home triumphantly," he finished with a confident expression on his face. "Yes, maybe. But don't you think the Frenchmen are just waiting to give our Kaiser and his soldiers a big reception in Paris? Furthermore, I don't see why [26]

WAR! we should go out and kill our neighbors, just because they're Frenchmen. They didn't do me any harm." "But if we don't fight, they will come, destroy and take our land." "Who will?" "The Frenchmen and the Russians." "Oh, I don't think the French people like it so well to go to war, get killed and crippled. Of course they're patriotic, like the most of us and so they go. What I mean, if those, a few on either side, who declare war, absolutely want to have it, well, let them fight it out amongst themselves." "Better be careful what you say, Joerg," answered the blacksmith, and went off.

Joerg did not understand the world now. He had another glass of beer and went on his way home. Many a thought went through his mind, as he walked the short distance along the highway towards his place, the Burghof. On both sides of the street there were apple and pear trees, heavy laden with their tasty and healthful fruit. It was a joy for Joerg to look at the fields of rye and wheat, some of it in sheaves and heaps ready to bring in, others awaiting the sharp cut of the scythe. He could see all over town and almost every speck of it. Here and there a chimney was smoking, caused by the kitchen fire, where women were preparing supper. Joerg saw the old castle, standing there like a rock, and the stone tower, telling of times long, long past. He saw the three tall chimneys in the industrial district, telling of man's brains and achievements-the church tower in the midst of all. He [ 27 ]

heard the whistle of a train, and then saw it pull into the station. The sky above was so clear, while in the west, the glowing sun, going down behind the hilltop, gave the whole a touch of beauty. Yes, it was a natural picture, truly more beautiful than any artist will ever be able to put down on canvas. 

In the twilight, some crickets and a nightingale started to practice for their evening vocal performance. 

Joerg sat down by the roadside to enjoy all this beauty of nature while he was able. It was so peaceful around him that a sigh of praise and thanks went up to the

Creator-the Origin. 

Although absorbed with his surroundings, Joerg now could not help thinking of the coming war, as much as he disliked it. In a vision he saw another picture.

Again he saw his home town and its environs. In the east the sun was rising through a cloudy sky. In the west an army of soldiers was pushing towards town as they trampled over the fields of grain, which was awaiting the harvest instead, to be the food of men; a little behind them the big guns and cannons fired shell after shell on the town. Buildings crashed apart in a thousand pieces. The towers and factory chimneys went over. Houses and barns started to go up in flames. Some of the population was fleeing towards the east, with as much of their belongings as they were able to carry along.

Never should this happen to his home town, Joerg hoped. But he also truly wished that it should not happen to any people, anywhere. He was just a simple [28]

WAR! farm-hand, but a human being who had humane feelings for other human beings. 

Unable to find an answer for himself, Joerg got up, and as if afraid of something, hurried home. There 

Mrs. Hofer, or, as he called her, Anna, like so many other women of the fatherland that evening, was busy packing underwear, smoked meats, summer-sausage and a few other things such as tobacco, cigars and the like. Tomorrow, the second day of mobilization, Tony was to go, fight for the fatherland, protect his home and family.

Tony had a talk with Joerg, giving him some advice and begging him to look after things while he was gone. Surely that wouldn't be so long, they all say so.

At least, Tony hoped to be home again at the arrival of the awaited son. 

Joerg assured his master that he would try his best and wished him good luck, hoping to see him safe home soon again. Tony knew Joerg meant what he said, for he was a true and honest servant, which was a bit of comfort for Tony, as he had to leave his wife and property in the old man's care. 

Anna and Tony spent most of the night in wakefulness and love, realizing more and more, how dear and close they were to each other, and how much they really loved one another. No, neither Anna nor Tony was a coward, they both were ready to do their duty any time. Neither of them shed a tear, although they had to fight hard to keep them back, and tried to be cheerful as possible, so as not to make it more grievous for the other. [ 29 ]

The hour of departing was at hand. Tony heartily kissed good-bye to his little daughter, who didn't seem to understand what it was all about. Then Joerg drove Anna and Tony into town, where the latter joined the rest of the boys that were to leave with him, and who were gathered in front of the town hall. 

The Burgomaster gave a short address, after that three cheers to the Kaiser and the fatherland. Of course everybody fell in on that as loud as their voices permitted, accompanied by the town band. 

Then the men put up in lines. The commander of the fire brigade played the role of captain. The command, "Forward, march," band playing, and a small part of the German army started on its war campaign. 

Many relatives, friends and kids like myself went along to the station. Joerg and Anna in their carriage followed.

When we passed the soldiers' monument, which had been erected in memory of the soldiers that fought in the war of 1870-1871, all of the men saluted. That time forty-eight men of our town took part in the fighting, and only seven died on the battlefield. How many would stay out there this time? But never mind about that now.

The soldier in stone up there, in his right hand held a sword pointing downward, in his left the banner of victory flying over his head, his right foot put forward, it all representing a storming soldier and the symbol of victory. Yes, and those men passing by and saluting, in their minds too were marching on to victory. What enthusiasm they all had, even the kids. In six, eight [30]

WAR! weeks they would come back with the laurel crown on their heads, and a much finer and bigger monument would be built for them, in memory of the war of 1914. 

I don't know exactly why, but the general opinion was, the German armies would march right towards Paris, take France's capital, and the war would be over. 

Some men, especially the veterans of the war of 1870, felt quite sure about that. They thought this war would be only a little rabbit-hunt compared to 1870. 

For, the well-trained armies of the German Reich, with all those big guns, zeppelins and airplanes, would put a quick stop to that little quarrel that Michel and the Franzmann were having. Time was passing by pretty fast, and would have seemed more speedy, if it had not been for the yearning for the return home of the soldiers; because everybody in the home-front was very busy to keep the fieldfronts supplied, mainly with food and ammunition. [61]

Already the war had lasted long enough to take my eldest brother, which we did not expect in the beginning, and also long enough to give our small town a new war industry.

The building industry, that is home-building or factories not included in the war industry, in those days took a complete and long vacation. So did our brickkiln, which before the war employed many Italians.

So the mill certificate was invented, the aim of which was that the farmers should not hide so much; for if they could not get the grain milled, there was no use in hiding such. A household was allowed to get its quota of grain milled every month. The allowance for one person was two hundred grams of flour per day, which is twenty-five grams less than half an American pound. To get the grain milled, one had to get a certificate from the town clerk. The miller was not permitted to mill anything without this so-called mill certificate, or anything above the amount written down on it. Now, if the farmer, or anyone for that matter, could not get milled any above the amount of the certificate, there was no use to keep back or hide more than their own needs.

This mill certificate made it somewhat difficult, even for the average farmer, to have enough good bread to eat during the whole year, and bread was our chief nourishment. PRISONERS of war, especially Russians, were taken in large numbers. The German government couldn't build enough barracks to put them all just under a roof. A still more difficult question was, how and what to feed them, at least enough to keep them alive and avert disease.

In the country, in fact everywhere, help was needed very much. The prisoners were put to work wherever circumstances allowed it. The question to give every little town some prisoners, to work on the farm and in the shops, was not so very simple. More guards would be needed and there were not many to spare. During winter most of the prisoners worked for the town out in the woods, cutting timber, sawing and piling it up to fathoms, to be sold for firewood; others to go out to the front to build dugouts with. More timber had to be cut down for firewood than usual, for no coal was to be had. The little substitute that was used made a lot of smoke and very little heat. Lucky for the people, our town owned much woods and forests, and so we didn't have to pay quite such high prices as other towns and villages whose forests were mostly owned by some prince, count or the like. Every year each town citizen received a certain amount of wood at cost price, that is, the wages the town had to pay to get the timber cut. 

Although there was no coal to be had, the question of heating material was not the most difficult to be solved, even for poor people. There were always dry limbs falling, and plenty of dry wood lying around on the ground. This the people were allowed to pick up.

It was this thought that made one so indifferent.
