PREFACE 

In October, 1943, the New York Mayflower Society's letter of inquiry on American History Teaching to the heads of New York universities and colleges received considerable publicity in New York newspapers. 

During the following fall, 1944, the resultant report, or parts of it, was published by New York newspapers of over a million circulation. The report also received attention in college publications of New York and other states. 

The reason this publicity was sought was to create interest in, and give impetus to the teaching of American History on a college level.

THE LETTER OF INQUIRY TO THE HEADS OF NEW YORK UNIVERSITIES AND COLLEGES 

In June 1942 the New York Times disclosed the following highly discouraging information: 

"82 percent of the colleges and universities do not require the study of United States history for an undergraduate degree .... More than half of the students who leave our colleges are unaware of the historic background of our country. Thoughtful educators do not believe this wholesome for democratic traditions and ideals, now that we are engaged in a life and death struggle. 

Some college administrations have claimed in defense that United States history is of high school level and that teaching it in college would be a duplication. .... 

The majority of educators, however, feel that whatever American history may be studied in high schools, it is insufficient to carry over to college level. Colleges at present require various subjects. Some require 

English, others a foreign language and still others chemistry or mathematics. 

It is difficult to see how these are more valuable than our own history. 

The way the subject is taught is important: Charles Warren, President of the 

Harvard Alumni Association, gives this warning: 'Much of the teaching, especially in the schools, has too often tended to empty American history of its thrill, its dignity, and its significance.' This is a criticism that demands immediate analysis. ... In the opinion of American educational leaders, American history is far too significant, vital and valuable to be relegated to secondary schools. 

It deserves more than the present half-hearted support of colleges. We have every reason to be proud of the exciting and heroic history of the United States."

On April 4, 1943 the Times in its second survey of the teaching of United States history in colleges and universities, covering 7000 students in 36 universities, disclosed this equally discouraging information: 

"American college students are appallingly ignorant of even the most elementary aspects of United States history. It is not a matter of failing to recall obscure dates or of becoming entangled in insignificant details. Actually 

25 percent of these students did not know that Lincoln was President of the United States during the Civil War; 30 percent did not know that Woodrow Wilson was President during the first world war. In this bicentennial year of Thomas 

Jefferson, 84 percent could not cite two of the contributions made by this great American.

Even more distressing than the lack of information is the astounding amount of misinformation disclosed by the students. Many college freshmen haven't apparently the haziest notion what this country looks like. 

Portland, Ore. is placed on the Atlantic Ocean, Hudson River or Great Lakes.

Illinois, Texas, California, and North Dakota are listed as being on the 

Atlantic seaboard. Oregon, Mississippi, Wyoming and Nevada are cited as among the thirteen original colonies. Students are uninformed even on fundamental questions such as the freedoms guaranteed by the Bill of Rights. To many the 

Bill of Rights guarantees the right to work, to play, to happiness, or to choose one's own recreation. A large number believe it secures us against 'want' or 'fear'. How can anyone study American history and not know that the

Bill of Fights guarantees us freedom of speech, press, religion and assembly? 

United States history should be required of all college students. When the Times survey of last June disclosed that 82 percent of the colleges and universities do not require their students to take this subject, the argument was raised that the students are taught American history in the high schools, and therefore repeating it on the college level would be a waste of time. would now appear that this argument is fallacious." A REPORT ON THE SURVEY MADE BY THE SOCIETY OF MAYFLOWER DESCENDANTS IN THE STATE OF NEW YORK ON THE TEACHING OF AMERICAN HISTORY IN NEW YORK UNIVERSITIES AND COLLEGES COMPILED BY McCLURE MEREDITH HOWLAND, HISTORIAN 

For some time past there has been a feeling by responsible persons that American History was being neglected in the schools, colleges, and universities of the United States. Because of this, during the Autumn of 1943, the New York Society of Mayflower Descendants undertook a survey of the teaching of American History in the universities and colleges in this State. 

As its first step, the Society ascertained that American History is required in all New York grade schools and high schools. (Without doubt American History is required in most New York private schools.)

Then a letter, which included four questions, was sent to the heads of the universities and colleges. The four questions were: 1. 

Is American History required in your institution. 

2. What courses are taught. 3. 4. 

What texts are used.

If American History is not required now, will it be made compulsory in the near future.

Out of the forty-three universities and colleges polled, twenty-six replied. 

Of these twenty-six, only three required American History by itself, or coordinated with other more or less similar courses. Two others required this subject, if it had not been taken previously at school. Two more replied that this subject was required for entrance. And one institution required it for nearly all students. ly, eighteen institutions, the overwhelming majority, did not require American History before entering or during the college term. In the case of the students of the last four universities reported, the Mayflower Society feels that they certainly will have a basic knowledge of American History, but that their approach to it may not be particularly adult, for they have not taken the course on a college level. 

A contrast to the four institutions just mentioned is Union College. Dr. Dixon

States History is a requirement for nearly all the students entering Union College. 

Two courses are taught, one a general course in American History, from the early beginnings to the present, which is the general requirement, and the other, a course in the intellectual history of the United States, which is an elective." 

We now reach the replies of the largest group, those eighteen Universities which definitely do not require American History. President Constance Warren, of 

Sara Lawrence, writes that while she is in sympathy with the desire to make the best possible citizens of our college students, "however this College does not believe in required courses of any kind, because we are convinced that students who are obliged to take work against their will gain nothing from it." Vassar states that the subject of American History is covered by twelve elective courses. Skidmore College offers two courses, one in Colonial American History, and one in the intellectual only and source history of the United States. It is estimated that one-sixth of their students has elected one or the other of these two courses in a four year period. 

Nevertheless their President thinks that the faculty would not feel justified in making a course in American History compulsory. The University of Rochester replies that the tendency in American Colleges in recent years has been to limit the number of absolute prescriptions. Mother Thomas Aquinas, O.S.U., Dean of the College of New Rochelle, replies, "It's not likely that the subject will be made compulsory, for the simple reason that under the Regents of the State of New York for the secondary school, you will find the program very comprehensive. She adds, though that "voluntary enrollment in American History and in Government is increasing considerably in this institution."

The three following colleges, while not requiring American History now, may require it soon. Dr. H. N. Wright of City College of New York writes: "The City College has six courses in American History in its curriculum. Although these courses are not required of all students, a considerable percentage takes one or more of them, frequently students take as many as three or four of these courses. Moreover, in the basic course of World Civilization, which is required of all students, the relation of America to the rest of the world is stressed. Father Flynn, Regent of St. John's, informs us that well over ninety percent of their students have, either by direction or election, taken American History courses. Syracuse University, while concerned over the knowledge of American History of students in colleges and schools, is not convinced that a required course in American History is the solution. However, in normal times, approximately seventy-five percent of the students in the College of Liberal Arts take a course in American History, some time in their college careers. 

President Allen of Keuka College writes that he has "very little faith in the value of required courses. Such academic offerings are likely to defeat their own purpose if presented as an onerous burden. The pattern of education at Keuka College is purposely kept flexible in order that the needs of each individual student may be met."

Dr. Norwood, President of Alfred University feels that "there has been a tendency to abandon systematic study of any history, including American History, and to deal with just the last twenty years and current events, and a smattering of civics and sociology and economics under the title of citizenship." As far as Alfred University is concerned, Dr. Norwood doubts if there will be a required course in United 

States History in the near future. He further states that "there is a very good course required of all freshmen concerning world history from early to modern times, It deals to some extent with the transfer of civilization from the old world to the new."

As a result of this survey, the Society of Mayflower Descendants in the State of New York has come to the following conclusions:

Since American History is required in both New York grade and high schools, as previously stated, the pupils of this state get a good enough basic foundation in this subject. And in the colleges and universities, the courses offered at present, as well as most of the texts used, seem adequate for students at college level. The texts are by Morison, Commager, John D. Hicks, and other able Historians. The students also study original documents, such as the Constitution, and the writings of great men, such as Washington and Jefferson. And although courses in American History are required in very few of the Universities and Colleges, they can be taken by those students who are interested. In respect to this, we do not necessarily feel that this subject should be made compulsory. For when students have reached college age, they should certainly know what they want to study. It would be well to apply here the old but still pertinent proverb, "you can lead a horse to water, but you can't make him drink."

This Society, however, advocates that not only more emphasis be placed upon making courses in American History more interesting, but also upon drawing the students' attention to the interest, so that they will want to take this subject. And it ought to be ever borne in mind that these courses should be taught on a college level. 

Namely, stress must not be placed too much upon bare facts, but more upon trends, ideas, interpretation of facts, and outside reading. Furthermore, and we feel this is most important of all, there trends and interpretations should be presented by professors sympathetic to the fundamental American way of life.

We also recommend that more attention be paid to American History coordinated as a whole. This fascinating subject ought to be stressed from its start rather than the last twenty years, important as they may be. And civics, economics and other similar subjects should be blended into the overall historical scene from the beginning. For till the student grasps the comlete picture, he is in danger of not seeing the forest for the trees. 

And as a climax to his college career, the student might take a comprehensive course accenting the cultural heritage of the United States and our peoples' place in the History of Western Civilization. But it is essential that the part devoted to American History has its full share of the spotlight. For while it is true that our History cannot really be understood without an adequate knowledge of foreign countries, still there is always the danger that too much emphasis will be placed upon the past and present of other nations. Thus students lose sight of our own background and interpret events from a foreign point of view. 

Finally, this Society agrees with the Report of the Committee on American History in Schools and Colleges* that if college students, many of whom will be the leaders of the future, are well instructed in American History, they will escape on the one hand extreme optimism which keeps men from seeing the existence of a problem till it has become acute, and on the other, extreme pessimism, which leads to hasty and ill-considered action. And by so basing our theories and ideas on realism we will be far more apt to evade for a long time, if not forever, a repetition of the present worldwide suffering and slaughter.

October 1, 1944 "The Report of the Committee on American History in Schools and Colleges of the American Historical

AN ADDITION TO THE REPORT MADE BY THE NEW YORK MAYFLOWER SOCIETY ON THE TEACHING OF AMERICAN HISTORY IN NEW YORK UNIVERSITIES AND COLLEGES 

It has been ascertained, since the report of October 1st, that the ten New York State. Teachers'

Colleges require American History. In the case of these institutions, the New York Mayflower Society feels that the requirement of American History is most necessary. 

For certainly teachers must have an adult viewpoint on this vital subject.
