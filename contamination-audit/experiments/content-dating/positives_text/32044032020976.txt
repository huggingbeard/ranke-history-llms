GETTING AND KEEPING EMPLOYEES DURING WAR TIME 

Any examination of the experience of the Navy Department in demobilizing its war-time civilian personnel should include a glance at the reverse side of that process - the methods by which the Department recruited and held its civilian forces during the emergency. The necessity for reducing personnel after the war brought about a natural reversal in policy, a procedure which added to the trials of the Department in separating from its pay rolls employees whom it had once so ardently sought after and cleaved to. 

The movement for preparedness for war had become very strong in the nation by 1916. 

Even before that year the ship-building program of the Navy Department had taken on new life, and activity at the Department in Washington and throughout the naval establishment was on the ascendant, with a small but steady rise in employment. The Naval Appropriation Act of August 

29, 1916, gave tremendous impetus to the expansion of the civil force employed by the Navy Department. This act appropriated $312,678,000 for the Navy, an amount never before equaled in time of peace. It provided for an unprecedented construction program which required even greater increases in civilian force. The act also created the Naval Reserve Force, part of which was used by the Department for supplementing its clerical forces when civilian personnel was unavailable because of the demands of the military services, the expansion of civil forces of other Governmental agencies, and the general rise in industrial activity, and when Congressional appropriations did not supply funds adequate for increase in civilian employment. 

The coming of the war intensified and increased activities throughout the Department and the naval establishment and caused enormous expansion in the civil forces. This expansion continued throughout the duration of hostilities and reached its peak on the day the Armistice was signed. Efforts to obtain and hold adequate personnel consumed much of the thought and energies of responsible naval officials.

Personnel matters at this time were under the jurisdiction of the Assistant Secretary of the Navy. During the war and for a few years following this position was held by Franklin 

Delano Roosevelt, who, with his assistant, Louis McHenry Howe, took an active interest in matters relating to all classes of Navy Department employees. Policy and activities concerning the Department's employees were centralized in the Appointment Division, a part of the Secretary's Office, under the joint supervision of the Chief Clerk of the Department and the Chief of the Appointment Division. All appointments to positions in the Washington offices, and appointments to clerical and technical (IVB) positions in the entire establishment, were made in this Division. Appointments to the labor and mechanical forces in the establishment were made by the commanding officer of the activity concerned, the workers being certified by local labor boards.

Even before the entry of the United States into the war action was necessary to keep civilian employees in their Government jobs. The Army Reorganization Act of June 3, 1916, included among those exempted from militia duty "artificers and workmen employed at the armories, arsenals, and navy yards of the United States." On June 24, 1916, the Secretary of the Navy sent a telegram to navy yards and stations, to be posted on bulletin boards.1 This telegram urged the yard and station employees to remain at their jobs, saying that their service to their country in that capacity was as important as their military service, and "to cripple the navy yard at this time is a most unpatriotic act." Such appeals on the grounds of patriotism for workers to remain at their civilian jobs rather than enlist in the armed forces furnished an ironical note in the later demobilization of civil personnel, and civilian workers, protesting the preference given to returning veterans, made full use of these early exhortations. 

The Department continued to feel some agitation over the possibility that its force of skilled workers might be depleted by calls to military service or by enlistments. On July 11, 1916, Assistant Secretary Roosevelt sent out a circular letter in which he quoted a telegram from the Secretary of War authorizing the discharge from the National Guard of those members who were employees of Governmental departments or private concerns engaged on Army and 1

Navy contracts, the loss of whose services in a civilian capacity would be serious.2 The 

Assistant Secretary called for lists of such workers who were eligible for discharge under the conditions set forth in the telegram from the Secretary of War. 

Executive Order No. 2570, of April 2, 1917, exerted control over the transfers of workers by directing the Civil Service Commission to refuse examination or certification to persons except upon presentation of the written assent of the department or office in which they were employed. A later Executive Order, No. 2762, further strengthened these restrictions. Within the month following the declaration of war by the United States the Chief of Naval Operations in a letter to the Solicitor of the Navy Department stated that the Department was doing all it could to prevent skilled labor from enlisting.3 

But despite all these efforts, patriotic fervor continued to cause a steady number of enlistments among naval civilian employees. Desire for better jobs also caused many workers to resign and take jobs elsewhere. The flux and change in Federal personnel as a whole is shown in the following letter of July 15, 1918, addressed to the Secretary of the Navy by J. A. McIlhenny, president of the Civil Service Commission, who wrote with desperate pathos:4 

Reports received by the Commission from time to time show that approximately fifty per cent of those who are appointed to positions in Washington resign and return to their homes after a short period of service. A turnover of fifty per cent could not possibly be borne by any commercial enterprise. It is a serious drain on the resources of the 

Government in the present crisis. This situation is so grave that it seems to call for the special attention of the departments for such corrective measures as may be practicable. 

The Commission is not in a position to make definite suggestions as to the best method of remedying this situation. It believes, however, that if each department should designate to some competent authority the work of making careful inquiry into the cause of each separation, a step in the right direction will have been taken. Each person who wishes to separate from the service might be directed to report to the official appointed for this work and make explanations as to the reasons for his desire to separate. In many cases the employees might be persuaded to reconsider and, in some instances, the cause of dissatisfaction might be removed. At least, the causes for separation could be determined by this system.

The Commission's task of recruiting the civil service to meet war needs is a difficult one, under present conditions. The demand for workers of all kinds is greater than ever before in the history of the country. Through extraordinary recruiting efforts large numbers of applications are obtained. An alarming number of eligibles decline appointment. If the proportion of separations remains at fifty per cent or more, the Commission's problem must continue indefinitely.

As a result of this letter Secretary Daniels directed that persons in the Navy Department who wished to resign should, after conferring with the chief clerk, be sent to Mrs. L. R. Ernst, general secretary of the Navy Recreation League. The Department also resorted to a method less gentle than that of sympathetic "exit interviews" in its effort to hold on to its workers. On 

September 19, 1918, Secretary Daniels specifically directed that resignations be not approved.

If an employee insisted on resigning, his resignation was to be accepted, but he was to be informed that notation of his unpatriotic act would be made on his record for future reference. 

Raiding of labor forces continued in spite of regulations. Firms working on Navy contracts persisted in enticing workers away from other firms engaged on similar work. Complaints on this score came to the Department, which usually replied by informing the contractor making the raids that such action was not in accord with understandings agreed to by the Shipping Board and the War and Navy Departments. In October 1918 the Secretary addressed a circular letter to outside concerns engaged on Government work, stating that navy yards were prohibited from employing men from plants working on Government contracts without the written consent of the former employer, and that the Department expected the same cooperation from firms working for the Government.6 2

II 

WOMEN FILL THE CIVIL AND YEOMAN RANKS 

Besides making great effort to retain its civilian personnel, the Navy Department also exploited all possible sources for recruiting new personnel, turning especially to women for the necessary replacement or increase. The act of August 29, 1916, which established the Naval 

Reserve Force, did not specify the sex. of the citizens who were eligible for enrollment in the 

Force. Frior to 1924 the scale of pay for civil employees in the Navy Department was exceedingly low, and civilians were not eager to accept or retain clerical appointments when the war boom gave them opportunity for more lucrative employment elsewhere. Pay offered reservists, counting the subsistence allowance, was higher than that of the average clerical employee. Finding even before the war that its clerical needs could not be met by the Civil Service Commission with sufficient rapidity and in adequate number, the Department decided to enroll women as yeomen in the Reserve, assigning them to clerical duties. This expedient was looked upon with suspicion in some quarters. On April 14, 1917, the Army and Navy Register published the following editorial on the subject:

Misuse of Naval Reservists 

There is trouble ahead for the Navy Department unless there is a check on the tendency of the authorities to apply the legislature of the last Congress, creating a naval reserve, so as to increase the pay of civilian employees of the bureaus and at navy yards. 

Desirable as is an increase of pay for those who are working without regard to office hours and who are rendering valuable services at this time, it is assuredly, according to memhers of the naval committee at the Capitol, not in the line contemplated by the naval reserve legislation to appoint men and women as naval reservists in any of the ratings of the service and employ them in purely clerical capacities; especially when the method is utilized to increase the pay of civilian clerks. It is pointed out that such increases of pay should be done openly and by authority of Congress; otherwise the committees of the House and 

Senate having to do with items of expenditure in the legislative, executive, and judicial appropriation bill are apt to file remonstrances and perhaps enact legislation that will, in the end,seriously interfere with the provisions of the naval reserve legislation. 

The appearance of this editorial created a flurry in the Secretary's Office. Chiefs of bureaus were directed to investigate for any foundation of truth in the accusations made by the editorial. Mr. Daniels also called a meeting of the chiefs in his office on the afternoon of the day on which the editorial appeared.7 The results of these investigations and deliberations are not apparent in the records of the Department, but obviously no serious complications developed. 

The Marine Corps, though somewhat belatedly, decided to enroll women in its reserve for clerical duty. The Major General Commandant, on August 2, 1918, wrote to the Secretary of the 

Navy that the volume of clerical work at Marine Headquarters had increased four-fold, while the clerical force had only tripled.8 The number of civil service clerks allowed was fixed by law and no appreciable increase had been allowed since the declaration of war, so that the Corps had been forced to detail enlisted men to clerical duties. "The ratio of Civil Service clerks to the enlisted clerks is about four per cent," the Commandant wrote, presumably meaning that the number of civilian clerks was four per cent of the number of enlisted clerks. So far as had been possible, he stated, the Corps had detailed men who were not of draft age or who were disqualified by some physical defect, but work had reached such a volume that it had become necessary to use some men in office work who should have been available for field service. About forty per cent of the work could be performed equally well by women, although the Commandant believed more women than men would be needed to do the same amount of work. He therefore requested authority to enroll women in the Marine Corps Reserve for clerical duty at Headquarters and at other Marine Corps offices in the United States where their services might be utilized to replace men who were qualified for active field duty. The Secretary promptly granted the permission.

Of the women reservists Secretary Daniels wrote in his book, Our Navy at War: "These women yeomen, enlisting as reservists, served as translators, stenographers, clerks, typists, on recruiting duty, and with hospital units in France. Too much could not be said of their efficiency, loyalty, and patriotism. The Department sought the services of women also in its regular civil forces. On June 

13, 1917, the Secretary issued the following instructions:9

Until further notice, it is directed that, in submitting recommendations for the filling of vacancies in the clerical force, or for the appointment of additional clerical employees from civil service registers, such recommendations shall specify that requests for certifications be made for females.

A circular letter dated August 8, 1918, ordered cessation of the practice of detailing employees carried on the schedule of wages to clerical jobs in shops and offices, and directed that, on account of war conditions, women eligibles should be utilized as far as possible in order to release men for more urgent war work.10

In spite of the increase in the number of women employed throughout the Department and the establishment, the Department did not place females in positions of administrative responsibility. To a request from Martin A. Morrison, president of the Civil Service Commission, for a list of women holding executive positions or positions paying $2400 or more, Assistant Secretary Woodbury replied on January 7, 1921, that the Department had no women employees receiving $2400 a year or more, and that it had only one woman holding an executive position - Miss Sara W. Buchanan, clerk at $1700 a year, who performed executive duties in the Bureau of Yards and Docks in the absence of the head of her division.12 

Although even after the reductions of force following the Armistice the proportion of women in the civil clerical ranks continued high, most of the reduction program, and even some of the relevant legislation, provided that first reductions be made among women workers. During the reduction period women made no organized protest against being singled out in this unflattering manner, as did other special groups, but it is evident that various women's organizations were keeping a watchful eye on the fate of women in Government service. In June 1923

Miss Belle Sherwin, first vice president of the National League of Women Voters, wrote to Secretary Denby to express gratification over the passage of the Classification Act of 1923, which, she said, "establishes the principle of an efficient employment policy and of equal compensation for equal work, regardless of sex. Miss Sherwin went on to express the hope that the Department would include women in the membership of its committees on reallocation. Not Secretary 

Denby, but Assistant Secretary Theodore Roosevelt replied. With crisp brevity he acknowledged

Miss Sherwin's communication, but made no comment on her gratifications and hopes concern- 13 ing the status of women employees in the Department.

III THE DEMOBILIZATION BEGINS 

Although the naval construction program continued for several years after the end of the war, the close of hostilities brought about a cessation of a considerable amount of the work performed during the war at navy yards and stations. The cessation of this work resulted in a rapid tapering off of activities throughout the naval establishment. But to most of the Department's bureaus and offices in Washington the end of the war brought an actual increase in activities relating to the immense job of winding up the war. Naval personnel had to be demobilized by the Bureau of Navigation; business relating to veterans' claims and insurance had to be attended to by the Bureau of Navigation and the Bureau of Medicine and Surgery; surplus property had to be disposed of and financial matters managed by the Bureau of Supplies and Accounts; naval hospitals had to be built by the Bureau of Yards and Docks; fleets and men had to be realigned by the Office of the Chief of Naval Operations; disciplinary matters and legal affairs, increased by the demobilization, had to be administered by the Office of the Judge Advocate General; and the ships being constructed under the continuing construction program had to be built under the care of the Bureau of Engineering and the Bureau of Construction and Repair. It was to be expected, therefore, that personnel reductions would be more immediately evident in the naval establishment than in the Department in Washington. 

Officials of the Navy Department, as of the other agencies of the Government, appear to have been for months prior to the Armistice aware of the impending end of the war and of the possibility of immediate dislocation of civilian forces. But the Navy Department, except for mulling over the question of the necessity for the disenrolling of the Naval Reserve Force and the resulting loss of the yeomen engaged on clerical duty, seems to have given little thought to a post-war plan for reduction of personnel. Nevertheless, the end of the war brought immediate action from the Department concerning its civilian personnel. The first action taken did not call for any actual reduction in force, but it emphatically ordered the cessation of all increase. Two days after the signing of the Armistice, Assistant Secretary Roosevelt, in a circular letter addressed to chiefs of bureaus, boards, and offices, made the following pronouncement:14 

Due to the recent changes in war conditions, no further increase in force will be allowed. Commandants of naval districts, navy yards, naval stations, officers in charge of inspection offices, etc., have been notified to this effect and, also, that Sunday and overtime work shall be discontinued, except in cases of plant maintenance and extraordinary emergency under pre-war conditions. 

Feeble efforts were made by certain agencies to develop a general plan which would bring about a coordinated reduction in the number of civilian employees of the whole Government with a minimum disruption of Governmental functions and as little hardship as possible to the individual worker. One instance of this effort is the appointment by the War Labor Policies 

Board of a Committee of Personnel Officers, consisting of representatives from the War Labor 

Policies Board, Food Administration, Fuel Administration, United States Employment Service, Women in Industry Service, War Department, and Navy Department. 15 The Committee met on the afternoon of November 15, 1918, to consider the subject, "Demobilizing the Civilian Personnel of the Various Departments. The meeting was called as a result of a discussion by certain officials of the War Labor Policies Board earlier that same day, in which, according to the minutes of the Committee, "the need for coordination on the part of the various departments in releasing war workers was felt to be immediate." After discussing both the existing and the future situation regarding the release of workers, the Committee voted the following resolutions: I. That the Departments give an early notification to the appropriate central recruiting agencies before making any separations from the service and that they endeavor to bring the employee concerned in touch with these agencies.

II. That the earliest possible notice of intended separations be given the employees concerned.

III. That there be no publicity concerning these matters at the present time.

A broader and more definite policy than that provided for in Assistant Secretary Roosevelt's circular letter of November 13, 1918, was announced in another circular letter dated November 19, 1918.16 This directive did not call for any drastic cutting down of the force beyond that necessitated by lack of work because of cessation of war-time activities. It took cognizance of the right of returning veterans, under the Executive Order of July 18, 1918, to reinstatement, and implied that such reinstatement, in addition to lack of work, was a potential cause for discharge of employees. Other than the order for the discontinuance of any increase in civilian forces, the letter contained no mandatory provisions for reductions; it simply touched lightly upon two possible causes for discharge: lack of work, and reinstatement of veterans. More specific and forceful directions for reduction were given by Secretary Daniels in a circular letter of November 30, 1918, addressed to the Department in Washington.17 This letter ordered actual reduction to be made and called for the accomplishment of a cut of twenty-five per cent by the end of December 1918. It also ordered the release of as many naval reservists engaged on clerical duties as could be spared. And it informed the chiefs of bureaus and other officials of the Washington offices that similar orders had been telegraphed to commandants of all yards, stations, and districts., The explicit order for a reduction of twenty-five per cent was softened by Despatch 

No. 16303, of December 3, 1918, addressed to the naval establishment, which stated that the earlier order "should not be construed as meaning a general reduction of twenty-five per cent is mandatory but that steps must be taken to eliminate from technical, mechanical, drafting, clerical, messenger, and police forces, including naval reservists on civilian duties, those whose services may no longer be required on account of lack of work and those who have not measured up to the standard.' 

An Executive Order, issued on November 29, 1918, held out some hope of aid and comfort to civilian war workers separated because of reduction in forces. The Order directed that such separated workers who had been in the service less than three years, and who were recommended for further employment by the Government, should, upon request, be entered by the Civil Service Commission upon appropriate registers of eligibles for re-appointment. The Order recommended, but did not require, that Governmental agencies, in seeking new personnel, should request certifications from these reemployment registers whenever possible. 

Complete figures on the immediate reduction resulting from the Secretary's orders of November 30, 1918, are not available, but it is evident that the reduction program was actually put into action. On this development the December 1918 issue of The Federal Employee, official magazine of the National Federation of Federal Employees, commented:19 

The Navy Department has recently issued instructions to all navy yards and stations to reduce their civil force, a reduction of twenty-five per cent to be made before January 1, 1919. Information has been received at our national headquarters that certain administrative officers, construing these instructions as mandatory, have ordered a reduction to that extent. Representation has been made to Secretary Daniels on the part of the National Federation of Federal Employees that, in the interest of justice and the well-being of the civil service, reduction in force at the navy yards and stations should be made first from the reservist force, which was brought into the service to meet the war emergency. 

However generously the Federation may have interpreted orders for reduction in force, it is apparent that Congress expected real results and was prepared to do its part to help soften the blow to separated employees. Discussions were held by the legislators concerning the preparation of a special bill to provide for Federal responsibility for the payment of the railroad fare home of discharged Federal employees who had been employed in Washington, D. C., and information was sought to guide the Congress in deciding on the wisdom and practicability of this plan.

The Marine Corps reported that all its temporary employees were enlisted in the Marine Corps Reserve. The Office of the Judge Advocate General reported that none of its workers had been dismissed up to November 30, 1918; that, in fact, two vacancies existed because of resignations. 

In general it would be the policy of that Office to make dismissals in order of appointment, but the number of future dismissals was as yet undetermined. The Bureau of Ordnance gave no figures showing the number of separations made or contemplated, but simply stated that, according to the directions of the Secretary, it expected to make a reduction of twenty-five per cent by January 1, 1919, and planned even further reductions after that date. The Bureau had already accomplished a reduction of twenty per cent in its enlisted force. 

The Office of Naval Records and Library reported tersely and firmly that "no increase was made in the force of this office during the war and no decrease can be made without lowering the efficiency of the office." The Office of the Chief of Naval Operations stated that it intended to make practically all its reductions from the naval reservists. The Bureau of Medicine and Surgery did not plan any dismissals from its present civilian force during the current fiscal year, and the Bureau of Navigation likewise did not expect to dismiss any civil service employees in the near future. The Board of Inspection and Survey expected no immediate dismissals, as the work of the Board was increasing because of the continuance of the naval building program and the return of vessels from foreign waters. 

The questionnaire contained one significant inquiry which was answered only by the Bureau of Steam Engineering. Under the heading, "General Statement of Hardship Involved," the Bureau stated: "No case has yet come to the attention of the Bureau in which an employee has been compelled to ask for outside assistance. However, it is understood that a number of young ladies have been compelled to secure funds from home in order to return." Concerning such financial

embarrassments caused employees by dismissal from their jobs, no further evidence has been found among the records of the Navy Department. According to reminiscent memories of Government employees who worked in Washington at the time, and according to contemporary newspaper accounts, the dismissals did result in hardship and difficulties for many released Government employees, doubtless including some dismissed by the Navy Department. Many were enabled to get home only through the generous help of friends and fellow workers. The Washington, D. C., Evening Star ran the following story in its issue for November 25, 1918:

Many young women war workers recently discharged from various bureaus here are said to be stranded and without sufficient money to pay their fare back to their homes. 

This situation was made the subject of an open letter by the Washington committee of the 

National Women's Trade Union League to Felix Frankfurter, chairman of the War Labor Policies Board. 

The committee complains that the women were dismissed from their employment without sufficient notice and that they were not given sufficient time to put their affairs in order. It was stated that, while the government had announced its purpose to demobilize the military forces gradually, this plan was not followed with reference to the civilian employees.

Demand that the same consideration be given the civil employees as is accorded workers for private firms was made by the committee. It was stated that many of the young women had come from sections remote from Washington, had been obliged to pay excessive prices for the necessities of life, and were, therefore, without money to pay their way home.

The Federal Government made definite efforts to assist the discharged workers by paying their fare home or to another place of work, and by serving as an employment agency in their behalf. On December 2, 1918, the Secretary of the Navy transmitted to the various bureaus and offices of the Department a letter from N. A. Smyth, assistant director general of the United 

States Employment Service, requesting that employees whose services were no longer required be directed to file registration blanks at the office of the Service on Sixteenth and "Eye" Streets 21 in Washington; the Secretary directed the Department to comply with this request. An act of 

Congress, approved January 7, 1919, authorized the heads of executive departments and other Governmental agencies in the District of Columbia to furnish civilian employees receiving compensation of not more than $1400 or less than $100 a year, whose services were no longer needed, and whose employment terminated between November 11, 1918, and February 20, 1919, their railroad transportation, including sleeping car accommodation, from the District of Columbia to their homes or to other places of work. Expenses under this act were to be paid by the 

Navy Department from the appropriation, "Pay, Miscellaneous."

There can be no doubt that, in spite of continuing pressure of work and strenuous objection on the part of bureau chiefs and other officials, the Navy Department, upon the conclusion of the war, began an immediate reduction in force, although the effectiveness of the reduction is not immediately evident in statistical presentation because of certain complications. IV DISENROLLMENT OF THE YEOMAN RESERVISTS 

The detail of members of the Naval Reserve Force, both men and women, to clerical duties in the Navy Department and naval establishment, made it possible for the vast amount of necessary office work to be accomplished in spite of the fact that the Civil Service Commission was not able to meet the demands of all the various Governmental agencies for additional civilian personnel. Although these reservists should be considered as entirely distinct from the civilian workers, their detail to work ordinarily performed by civilians, and their later discharge from the service, had a great influence on the regular civilian personnel. Prior to the end of the war, the Bureau of Navigation foresaw the problems which would arise in the Department and establishment upon the disenrollment of the Naval Reserve, including the yeoman on clerical duty, and issued something of a warning in a communication dated August 8, 1918, addressed to the Chief of Naval Operations, chiefs of bureaus, and commandants of all naval districts:23 

Upon the conclusion of the war it will become necessary for the Bureau to relieve from active duty the vast majority of the Naval Reserve Force, and this will apply to the enrolled clerical force of the Department as well as to the force serving afloat. 

It is requested that careful consideration be given to this condition, as far as it concerns the personnel under your command, and that suggestions covering steps to meet the situation by legislation or otherwise be submitted to the Bureau of Navigation at as early a date as possible. 

On September 4, 1918, the Bureau of Navigation asked the Judge Advocate General whether existing laws would permit the retention of members of the Naval Reserve Force and Marine Corps Reserve who were employed on clerical duty, for as long as necessary to complete activities relating to the war, or to transfer to civil service status.24 No reply to this inquiry was found in the records of the Department, but on October 30, 1918, the Bureau of Navigation asked the Judge Advocate General if the act of August 29, 1916, which established the Naval Reserve Force, contained any mandatory provision for the disenrollment of the Force immediately upon the declaration by the President of the end of the national emergency.25 Within the week the 

Judge Advocate General replied, with comforting ambiguity, that there was no authority of law for the indefinite retention of members of the Naval Reserve Force after the termination of the present emergency, but that "it does not follow that all members of the Naval Reserve Force on active duty must, immediately upon the termination of the present national emergency, be instantly and in a body relieved from active duty. There is nothing in the law that demands such action, and it is unreasonable to suppose that the relief of all Naval Reserve members could be accomplished instantly without seriously disrupting the Navy and impairing its efficiency." The 

Judge Advocate General was of the opinion that six months might be regarded as a reasonable period within which to relieve from active duty all members of the Force. 

The Naval Appropriation Act of July 11, 1919, brought to an end the force of reservist yeomen that had played so important a part in the clerical work of the Department. This act required the disenrollment of female members, except nurses, of the Naval Reserve Force and the Marine Corps Reserve, and of all reservists detailed to duty ordinarily performed by civilians, not later than thirty days after the approval of the act. It also directed that no members of the Force should thereafter be detailed to active duty on shore of a kind which was ordinarily performed by civilians. The act provided for the transfer of these disenrolled reservists, at the discretion of the Secretary of the Navy, to temporary civil appointments, and it directed that members of the Force who accepted temporary appointment should be given "an opportunity to qualify by civil service examination for certification in accordance with civil service rules to fill such vacancies as may occur.' All temporary appointments made under these provisions were to terminate not later than June 30, 1920. For pay of transferred reservists the sum of $8,613,220 was appropriated. 11

This bill was introduced on June 12, 1919. The following day the Secretary of the Navy issued a circular letter in which he said that it had been brought to the attention of the Department that a large proportion of the naval reserve personnel did not wish to continue in the employ of the Department in a civil capacity at the rates of pay accorded civilians for work of the character on which they had been engaged as reservists.26 The Department expected many disenrolled reservists therefore to seek more remunerative employment elsewhere. The Secretary pointed out that the bill made no provision for filling the places of reservists who either did not wish to transfer to civil status or who failed in civil service examination to qualify, and he expected that this situation would cripple the clerical work of the Department, as, under the act, it would be impossible to fill the vacancies with appointees from the registers of the Civil Service Commission. Preparatory to the disenrollment and transfer of reservists, the Secretary requested each bureau, board, and office to make a careful survey of the situation and to submit a report showing the number of reservists employed, the number intending to transfer to civil capacity, and the number who would not transfer to civil status.

It soon appeared that the expectations of the Secretary that a large number of reservists would be unwilling to transfer to civil status were without foundation. So many reservists, in fact, reported their intention to transfer and continue in the service of the Department that complications arose concerning funds for their pay. A month after the approval of the act providing for the transfer of reservists on clerical duty, the Secretary found it necessary to announce in a circular letter an overobligation of $3,500,000 of the appropriation of $8,613,220 for "Pay, Reservists Transferred." Immediate steps, he said, would be necessary to wipe out this overobligation by drastic reduction of the force of reservists taken over in civilian capacity. It was hoped that the overobligation would be removed by normal separations based on the completion of certain work scheduled to be finished within a short time. The Secretary asked that careful consideration be given to the necessity for filling any vacancy, and that the efficiency and importance of the work of reservists in civil status be scrutinized.28 

The presence of reservists in the clerical force of the Department obscures and complicates statistics on the size and rate of reduction of the civilian force of the Navy Department. 

In many of its figures on the number of civilian employees, the Department made no distinction between civilians and yeomen, but lumped them all in one figure which, unless analyzed, would appear to indicate an increase in civilian clerical force during the war that is contrary to the facts. The post-war transfer of disenrolled yeomen to a civil status, completed eight months after the end of the war, resulted in an increase in the number of civilians employed in the clerical force of the Department. But at the same time the total clerical force, which before 

August 1, 1919, had consisted of both reservists and civilians, actually diminished. This diminution was brought about by the general reduction program of the Department and by the failure of some reservists to transfer, or, once transferred, to retain their new civil status. 

Statements of bureau chiefs included in the Annual Reports for 1919 emphasize this situation. 

The chief of the Bureau of Supplies and Accounts reported: "In general, however, there has been no slackening of effort to reduce the work and the organization to a normal NAVY basis, as is evidenced by the fact that the force in S. and A. has been reduced from a total of 1,821 employees 11 November 1918, consisting of 1,052 Reservists, 593 civilians and 176 officers, to a force of 818 to-day 778 civil service employees and 40 officers; thus making a net reduction of 53 per cent in the clerical force and 77 per cent in the number of officers on duty here. The chief of the Bureau of Yards and Docks stated: "The maximum personnel was reached on November 11, 1918, and was as follows: Nine naval officers, 15 reserve officers, 228 reservists, 352 technical employees, 134 clerks, messengers, laborers, and charwomen (exclusive of reservists). At the present time the bureau's force consists of the following: Nine naval officers, 4 reserve officers, 173 technical employees, 170 clerks (including reservists transferred), messengers, laborers, and charwomen. These figures indicate a reduction in the bureau's personnel since November 11, 1918, of 51.8 per cent."

During the economic depression which followed the end of the war, in the resulting intense competition for jobs, the disenrolled Yeoman (F) found herself in an ironic situation. Her right to veteran's preference was questioned by both the veteran returning from active combat duty and by the non-veteran civil employee of long years of service. To one such protestation against preferential privileges for women reservists, directed to Assistant Secretary Theodore 

Roosevelt by J. Allan McDonald, of Wilmington, Delaware, the Assistant Secretary replied:29 “You are advised that women enlisted in the Navy during the World War in the rating Yeoman (F) whose discharge was not dishonorable or for bad conduct are, under the Act of July 11, 1919, and the Executive Order of March 3rd, 1923, entitled to veteran preference to the same extent and in the same manner as men who served in the Army, Navy, or Marine Corps." 13

REDUCTION IN CIVIL FORCES 

Although the Navy Department reported a reduction of 3,724 in its civilian forces during the first two weeks of January 1919 - a number of considerable size - the Department continued to drive even harder on its retrenchment program during the months to follow. ALNAV 28, dated 

January 20, 1919, was emphatic in its call for reduction in force and economy in all expenditures throughout Washington headquarters and the entire naval establishment.30 The ALNAV opened with a strong and comprehensive statement of policy: "It is the policy of the Navy Department that the most rigid economy be practiced in the employment of personnel and purchase of material in all offices and bureaus of the Navy Department and of the Marine Corps in Washington and in all navy yards and stations in the naval establishment." Secretary Daniels made it forcefully clear that he expected his earlier directives regarding reduction in force to be, in his own words, "strictly carried out." No requisitions were to be approved by the chief of any bureau or the commandant of the Marine Corps until the absolute necessity for filling the position had been proved. The message ended: "Deficiencies of any kind are especially prohibited. Every effort must be made to turn into the Treasury the largest possible unobligated balance." 

Despite such vigorous exhortations from the Secretary of the Navy, statistics show that the total amount of reductions in civilian forces accomplished between November 11, 1918, and June 30, 1919, was exceedingly small. In his Annual Report for 1922 the Secretary gave the total civilian employment of the naval establishment at large on December 31, 1918, as 123,435, and indicated in a footnote that 15,000 naval reservists were included in that figure. Deducting the number of reservists, the figure for civilian employees in the naval establishment is 108,435. 

In the same tabulation the Secretary gave 118,233 as the total for the civil force employed in the naval establishment on June 30, 1919, and indicated in a footnote that this figure included 10,000 naval reservists. Deducting the number of reservists, the figure for civilian employees becomes 

108,233 a difference of only 202 between the figures given for December 31, 1918, and June 30, 1919. In contrast to these figures and to what one might normally expect because of additional work brought by the aftermath of war, reductions in civilian force at departmental headquarters at Washington were fairly extensive for the six months immediately following the end of the war. 

Figures compiled in December 1944 by the Statistical Section of the Division of Shore Establishments and Civilian Personnel of the Navy Department, give 2,592 as the total number of civilian employees in departmental offices in Washington (exclusive of the Marine Corps and Coast 

Guard) on November 11, 1918, and 2,211 for June 30, 1919 - a total reduction of 381 employees, or about fourteen per cent. 

Reductions continued in the offices and bureaus during the subsequent fiscal year, under pressure of curtailed appropriations, but total figures for the civil force of departmental offices in Washington show an actual increase in number of civilians employed. This increase was brought about by the transfer of reservists, between June 30, 1919, and June 30, 1920. The figures recently compiled in the Division of Shore Establishments and Civilian Personnel show that in the Washington offices there were 2,211 civilian employees on June 30, 1919, and 3,209 on June 30, 1920. In this same period, however, a considerable reduction, according to the Annual Report for 1922, appears to have been accomplished in the naval establishment: a decline from 108,233 civilian workers on June 30, 1919, to 87,312 on June 30, 1920 - a total reduction of almost 21,000 persons.

Other efforts at economy in personnel expenditures were made by the Department through halting promotions. On August 26, 1919, a circular letter from Acting Secretary Franklin

Roosevelt stated that, because of greatly curtailed appropriations, the Department had adopted the policy of making no promotions "except those incident to a vacancy occurring in a higher grade when the employees recommended for promotion take over the higher grade work."31 

This direction brought forth a number of protests from members of Congress, among them 

Ernest R. Ackerman, Isaac R. Sherwood, John B. Baker, F. H. Gillette, F. E. Doremus, and

James O'Connor. Other protests were received from officials of labor union locals. To all these protests Secretary Daniels replied with courtesy and firmness that, owing to the greatly curtailed appropriations made by Congress for the conduct of the work of the naval establishment 14

during the current year, the Department had been obliged to take the action indicated in the circular letter.

Organized labor did not stand idle during the Department's activities in discharging civilian employees, especially when the results of the Disarmament Conference seemed to threaten deeper and more extensive personnel cuts. In February 1922 Samuel Gompers, president of the 

American Federation of Labor, accompanied by other officials and representatives of the Feder,ation and of the International Association of Machinists, called on Secretary Denby and discussed with him the effect of the Department's reduction policy on workers at navy yards and stations. 

On departing, Mr. Gompers left with the Secretary an undated and unsigned memorandum, the tone of which is summed up in its statement, "We support with all our strength, in accordance with long established principles, the disarmament proposals which have been written into treaty form, but we hope that it will not be necessary to visit this blessing to all humanity upon the heads of those who have rendered such excellent service to our government and to our people as a punishment." Besides presenting objections to a general reduction program, the memorandum made a number of definite and constructive suggestions through which work could be assured for employees of the Department at its shore establishments. Something of a public works program was proposed, calling for such activities as (1) concentration on the maintenance, conversion, reconditioning, modernizing, and modification of existing naval craft and equipment; (2) allocation to navy yards of all activities preparatory to the dismantling and scrapping of those vessels slated for disposal; (3) construction at navy yards of new vessels already authorized and of the new vessels which would be required as a result of the revised naval building program resulting from the Washington Naval Conference; (4) establishment of an agency in the 

Navy Department which would actively seek work from other Federal agencies for naval industrial plants; and (5) performance of all work of maintenance, reconditioning, and new construction of vessels for the Emergency Fleet Corporation, Light House Bureau, and Harbor Board.3 

32 In reply to this memorandum Secretary Denby wrote a lengthy letter to Mr. Gompers, taking up the proposals point by point, showing that some of the activities suggested were already being carried on by the Department, and giving clear reasons why others were either impractical or impossible.

For several years following the Armistice the Department continued to feel pressed and harassed by reduced appropriations. On October 25, 1919, the Secretary informed chiefs of bureaus and offices that vacancies which existed or which might occur were not to be filled unless the authority of the Department in writing were first secured, adding, "and this authority will not be granted unless good and sufficient reasons are presented for filling such vacancies." The overobligation of the appropriation, "Pay, Reservists Transferred," and of nearly every other appropriation of the Navy Department required this action. The Secretary directed that selection for reduction in force be first made from among transferred reservists who had not qualified under civil service rules for probational appointment.33 The decline in the number of former reservists reported as employed in the civil force of the Washington offices - 

2,290 on September 30, 1919, and 1,109 on June 30, 1920 seems to indicate that drastic cuts were made among the former reservists. 

Another group singled out in the reduction program was the aliens employed by the Government after a suspension of the civil service rule requiring United States citizenship of applicants for Government service. On June 13, 1921, ALNAV 50 was issued, calling for reduction on the rolls through the separation of all probational or absolute aliens whenever citizen eligibles or qualified citizen temporary appointees became available.34 This order met with the approval of the American Federation of Labor, although the Federation would have preferred more drastic action, and at its forty-first annual convention held in June 1921 passed a resolution recommending that all so-called friendly aliens in the employ of the Government be called on immediately to qualify for citizenship, and that all who failed to do so be summarily dismissed, 35

The Admiral went on to report that he had unearthed a lieutenant commander who for a considerable time had had as his sole duty activities as secretary and treasurer of the Navy Mutual Aid Society. The Admiral had arranged with the Bureau of Navigation to have this officer detached from the Bureau of Supplies and Accounts. During all this painful time of civilian reductions, the Department of Labor stood ready and eager to come to the aid of the discharged civil servants of the Government. On April 24, 1920, W. B. Wilson, Secretary of Labor, wrote to the Secretary of the Navy that his Department understood that lack of funds would require the Navy Department to discontinue the services of an extensive number of its clerical force before July 1.37 "If our understanding is correct," wrote

The Navy Department, in spite of its determination to reduce force, was itself also concerned with the welfare of its employees, and strove to follow humanitarian principles in its procedure of selecting individuals for dismissal. On June 10, 1921 

Assistant Secretary Theodore Roosevelt wrote to commandants of navy yards and naval stations: "On account of the reduction in appropriations it will be unquestionably necessary to cut down our working forces in the various yards. For the above reasons the Department desires that the commandant will carefully review each case before discharges are made effective and will, whenever possible under the regulations, decide in favor of retaining those men who have families or other dependents.

"9 Despite cries from bureau chiefs that reductions were crippling the ability of their staffs to perform efficiently and quickly the increased functions brought by the aftermath of war, Congress continued to draw tight the purse strings and to keep a controlling hand on the size of Navy Department civil personnel. Early in 1920 Assistant Secretary Franklin Roosevelt took full advantage of an opportunity to lay on some good blows against this practice of Congress. 

Replying to a letter from Clifford Ireland, dated April 5, 1920, asking for suggestions for means of placing the Governmental establishment on a more modern, scientific, and businesslike basis, for the purpose of increasing efficiency and decreasing appropriations, Assistant Secretary 

Roosevelt replied:39

First you ask me, "Could any reorganization or reform be introduced in your Department, through legislation or otherwise, tending to increase its efficiency and decrease appropriations by the establishment of more modern business methods?" The answer to that is primarily this: The entire system of relationship which exists between Congress and the Executive Departments is fundamentally wrong. I made an offer this spring to the Appropriations Committees of both the House and Senate, telling them I would tomorrow discharge fifteen per cent of the employees of the Navy Department if they, the Committees, would give me complete authority to take one half of the salaries of the employees so discharged and add this one half to the salaries of the other eighty-five per cent of the employees still left in the Department. In other words, I would guarantee a monetary saving to the Government of the pay of seven and one-half per cent of the employees of the Navy Department if I could use the other seven and one-half per cent where it would do the most good. Of course, however, 38 16

under the present system Congress would not think of giving executive discretion of this kind.

Congress, for various reasons, has so tied the hands of the executive officers of the Government that they have no discretion in the fundamental questions of employment. I do not believe, of course, that the Civil Service System should be wiped out or that we should return to the spoils sytem, but there is altogether too much assumption in this Government that executive officers will use their authority for political purposes. My own wonder is that, considering the existing circumstances, the employees of the Government are as efficient as they actually are. As conditions exist, Congress legislates for every little minute item of employment. It tells me that I can have in a certain office, for instance, ten clerks, of which one can get $1,800 per year; two can get $1,600 per year; two can get $1400 per year; two can get $1,200 per year; two can get $1,000 per year; and one can be a messenger at $720 per year. In the first place, the scale of pay of employees is entirely inadequate, especially so at the top and the bottom. In the second place, the needs of different offices vary very much. One office may require a large number of low-grade clerks, another office may require a small number of highly-paid specialists. We ought to have better pay and a system of promotion based on efficiency and not necessarily longevity of service or on hard and fast individual rules laid down by appropriations bills from year to year.

One of the first steps to improve matters would be to enact into law the recommendations, or at least most of them, made by the Reclassification Committee of the House and Senate which has already made its report. I do not agree with everything in the report, of course, but on the whole it is a step in the right direction. 

About three years after Mr. Roosevelt had written this letter, the Classification Act was passed, putting into effect several of the reforms so effectively recommended by the Assistant Secretary, and bringing great improvement to the lot of the average Government employee. And beginning with the Naval Appropriation Act for the fiscal year 1924, Congress ceased the practice of legislating for specific numbers and salaries of clerks allowed individual offices. 

Vigorous protests were made annually by officials in the Department over the continued reduction in forces. The Bureau of Navigation was swamped with work incident to the wholesale disenrollment of the Naval Reserve Force and the general demobilization of naval forces, and with work for the Veterans' Bureau relating to service records of veterans of the European War. 

The Bureau of Yards and Docks was busy with the planning and construction of naval hospitals for the veterans. The Bureau of Medicine and Surgery was deluged with the receipt and filing of medical records from the naval districts and with work in connection with claims of ex-service men under the War Risk Insurance Act. Work in other bureaus had also increased, and the reduced forces of many of the operating units of the Department were inadequate for keeping up with the mounting work. To carry the Department through this critical situation various emergency measures were taken. Funds were allotted by the Veterans' Bureau for clerks to be engaged in the Bureau of Navigation and the Bureau of Medicine and Surgery on work exclusively for the Veterans' Bureau. For its work of furnishing the adjutant generals of the various States with the service records of the citizens of those States who had been in naval service during the war, a special appropriation for the necessary additional personnel was made by Congress. 

The Veterans' Bureau allotted 35 clerks to the Bureau of Yards and Docks for work relating to the construction of naval hospitals. By special permission of Congress, the Bureau of Navigation was permitted to use enlisted personnel in clerical work. 

The high rate of turnover in civilian personnel which existed during the war conunued throughout the early 1920's, and with dismissals and resignations, the force was in a steady state of flux. It was hoped by bureau chiefs that one cause of the turnover, that of low pay, would be eliminated by the passage of the Classification Act and the increase in salaries of the civil employees. Throughout all the busy travail of the Navy Department, the size of its civilian force continued to shrink. VI 

SPREADING THE WORK 

The industrial depression following the war brought added difficulties and complications to the Department in its effort to reduce its civil forces. Because of the tremendous size of the forces employed at navy yards and naval stations, as compared to the force working in the departmental offices in Washington, these difficulties and complications are especially apparent in connection with the reduction of forces at the shore establishments. The Department was reluctant to add to the serious unemployment situation by throwing additional thousands out of work, but with an ungenerous Congress and an administration committed to economy, the Department found itself in an uncomfortable devil-and-deep-blue-sea position. A partial answer to the dilemma was to spread the work by shortening the work week and by the discontinuance of overtime work. 

On the day the Armistice was signed Secretary of the Navy Daniels conferred with Secretary of War Baker and Chairman Hurley of the Shipping Board, and the three agreed that all overtime and Sunday work on Government contracts should stop immediately. This decision was announced at once in the newspapers, and in Despatch No. 13311 the Secretary of the Navy on November 11, 1918, sent the following order to commandants of naval establishments:40 "Discontinue immediately all work in excess of eight hours per diem except such as may be necessary due to emergency conditions as obtained prior to declaration of war. Also discontinue overtime work on part of electrical, drafting, inspection, messenger force, etc." Two days later another order was issued calling for the immediate stoppage of all Sunday and overtime work throughout the Navy Department and naval establishment. The order for the discontinuance of the seven-day week was repeated in a circular letter dated December 

21, 1918,42

The six-day week continued in effect in the naval establishment until September 16, 1920, when, urged by labor organizations and organizations of Federal employees, and goaded by the inescapable necessity to retrench, the Department instituted a work week of five and a half days.43 The work week was shortened stili further on July 14, 1921, upon the issuance of ALNAVSTA 3, calling for a five-day week by the closing of navy yards, naval stations, and offices throughout the service on Saturday.44 The offices of the Department in Washington were excepted from this order and therefore they continued on the six-day week, with fourhour Saturdays during the summer months. Cacophonous comments poured into the Department in response to the order for the five-day week, the majority protesting, the minority trying to shout down the majority. Dissident opinion came from many officials in the naval establishment itself, who pointed out the impossibility of a blanket application of the ruling. 

What, they asked, of hospitals? of laundries? of station libraries? of the surveillance activities of agents of the Office of Naval Intelligence? of the Central Sales Organization at the Washington Navy Yard, where Saturday closing hours would interfere with one of the best days for the sale of surplus property? In response to some of these protests Assistant 

Secretary Theodore Roosevelt made exception to the ruling; in other cases, such as that of the civil Intelligence agents, he recommended staggering the hours. 

The commandant of the Boston navy yard asked for, and was refused, permission to keep his power-plant force on a seven-day week. The commandant of the New York navy yard requested resumption of the six-day week for all his force.45 Prior to July 1, 1921, he wrote, his total yard roll had been about 10,000. Successive discharges had reduced the force to about half that number. Pursuant to instructions the men recently discharged had been given to understand that these reductions were permanent and that it would be to their advantage to seek employment elsewhere. The commandant advocated the return to the six-day week because wage reductions, discharges, and reductions in working days presented a serious problem to the average employee, and he felt that the welfare and contentment of the 5,000 employees then on the rolls merited serious consideration. The Department was unmoved by this plea. 19

Members of Congress, sensitive to the appeals of constituents stung to protest by cuts in earnings, wrote to the Secretary inquiring about the necessity for the five-day week. 

Congressman Clark Burdick, of Rhode Island, suggested that draftsmen and clerks at the Newport Naval Torpedo Station be returned to a six-day week, and that the station's furloughed employees be reinstated for a five-day week.46 To this the Department replied that the fiveday week had been instituted to save a day's work for each employee and to permit the retention of a certain number of employees who under the six-day week would have had to be discharged because of the shortage of appropriations. Work at the Newport station did not warrant any exception to the ruling.

Congressman Burdick wrote again, this time to ask for the reinstatement of the six-day week at the Naval War College and Naval Training Station at Newport, indicating that Congress had made specific appropriation for those stations sufficient to finance the longer work week.47 

Assistant Secretary Theodore Roosevelt replied virtuously and in the spirit of the time that no exception could be made in these cases, "as it must be remembered that the present administration is committed to a policy of rigid economy. All existing appropriations are to be considered the maximum amounts that may be expended, and any sums which may be saved revert to the Treasury for the general good of the country's finances, because of the burden of taxation upon the people, and the fact that the expenditures of the Government are far in excess of its income."

Representative John H. Smithwick, of Florida, expressed a particular interest in the welfare and happiness of the employees of the Naval Air Station at Pensacola, and requested, because of the approach of Christmas, that they be given full-time work during December in order to be able to provide more effectively for a merry Christmas for their families.48 

In his reply to this sentimental appeal, the Assistant Secretary dryly presented the economic and financial bases of the Department's decision for the five-day week, and stated that there was no justification for making any exception at Pensacola. 

Labor unions, for the most part, expressed great dissatisfaction with the shortened work week, although opinions among employees themselves conflicted. Employees at the naval stations at Cavite and Olongapo transmitted through their commandant a petition for exemption from the five-day week, on the ground that employees at those stations had not been granted increases in pay allowed employees in the continental United States, yet their rents and living costs had continued to increase.49 Secretary Denby refused the petition. 

In May 1922 the shop committees of outside shipfitters, shipfitters inside, gas welders and helpers at Puget Sound conveyed to their commandant their disapproval of the intention of the Bremerton Chamber of Commerce, as reported in the local press, to launch a movement for the return of the six-day week.50 Employees of the clerical, drafting, inspection, and messenger forces at the Key West Station asked for a return to the six-day week because of low pay, increased living costs, and large numbers of resignations, and because private corporations had not caused any reduction in pay of their employees doing similar work.51

Toward the end of 1922 employment conditions throughout the country appeared to improve.

The yards began to experience difficulty in obtaining the necessary skilled labor for their operations. VII THE RETURNING VETERAN AND HIS JOB - REINSTATEMENT 

One of the fundamental problems of the demobilized veteran of the first World War was to get back his job and, once he had it, to keep it. On both these points the civilian employee of the Federal Government who had been in the military service of his country in that war was given tremendous aid through executive directives and statutory regulations. Later stagnation in the industrial life of the nation, and the long-continued need for reduction in the civilian forces of the Government, to a considerable extent circumvented the efforts of the President and of Congress and undermined the economic security of the returned members of the fighting forces.

Efforts of the Government in behalf of veterans of the war with Germany who were former Government employees developed along three lines: (1) restoration to the job he had left to enter the armed service; (2) special rights to reinstatement in the classified civil service; and (3) special rights to retention of his job during reduction in force. 

Early evidence of special consideration to be given former Federal employees who had fought in the first World War is to be found in Executive Order No. 2917, issued on July 18, 1918, which stated: "A person leaving the classified civil service to engage in the military or naval service of the Government during the present war with Germany and who has been honorably discharged may be reinstated in the civil service at any time within five years after his discharge, provided that at the time of reinstatement he has the required fitness to perform the duties of the position to which reinstatement is sought." 

Demobilization of naval forces began promptly after the Armistice, and even before the Armistice members of the service were discharged for various reasons. Within the month following the end of the war inquiries from veterans concerning their status began to raise problems for the Navy Department. On November 16, 1918, the commandant of the Thirteenth

Naval District asked for information on this point.56 A week later the commandant at the 

Mare Island navy yard reported that he had received a number of such inquiries from exclerical employees.56 In some cases, he said, the positions had already been filled by men or women, and therefore no vacancy existed in the office in which the veteran had formerly been employed. The commandant requested a statement of the Department's policy for reinstating former employees in accordance with the Executive Order of July 18, 1918. 

Assistant Secretary Franklin Roosevelt, on December 11, 1918, wrote to the commandant of the Thirteenth Naval District and to the commandant of the Mare Island yard, giving instructions to cover this situation.56 Employees who had resigned to enter the military or naval service of the United States in the war against Germany were to be reinstated to any existing vacancies or to any additional positions that might be authorized. If no vacancy existed at the time of the request for reinstatement, favorable action was not to be taken until such a vacancy occurred or a new position should be authorized. Mr. Roosevelt called attention to the Department's Despatch No. 16303, of December 3, 1918, which directed the elimination of those employees who had not met standards of efficiency. 57 On December 9, 1918, the United States Employment Service telegraphed from Camp 

Upton, New York, to the Secretary of the Navy: "What provisions is your department making for reinstatement of honorably discharged soldiers who resigned civil positions to enter the army? "58Assistant Secretary Roosevelt wired back: "Under Executive Order July 18 Navy

Department hopes to be able to provide for reinstatement of employees who entered military or naval service to such vacancies as may occur from time to time."58 

It is evident that during the first few months of the influx of demobilized veterans into the economic life of the nation the Navy Department, strongly conscious of the permissive 22.

quality of Executive Order No. 2917, felt no mandatory obligation to reinstate immediately the returning veteran, and considered as prerequisites for reinstatement the existence of a vacancy and an efficiency rating favorable to the veteran.

In late December 1918, the Navy Department asked the Civil Service Commission for information as to the procedure to be followed, under the Executive Order of July 18, 1918, in the reinstatement of employees in the navy yard service who had been employed through labor boards and who had resigned to enter the armed services.59 The following January 

John A. McIlhenny, president of the Civil Service Commission, replied with a full statement of a procedure based on recommendations of W. D. Bergman, chief of the Appointment Division of the Navy Department, H. L. Fisher, secretary of the Fourth Civil Service District, and other employees of the Commission who had a knowledge of navy yard regulations.59 

In summary, this procedure provided that, under the Executive Order cited, a former navy yard employee, classified or unclassified under Groups I (unskilled laborers), II (apprentices, helpers, and other semi-skilled laborers), or III (skilled or journeyman tradesmen), of the navy yard regulations, who had left his work to enter the military or naval service, could be immediately reinstated to his former position or appropriate position if honorably discharged from that service. A reexamination should be made by the yard physician in cases where the discharge certificate did not show the veteran to be physically fit, in order to ascertain his physical condition. 

The procedure provided that, upon receipt of the request of the former employee for reinstatement, the labor board should make inquiry of the heads of yard departments to determine whether his services were needed for immediate employment, and, if not, whether it was desired that his name be placed on a reinstatement list to be certified when men were needed in the particular trade. If this inquiry indicated an immediate need for the services of the veteran, he should be reemployed at once. If there were no immediate need for his services, his name should be entered on a reinstatement list containing names of veterans for whose services there was no immediate need, in a relative order according to marks given when separated from the navy yard service, or, in cases of equality in such marks, according to date of original appointment. Certification was to be made from the reinstatement lists before employees were drawn from the furlough, preference, or general lists. If, because of the separation marks of a former employee seeking reinstatement, the yard departments did not request either his immediate reinstatement or the entry of his name on the reinstatement list, the reemployment of such a veteran should be governed by the general navy yard regulations, or he should be required to file application and compete in a new examination. 

A former employee could be reinstated at a yard other than that from which he had separated, but the labor board at the yard at which reinstatement was requested was either to require the discharge card from the yard from which the applicant was separated, or otherwise to verify the employee's record at the yard of former employment. Reinstatement under the 

Executive Order, in the opinion of the Commission, was subject to Section IX of the civil service law, limiting the number of members of the same family in the classified service. 

This procedure, as set forth by the Civil Service Commission, almost wholly vitiated any effect the Executive Order might have had on immediate reinstatement of veterans, as it imposed certain conditions of need for the veteran's service and qualifications of efficiency upon which the reinstatement depended. But the procedure was accepted by the Navy Department and was promulgated in a circular letter of January 21, 1919, to commandants and industrial managers of navy yards and naval stations, inspectors of ordnance in charge, and other like officials.59 No mention was made in the procedure or in the circular letter concerning the creation of a vacancy, where none existed, by the discharge of the non-veteran incumbent, in order to make possible the reinstatement of the veteran. But that possibility, with certain considerations of relative efficiency, was presented in a circular letter of January 27, 1919, addressed to certain administrative officials of the departmental Washington offices and of the naval establishment.59 This circular letter directed that reinstatement of employees in the navy yard service under Groups I, II, and III should be made to fill either a vacancy or an additional position created through an increase in force necessitated by 23

additional authorized work. The letter stated. "When no increase in force is necessary and no vacancy exists, consideration shall be given to the efficiency of the members of the force and, if found that any member of the force is less efficient than the applicant, his services shall be terminated in order to provide the necessary vacancy. In cases of equal efficiency as between a member of the force and the applicant for reinstatment, preference shall be given to the employee returning from military or naval service." The same procedure was directed to be applied also to requests of veterans for reinstatement in the clerical, technical, drafting, messenger, and police force.

According to instructions from the Civil Service Commission passed on to the Washington offices and the naval establishment in a circular letter of February 17, 1919, the reinstatement of a veteran into the clerical, technical, drafting, messenger, and police forces was only temporary until certification of reinstatement had been issued by the Commission following the requisition by the Department for such certificate59This instruction was later modified following an opinion of the Attorney General pronounced the following April.

The Navy Department, in its early procedures, attempted to fulfill the provisions of the Executive Order of July 18, 1918, without creating any increase in civil forces except on the basis of additional authorized work, and without lowering the standards of efficiency. A change in this early procedure was brought about by the mandatory wording of an act approved on February 25, 1919, which provided that "all former Government employees who have been drafted or enlisted in the military service of the United States in the war with Germany shall be reinstated on application to their former positions, if they have received an honorable discharge and are qualified to perform the duties of the position." Directions based upon this statutory order were promulgated throughout the entire Navy Department and naval establishment, in a circular letter59 of the Secretary of the Navy dated March 7, 1919, which amended the earlier directions regarding relative efficiency of returning veterans and existing forces by stating categorically that all former employees who had left to serve in the war with Germany and who applied for reinstatement under the provisions of the Executive Order were to be reinstated immediately. The circular made no mention of a method for reducing the resulting increase in force, but an unsigned memorandum59 for the Secretary of the Navy, presumably originating with Assistant Secretary Franklin D. Roosevelt, accompanying a draft of the circular letter, stated that consideration of the relative efficiency of the personnel in order to effect a reduction in force should not be taken up until a period of sixty days had elapsed, and then only in the event that the increase in force caused by reinstatements had not been absorbed into the existing organization. 

Although there are no statistics available to substantiate the supposition, it is logical to assume that this step was taken because of the high rate of turnover in personnel, on the subject of which considerable plaintive comment appears in the annual reports from bureau chiefs for the years immediately following the end of the war. By reinstating the veteran at once, without the imposition of its own requirements relating to vacancies and relative efficiency, the Department was acting in obedience to the law of the land; and by using any surplus thus created in one part of the organization to fill vacancies in the other parts, it was also maintaining its own working strength.

The circular letter of March 7, 1919, also directed that each bureau, navy yard, board, or office should provide reinstatement for its own former employees only. . The letter further called for the assignment of the reinstated veteran to the particular position and rate of pay held by him prior to leaving to enter the military or naval service unless, because of any reorganization of the shop or office that might meanwhile have been effected, the particular position occupied by the veteran before leaving no longer existed. 

The unsigned memorandum submitted to the Secretary of the Navy in connection with this circular letter called his attention to the fact that a certain proportion of employees requesting reinstatement were not old employees who had served the Department for a number of years, but were newcomers who had come into its employ as war workers during emergency conditions. For this reason it was not advisable to tie the heads of departments and offices too rigidly to the requirement for placing reinstated employees in the exact positions they had 24

occupied before entering the armed services, as the tremendous expansion resulting from the war had considerably changed the organization of the Department and of the naval establishment, and a great many positions which existed prior to the war, or at its beginning, no longer existed.

Apparently the provisions of the act of February 25, 1919, for the restoration of Government jobs to veterans were not sufficiently clear, as the Civil Service Commission requested clarification on certain points from the Attorney General, who gave an opinion on April 11, 1919. One of the questions asked was whether the act required the reinstatement of persons who had served in Government positions only temporarily, and who had had no permanent status in the civil service prior to their entrance into military service. On this question the 

Attorney General held that a person so employed was no doubt a former Government employee within the meaning of the act and that, therefore, he must be reinstated if he fulfilled the requirements stipulated in the act. The Attorney General further held that upon reinstatement the former temporary employee should occupy the same status that he had occupied at the time of his entrance into military service; that his appointment should continue as long as necessary to make a permanent appointment through certification of eligibles; and that he must be removed within thirty days after receipt by the appointing officer of the Civil Service Commission's certificate, unless the Commission either authorized his retention in the service beyond that time, or unless he could within that time qualify himself for permanent appointment. 

In this ruling the Attorney General also reversed the opinion expressed by the Civil Service Commission the preceding January, by holding that Section IX of the civil service act, restricting the number of members of the same family eligible for appointment to the classified service, applied only to the original appointment and not to the reinstatement of a veteran. 

The Attorney General also held that the provision for reinstatement applied only to those who had been drafted or had enlisted in the military service as privates, or those who had enlisted for the purpose of entering officers' training camps and had afterward been commissioned; but he held that it did not apply to those who had been commissioned directly from civil life without any previous enlistment. 

Certain fine points in terminology were also clarified by the Attorney General. Because the provision for the reinstatement of veterans had been included in that portion of the act which concerned solely the War Department, and because the words "military service" had been used, the question was raised whether persons returning from naval service were covered. The Attorney General ruled that the term "military service" was used in its broad sense and applied to the entire military establishment, including the Navy and the Marine Corps as well as the Army.

In the opinion of the Attorney General, the provision for reinstatement did not apply to every veteran who at any time might have been an employee of the Government, but only to those who had enlisted or been drafted while in the Federal civil service. 

This opinion of the Attorney General was transmitted to the Secretary of the Navy by 

Martin A. Morrison, president of the Civil Service Commission, on May 19, 1919.59 Because of the mandatory provision of the act the Commission withdrew its earlier requirement that the Department secure authority from the Commission before reinstating former employees, providing that the reinstatement were to the same grade formerly held by the veteran. 

Commission pointed out that the act provided only for reinstatement to the position formerly held by the applicant, and that in any case where reinstatement in some other position was desired, prior authority of the Commission should be obtained. The Another aspect of the special status accorded the returning veteran who had formerly been in Government service, in addition to the right of restoration of former jobs, was the special preference for appointment to any part of the competitive classified service within five years from the date of discharge from military service. This right was enunciated in the Executive 

Order of July 18, 1918. The preference was broadened in a rider attached to the Census Act of March 3, 1919, which extended the preference to widows of veterans who were qualified to hold the positions. Furthermore, this act did not limit such preference to veterans of the first 25

World War whose civil service had been interrupted by their entry into military service. The provisions of the act of March 3, 1919, were repeated in a clause of the Deficiency Act of July 11, 1919, which also extended the preference to qualified wives of disabled veterans. An Executive Order of April 16, 1919, revoked Executive Order No. 2917 and amended civil service rule IX. Under its provisions a veteran formerly in the employ of the Government, although not in such employment immediately preceding his entrance into military service, and therefore not entitled to claim the right of restoration to his position under the act of February 25, 1919, was eligible for reinstatement to an appropriate position in the discretion of the head of the same department or office, if requisition for his services were made within five years of the date of his discharge from military service.

Such were the statutory, executive, and administrative mandates and directives followed by the Navy Department in the reinstatment and reemployment of its former employees returning home from military service. No statistics are available from either the Navy Department or the Civil Service Commission to show the number of veterans reinstated by the Department, but the existence of a body of correspondence and directives on the subject of the reemployment of veterans is a clear indication of considerable activity in this connection. In spite of the many and various directives in behalf of special privileges for veterans, it appears that there was a great deal of dissatisfaction and complaint among veterans and friends of veterans. 

Representative Ernest R. Ackerman, for example, wrote to the President of the United States that he had read an article in the issue of the New York Evening World for July 1, 1919, which recounted the difficulties returning soldiers were experiencing in their efforts to be reinstated in their former positions in Government departments in Washington.60 According to this article, in many instances the men had found it necessary to accept positions other than those which they had left, and frequently at lower ratings. Ackerman suggested that the President correct the situation by executive order.

Copies of Representative Ackerman's letter were transmitted by the President to heads of the various agencies in Washington. In response, Secretary Daniels wrote directly to Ackerman, enclosing a copy of his circular letter of March 7, 1919, which, he stated, set forth the attitude of the Navy Department relative to the reinstatement of its former employees return- 60 ing from the war.

The statutory and executive requirements covering the reinstatement of veterans complicated and made more difficult the efforts of the Navy Department to cut its forces down to the limits imposed by curtailed legislative appropriations. To fulfill the requirements laid down in the statutes and executive orders, the Department could not escape the necessity of separating a considerable number of workers who had no claim to the special preference accorded veterans. One result of this situation was the endangering of the efficiency of the civil forces, but the Department found ways of protecting itself from this highly undesirable possibility. 26

VIII THE REINSTATED VETERAN AND REDUCTION IN FORCE 

For more than four years following the end of the first World War, the veteran's only special privilege for retention of his job during reduction in force was a somewhat frayed heritage from post-Civil War times when Congress, in its usual frenzy of post-war economy, was striving to deflate the swollen civil forces of the Government and at the same time demonstrate in a practical way the nation's gratitude toward its returned soldiers and sailors. The Appropriation 

Act of August 15, 1876, contained a provision which directed that heads of departments, in making any reduction in force, should "retain those persons who may be equally qualified who have been honorably discharged from the military or naval service of the United States, and the widows and orphans of deceased soldiers and sailors." The requisites concerning relative efficiency, so vaguely implied in this statute, were made a little more definite in the Appropriation Act of August 23, 1912, which directed the Civil Service Commission to establish a system of efficiency ratings for the classified service. and specified that "in the event of reductions being made in the force in any of the executive departments no honorably discharged soldier or sailor whose record in said department is rated good shall be discharged or dropped or reduced in rank or salary. It was held by the Navy Department that the effect of the provision of this 1912 law for the retention of veterans whose rating was good was nullified by the fact that neither the Civil Service Commission nor the Bureau of Efficiency (the latter had been given responsibility in the matter in 1916) had issued a system of efficiency rating.61 This interpretation left only the Act of August 15, 1876, as a binding directive governing the retention of veterans during reduction in force. Until 1923, in accordance with this act the Navy Department gave preference to veterans during reductions only on the basis of their having an efficiency rating equal or superior to that of non-veterans. Despatch No. 100019, dated July 19, 1919, sent by the Secretary to the whole Department and naval establishment, called attention to the provisions of the 1876 statute relative to discharges, and ordered that employees who had served in the military or naval forces should be given preference in retention provided their efficiency equaled that of other employees.62

The extensive amount of statutory and executive directives concerning their reinstatement in the civil service of the Government naturally resulted, among veterans and their friends, in confusing preference in reinstatement with preference in retention. When lack of work and diminished appropriations forced the Department to discharge employees, including reinstated veterans of low efficiency, many protests arose and the charge was made that veterans were being denied their legal rights. In March 1920 Representative Patrick H. Kelley wrote to Assistant Secretary of the Navy Franklin Roosevelt concerning complaints he had received that ex-service men at the Norfolk yard were not being given the preference provided them by law.63 

Mr. Kelley cited the Deficiency Act of July 11, 1919, and wrote that, contrary to its provisions for veterans' preference, the navy yard officials at Norfolk held that ex-service men were entitled to preference in retention only when their efficiency equaled or excelled that of other members of the force. The Representative expressed the opinion that this amounted to no preference at all, and he asked Mr. Roosevelt to have the situation looked into and to advise him on the matter. 

A few days later the Assistant Secretary wrote Mr. Kelley a lengthy letter setting forth the legal authority for the Department's action in discharging veterans.63 He pointed out that the act of 

July 11, 1919, pertained to preference only in the matter of appointment and did not mention any special privilege in retention. He then quoted the provisions of the Appropriation Act of 

August 15, 1876, and stated that, in the absence of further legislation, the Department could grant preference in retention to veterans only in case of equal efficiency. The letter ended with the firm statement, "Ex-service men are now receiving all preference to which they are entitled under existing law, as regards the Navy Department and naval establishment. 

Because of the number of protests received concerning the discharge of veterans, Secretary Daniels on May 27, 1920, asked the Attorney General for an opinion on veterans' 27

preference in connection with reduction in force, and set forth the practice followed by the Department of the Navy. The Attorney General replied that the Department's interpretation of existing law was correct, and that preference in retention should under the law be given only those veterans whose efficiency equaled that of other members of the force.64 

Puzzling details arose in connection with the discharge of veteran, and Assistant Secretary Roosevelt asked the Civil Service Commission for answers to certain specific questions. 55 

If an employee, he asked, who had been reinstated under the act of February 25, 1919, or the Executive Order of April 16, 1919, resigned for reasons of his own, was he still eligible for reinstatement during the prescribed period? The Commission answered that the original reinstatement had fulfilled the law, and that if an employee voluntarily relinquished it, further reinstatement would fall under the regular provisions of civil service rules. 

The Assistant Secretary also presented the following special problem: Employees in

Groups I, II, and III, reinstated under the act of February 25, 1919, or the Executive Order of April 16, 1919, might be discharged at their own request or for cause, or on account of reduction in force or lack of funds. Under labor board regulations such employees rated "very good" could be entered on furlough lists for reinstatement; those with less than "very good" would be required to file another application for employment. The Assistant Secretary wanted to know whether, in the event of the discharge of employees for cause or because of reduction, they were eligible, under the statute or Executive Order cited, for reinstatement again within five years. 

To this question the Commission replied that, whether the separation were voluntary, for cause, or because of reduction in force, the law had been fulfilled upon the original reinstatement of the employee, and further reemployment would have to follow normal civil service procedure. 28

IX 

EFFICIENCY RATINGS AND REDUCTION IN FORCE 

Charges of unfairness and favoritism continued against the Department's program for reduction, and many of its discharged employees, both veterans and non-veterans, vociferously complained that they had been separated while persons of less efficiency had been retained. 

The Department, in investigating these charges, became acutely aware of the lack of uniformity in its efficiency-rating system, especially in the systems used in the field for rating members of Groups I, II, and III, and it therefore undertook a survey of its current practices as a step toward the establishment of a uniform system. 

In the fall of 1919 boards were established at twenty of the largest navy yards and shore stations, charged with the responsibility of making a report on the existing system and recommendations for improvements. 66 A board was appointed in January 1920 to consider the recommendations from the several yards and stations and to devise a uniform system to be established for grading all employees of the navy yard service, exclusive of those in Group IV. The 

Board made its report the following March, expressing the opinion that it was impracticable to institute such a system in the navy yard service or elsewhere. The board bolstered this opinion by stating that it had not been able to find any such system in operation in private con-* cerns.

Commenting on the report of the board, Charles Piozet, acting chief of the Appointment Division, wrote on March 11, 1920, in a memorandum to Louis McHenry Howe, Mr. Roosevelt's assistant: "In this connection it may be stated that the necessity for an efficiency marking system in private industrial plants is not as apparent as under the Government, where, because of the scheme of employment and appeal to Congressmen, Senators, and others for the revocation of discharge means a burden thrown upon the Department in adjudicating such complaints, the Department feels there is a real necessity for the establishment of such a system for its own convenience and protection, in order that charges of favoritism on the part of supervisors in selecting employees for discharge be eliminated."66 Mr. Piozet suggested that the report be returned to the board with directions to formulate a simple efficiency marking system, adding, "If necessary, as a last recourse, I believe I could formulate a workable system. Harassed by a rising sense of the lack of efficiency in its forces, the Department sought to derive some benefit from the reductions forced upon it by lack of funds by seeing to it that the least efficient workers were the first to be eliminated. On January 28, 1920, the Assistant 

Secretary issued a circular letter to the entire Department and naval establishment in which he frankly stated his recognition of the fact that the enormous expansion in civil forces 67 brought on by the war had lowered the efficiency and general morale of the force. He called for the preparation of a report which would indicate in general the relative efficiency of the force then employed with that if the force employed immediately prior to the declaration of war. Points of comparison should be tardiness, interest in and application to work, punctuality and attendance, and so forth. "The Department desires, wrote the Assistant Secretary, "to eliminate from the service employees who do not measure up to a standard, and by the application of such disciplinary measures as may be made in individual cases, attempt to restore efficiency and morale." 

Executive Order No. 3567, dated October 24, 1921, directed the Bureau of Efficiency to prescribe a system of efficiency ratings for employees in the clerical service of the Government, and to fix rating standards for promotions and dismissals. The order ended: "In cases of reductions in the number of employees on account of insufficient funds or otherwise, necessary demotions and dismissals shall be made in order, beginning with the employees having the lowest ratings in each class, but honorably discharged soldiers and sailors whose ratings are good shall be given preference in selecting employees for retention." As with the provisions of the law of August 23, 1912, the provisions of this Executive Order governing 29

retention of veterans were considered by the Navy Department to be without force and effect because of the failure of the responsible agency to prescribe and promulgate a system of efficiency ratings, and the Navy Department continued to act under the provisions of the law of August 15, 1876, dismissing veterans when their efficiency was not equal to that of other members of the force. 68

Strong protestations against the dismissal of veterans continued to come fo the Department from veterans' organizations, individual veterans, and members of Congress. On July 8, 1921, in response to a letter from Warren S. Fisher, National Commander of the United American 

War Veterans, Assistant Secretary Theodore Roosevelt wrote, "The attitude of the Navy concerning veterans' preference in employment is being taken up at a Cabinet meeting today. I will have you advised of the ruling when a definite determination is reached."69 No notification to Fisher by the Assistant Secretary has been found among the records of the Department, but it is evident that the President was at least willing to go along with the veterans in giving them effective preference in retention. The direction for the retention of veterans in reduction in force was repeated in Executive Order No. 3801, of March 3, 1923: "In harmony with statutory provisions, when reductions are being made in the force, in any part of the classified service, no employee entitled to military preference in appointment shall be discharged or dropped or reduced in rank or salary if his record is good." Although no system for rating the efficiency of Government employees had as yet been formally promulgated by the Bureau of Efficiency, the Navy Department did not on that ground, as it had with the statute of August 23, 1912, and the Executive Order of October 24, 1921, consider the provisions of Executive Order No. 3801 

as nullified; instead it interpreted that Order as placing in full force and effect the statute of

reasonable and consonant with the rights of other citizens and the efficiency and reliability of the yard forces. "It is contrary to the spirit of our American Institutions," wrote Mr. Alifas, "to create a super-citizen or preferred class of citizens. If our country is indebted to our veterans in a material way, and we believe such to be the case, the debt should be paid by all the people in the form of a bonus or grant for which the people as a whole could pay. 

Mr. Alifas went on to plead the case of the non-veteran by citing earlier official pronouncements and actions showing the equal value of the civilian worker with that of the man in the armed service in times of national emergency: the exemption of the navy yard worker from militia service by the Army Reorganization Act of 1916; the message of the Secretary of the 

Navy in 1916 exhorting navy yard workers to remain in their civilian jobs, saying that they were as important to their country in that capacity as soldiers in the field; the Secretary's order in 

1916 directing that resignations be not approved, and only accepted with notation of unpatriotic action; and the provisions of the Selective Service law under which draft boards released thousands of navy yard employees because their service as navy-yard workmen was of greater importance to the nation than their service as draftees. In the light of these earlier evaluations of the importance of the civilian worker during critical war conditions, was not some consider ation due him after the end of the war?

On April 6, 1923, the commandant of the navy yard at Puget Sound wrote to the Assistant Secretary of the Navy, calling his attention to the gradual decrease in efficiency that would result from the retention of less efficient veterans in preference to skilled non-veterans.72 

Captain C. P. Plunkett, commandant of the New York navy yard, on July 11, 1923, recommended the extension of the provisions of Executive Order No. 3801 to cover the war-time civilian worker at the yard, adding an idea suggestive of a national-service law: "Experience in the last war, and the general concensus of opinion, points to the fact that, in the next war, all men of proper military age will be drafted for service under the Colors, and very probably most of the men now employed in this yard, and some of those who were here during the last war, will · still find themselves doing the same work for the Government in the yard, but wearing the uniform of their country though not actually serving in the military forces at the front, and will, therefore, at the end of the war, be veterans and entitled to the privileges set forth in the 

Executive Order of 3 March 1923." 73 

Rear Admiral L. R. de Steiguer, commandant at the Boston navy yard, on September 19, 1923, communicated to the Secretary of the Navy his grave questioning of the wisdom of the Executive Order of March 3, 1923, saying that the backbone of the yard force consisted of men of long service who had no veteran's preference. 73 The application of the provisions of the 

Executive Order, he said, would result in a reduction of efficiency in the yard through the discharge of men of higher efficiency and the retention of men of lower efficiency who had military preference. Admiral de Steiguer recommended the extension of the Order to cover civilian employees who had been employed in naval or military establishments during the war with Germany and any previous wars, and who were still employed. 

The executive committee of the National Association of United States Civil Service Employ ees at Navy Yards and Stations presented a plea for equal rights between veterans and nonveterans. 74

To this letter, as in substance to all letters of similar content, the Assistant Secretary replied that the matter was not within the discretion of the Navy Department. The 

Executive Order had been issued by the President of the United States; the Order was in accordance with existing laws, and it applied not to the Navy Department alone but to all Governmental departments.

Department's efforts to retain skilled and competent workers, as it raised the standards higher than those of the previous Navy system. FURLOUGHING OF EMPLOYEES 

Under the new efficiency-rating system the Navy Department continued its effort to retain the most efficient workers. The majority of those who were separated, because of the force of the various statutes and Executive Orders, were non-veterans, but employees with veterans' preference were also included among those who were discharged or furloughed. By means of the furlough the Department was able to rid its rolls of members of low relative efficiency, yet to keep within the letter of the law. Section 69(a) of Civil Service Commission Form 2009 provided for furloughs up to thirty days and for selection for furloughs to be made on the basis of relative efficiency. As this Form made no mention of military preference, and as the 

Executive Order prescribing retention of veterans made no mention of furloughs, the Department concluded that it was free during periods of reduction to furlough the less efficient members of the force, including veterans. 

The furloughing of employees, however, was not introduced as a device for removing specially privileged employees whose efficiency ratings were low. During the first extensive reductions in force following the end of the war, the Department had been besieged with requests from labor organizations that employees be furloughed instead of discharged. In response to these appeals, on September 4, 1919, the Secretary issued a circular letter stating that furloughing was contrary to the desire of the Department. 79 "However," he conceded, "in consideration of the present state of unrest and industrial conditions generally obtaining during the reconstruction period following the conclusion of hostilities, upon recommendation of the Commandant, Industrial Manager or other officer in charge, the Department will consider authorizing the furlough employees for a period in excess of the fifteen days provided for in Article 321(61) (a), U. S. Naval Instructions, 19137 whose conduct and workmanship have proved 'very good' or better, provided there is reasonable prospect of securing additional funds or work as the case may be, in order that those employees whose services it may be necessary to dispense with temporarily would be within immediate reach upon resumption of activities without going through the process of requisition and certification by the Labor Board." 

The furloughing of veterans brought the usual protests, this time from ex-service men and their organizations, and also from their representatives in the House and Senate. The 

Department's reply to these protests was a patient explanation of the occasional need to stop work temporarily because of such reasons as non-delivery or lack of materials, lack of funds, weather conditions, and various administrative considerations.80 Furloughs were made, the 

Department explained, according to Navy Regulations and in order of relative efficiency; the 

Executive Order of March 3, 1923, was not violated by this practice, since the Order related to discharges, and furloughed men were not discharged but were carried on the pay rolls in a non-pay status. This specious reasoning brought cold comfort to the veterans, whom the 

Department continued to feel free to furlough until an opinion of the Attorney General stopped the practice in 1929.

In that year the President of the United States asked the Attorney General for an opinion on whether Executive Order No. 5068, of March 2, 1929, forbade the furloughing of employees entitled to veterans' preference whose ratings were good or better. The Attorney General was of the opinion that the Order did forbid the furloughing of such employees.81 

Sufficient time has now elapsed to give a clear perspective to a study of the experience of the Navy Department in demobilizing its civilian forces after the first World War. The complete record of that experience, however, is imbedded in the extensive and imperfect archives of the Department. For this study, the user of those sources has had to follow somewhat the same practice as that of the anthropologist who makes his reconstruction from a bone here and a bone there. Nevertheless the digging has revealed a clear outline of the Department's struggle against the many forces naturally aroused in a complex society by actions which so 34

strongly affect both public funds and private earnings. And the findings show that the Department did succeed in reducing the number of its civilian employees, and continued the reduction until the 1930's,when the groundswells of the coming hurricane were felt, and the Navy again began to prepare itself for war. 35
