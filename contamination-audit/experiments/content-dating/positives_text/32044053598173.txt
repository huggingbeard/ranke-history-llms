REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE ON SERVICE OF PUBLIC UTILITIES TO THE NATIONAL ASSOCIATION OF RAILROAD AND UTILITIES COMMISSIONERS: 

It is a pleasure to submit to the members of the N.A.R.U.C. this 

Committee's report on the electric, telephone, water and gas industries covering the calendar year 1962. It covers four principal sections; namely, service and facilities, operations, research and safety. Again, the industry statistics show a definite trend in the direction of better, more efficient service for the consumer public at the lowest possible cost through continued growth and the increased use of modern equipment and laboratory research. The electrical industry in 1962 served 1.2 million more customers than they did in the previous calendar year, bringing electrical service to 98 per cent of the nation's occupied rural and urban homes. Likewise, the telephone industry increased their service by more than 3.5 million in 1962, bringing to 80 per cent of the country's total having telephone service. In 1962 the water utility industry served 140 million people in 22,000 communities through 18,500 utilities. The gas industry likewise continued to show record gains in all departments for the calendar year 1962. More detailed factual information on each of these particular utility industries will be found throughout the pages of this report, together with the latest graphs and photographs of recent industry developments.

Your Committee respectifully submits this, their 1963 report, and hopes that the following sections of the report will be of some help and assistance for those who have occasion to refer to it:

Telstar II 

Telstar II, an almost identical but improved version of its famous. predecessor, was launched into space from Cape Canaveral on May 7, 1963, to provide further satellite communications experiments. An important objective of the new Telstar is to learn how to extend the useful life of communications satellites in space by minimizing or overcoming the effects of radiation.

Launched for the Bell System by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, it rode into space aboard an improved DELTA rocket which placed Telstar II in a higher orbit than Telstar I where it will encounter less radiation.

This higher orbit, along with some changes in the satellite itself, should help overcome the effects of radiation that disabled the first Telstar. 

Telstar II's apogee (furthest distance from earth) is 6,713 miles almost twice that of the first Telstar. 

As with the first Telstar, all technical information is being made available to NASA, to the Communications Satellite Corporation and to the scientific community in general. 

Basically Same in Appearance

Telstar II is basically the same in appearance and equipment as its predecessor, although it weighs nearly 175 pounds in comparison with the 170-pound Telstar I. The new Telstar weighs slightly more because of modifications within the satellite.

Telstar II is capable of sending its telemetry reports on the same microwave beacon frequency that is used for precision tracking of both Telstar satellites. This is something which would be desirable in a commercial satellite communications system.

Telstar II Has Longer Orbit 

With a perigee (point of orbit nearest the earth) of some 604 miles and an apogee of about 6,713, Telstar II's orbit takes about 225 minutes. compared to 158 minutes for Telstar I. This higher apogee provides longer mutual visibility between the Bell System's ground station at Andover, Maine and the ground stations in Europe. It also will give some mutual visibility between the Andover site and Japan, where ground stations are now under construction. 

Ground stations in England, France and Italy are participating again in the experiments, and by the end of the year, the West German station near Munich is also expected to be in operation.

Experiments are planned between Andover and Japan after the first Japanese ground station near Tokyo is completed. The International 

Telephone and Telegraph Company's ground station near Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, will be able to receive telephone, data and facsimile signals from 

Telstar II. 

Telstar is the only project where a private company has built and paid for satellites with its own funds. Under a cooperative agreement, NASA launched the satellites for A. T. & T. NASA was reimbursed by 

A. T. & T. for tracking services and launching expenses, including the cost of the DELTA rocket.

New Switching Network Developed for Government Agencies 

A new nationwide communications network which features economy, flexibility and reliability of operation has been developed for the Federal Government. It is known as the Federal Telecommunications System. It is a private switching system engineered to consolidate the communications requirements of civilian agencies of the Federal Government that are widely dispersed in distance but closely related in function and interests.

At the time of its initial cutover on February 15, 1963-less than a year from the time when it was first ordered-it linked more than 400 PBX's in 42 cities through four switching centers. The system was requested by the Government's General Services Administration. 

A major attraction of this type of system was attainment of a fast, efficient network for day-to-day communications needs, which would also be survivable-an advantage critically important in times of emergency when vital lines of communications must be maintained. For although the system serves civil agencies, many of the agencies would be called upon to perform essential work in event of national crisis. 

The complete plan for the system envisions the eventual inter-connection of practically all Federal Government agencies, and is divided into two phases. Phase I includes those agencies located in 42 major metropolitan centers and is designed to handle voice communications. Approximately 65% of the agencies' long distance traffic will travel over this private network. Eventually some 2,500 government offices in 355 cities will have access to the network. 

Europe Now Only a Dial Tone Away 

Overseas operators in the United States and England began dialing direct to telephones across the Atlantic on March 27, 1963. A few days later, direct operator dialing was extended to telephone calling between

the United States and West Germany, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. Later in 1963, this service will be extended to France, Italy and 

Switzerland. This forward step in intercontinental telephony means faster service for trans-Atlantic callers, and more economical use of the ocean cable systems. It is also a step toward the eventual development of a worldwide direct distance dialing network.

Telephones in foreign countries may, of course, be answered by persons who speak languages other than English, but this barrier has been overcome. To help American operators surmount any language difficulties, an arrangement has been provided to transmit a "language" digit in dialing each overseas call. Then, if the American operator needs help, she presses a key which activates a signal carried by the "language" digit. 

This brings in a specially trained foreign operator to assist on the call. 

The dialing system now used between the U. S. and Europe has been agreed upon for use between the United States and Australia, Japan and New Zealand. It will be introduced for service between the United States and Australia late in 1963, when North America and Australia are linked by a cable of the British Commonwealth countries. Operator dialing with 

Japan will follow in 1964 upon completion of a cable between Hawaii and Japan.

Other energetic deep-sea cable plans are now possible with the completion of the first commercial cable-laying ship to sail under the United States flag, C. S. Long Lines. During the next three years she will place nearly 18,000 miles of deep-sea cable in Atlantic, Caribbean and Pacific waters.

Deep-Sea Cable Linking Florida and Panama Completed 

A new deep-sea telephone cable between Jamaica, W. I., and the 

Panama Canal Zone was placed in service in April, 1963. It is part of an underseas network planned for Central and South American points, and is linked to the Florida-Jamaica cable completed in February, 1963. Linking of the two cables provides high-quality voice paths between the U. S. and some 45,000 telephones on the Isthmus of Panama. 

Service over the new communications route was highlighted by calls from government officials in Washington, D. C. to representatives in 

Panama and in the Canal Zone. The $25,000,000 system is a joint undertaking of A. T. & T., its subsidiary, Transoceanic Communications, Inc. and the International Telephone and Telegraph System. The cable extends 1,450 miles from Florida via Kingston, Jamaica, to Port Sherman in the Canal Zone.

The newly completed route consists of a single cable capable of transmitting 128 simultaneous conversations. The cable is armorless in the deep-sea section and gets its strength from a core of spiraling steel wires. 

Spliced into the cable every 20 miles are torpedo-shaped amplifiers which contain directional filters to permit two-way transmission over a single cable.

Safety 

The 1962 Communications Industry lost time injury rate was .98 (Lost time work injuries per million hours worked). The rate for all industries reporting to the National Safety Council was 6.19. For 1961 the Communications rate was .93 compared to 5.99 for all industries. 

The .67 rate of the Bell Telephone Companies was the same for 1961 and 1962.

Off-duty accidents continue to be a major concern to the telephone industry and much attention is being focused on employee safety at home as well as at work. In this area, motor vehicle safety is especially important. In 1962, off-duty motor vehicle accidents took the lives of 72 

Bell System employees, while other injuries were responsible for the loss of the lives of 50 others.

During 1962, all Bell System safety publications were reviewed and plans made for their revision in 1963 to provide a more complete coverage for the promotion of on and off-the-job accident prevention and reporting. Representatives of each of the Bell Telephone Companies met in regional conferences during 1962 to develop improvd accident prevention measures.

ELECTRIC UTILITY INDUSTRY (Excluding Alaska and Hawaii) Service and Facilities 

The electric industry continued its record-making growth in 1962, advancing to new levels of power-producing capability. During the year in which the American economy again moved forward, residential and industrial utilization of electricity resulted in new records of service to the nation's life and economy.

At the close of 1962, the electric industry was serving more than 6.1 million customers, which was approximately 1.2 million more than in 

1961. Today, more than 98 per cent of the nation's occupied urban and rural homes are connected for electric service. 

Electricity's contribution to better living continues to gain wide acceptance. The steady increase in the use of electricity in the home reflects the utilization of an ever-expanding range of electrical appliances and increased, widespread popularity of home heating and air conditioning. 

In 1962 the average annual use per residential customer was 4,257 kilowatt-hours, an increase of 241 kilowatt-hours over the 1961 average use. 

During the past ten years, average annual use per residential customer has increased 2,071 kilowatt-hours, which increase is more than the total annual usage per residential customer during the year 1951. 

As a result of increased consumption of electricity and continued improvements in operating efficiency, the industry has been consistently able to reduce the average cost per kilowatt-hour to the customer. Average revenue per kilowatt-hour sold to residential customers during 1962 was 2.414, as compared to 2.45¢ in 1961 and 2.77¢ in 1952. WATER UTILITY INDUSTRY Service and Facilities 

The water utility industry now serves 140 million people in 22,000 communities through 18,500 utilities. The present installed replacement value in physical facilities (1960 construction cost) is estimated at $35 billion, or an average of $250 per person served. The industry supplies over 21 billion gallons daily for domestic, industrial and commercial purposes, or an average per capita amount of 150 gallons per day. The mean annual per capita revenue of water utilities is $13.50.

Growth and Needs 

There has been a steady though unspectacular increase in water works construction over the past decade. In 1962, contract awards totaled slightly over $500 million. Substantial construction expenditures not included in this figure were made for regular construction employees, force account labor, and direct purchases of materials and equipment. 

Larger investments in the order of $2 billion per year are needed if the industry is to keep pace with the water supply demands of industrial and urban population growth, and is to offset obsolescence and depreciation and eliminate deficiencies.

A critical step for water utilities comes in the financing of capital improvements. It is significant in this respect that water rates are being

increased to bring in greater revenues. It has been estimated that the average rate utilities charge for water rose 25% from 1955 to 1960, compared with an increase of 17.5% in the preceding five years.

Advance planning for new facilities, always an important factor because of size, complexity and financing of projects, assumes a more important role as many utilities find they must seek more distant sources of supply or rely on nearby sources which are less attractive in quality and require more extensive treatment. In either case, higher capital and operating costs are involved. It is vital that utilities project their needs at least 10 and preferably 20 or more years into the future. This will permit setting up realistic schedules for design, construction and financing of necessary works. Only in this way can good service be maintained and serious shortages prevented.

Water Resources 

After much controversy, it is now becoming clear that the nation is not in imminent danger of running out of fresh water. On the other hand, there is growing awareness that we will only have abundant water supplies in the years ahead if we apply wise management to the use of this valuable resource. Furthermore, much more scientific knowledge than we presently have will be required for full development of water. 

Before Congress are two major bills concerned with water research and planning. S. 2, a bill "to establish water resources research centers at land-grant colleges and State universities, to stimulate water research at other colleges, universities, and centers of competence, and to promote a more adequate national program of water research" was introduced early in the 88th Congress and at this writing had passed the Senate. 

S. 1111, the Water Resources Planning Act of 1963, has as its objective the comprehensive and coordinated planning of water and related land resources, and a program of financial aid to the States for water resources planning.

Meanwhile, on other fronts, there was a step-up in work on such varied research as evaporation control, desalination, re-use, recharge, pollution control, etc., each of which will play a more important role in the better utilization of water.

Of importance in the West is the Supreme Court's recent decision reallocating the lower basin flow of the Colorado River, reducing the share to California and increasing the amount to Arizona, and leaving it up to the Secretary of Interior to divide the surplus or deficit flows. The decision opens the way for expanded water development in Arizona.

California has had under way its Feather River Project aimed at piping water from the water-plentiful North over 500 miles to the arid South. 

This billion dollar project may have to be enlarged on the basis of the loss in Colorado River flow.

Contract award for municipal sewage treatment works reached $449 million in 1961 and $545 million in 1962 under the Water Pollution 

Control Act administered by the U. S. Public Health Service. It was reported that under this program the construction backlog in sewage treatment works would be met in about ten years. Many of our cities obtain their water supplies from rivers which have already received inadequately treated wastes. Improvement in stream water quality through municipal sewage treatment reduces the load on water treatment plants and is in the interest of production of a high quality water for the public.

Bills were introduced in a number of state legislatures and in the Congress to control synthetic detergents which may cause foaming in water, particularly in some private wells located close to subsurface sewage disposal systems. Aimed at requiring the marketing of a detergent that would be quickly decomposed after use, the fate of the bills is uncertain in view of voluntary developments of new, easily degraded detergents by industry.

Other Developments A new revision of the Public Health Service Drinking Water Stand ards became effective April 5, 1962. This standard sets forth the requirements for drinking water and water supply systems used by carriers and others subject to Federal quarantine regulations. Also, the standard receives wide general acceptance by other water utilities. Recognizing that the standard is primarily concerned with minimum health requirements, the American Water Works Association is actively developing a "quality water" standard to serve as a target or goal for the industry. The Association's "Progress Report on Water Quality Criteria," published in November, 1962, establishes the levels for evaluating highly finished water quality.

A number of water utilities have made effective use of instrumentation to achieve a higher degree of operating efficiency and control, resulting in a better product and improved service to the consumer. Telemetering systems have been installed to obtain operating data from distant pumping stations and to permit operation from a central station of remote facilities such as pumps and valves. It is becoming common practice to install

instruments in water plants to measure operating performance and to control treatment functions. Instruments are being used to measure the quality of raw water sources such as rivers to forewarn the treatment plant of changing conditions. Engineers are studying applications of quality control instruments for the water distribution system to insure that quality remains undiminished from plant to consumer. Although telemetry may permit a reduction in the labor force, the improvement in operating performance is often sufficient justification for its use. 

The industry is using electronic equipment for water billing, and for materials and supplies and payroll accounting. Watched with much interest are field research studies on automatic remote reading of consumer gas and electric meters.

Interest in civil defense planning wavers. Water utilities have traditionally planned for and successfully met natural disaster problems; however, only a few utilities have worked out detailed programs to cope with civil defense emergencies. A planning guide on radioactivity and civil defense for water utilities is being developed by the Office of Civil Defense, with the help of the American Water Works Association. To be made available in late 1963 or early 1964, it will render valuable help in the formulation of utility programs. 

In striving to improve water service, water utilities are actively engaged in efforts to inform the public and to enlist support for the facilities that will be required. The industry is concerned, too, with providing more and better training programs for managers, operators and other personnel.

Safety Several hundred water utilities have organized safety programs to prevent human and property loss. Nevertheless, the over-all industry injury frequency rate of about 20 disabling injuries per million man hours of work leaves much room for improvement. Many more utilities need to be made aware of the benefits of the planned safety program and encouraged to take positive action to prevent costly injuries. 

The American Water Works Association, through its National Safety 

Practices Committee and 32 Section Safety Committees, has an active program to stimulate utility programs, collect annual injury statistics and issue awards to utilities for outstanding safety records. Safety is a regular feature of the Association's publications, and the manual “Safety Practice for Water Utilities" has been prepared as a guide for safety programs.

GAS INDUSTRY Service and Facilities 

Record customers, sales and revenues were reached by the gas utility and pipeline industry in 1962. An average of 34.7 million residential, commercial and industrial customers were served during the year, an increase of 853,000 over the number served in 1961. Gas sales to these consumers totaled 102.3 billion therms, a gain of 6.7 per cent over sales of 95.9 billion therms in 1961. Revenues rose 7.5 per cent over the total of the preceding year to a new peak of $6.4 billion. 

Despite a record production of 13.7 trillion cubic feet in 1962, proved recoverable reserves of natural gas in the United States were at an alltime peak of 273.8 trillion cubic feet at the end of the year. Of the gross additions to reserves in 1962, 13.3 trillion cubic feet was realized from extensions and revisions of reserves in existing fields, and 6.3 trillion cubic feet was a result of new discoveries.

Operating 

Significant technical strides have been made in the storage of liquefied natural gas. One large pipeline company will complete construction of such a facility in 1964. Others are sure to follow. 

Lowering the temperature of natural gas to approximately 260° F transforms the gas to its liquid state at atmospheric pressure, thereby reducing its volume to a point where one cubic foot of liquid natural gas will produce approximately 700 cubic feet of natural gas at standard conditions.

Storage of liquid natural gas will provide a ready supply of natural gas where conventional storage facilities are not available. Research

In 1962, the gas industry's cooperative program of research and development continued to increase, with an A.G.A. PAR Research budget of over $3,100,000. This compares to the 1959 budget of $1,800,000. 

A number of important projects were complete and where applicable were turned over to manufacturers for commercialization. Examples are an improved efficiency absorption air conditioner, the design of belowground prestressed concrete storage tank for liquified natural gas, a 300 horsepower natural gas fueled turbine, a non-destructive pipe inspection device, a gas engine driven heat pump, a baseboard room heater requiring no chimney, and a gas engine driven air-conditioner suitable for the home.

Efforts continued, in some cases at an accelerated pace, on improving the quality of pipeline materials, the study of transient flow in pipelines, the development of processes for base and peak-load production of synthetic gas, and improved distribution operating procedures. In addition, the utilization of gas in residential, commercial and industrial applications received increasing attention. These projects ranged from the construction of a prototype small domestic furnace (24" x 18" x 30" in overall size for 90,000 Btu input) to improved brass melting equipment.

Accident Prevention in the Gas Industry 

The gas utility and pipeline industry of the United States in 1962 continued for the 15th straight year to reduce its frequency rate from the 1947 post-war peak of 21.86 injuries per million man-hours worked.

The 1962 frequency rate of 6.41 injuries per million man-hours worked is the lowest level since the American Gas Association began compiling accident statistics in 1929, and represents a 2.5 per cent decline from the 1961 rate of 6.57.

There were 490 days chargeable to disabling injuries per million manhours worked during 1962, as compared to 480 days charge in 1961. 

The outstanding employee safety accomplishment in 1962 was the establishment of a new all-time gas industry record by the Washington Gas Light Company. This company (2,432 employees) operated for more than 7,200,000 man-hours without a disabling injury.

Based on the industry's "Standard Method of Recording Basic Facts 

Relating to the Nature and Occurrence of Work Injuries" that was distributed throughout the gas industry in 1961, the gas industry prepared its second comprehensive cause analysis for the year 1962. 

There were six types of accidents which produced about 78% of the total number of disabling injuries. Now one might think that injuries from burns, from flashes or puffs, from asphyxiation; that is, those directly related to the product, would be included in this list. The analysis revealed that again in 1962 the first six accident types have nothing to do with GAS. The type of accidents experienced by gas industry personnel are the same types that result in any kind of industrial activity where one has to move about. Number one is "struck by falling and flying objects." Number two is "Overexertion" in pulling or pushing. Number three is "bodily reaction" involuntary or voluntary motions; stepping on, slipping, and so forth, where the unsafe act was inattention to footing but from which no fall resulted. "Falls from the same level" is number four. "Falls on different levels" is number five, and "struck against" is number six.

An analysis was made of the accident experience of companies using a hard hat program versus companies not having one. Companies using a hard hat program experienced an accident frequency rate of 5.35 for the calendar year 1961, which was 18% below the frequency rate of 6.57 experienced by the entire gas utility and pipeline industry in that year. 

A severity rate of 390 for these companies was 18% below the 1961 average industry severity rate of 480. 

Companies reported as having no type of hard hat program experienced a frequency rate of 11.94, 81% greater than the industry average, and a severity rate of 1071, 123% above the industry average.
