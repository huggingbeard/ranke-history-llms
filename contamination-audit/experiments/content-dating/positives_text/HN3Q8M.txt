CHAPTER I THE PSYCHOLOGY OF READING 

Reading is mental exploration. The intelligent reader penetrates into new and unexplored territory, meeting broader and richer experiences as he goes. New vistas open up before him; unsettled questions receive their answers; puzzling problems find their solution. The more matured and efficient his reading, the more meaningful become the reading situations that confront him. 

He comprehends and interprets them in terms of his past experiences. On the other hand, through these new contacts he amplifies, clarifies, and re-interprets his present knowledge and points of view. If he thinks as he goes-and reading and travel alike are vitalized and interpreted by comparisons, anticipations, judgments, and evaluations-he will carry away from his explorations increased mental organization, wealth, and power. 

Through intelligent, appreciative, thoughtful reading, the reader's mind moves forward into the minds of other men, into the hearts and souls of those who have satisfied their urge for self-expression through creative writing. 

The interested, sympathetic reader realizes the motives, feelings, and behavior of his fellows. He apprehends the problems and conflicts that beset them. Through reading, he enters into intellectual, imaginative, and emotional relationship with the life of the past and of the present. 

Learning to read implies mastery of the symbols of reading as a basic objective. But it signifies much more 1

than that. It implies the development of ability to interpret these symbols in meaningful groupings so that words, phrases, and sentences become tools that open to the reader the doors to vicarious experience. They become the representations of occurrences outside the child's immediate world. They become experience itself, interesting, enlightening, and inspiring.

The only criterion acceptable today for the soundness of any method of teaching reading to beginners is this: 

Does the method from the start train in power to grasp, not merely a sound or a word, but a related set of words, a sentence, and through it, an entire situation? 

Does it advance from a unit of thought to the words as words without loss of the contextual relationships among the words? Is the flow of associations maintained among the words as parts of the original whole from which they have been disentangled, or as parts of a different whole created anew out of the same words set in newer relationships?

It is this relation idea that makes for true reading, as opposed to mere word calling with little or no consciousness of a higher unity of comprehended meaning. An effective, economical method of teaching reading seeks the attainment of all the specific goals that make up the complex totality termed reading ability. Among the essential achievements of early reading mastery are: 

1. Development of direct associations between words or word-groups and their symbolized meanings. 

2. Gaining of ability to interpret word-units in terms of overt responses. 

3. Growth in ability to find answers to questions, and to locate facts needed to fill gaps in thought associations.

3 4. Cumulative growth in ability to find meaning in larger and larger reading units. 

5. Development of power in oral reading. 

6. Gaining of the techniques of independent recognition of words and phrases, even though these words and phrases have not been previously mastered as sight units. 

7. Growth of interest in reading, and in desire to read extensively apart from formal instruction. 

8. Habits of caring for a book and knowledge of how to use it to best advantage.

In New Progressive Road to Reading, vital interest in a story is the stimulus to reading. The surest way to catch a child's attention and to hold it is to arouse his imagination and give it ample room for play. When this has been successfully accomplished, the strongest possible motive to read well has been created. Consequently, artificial arrangements of words without natural story interest have been avoided in the construction of the readers.

The lure of the story brings the child to the book. 

Successful attainment of power in gaining the story from independent reading intensifies the appeal. That teacher does well who utilizes the supplemental stories so as to make most evident to the pupil his growing mastery of the tools of reading, who introduces the child early to the "library table" equipped with many books wellillustrated and simply worded, who uses the devices of bulletin board, chart, labeling, work-sheet, game, and illustrated conversation to apply the newly-found powers to further successful accomplishment.

A complete program must make reading a process of thought-getting and thought-expression from the beginning. Consequently, silent reading must be included to a progressively increasing degree as the work advances. 

Silent reading should be made an intrinsic part of every lesson-whole. 

1. PRE-PRIMER STAGE: Presentation of Words 

Exercises in advance of formal book reading should be programmed. These should be continued after primer work is begun, both apart from and in connection with the formal work. The pupils should be given a fundamental equipment of sight words and phrases which they should master by direct association with the implied action or the object signified. Among the words and phrases that may be taught through dramatization are: stand drink run eat jump sit talk walk sing laugh cry hide lie down get up write climb draw fall play dance catch fly blow bite cough sneeze drop shake sleep cut These words should be presented on the blackboard or chart as the action is performed. The pupils should then be told to do what the word says they should do. Flash cards should be exposed; the child called upon should

prove his recognition of the word by performing the action. Practice should follow.

3. ANTICIPATORY BLACKBOARD EXERCISES 

Anticipatory work should be done in preparation for formal reading lessons to follow. Most teachers carry on anticipatory blackboard exercises to forestall difficulties in the pronunciation and meaning of words and phrases found in reading material. This manual recommends this procedure as an aid to fluent, intelligent, and expressive silent and oral reading. Furthermore, it urges the anticipation of these new words and phrases in the form of silent reading exercises, in order to secure recognition of meaning as well as recognition of form and sound. 

4. PRACTICE EXERCISES AND TESTS

CHAPTER III TEACHING SIGHT WORDS 

Phrases and words should be introduced in contextual relationships. They should be approached through situations that make the words meaningful. Every one of the devices thus far suggested unites both form and meaning, and makes the word or word group a part of the meaningful experience. It is not necessary, however, to adopt the extreme view, that all practice be given through "intrinsic devices," in terms of the entire reading situation in which the word occurs. The principle of focalized drill justifies, and in fact demands, the temporary isolation of elements of difficulty for intensive work. Sight words should be fixed as habits, and should be re-introduced into entire reading situations for contextual application. The authors of this series therefore recommend the following procedure in the teaching of sight words and phrases: 

1. PRESENTATION 

a. The development of the situation in which the word occurs. This is usually the story in the reader. b. The symbolization of the situation in words; i.e., the presentation either on the blackboard or on a worksheet, of the sentence or paragraph in which the word or phrase occurs. c. The reading of the sentence, first silently for meaning and then orally for pronunciation and expression. 16

17 d. Recognition and fixation of the new word unit. No arbitrary formula can be furnished; but among the procedures that may be employed are the following: (1) Tentative recognition and naming of the word or word-group by inference from the total situation involved. (2) Inference from the context of the sentence. (3) Recognition from suggestions offered by the teacher or from questions asked by her. (4) Association with words of similar construction. (5) Recognition from familiar elements; i.e., seeing smaller words or noting other clues within the words in question. (6) Association with object, picture, performance, question-answer, or meaning-whole expressed by the teacher or recalled by the pupil. (7) Phonetic recognition. (8) Direct telling by the teacher.

CHAPTER IV TEACHING PHONETICS 

Phonetics is the study of sound-sight associations. Its purpose is to give to the child the tools by which he can discover independently the pronunciation of words not previously mastered as sight words. Words are made up of sound units, called phonograms. These sound units, represented by definite letters or groups of letters, recur in different words. Were our language purely phonetic, the mastery of the phonograms would give to the pupil a completely serviceable sight-sound alphabet that would enable him to read any word that he might encounter.

Many English words are unphonetic, however. Such words must be recognized and read through sight mastery, contextual comprehension, comparison, or word-building. 

An appreciably large number of words do fit into phonetic families or else are pronounced precisely as they are spelled, that is, in terms of their constituent phonograms. 

Studies of the vocabularies of second and third year children indicate that about eighty-six and one-half per cent of the syllables involved are phonetic.

Over-emphasis upon analysis of words will undoubtedly prove harmful. It is important that children be taught ways of recognizing words other than by pure phonetic analysis and synthesis (see page 17). Nevertheless, most authorities are agreed that phonetic training is of great value in giving the child power to analyze spoken words into their sounds, and to make the associations between 20

TEACHING PHONETICS 

21 the sound and the letter or group of letters represented.

In the Twenty-Fourth Yearbook of the National Society for the Study of Education, the National Committee on Read ing reports as follows: "A definite system of training in phonetics should be adopted, this system to be carefully related to a rich reading program. The system should stress the functional use of phonetic elements in reading situations. The use of phonetic training in such situations should not interfere with thought-getting. Teachers should be trained to consider this system as a source of help to be used rationally."

It is also advised that the teaching of phonetics be carried on in a period entirely separate and distinct from the reading period. Otherwise there is danger that the pupil's attention will become centered on word-forms rather than on meanings. The phonetic content should have direct bearing on the reading lesson at hand and application of the phonetics taught should be made immediately after the practice exercise.

It is not necessary that all pupils be subjected to the same amount of practice in phonetics. Some children have a strong phonic sense; they grasp sounds quickly and are able to make sound-sight associations with great facility. These pupils should be excused from intensive drills as soon as a diagnostic test indicates that they no longer need the exercise. Frequent phonetic reviews should be held, however, to which all children, even the proficient ones, should attend. 

Other pupils experience difficulty in making the sightsound associations. They do not easily see resemblances in words. They find difficulty in pronouncing new words.

These children should be given continued, systematic

phonetic training until they have developed phonetic sensitivity and power. Phonetic work should be individualized. Very little concert work should be done. 

Phonetic analysis should not be begun until the children have established the habit of thought-getting, have gained a reasonable sight-word equipment, and have begun to feel the need for aid in recognizing new words. 

It is here recommended that the formal study of phonetics begin after the first two stories of Story Steps have been taught. 

1. PHONICS 

Ear and voice training should precede eye training. In the beginning only the ear and the tongue are involved. 

Sound leads to thought, and thought to speech. "Tell me a story" is the dominant expression. This creates a golden opportunity for pure phonics-clear enunciation, clean articulation, and full, rich, deep tones of voice. 

Correct habits of speech will follow if the teacher insists. 

Clear enunciation and articulation must be insisted upon even to the point of dra-mat-ic ex-ag-ger-a-tion. Let the t be plainly heard in went; and d in and and found; in short, never slight the final consonant. 

Careful work in phonics during the teaching of the first group of stories must, therefore, precede any attempt at phonetics. Not until the ear has been taught to discriminate nicely and the tongue to execute accurately, should the eye be called upon to analyze sight words into their phonetic parts. Train the ear before the eye. Always let phonics (ear-and-tongue exercises) precede phonetics (eye analysis-synthesis).

2. PHONETICS 

At the same time that the child is growing in phonetic sensitivity, he is developing in reading power. Visual relations between the thought and the printed, or written, sentence are established, so that the pupil may get the thought as promptly from a sentence seen as from one heard. Now the pupil is ready for the establishment of the visual relations between the separate ideas of a sentence and the separate words printed, or written, to represent the ideas.

By a still further analysis the pupil is led to discover the auditory relations between the separable sounds, or phonic elements, of a spoken word, and the visual relations between the letters, or phonetic elements, used to represent these sounds to the eye. Suppose we take the order suggested on page 61. Write cow. Cover ow and sound c. Then cover c and sound ow. Soon the ear will perceive the sound that is attached to the particular part. 

This is the process in phonetics: Tear sight words to pieces and find smaller parts, with which (1) to rebuild the original word and so get a firmer grasp on it, and (2) to build new word wholes.

CHAPTER V  1. ORAL PHASE: Development of Content a. Telling the Story 

From the outset reading must be a process of thoughtgetting and thought-reproduction. The primary interest of the child remains in the story. The problem of the teacher is that of associating the story initially grasped by the child with its visual symbolization in sentences, phrases, and words.

The teacher should tell the first story simply, dramatically, and vividly so that it will take instant hold on the child's imagination. 

b. Conversation 

The story should be vitalized and enriched by combining conversation with manual activity, nature study, and dramatization. Pupils should become familiar with the animals appearing in the story—their appearance, their habits, and their calls. Informal conversation about the cow, the sheep, the pig, and the goose should lead to fuller knowledge of these animals and to greater interest in their friendship with Dicky Dare. Pupils should be led to reproduce the animal calls dramatically and realistically. 

c. Expression 

Various forms of expression should be utilized to enhance appreciation and interest. After the children have 26

become familiar with the different characters through pictures, they should be asked to draw pictures of the characters themselves. Construction work should also be carried on. The teacher should encourage the pupils to trace figures from models furnished by her. These outline figures should be cut out, colored, and used either as part of a farm project or as a temporary frieze around the blackboard. Word-recognition exercises may later be based upon the matching of these figures with word cards provided for the purpose. 

In addition let the children dramatize the stories. 

Spontaneous dramatization should be encouraged in order to gain freedom of expression and definiteness of imaginative recall. Let this dramatization remain informal and consider it acceptable, however crudely it may be done. 

The vivid imagination of the child will cover all crudity. 

d. Oral Reproduction 

The work in oral reproduction must go hand in hand with the work in reading. It must by no means be neglected, especially in classes composed of children of foreign parentage. In these classes the power to read will very quickly outstrip the power to tell what has been read. Therefore have abundant oral work based on stories of your own selection.

Do not require the children to repeat a story with. many incidents and characters, and with frequent changes of the point of view. Select stories with but one incident and few characters. Rewrite the story, cutting out all description and all difficult idiomatic phrases. 

It is to be remembered that before a child can express what he has heard, he must have command of the neces-

sary words. Some children at the age of six have a vocabulary which enables them to reproduce in their own way any simple story; others seem to have almost no vocabulary. These children will not be able to reproduce at all; they will have to acquire a vocabulary before any reproduction is required. The reading will teach new words, and the daily story-telling will give facility in using them.

This practice work should by no means be omitted or slighted, as it is the principal means of guarding against rote work, which, if allowed to creep in, will destroy real progress.

In giving the rearranged sentences, remember that rereading the same sentence from the blackboard to get greater facility is not nearly so effective as rewriting the sentence for rereading, just as if what once had been

attempted from the board had disappeared and could no longer be seen. This prevents all possible local association with the top, the bottom, or the side of the board. 

This is true also of words. Keep on filling the board, even if it be with the repeated writing of only half a dozen words. Never try to run the mill with water that has gone by.

Do not attempt the practice exercises in rearrangement of words until the individual words are firmly fixed in the child's mind. If the little steps are hurried over, and the words but half known, the reading of the sentences will be halting.

Sometimes a child does not get the thought in the sentence. This will be shown by his inability to repeat the sentence without looking at the board, or by a slow and uncertain repetition of the sentence, showing that he remembers merely a collection of words and not the thought contained in them. The best guard against this meaningless reading is repeated practice in independent recognition of individual words.

Practice at least two days on the words of a new sentence, before attempting to rearrange the words. An experienced teacher may allow herself a little liberty in this direction, but not the beginner. 

f. Seat Work-Bridging the Gap between Blackboard and Book

At each stage of the blackboard work with the first two stories, seat-work exercises should be given to apply the power gained and to bridge the gap between the blackboard work and the book work. Work-sheets should be prepared, duplicating the text of the begin-

ning pages in the primer; letters should be of the same size or slightly larger. Exercises to drill sentences, phrases, and words in the story, and to test ability to read rearranged sentences should be incorporated in the work-sheets. If the child has difficulty in reading any of the seat material, he should be permitted to refer to the blackboard for comparison. Seat-work exercises composed of rearranged sentences will serve as contextual application anticipatory to reading from the book. 

g. Class Grouping 

After several sentences have been read by the pupils, the class may be grouped.

First group: Those who, when the sentence is printed on the blackboard, recognize the words without effort. 

Second group: Those who need to be taught, whose minds follow the steps easily but never skip over any. 

Third group: Those who must be taught the same thing two or three times before grasping it. This group will include those who find difficulty in recognizing words without reference to their position in the sentence. 

Foreign children will take a long time to get the thought, and there may be some children who will not recognize the words in their new relations. These children should be helped, and a drill should be given, then and there, with the entire group on all the words that have been forgotten.

The grouping done at this stage of the work must necessarily be tentative, and for some weeks children will shift from group to group; but the work of the first year progresses so much more rapidly if done in groups that it is well to begin grouping as soon as possible.

The rating should be low. If the rating is high, the teacher is constantly pulling some one up to the level. 

Good grouping does away with this trouble. 

At this stage, it is far better to place the nervous and the slow children in the third group, where they will have time to accustom themselves to strange surroundings, and where they will develop naturally, than to place them in the second group and drag them up to the level of the others.

Each group must be allowed to advance as far and as fast as it can. Promotion from lower to higher groups during the term must be allowed. This will prevent arrested development and allow natural progress. Often the lowest group disappears altogether, especially when they have a maximum of silent work just a little beyond their ordinary power. Here lies the secret of the grada tion in New Progressive Road to Reading. 

3. BOOK WORK: Reading of the Story as a Whole 

When the children have gone over the entire story, sentence by sentence, in the manner indicated in the foregoing steps, the book should be placed in their hands so that they may read the story as a whole. Have them read a certain portion silently, and when they have finished, let them stand. Observe proper grouping and help the slow ones. In oral reading when the book is first used, let each child read only a single sentence. When some degree of power is attained he may be trusted to read a paragraph; later, several paragraphs; and finally, a page or the entire story. 

The procedure outlined on the preceding pages for the development of the first story should be followed with regard to the second story, "The Sheep, the Pig,

the Cow, and the Goose." The preliminary blackboard drill must not be slighted. 

In the case of the remaining stories only the new phrases and words need be presented on the blackboard previous to the reading of the stories from the book. 

The primary teacher shows her power by the number and variety of the devices which she uses for clinching the results of her teaching. She will never neglect the preliminary oral and blackboard work prescribed as absolutely necessary before the book may be put into the hands of the children. Therefore her pupils will soon find it possible to read silently an entire story, and will do it repeatedly for very pleasure. 

This silent reading is the direct road to power. The child gradually learns from the context to determine for himself new words without having had them presented on the blackboard.

5. SILENT READING: A Parallel Program 

The silent reading program recommended in Chapter II should be carried beyond the pre-primer stage to the collateral stage, and should parallel the work presented in this chapter. 

a. Practice Exercise 

After three or four sentences have been taught on the blackboard, a number of work-sheet exercises should be given, the aim of which is to secure the silent reading values of the reading program, to apply what has been taught on the blackboard, and to transfer the mental results of the blackboard training to work-sheet and book activity.

At times, silent reading should precede the blackboard work; at other times, the formal teaching of the new sight words should be done from the work-sheets instead of from the blackboard. The class teacher is referred to the next chapter for appropriate silent reading seat-work exercises. 

b. Diagnostic Testing and Remedial Work 

Work-sheet exercises of the kinds described in the next chapter should be used as diagnostic tests at regular intervals during the conduct of the oral and silent reading program. These tests may be prepared by the teacher and mimeographed. They should be carefully graded in difficulty and should be specifically adapted to measure specific reading abilities. Tests to measure word mastery (comprehension and oral expression), phrase mastery (comprehension and expression), paragraph interpretation, ability to select relevant data, ability to organize material in time sequence, and ability to evaluate and apply judgments, should be included in the testing procedure. The work-sheets should employ the vocabulary and the content of the stories studied. At times, different content expressed in the vocabulary of the readers should be introduced. 

As soon as weaknesses are discovered, the teacher should apply the needed remedial measures. She may have to re-teach the sight words, or she may find it necessary to give additional drill work. Practice tests similar in construction to the diagnostic tests and paralleling them in content and vocabulary should be employed for this purpose. This remedial work must be individualized.

CHAPTER VII PHONETIC DEVELOPMENT 1. TEACHING THE BLEND 

The vocabulary of the first few stories serves as a foundation for sound-work. The phonetic development begins after the completion of the second story. Cow is a good word with which to commence, because it forms part of the child's vocabulary, and because it is readily visualized and easily pronounced. 

In teaching the blend there are four steps: 

a. Presentation of the Word 

Print the word on the blackboard. Do not underline or distinguish the phonogram or the initial consonant in any way. 

b. Analysis 

Pronounce the word slowly. While sounding c, cover the phonogram ow; while pronouncing the phonogram ow, cover the consonant c. (In teaching a sound or a phonogram, take it from a known sight word, always having the children discover for themselves the sound of the letter or the phonogram.) 

c. Blending the Elements 

Be sure that the children not only perceive that the word is made up of two parts, c and ow, but that they understand how these parts are blended. 61

d. Building New Words 

Take consonants from sight words which occur in the first stories; for example, c from cow, b from by, h from he, etc. Then give the words cow, bow, how, etc. Let this stage of the work take as much time as the children require. A comprehension of the blend is the principal thing.

From the beginning insist that the child pronounce the entire word at once. Never allow him in attacking a word to voice separate phonetic symbols, but always have him sound them in the full and perfect blend of the word.

Do not add a sound or a phonogram to the list for daily perception-card drill until the children have thoroughly associated it with the word of which it is a part. Be particularly careful in the case of phonograms that are not words in themselves, as ing. 

2. TEACHING VOWEL SOUNDS a. Discovering Vowel Sounds 

When vowel sounds are taught as such, diacritical marks are used for the first time. The teacher wants to teach the long sound of o. She prints the word so on the blackboard. The children pronounce it. Then she covers the s and pronounces the o. When the children have discovered that o says ō, mark the letter and drill on new words.

When the phonetic study has somewhat advanced, the reading will go very quickly, because only those words containing the modified sounds of the vowels will be outside the key.

b. Use of Diacritical Marks 

Discontinue the markings ā, ē, i, ō, ū when the children know the effect of final e. This step is reached in the second half of the first year (Book One). This long mark is not again used except in case of words like bow, etc., in which the vowel may have one of two sounds. 

After one month of work on any vowel drill, mark only the first word in a set, as bag, beg, big, bog, bug. 

Discontinue the use of this mark as soon as possible. 

The marks are to be used in the blackboard work because such preliminary blackboard drill smooths out most difficulties. After a time these marks in blackboard work may be discontinued. 

c. Crossing Off Letters 

As soon as the work in phonetics is begun, cross off: 

1. The e before d, as in the word walked. 

2. All other silent letters, as in build afraid hear coal NOTE. Ea (hear), ai (afraid), oa (coal), are taught later as phonograms, so the marking in these words is discontinued at that time. 

Do not cross off: 

1. One of double letters, as in little. 

2. The y following a, as in lay. 

3. Silent e at the end of a word, as in lame. 

4. The second e in double e, as in seen. 

3. DEVELOPMENT OF PHONETIC RAPIDITY a. Meaning and Practice 

By phonetic rapidity is meant the power to see and at the same time to say. Phonetic rapidity will be

acquired by degrees. The children may be able to recognize and to pronounce the consonant and the vowel sounds perfectly, but the power to pronounce a word as quickly as the eye takes it in will come only by practice. 

The first and the second groups of pupils will have acquired this rapidity at the end of six months. The third group will take perhaps twelve months. 

But rapidity in blackboard work does not mean facility in reading from the book. Up to this point, phonetic drills may have taken first place; but when rapidity in blackboard work has been gained, more time may be given to reading from the book to insure fluency in phrasing.

The blackboard drills, however, must not be neglected for a day. As the children acquire power, the drills may be made shorter, but they must not be dropped altogether. 

Since the phonetic key is an important feature of the method, it is essential that the work be done with the utmost thoroughness.

Children who do not appear to acquire rapidity naturally must be taught to do so. For this purpose a small group is a necessity. Take the children by fives for a short, sharp exercise every day. Do not allow them to dawdle over the phonetic work. Train them to say at once, without hesitation, whatever you put on the blackboard. Until some degree of phonetic rapidity is acquired, a child will not read for pleasure. Therefore, give him this power as quickly as possible, for then he will do more than half the work himself. 

There is a time when the child seems to be able to pronounce a new word only after slowly, and often audibly, putting the different parts of it together. From the beginning teach him to do this silently, and quickly.

b. Perception Card Exercises 

Every phonogram, consonant, and vowel sound should be reviewed daily by means of perception cards. The teacher may obtain these cards from the publishers or she may make them herself, printing each sound as it is taught, on cardboard in letters large enough to be read at the back of the room. 

Take third-group work with the whole class. 

Take second-group work with the second and first groups.

Take first-group work with the first group only. 

When a group stops reciting with the others, let it begin seat work specified for that group at the commencement of the lesson. 

c. Practice on Miscellaneous Words 

A practice exercise on miscellaneous words is a pleasant way of reviewing what the children know. It uses the same knowledge in ever-varying form, and is the principal means of increasing phonetic rapidity. 

The idea is not to teach a certain number of words, but to give the children facility in dealing with new reading matter. The blending of words in series only will not give the necessary phonetic strength to attack new material. Pupils must have daily experience in blending words from varying lists. The words used in these exercises must contain phonetic elements with which the children are familiar. As each point in the phonetic work is taken up, words representing that point should appear in the miscellaneous drill.

6. REVIEW IN CONNECTION WITH HIGHER BOOKS 

The work in connection with Book Three and the higher books of the series is chiefly review. The new work consists in taking up exceptions to the phonetic key which occur in words commonly used. a. Daily reviews on lists of miscellaneous words containing phonograms or phonetic elements which have been taught in former grades. b. Daily reviews on contrasting lists showing the effect of ed on the final consonant; as fitted, dropped, created, received, etc. c. Occasional review on lists showing change of final y on addition of suffixes ed, er, est, ly, ness; as carry, carried, carrier; happy, happier, happiest, happily, happiness. d. Whenever exceptional phonetic difficulties appear, give a blackboard exercise, showing the ordinary value of the phonetic element involved, and at the same time noting the exception; as, gave have hive give creak break treat great road broad maid said e.
