The Commonwealth of Massachusetts STATE REIMBURSEMENT FOR UTILITY RELOCATIONS REQUIRED BY HIGHWAY CONSTRUCTION SUMMARY OF REPORT 

In conformance with a directive of the General Court, this report discusses the question of state reimbursement of utility relocation costs made necessary on public rights-of-way by federal-aid highway construction. At present such reimbursement may be made, but a pending proposal would eliminate that statutory provision. 

FEDERAL-AID HIGHWAY LEGISLATION 

To provide background, this report first describes the huge federal-aid highway system and the nature of state participation therein.

The Federal Government does not own highways except in a few federal areas. They are owned, maintained and controlled by the states and their political subdivisions. On the basis of federal-aid, however, the Federal Government has established certain standards of highway construction which the states must meet if they are to obtain federal funds. 

The federally-aided highway program has been enormously expanded by the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 in which Congress made available $25 billion of federal spending on federal-aid highways over the succeeding 13 years. This federal act included the authorization, where state laws so permit, for states to use federal funds to share in reimbursement of public utility companies for their costs of relocating any facilities established on public rightsof-way, when required by the highway improvement. Reimbursement for relocations on private property has always been made by Massachusetts and is not being questioned in any way. 

The effect of this huge new program of federal aid on state utility relocation costs depends on the scope of the state laws which

[Feb. 

were enacted to implement the 1956 federal act. In Massachusetts, all of its federal aid highways were included, as follows: (1) the 

Interstate System on which the Federal Government bears 90% of the construction cost; (2) the primary and secondary systems thereof, on which the cost is equally divided between the Federal Government and the state; and (3) urban extensions of the latter systems, also on a 50-50 basis. 

Acceptance of this federal offer to share in utility reimbursement has stirred up much controversy in the states. In the year after passage of the 1956 Federal Act, 39 states considered bills to provide for reimbursement; 18, or nearly half of them, rejected the proposals. In the remaining 21 states where favorable legislative action had occurred, gubernatorial vetoes intervened in six states to kill the bills.

In the remaining states where reimbursement laws finally prevailed, there at once developed the struggle over constitutional issues connected with payment of public funds to private corporations, which is discussed at some length in the report. 

Perhaps reflecting this mounting legal storm, at least in part, the U. S. Secretary of Commerce has expressed public opposition to the use of federal funds for sharing reimbursement payments for utility relocations on public rights-of-way. Similarly, a proposal of last year in the U. S. Senate to decrease such federal sharing on the 

Interstate System from 90% to 70%, was carried to the debate stage before being defeated by a roll call vote.

STATE LEGISLATION 

Massachusetts was the first state to pass legislation to make adjustment for the expanded highway program under the new federal statute. Its wholly permissive statute (Acts of 1956, c. 718) gave the State Department of Public Works discretion to make utility relocation payments. In practice, that Department has followed the policy of limiting reimbursements to the municipally owned utilities, as was being done on a common law basis before passage of the 1956 Act. In other words, the Department does not reimburse any privately owned utility for the expense of relocating its facilities within a public right-of-way in which the utility has no property right.

A study of the provisions of the Massachusetts Act indicates that efforts were made therein (a) to curb "incidental" spending, and (b) to restrict the use of federal funds received, to those highway purposes described in the act. 

LEGAL ASPECTS OF REIMBURSEMENT LEGISLATION Legal Relationships of State Highway Departments and Public Utilities 

The state has sovereign and paramount control of highways and streets within its borders; this is an essential part of its police power. This control is usually delegated by the Legislature to a state highway agency or to local governments. But regardless of the level of government at which control is exercised, power is held in trust and is used for the primary benefit of the traveling public. 

Land used for highway purposes cannot be diverted from public use.

Although highways are designed primarily for the traveling public, they may be used for any purpose which serves the public interest in transportation, communication, health, sewage and related matters. The state may therefore permit the location of any facilities within the public way which are not inconsistent with the superior rights of the traveling public. While public utilities may use the public ways their status is subordinate and subject to supervision and control.

Courts have uniformly held that in the absence of a clear statutory mandate shifting the burden of relocation costs from the utilities to the state, utilities can be required by the state to move their facilities at their own expense whenever highway improvement so requires. 

Federal participation in a highway project does not stop state exercise of its police power. Federal sharing of utility relocation costs under the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, does not alter the fact that the states can compel utilities to relocate from public ways at their own expense.

Utilities may locate within a public way with or without a property right but no utility can acquire a vested right to remain in any specific highway location. Utilities cannot however be required to bear the cost of relocating facilities merely to benefit some other privately owned utility, for example.

[Feb. 

Judicial Decisions and Opinions 

State supreme court decisions on the reimbursement issue have been sharply divided as to the equity and constitutionality of state reimbursement statutes. The question narrows to three basic constitutional issues: (a) the lending of state credit to a private corporation, (b) the diversion of constitutionally protected highway funds for non-highway purposes, and (c) the placing of a limitation on state police power.

On the one hand, some courts have deemed relocation of utility facilities to be an integral part of highway improvement, or at least reasonably incidental to highway construction, and hence relocation costs to be legitimate highway fund expenditures. 

On the other hand, other courts place relocation of facilities outside the scope and meaning of highway improvement. These courts characterize utility facilities as serving no useful or desirable purpose in the construction and maintenance of a highway. They reject the argument that utility facilities are a usual and necessary adjunct of highway construction.

Another court held that utility relocation costs may properly be met from the state's general fund but not from highway funds without violation of the state's constitutional provision protecting highway funds from diversion to non-highway purposes. 

The courts are also divided over the question of a public purpose being served. One court has ruled out any state or public purpose being served by public payments to private utility companies. Other courts not only recognize the public purpose but further describe such service as one of primary benefit to the whole community. 

Legal Doctrine in Massachusetts

The law in Massachusetts before passage of Acts of 1956, c. 7718, as it related to the relocation problem provided that privately owned utilities would be reimbursed for relocation costs caused by highway construction only if such relocation interfered with a property right of a utility.

In general, the various Massachusetts cases relating to this problem indicate that utilities did not acquire a property right by locating their facilities on a public way. Instead they acquired a special

1959.] 

privilege which could be taken away without reimbursement if it interfered with public rights. 

Massachusetts General Laws authorize the location of utility facilities within state highways and other public ways. In these instances the utilities are granted a privilege to modify to some extent the use of the public way. No right of private property in the street is thereby acquired. A 1904 decision which discusses this problem generally, is quoted at length in the report. (New England 

Tel. & Tel. Co. v. Boston Terminal Co., 182 Mass. 97).

On the other hand, a 1942 decision found that a utility, under some circumstances, may obtain a property right in a public way, and interference with that property right caused by highway construction would entitle the utility to reimbursement. (Boston Elevated Ry. Co. v. Commonwealth, 310 Mass. 528).

The present Massachusetts reimbursement provision (Acts of 1956, c. 718) raised the question as to whether the General Court intended to create a new right of utility reimbursement. If a new right of reimbursement was intended, two problems are paramount: 

The first of these two problems concerns payment to a utility being unconstitutional in that it may be a gift of public funds. 

There is no Massachusetts decision on this question, but the court did rule in connection with the recent Old Colony Railroad subsidy proposal that payment to that utility was in the public interest and a proper expenditure of public moneys for a public purpose. 

The second big problem is whether highway funds may be used for reimbursement purposes. Considerable doubt exists as to whether the General Court may now increase the scope of highway fund items as defined in a 1948 Amendment to the Constitution of Massachusetts.

Prior to passage of the 1956 state law, publicly owned utilities were reimbursed for costs of all relocations occasioned by highway construction. But the municipality is a creature of the state, hence, the cost of relocating municipally owned facilities is a public cost. 

The past policy of the State Department of Public Works relative to reimbursing municipally owned utilities appears to have been correct even though the same policy might not be applied to privately owned utilities.

[Feb. 

MASSACHUSETTS HIGHWAY SYSTEM 

That portion of the Massachusetts highway system which is to be part of the great federal interstate highway network will consist of 444 miles to be built at a total estimated cost of $1.4 billion. Only 

162 miles of this state system have thus far been completed at a total cost of $314 million. 

Total federal and state highway expenditures in Massachusetts have amounted to $465 million during the past five years, of which a little over half has qualified for federal aid, under formulae which have changed the federal-state ratio from 50%-50% to 60%- 40% and most recently to a 90% federal-10% state basis. 

The routes of the new superhighways will carry them through densely populated areas in the Commonwealth. Their great widths of six and eight lanes will require expensive relocations of heavy concentrations of utility facilities which serve the masses of urban dwellers.

UTILITY RELOCATION COSTS 

The Department of Public Utilities asked the private utility companies for information relative to past costs and future estimated costs of relocating facilities on public ways incident to highway improvement. Those companies which responded reported recent past costs to be more than $3 million, most of them involving electric lines. This figure is much lower than the complete total because one of the largest electric utilities did not provide information. 

On the basis of present plans for new highways, these same companies expect to sustain future costs of some $9 million to relocate facilities on public rights-of-way only. Analysis of payments by the Department of Public Works for relocations on private rights of way indicates that a conservative estimate of costs to relocate facilities on future known projects will run somewhere around $10 million. Taken together, these future utility relocation costs on both public and private ways will be in the general magnitude of $20 million.

There is a great spread in resultant costs to the state contingent on whether reimbursement costs are made for relocations of public utilities on private as against public rights-of-way. Thus the New 

England Telephone and Telegraph Company received about $56,000

1959.] 

on the former basis during the past three years, whereas, it would have received about $520,000 if the state had followed the latter basis and not adhered to the common law doctrine despite statutory authorization to make such payments. It is believed that most public utilities (about 80%) are located on public rights-of-way. 

The Commonwealth has reimbursed more than $2.75 million for non-rail utility relocations of the past five years to both privately and publicly owned utilities. Privately owned facilities received only half of that total amount ($1.57 million) even though far more privately than publicly owned utilities were moved. This strengthens the claim that the bulk of utility facilities are on public ways and thus non-reimbursable. 

Most of the highways of the state run through open or non-urban areas. Nevertheless, half the $2.75 million in state reimbursement is attributable to projects confined to urban areas, which underscores the high costs of relocating urban facilities. Figures submitted by the utilities themselves confirm this. For example, a single project in Worcester is expected to cost the private utility $183,000 to move less than a mile of its facilities. 

The more heavily congested the urban area, the less likelihood that utility facilities will be located on high-priced private properties. Thus the greater part of the total number of facilities will be on public ways and in the areas of highest population density, business activity and industrial zones. It may be expected that a large percentage of these facilities will be underground which is by far the most expensive type to move.

MUNICIPALLY OWNED UTILITIES 

Municipally owned utilities which have been reimbursed for the cost of moving facilities to accommodate highway improvement, include water, sewer, gas, electric light and police and fire alarm systems. For purposes of this report attention is focused on the municipal electric light plants. Among all publicly owned utilities, they have received the greatest share of reimbursement funds. 

Municipally owned electric plants in Massachusetts were first authorized by law in 1891 and today number 40. Most of them operate at a profit although large profits are usually sacrificed to achieve lower rates.

[Feb. SENATE — 

No. 501. 

Reimbursements to municipally owned utilities from 1954 through 1958 totalled nearly $1.2 million, with electric utilities accounting for two-thirds of this figure. Future estimated costs of those municipal utilities which replied to a Research Bureau questionnaire (25% of all municipals) total roughly $1.5 million. 

Critics of reimbursement to municipal utilities argue that municipals are able to pay for their relocation expenses, and that reimbursements place privately owned utilities at a competitive disadvantage forcing higher private rates to offset these municipal reimbursements. They add that the freedom of “municipals” from taxes and from obligations to stockholders, and their use of inexpensive municipal credit provide favored treatment which is inequitable.

Friends of the "municipals" counter by pointing out that such plants are part of the municipal government, hence subject to its financial control. Relocation costs would not only imperil the existence of some "municipals", but would probably cause their abandonment. The more strict regulation to which municipals are subject indicates the state's concern for their success. It is emphasized that privately owned utilities have the advantages of selling stock which reduces debt retirement costs. Finally, although free from taxes, municipals must make substantial in "lieu of taxes" payments.

PROS AND CONS OF REIMBURSEMENT For Reimbursement 

Federal assumption of utility relocation costs should not be made to turn on considerations of local law or contractual obligations or the exercise of local police powers. The federal-aid highway system provides general benefits to the general public all over the nation rather than merely local benefits to local residents. Thus it is not a proper or reasonable exercise of the local police power to compel relocation and to disallow reimbursement.

Because relocation expense is the result of governmental directive rather than an expense attributable to an operation planned by private management, it is in reality a tax. As general taxpayers, utility customers contribute to highway construction costs when they pay their federal taxes. They pay again when rates are

Highway routing is unpredictable to private utility companies and final locations are fortuitous in nature. In the absence of reimbursement, severe hardship is placed on some utility companies, whereas no great burden falls on the Federal Government when it

Against Reimbursement 

Utility companies plead the economic issues but ignore the provisions of constitutional and statutory law. Utilities want a gift. 

They want compensation for the loss of something they never had, either as a property right or other form of ownership. If reimbursement is not to be a gift the state must receive direct tangible benefits, yet in making such payments to utilities, the state would receive no consideration in return. Merely because government initiates a highway program which causes expense to private companies who make free use of the public way, it does not become constitutionally permissible to donate public funds as reimbursements.

In addition to federal moneys allocated from the general fund 2 for highway construction, much federal-aid money comes from fed- Ieral highway taxes. Highway users who pay these taxes receive no benefit from private utilities which make free use of the highway. It therefore does not seem unfair to ask the customers of the 

I utilities who are the indirect beneficiaries of free occupancy by the I utilities on public ways, to bear the cost of relocating those facilities. 

Highways are planned to accommodate the general public, not · merely the rate-paying public. Inasmuch as utilities are allowed a 

1 fair return for their investors, rates should adequately provide for such contingencies.

Although the extra burden on the Federal Government would be slight if it assumes all utility relocation costs, various local projects

[Feb. 

would be cut back because of reduced funds available for actual road construction. More important, this precedent would set the stage for further inroads on highway fund money for use for nonhighway purposes. Highway users who pay federal and state taxes for constitutionally-earmarked highway purposes would be unfairly penalized if their tax moneys were handed over to private enterprise to pay company operational expenses. It is for the state, not the Federal Government, to decide whether or not utility relocation costs are integral, incidental, or alien to, highway construction costs.

CHAPTER II. FEDERAL-AID HIGHWAY LEGISLATION Development of Federal Aid 

To provide some background for this discussion of state utility reimbursements, this chapter describes the huge federal-aid highway system and program, and the nature of state participation therein. The discussion will demonstrate that the states and not the Federal Government make the final decisions as to location and types of highways to be built and that state, not federal, law is controlling.

The United States, unlike many countries, has no national system of highways owned or controlled by the Federal Government. With the exception of federally owned roads in national forests, parks and Indian reservations, the roads of the nation are owned and maintained by the states and other political subdivisions. 

Thus, the highways marked by the familiar shields containing the "U. S." designation, unless they are on public lands, are not owned or maintained by the Federal Government: the shield merely constitutes a national numbering scheme established for the convenience of the traveling public. The Federal Government does administer a program to subsidize most of the construction costs for selected major highways. That program is the subject of this chapter. 

It is only within comparatively recent years that the Federal Government has played an important role in highway construction and financing. True, the Federal Government did participate in some highway construction during its early history, but no federal agency was primarily concerned with roads until an Office of Road Inquiry was established in the Department of Agriculture in 1895. 

Although early experiments by the Federal Government relative to federal-aid highway programming were small, they did clarify certain issues, e.g., the unwillingness of states to make contributions toward direct national projects; the demand of most states that they have the right to determine the type of construction and the location of the projects; the need of a definite formula for apportioning funds; and the necessity of placing the work under state control as opposed to national or local control.

[Feb. 

More than a score of years later, the modern concept of federal participation in a highway system began to evolve. With the advantage of earlier experience, Congress was able to draft legislation enacted as the Federal-Aid Road Act of 1916 embodying certain principles contained in all subsequent highway legislation. The 

State became the smallest governmental unit with which the Federal Government would deal. And each state, in order to obtain federal-aid, was required to establish an adequate highway department; in Massachusetts this was accomplished by designating the Department of Public Works to implement the federal act (G.L. c.81, s. 30). The 1916 Federal Act also provided a definite formula for apportioning federal funds on the combined basis of area, population, and mileage of U. S. rural mail routes. State planning and estimates were subject to appraisal and approval of the Federal Government. Maintenance of completed roads became the responsibility of the States. 

On this basis the foundation was laid for a national highway system, but major developments thereunder did not take shape until later. Funds were too widely dispersed and adequate national highway planning was impossible in the light of weaknesses in the 1916 act. In 1921, a Federal Highway Act tightened federal controls, established a system of approved interstate and intercounty roads to be proposed by state highway departments, and provided an appropriation of $75 millions. 

In the thirties, during the Great Depression, about a billion dollars of federal money was poured into highway grants without any required matching by the states. Urban roads received much of this money. Secondary roads and the elimination of dangerous rail crossings were also added to the federal program. 

In 1941, the Federal Defense Highway Act added $150 millions for highway construction, concentrating on roads, bridges, and rights of way leading to military and naval sites, defense plants, and sources of vital raw materials. Strategic military routes received another $50 millions.

Economic Impact of 1956 Act on Utilities 

Until recent years, utilities were not greatly concerned with highway construction, even though their facilities had long been concentrated along public highway rights-of-way. Limited highway improvement programs affected too few utilities to cause deep concern.

But the recent vast increase in the volume of highway projects and the generous scale of design and construction of these projects require a major degree of relocation of utility facilities. Whereas earlier highway improvements were frequently confined to resurfacing narrow ribbons of concrete, today's projects call for many major features such as multiple traffic lanes, landscaped divider strips, roads for access and egress, underpasses, overpasses, mini- I mum grading, mazes of traffic circles and clover leaf traffic interchanges, and roadside rest areas. These requirements not only cause high road building expenditures, but heavy costs for plant and facility relocation. Salient Problems Generated by State Legislation As indicated above, the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 provides in section 111 that whenever a state pays for the costs of relocation of utility facilities necessitated by the construction of a federal-aid highway, federal funds may be used to reimburse the state in the same proportion as federal funds are expended on the rest of the project.

It has been claimed that this federal provision is merely a statutory expression of the federal policy as it already existed before enactment of the 1956 legislation. However, common law and statutory law in most state jurisdictions at that time required the utility to bear the cost of relocation if the altered facility was located in a public right-of-way, while the states paid such costs if utility facilities located on their own right-of-way had to be moved. The state also paid for the acquisition of private rights-ofways.

When the 1956 act went into force, the three states of Connecticut, California, and New Jersey had laws providing for reimbursement to utilities for relocated facilities within the public right-ofway when made necessary by construction of express roads. Connecticut shared the cost with the utility, and California made payments only under certain conditions, governed by local considerations. For some time New Jersey has reimbursed utilities for facilities relocated on freeways and parkways.

CHAPTER IV. LEGAL ASPECTS OF REIMBURSEMENT LEGISLATION LEGAL RELATIONSHIPS OF STATE HIGHWAY DEPARTMENTS AND PUBLIC UTILITIES¹ State Control Over Highways 

Control over public thoroughfares is an essential part of the state's police power and the construction and control of public highways within a government's jurisdiction is a fundamental function. A state, subject to constitutional limitations and acting through its Legislature, has sovereign and paramount control of the highways and streets within its borders, even if fee title of streets rests in a political subdivision. Title obtained by the state in condemnation proceedings may be in fee simple, as in some states, or may be only an acquisition of an easement for highway purposes.

The Legislature often delegates control over some highways to a state highway agency, and over other highways, to local governments. At present, at least three levels of government appear in the highway picture: local, state and federal. At the local level there is an interest in public ways located within local boundaries; at the state level, an interest in state highways, which include interstate highways; at the federal level, an interest in a highway network basic to national defense and interstate commerce.

Although the public highways are designed primarily for the use of the traveling public, such highways may be used for any purpose which serves the public's interest in transportation, communication, sewage, health, or related matters. Accordingly, the state may permit the location of any facilities in or on the public rightof-way which are not inconsistent with the superior rights of the traveling public. Thus, it is a generally accepted principle of common law, which has been codified in statutes in some states, that public utilities designed to serve these public purposes may also I use the highways to locate facilities and equipment. Such use of the highway, even with express governmental permission is, however, still subordinate to the paramount use of the traveling public. 

The state therefore retains the power to regulate placement, construction and maintenance of utility facilities to ensure that they do not inconvenience or hamper the public's ordinary use of the highway. In all states, the erection, maintenance and repair of utility facilities are subject to the supervision and control of the highway agency or the local government. 

- Application of Police Power 

1 Utilities which locate their facilities within the public right-ofway, whether or not the condition is expressed in writing, do so subject to reasonable regulations of the police power of the state. 

While the term "police power" defies exact definition, it is generally defined as a government's inherent power to make reasonable rules and regulations for the health, safety, general welfare, and convenience of the people. No compensation need be paid to citizens for their obedience to reasonable police regulations. The police power cannot be contracted away or limited by any government agency. ľ It is universally recognized that improvement or control of highways is a proper subject for application of the government's police power. Therefore, highway users of any description are required to obey without compensation reasonable police regulations aimed at facilitating highway use by the traveling public. 

The courts have uniformly held that, in the absence of a clear statutory mandate shifting the burden of relocation costs from the utilities to the state, utilities can be required by the state, acting through (a) the Legislature, (b) the state highway agency, or

[Feb. 

(c) local governments, to move their facilities at their own expense whenever the necessities of highway improvement require. 

If there is any subsequent damage to the utility during relocation operations, that cost also is considered to be merely incidental to reasonable regulation in the interest of the public. 

This court holding applies regardless of (a) the position of the utility within the public right-of-way, either under, over, or on the highway, (b) the type of highway improvement, and (c) the nature or source of the utility's permission to locate where it is. 

Federal participation in highway projects does not preclude the state from invoking its police powers. Such federal participation merely recognizes a legitimate national interest in the improvement of existing highways, as the result of which federal moneys are appropriated to be matched by participating states. The combined resources are then spent on designated highway systems subject to minimum standards established by the responsible federal agency. The Federal Government itself does not initiate highway construction projects; this prerogative, as well as the determination of the nature and financing of the project, rests with the states. Federal authorization to pay a share of utility relocation costs in line with the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 (S.111), does not alter the fact that states can compel utilities to relocate at their own expense any facilities located within a public rightof-way.

Utility Rights 

There are at least five ways a utility may locate within a public right-of-way: 

1. By virtue of constitutional or statutory provisions, with or without consent of the local government. 

2. By permission, where a state or local government grants a franchise or permit, or agrees to a contract, or authorizes location by ordinance. 

3. By virtue of a license, revocable or irrevocable, from the state or local government. 

4. By sufferance or tenancy at will. 

5. By trespass, where occupancy is without permission or legally enforceable right.

When a utility incurs expenditures upon the express or implied

1959.] 

2 Te 1 1 2 consent of an appropriate highway agency to locate on a public right-of-way, it obtains an interest in the highway location which is considered by some courts to be a "privilege" for limited purposes, and by other courts to be a "vested property right." In either event, the utility then achieves certain rights and incurs certain obligations. Thus, a governmental agency may not, without valid reason, (1) rescind the consent and require relocation, (2) impose a charge after having permitted free use, or (3) impair the obligation of its "contract" with the utility. On the other hand, a utility's rights or franchise to locate in a public right-of-way may be taxed by a municipality or by the state, or a charge may be levied by the government as a condition of original consent. 

Even where courts have referred to the utility's location privilege as a vested property right which then protects the utility against an arbitrary extinction of that right, it has been recognized that no utility can acquire a vested right to remain in any specific highway location.

It is a prerequisite to the right of reimbursement for cost of utility relocations that a utility prove its legal right to be located on the highway. But a determination that such right exists does not inevitably lead to reimbursement since all rights in highways are subject to a reasonable exercise of the police power. A utility claimant must prove that a required relocation is a taking of its property for public use rather than a mere limitation on the enjoyment of its private rights. The taking of an easement would not entitle the holder to any recovery if the court decides that the easement was granted by grace of the legislature and subject to extinction or alteration upon its demand. And if the state provides a substitute right-of-way for the utility, no utility property has been "taken."

The decision that a utility can be required to move its facilities does not determine the separate question of whether the utility can be required to bear resultant costs. On this score the courts reason that (1) the primary use of the highway is to accommodate the traveling public, (2) the use of the highway by utilities is a secondary and subordinate use, and (3) highway rights are subject to the exercise of the police power. It is therefore frequently stated that "in the absence of a specific statutory mandate placing the

[Feb. 

burden of the cost of relocations upon the state or municipality, the common law principles control, imposing this burden on the utilities." (See Special Report 21, Highway Research Board, Relocation of Public Utilities Due to Highway Improvement, p. 24.)

Utilities cannot, however, be required to bear the cost of relocation merely to benefit some other privately owned utility or a municipally-operated utility where the municipality is acting in a proprietary capacity. Where the courts which have faced this problem, distinction is made between "proprietary” and “governmental" activities of municipally-owned utilities. 

Constitutional and Statutory Provisions Re Utility Location 

Eighteen states have constitutional provisions governing the location of public utility facilities within public rights-of-way; 

Massachusetts is not one of them. Mostly, these state constitutions require the utility to obtain consent of the local governing body before occupying the way, and do not directly cover the problem of relocation of facilities.

Statutory provisions permitting the use of public rights-of-way by public utilities are found in every state in the union. Fortythree states, including Massachusetts, have laws specifically applicable to the occupation of state highways as distinguished from other types of public highways. The half dozen remaining states have statutes applicable to all public roads. 

Various kinds of statutory restrictions are usually placed on utility occupancy of public rights-of-way. In 14 states (Massachusetts included) and Hawaii, a franchise, permit or other permission is required before occupancy of the highway right-of-way by any utility. In 26 states the same requirement holds for designated utilities only. The few remaining jurisdictions distinguish between occupancy by designated utilities of (a) all public highways, and (b) state highways only.

Such statutory provisions sometimes require utilities to conform to regulations promulgated by the state highway department. 

Massachusetts is among 17 states which have laws containing such requirements for all utilities. Similar laws applicable to only specified utilities are in force in 18 states and Hawaii; four other states and the District of Columbia also authorize relevant regulations to be promulgated by authorized agencies.

JUDICIAL DECISIONS AND OPINIONS 

Problems at Issue in State Courts In several state courts which have considered the principle of reimbursing utilities for relocated facilities on public rights-of-way, views have been sharply divided as to the equity and constitutionality of related statutory provisions. Between the rationale of the 

Minnesota and New Mexico courts there is a wide gulf. This division of thought is true even for states which have similar economic circumstances and constitutional language. Such was the case, for example, with the Maine and New Hampshire courts, yet their opinions were at opposite poles.

While the issues and circumstances in no two courts have been exactly the same, the problem can be narrowed to these three basic constitutional issues: (1) the lending of state credit to a private corporation, (2) the diversion of constitutionally protected highway funds for use for non-highway purposes, and (3) the placing of a limitation on state police power. Another area of debate is outside the constitutional framework and concerns the problem of equity; strong economic arguments in support of "equitable" solutions to the problem have not fared well when exposed to legalistic consideration.

Should terms like "construction," "incidental," and "improvement" be defined so as to include or exclude project costs of moving a utility facility? What are the primary purposes of a highway and what are its essential parts?

Settlement of the lesser points of legal conflict is often vital to the main struggle. As indicated in an earlier chapter of this report, the 1956 changes in the Massachusetts law (Acts of 1956, C. 718) made provision for permissive reimbursement to utilities, as defined by the 

Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956, for relocation costs necessitated by federal aid highway construction. In order to determine the scope of this amendment, it is desirable to examine the prior status of Massachusetts law.

The related aspects of Massachusetts law prior to the 1956 Act, provided that utilities would only be reimbursed for their relocation costs as a result of highway construction if such relocation interfered with their property rights. There is no question that a utility under these circumstances could be reimbursed if it were required to remove its facilities located in or on land owned by it. 

Similarly, there is no question that a utility could be reimbursed if those facilities were located in or on land owned by another, provided it had an easement or other property right in such land. 

Difficulties arose, however, in connection with the facilities of a utility located within the confines of a public way. An analysis of the various Massachusetts cases relating to this problem indicates that, in general, such utilities did not acquire a property right by locating their facilities in a public way but, instead, acquired a special privilege to enjoy the public right which otherwise existed in said highway. For the interference with such public rights the utilities would not be entitled to reimbursement and must assume the cost of relocating their facilities.

The House Document further states that federal funds used to reimburse the cost of removing utility facilities located within highway rights-of-way have been so used "only to the extent to which the states involved are permitted by their own laws to reimburse therefor." A determination of this problem, however, can not be reached without assurance of one sort or another from the Federal Government itself. 

The foregoing discussion has been directed to the Massachusetts law as it relates to utilities which are not publicly owned. The practice of the Department of Public Works in the past, and prior

1959.] 

to C.718 of the Acts of 1956, has been to reimburse municipalities where such municipalities have been put to the expense of relocating their facilities located in public rights-of-way. The municipality is, of course, a creature of the state. Thus the cost of relocating the facilities owned by a municipality would be a public cost in any event. It would also be a public cost necessitated by highway construction.

For these reasons the past policy of the Commonwealth relative to reimbursements paid to municipally owned utilities appears to have been correct, even though the same policy might not be applied to privately owned utilities.

CHAPTER VI. UTILITY RELOCATION COSTS AND REIMBURSEMENTS 

Nature of Available Data

Accurate analyses of utility relocation costs incident to so huge a highway program for Massachusetts require much data which can only be collected with the cooperation of governmental agencies and of all the utilities in the state, whether publicly or privately owned. Such cooperation calls for reasonably complete information from the utility companies covering (a) past relocation costs, (b) reimbursements received, (c) estimated relocation costs on highway projects which have been planned in detail and (d) rough estimates on proposed projects. 

In the attempt to assemble such statistical data, the Legislative Research Bureau enlisted the aid of the Department of Public Works, the Metropolitan District Commission, and the Department of Public Utilities. Moreover, through the last named agency, the 

Bureau sought the help of all privately and municipally owned public utilities in the Commonwealth.

The Department of Public Works and the Metropolitan District Commission were asked to prepare detailed tabulations of all highway fund expenditures for utility relocation costs on all types of highways in the Commonwealth over the past five years. This period was selected to compare relocation expenditures before and after authorization of the vast new federal highway program in 1956. On the basis of this information, projections of future costs could be made.

The Department of Public Utilities was asked to gather similar information from the utilities under its jurisdiction, distinguishing relocation costs on public rights-of-way from costs on private rights-of-way. In addition this Department was asked to provide detailed cost figures on municipally owned utility facilities. 

From the beginning, this study has been handicapped by the failure of many utilities to give reasonably complete answers to a questionnaire circulated by the Department of Public Utilities. One of the largest of them, the Boston Edison Company, provided no information at all.

Estimates of the savings which have accrued to the Commonwealth from its policy of limited reimbursements for public rightof-way utility relocations, must be based on information obtained at from the few utility companies willing to submit cost figures on past and future relocations. The Department of Public Works, unfortunately, has not collected information about the number and types of utility facilities whose relocation from public rights-ofway are ordered to accommodate highway improvement. Nor does the department keep central records of any estimated total costs thereof. Hence, the accuracy of the cost estimates prepared by the utility companies could not be verified.

[Feb. 

The Department of Public Works has the usual policy of notifying a utility company that its facilities on public rights-of-way must be moved and details of relocation are then carried out on the district level. The actual work of relocation is done by utility company employees, subject to approval by the Department of Public Works when facilities have been reinstalled within the public right-of-way.

The Metropolitan District Commission makes no payments to privately owned utilities for the relocation of their facilities made necessary by MDC highway projects. The Commission is however, required to reimburse the Metropolitan Transit Authority for the cost of relocating MTA facilities and for the cost of the services of MTA employees, such as flagmen, needed to control interrupted street car and bus service while the construction of highway projects is in progress. 

On recently completed MDC projects, the MTA was reimbursed $474,118 for relocation costs. The MDC estimates that it will spend $186,000 to reimburse the MTA for relocation expenses on projects which are now under construction. 

Analysis of the costs of relocating facilities for highway construction must take into account several factors, often variable. 

For example, relocation costs of electric and power utilities will vary widely according to the amount of work done at each particular location, the type of facility affected, the number of circuits involved, and the number of consumers to whom service must be maintained during construction. Costs are relatively small for the reconstruction of rural electric lines to accommodate highway overpasses and the widening of secondary roads. But both overhead and underground urban electric lines which must be relocated for a highway, usually involve considerably more work and much greater expense because of structural complexities, a greater number of circuits and the general load density in an urban area. In some locations, duplicate electrical facilities must be built to supply the two sides of an area originally fed by a single line which is split into two parts by a highway.

CHAPTER VII. MUNICIPALLY-OWNED UTILITIES 

Types of "Municipals"

Since publicly and privately owned utilities both maintain facilities along public rights-of-way, they are affected by highway construction programs which require utility relocations. Massachusetts law, and in the case of federal-aid highways, federal law also, permit reimbursement to utilities forced to relocate property for this reason. In Massachusetts, however, such reimbursement has been made only to municipal utilities. 

The question of reimbursing the private companies has already been considered. The present section deals with the special case of the municipally owned utilities, hereinafter termed "municipals." 

Payment for relocation costs is currently made or anticipated to the following types of utilities: municipal water and sewer systems, electrical police and fire installations, and, where they exist, municipal gas and electric lighting plants. This list does not exhaust the types of municipals which might be eligible for reimbursement. 

However, this term is susceptible of varied interpretations and will be narrowly considered in the present section. 

Municipal water and sewer systems are the most important of this group of undertakings. However, they seem to be less frequently disturbed by highway construction and involve smaller relocation payments. They are predominantly under public ownership, there being relatively few private water companies of any size.

The municipal lighting plants, on the other hand, not only receive a larger amount of relocation funds but do so in circumstances which permit certain comparisons with the private electric companies. In addition, detailed information about the municipal lighting plants may be obtained from the Public Utilities Department, which by law oversees their operations. For these reasons the following discussion will be confined to the municipal lighting plants. The privately owned electrical companies will be discussed only incidentally. It is assumed that general conclusions based on lighting plant operation may be applied without substantial modi-

[Feb. 

fication to other municipal utilities such as water and sewer systems.

The Municipal Lighting Plants 

Municipal lighting plants are not new in this state. The first such plant was authorized by law in 1891. In the preceding year it had been held that municipalities have no inherent or corporate general power to establish lighting plants, Spaulding v. Peabody 

153 Mass. 129, 26 N.E. 421 (1890), motivating the statute which was promptly enacted (Acts, 1891, c. 370). By 1909 there were 

20 municipal plants and this number increased to the peak of 43 in 1929. Three plants have discontinued operation since then, leaving a total of 40. Four municipalities distributing electricity also distribute gas. In addition eight towns are served by plants in a neighboring city or town.

The plants vary in size from small to moderate. The smallest, in Russell, is a plant which originally cost $50,700. The largest, in 

Holyoke, is an electric plant valued at $11,005,700 and a gas plant at $4,317,860. All the plants are expected to be self supporting and seem to be solvent and even to be operating at a profit with one or two exceptions. They are required by law to use the yearly excess of revenue over operating expenses to meet debt service costs on bonds which may not have more than a 20 year life and also to provide for depreciation before their earnings may be put to any other use. They are also required by law to repay money appropriated to them by the municipality. 

There is nothing to prevent operation of municipals on a nonprofit basis, provided the plant meets statutory requirements. But the term "profit" must be taken in a special sense, because publicly owned plants tend to charge lower rates. Not all of them are able to achieve this objective and some of the smaller plants have distinctly higher rates than the larger private companies. With the larger municipals the comparison becomes more favorable. 

While it is difficult to make an exact comparison of rates because of varying schedules of charges, locations, customer density and other operating conditions, some assistance is obtained by comparing typical bills. Comparison of residential bills for con-

1959.] 

sumption of 40 kilowatt hours, 100 KWH, 250 KWH and 500 KWH¹ shows an appreciable advantage to the ratepayer in the case of the four largest municipal plants in Braintree, Chicopee, Holyoke and Taunton.

In general the profit derived from a municipal plant must be viewed as a compromise between (a) the need to maintain and improve the plant while also using part of its earnings for the costs of government, and (b) the need to justify the plant's existence in terms of the rate schedule of a business enterprise. This is not an abstract problem when the ratepayers are also voters. 

None of the municipalities having a gas plant manufacture their gas. Of the 40 municipalities with electric plants, those in Braintree, Hudson, Ipswich and Taunton produce all their own current. 

Holyoke produces all but a small amount of its current — 95% two years ago. Peabody produces about half and Marblehead less than 

4 percent. The other municipals buy their current. One of them not only buys its current but resells part of it to three neighboring towns.

It is also necessary to consider their relationship to state and municipal government. Statements have been made which imply that the municipals are quasi-independent agents which could, if need be, meet the costs of relocation by charging more for electric current or gas.

So far as the law is concerned the municipals are unable to act on their own accord. They are municipal property, owned and operated by the municipality. Its citizens, who in a corporate sense own the plant and are obliged by law to pass on important decisions affecting it, either delegate decisions to officers they have elected or meet problems by direct vote (a 2/3 vote is usually required). The same citizens of course are the customers and ratepayers of their utility. The significance of this dual role is that in situations of various kinds, these citizens are not apt to see themselves or plant operations exclusively from the viewpoint of plant profits. In general, any statement respecting a plant's capacity to pay relocation costs is a statement respecting the capacity of the city or town to do so. 

As such it is inevitably involved with the issue of municipal finance.

Municipals are subject to the laws of the Commonwealth. With respect to finance these laws operate largely as prior requirements. 

Once these requirements are met, however, decisions respecting rates, plant earnings and the disposition of surplus profit become matters of municipal policy. This policy may vary from a rate of profit which ranges from the maximum reflecting the highest rates the public will accept, down to the status of non-profit operations. 

Statutory requirements may be considered under the two headings of (a) the requirements of finance and (b) the requirements of operation: (a) Finance. As indicated above the General Laws authorize the operation of municipal gas or electric plants (G.L. c. 164, s. 34). 

The local decision to acquire a plant can only be made in the case of a city by a 2/3 vote of the city council or a majority vote of the city commissioners (G.L. c. 164, s. 35). In the case of a town a 

2/3 vote by ballot is required at each of two special town meetings. (s. 36).

[Feb. 

Specific authorization to incur debt for purchasing, extending or enlarging plant is provided; thus a 2/3 vote is required for major reconstructions, enlargements, or extensions, in conformity with statutory provisions governing indebtedness beyond the general debt limit. (ss. 40, 41). Another section authorizes the municipality to appropriate money for a plant in advance of receipts and to pay its bills on account (s. 57A). The municipality pledges its faith and credit when money is borrowed.

The large sums of money needed in the purchase or construction. of a utility plant or in major reconstruction thereof usually require a bond issue which is apt to require borrowing beyond the general debt limit. In the case of a lighting plant the statutes authorize cities and towns to borrow money outside the general debt limit to an outstanding indebtedness of 5 percent of the last preceding assessed property valuation, the bonds to mature within 20 years. (G.L. c. 44, s. 8, as amended by Acts of 1958, C. 70). Cities and towns, and certain districts are authorized, by a majority vote, to issue 1-year notes in anticipation of revenue from certain sources, including lighting plants, in an amount not to exceed the previous year's tax levy plus corporation and income tax. (G.L. c. 44, s. 4). (b) Operation. The statutes also contain the important provisions respecting plant operation and the integration of the plant with municipal government. (G.L. c. 164, ss. 55-58). The municipality may decide to put administration of the authorized plant under an elected board which may build, buy or lease a plant and maintain and operate it (s. 55). Moneys earned by the plant must be paid over to the city or town treasurer (s. 56). Various other statutory provisions indicate the scope of operating control permitted the municipalities.

CHAPTER VIII. PROS AND CONS OF PUBLIC PAYMENT OF UTILITY RELOCATION COSTS 

This chapter presents a general discussion pro and con of government vs. utility company payment of the utility relocation costs made necessary by the huge federal-aid highway system now under way throughout all the states of the nation. It is drawn from arguments in all corners of the nation and endeavors to give the broad background implicit in the clashing attitudes on the various major subjects indicated below by center headings. Their application to the Massachusetts scene will of course be tempered by qualifying considerations imbedded in the law of this Commonwealth. 

LEGITIMATE COST v. GIFT OF PUBLIC FUNDS Pro-Reimbursement — Utility relocation costs on public rights of-way which are made necessary by the federal highway program are an improper burden on utility customers and are only properly chargeable to the highway projects. Federal assumption of such costs should not be made to turn on considerations of local law or contractual obligations or the exercise of police power by the states or municipalities. This leads only to discrimination against utility users, and produces confusion and delay. 

Anti-Reimbursement - The utilities want the issue decided on economic grounds instead of constitutional grounds. Their arguments do not raise a constitutional question - they are no more than pleas for sympathy. Before there can be a claim of a utility against the state which is based upon equity, justice or honor, some kind of obligation must have been incurred by the state. The utilities want a gift; they want compensation for the loss of something they never had either as a property right or some other form of ownership.

If a reimbursement is not to be a gift, the state must receive direct tangible benefits, yet in making such payment to the utilities the state would receive no consideration in return. Because the State or Federal Government initiated the highway program which causes the expense, it does not become constitutionally permissible for the state to donate funds to equalize the burden. It is still a gift under any guise.

[Feb. SENATE ― 

RIGHTS OF UTILITIES IN THE PUBLIC RIGHT-OF-WAY 

Pro-Reimbursement - Once utilities occupy public ways, even though occupancy is free, they acquire vested rights. They are neither trespassers nor tenants at will. State and local governments cannot subsequently impair such rights without valid reason. 

Governments cannot compel utilities to relocate facilities unless there is an obstruction or hampering of free public travel. It is agreed that the government may exercise the police power if there is danger to the public. It is not conceded that the proposed interstate system of highway improvement, which provides general benefits to the general public rather than local residents, is a proper or reasonable exercise of the local police power. 

Anti-Reimbursement -Utilities never acquire vested rights from occupancy of public rights-of-way. Their "rights" are more correctly described as a privilege. Their occupancy is usually free of charge, or at a nominal cost, and such occupancy is always subject to the police power, which a government cannot contract or bargain away. The so-called vested right of a utility is vested only to the extent that it (a) can be alienated by the utilities, (b) can be taxed by the government, (c) cannot be arbitrarily extinguished by the government which permits utilities to use the public rights-of-way. The courts have held that highway improvement is an activity for which a government can legitimately exercise the police power. Utility facilities in many instances do impair the free, unhampered and safe flow of traffic on an improved highway and the courts have held that such impairment to the highways is within the terms of contracts prohibiting the obstruction of normal use of the highways. 

DISTINGUISHING FEATURES OF FEDERAL-AID HIGHWAYS 

Pro-Reimbursement - The federal-aid improvements are designed to benefit the public at large, rather than a local community and are in effect not a valid exercise of a state's police powers. 

Their very nature underscores the problem - they are usually huge, costly undertakings on interstate systems involving costly plant relocation. Since the relocation is initiated as a requirement of the government, it is in reality in the nature of a tax rather than a planned expenditure of private management. Even if benefits

1959.] 

do accrue to the utility, e.g. elimination of obsolete equipment or provision for anticipated future growth, these are forced benefits requiring investment of funds long before they are normally needed. Often, modern and adequate facilities must be relocated with no better service resulting.

This highway activity is a national program, not a local program, and the Federal Government establishes, controls, and supervises all highway construction. Hence, the states are acting as mere agents of the national government and cannot legitimately apply their police powers.

Anti-Reimbursement - To the contrary, the local community does benefit from the federal-aid highway program. New and better highways will increase traffic and commerce, the by-products of which will be increased local land values and increased local business. Local transportation will benefit through decreased costs, and easier and quicker movement, thus making for all around efficiency. Since local rate payers make up the local community, they will benefit as individuals and as a group. 

Modern highways are vast and costly construction projects designed not only as essential to national defense and interstate commerce, but also for the safety, convenience, health and welfare of the travelling public. Ratepayers are part of this public and derive these benefits as well as non-rate payers. Ratepayers will continue to be served by utility facilities which freely use the public right-of-way.

It must be emphasized that highway projects are initiated and controlled solely by the states. There is no requirement by the 

Federal Government that states shall initiate any highway project. 

In offering financial aid to states, the Federal Government does specify minimum standards to be met but the state can reject these standards if it does not elect to participate in the federal-aid program. This sole sanction wielded by the Federal Government of withholding of funds until its minimum standards are met, does not make the state a federal agent. 

UTILITY RATEPAYERS AND DOUBLE TAXATION 

Pro-Reimbursement - As general taxpayers, utility ratepayers contribute to the cost of highway construction when they pay their

[Feb. 

federal taxes. Simultaneously, in the form of higher utility rates, they pay the cost of relocating facilities which is caused by this very highway construction which usually benefits "Thruway" traffic rather than local ratepayers who may be highway-users. The ratepayers receive no better utility service from such relocation. 

Moreover, utility ratepayers, when they pay their bills, also contribute toward the corporation taxes levied on all businesses. 

Anti-Reimbursement - A substantial segment of federal-aid highway funds come from motor vehicle fuel and excise taxes, not from general funds. Thus, highway users are large contributors. 

Payment of reimbursement costs from federal-aid funds will result either in less road construction or in higher taxes to meet the increased costs. If utility ratepayers do not benefit from relocation, can it be said that highway users who pay for the roads, allegedly "Thruway" traffic, benefit from the free use of the road by utilities? Ratepayers, if they are so burdened by a third tax, are also relieved from a heavy fourth burden, the cost of acquiring rights-of-way which the utilities now occupy and have occupied free of charge.

THE BURDEN ON UTILITIES 

Pro-Reimbursement - Small private utilities and many municipal utilities will founder because of relocation costs due to highway construction. They cannot maintain reserves to cope with such expenses, which are usually unpredictable or unexpected because of the lack of information about highway routes. Moreover, merely because of the accident of location, only a few utilities within the state are penalized. While great hardship will result from some utilities in the absence of reimbursement, there is no great burden on the Federal Government which can readily assume almost the entire cost.

Anti-Reimbursement - Insofar as it is possible, highway construction is planned far enough in advance of the actual work to allow utilities to make proper adjustments to meet the situation. 

The public utility bodies allow a fair return for investors; thus rates are set to provide for such contingencies. No investor can be hurt since relocation costs are the burden of the consumer. 

Regardless of how slight the overall burden would be to the

1959.] SENATE — 

No. 501. 

Federal Government if utilities were relieved of this fiscal responsibility, local projects would be cut back, because of the diversion of construction funds to cover relocation costs. This diversion would in turn lower the matching funds put up by the participating state now on a 90%-10% basis and thus further reduce available highway construction funds. And it would establish a dangerous precedent for future diversion of highway funds for non-highway purposes. 

NON-RAIL UTILITIES AND ALLEGED DISCRIMINATION 

Pro-Reimbursement Railroads are privately-owned, profitmaking public utilities yet they are reimbursed for relocation costs. 

If any utility is dangerous and inconvenient to the highway traveling public, it is the railroad. But railroads are reimbursed when they are required to eliminate grade crossings, even though these changes often benefit the railroads by facilitating operations and reducing the possibility of accidents and consequential tort liability. 

Reimbursement would clearly discriminate against non-rail utilities.

Anti-Reimbursement — The danger from grade crossings is more the result of increased highway traffic than of any railroad cause. 

Reimbursement for grade crossing eliminations is merely government recognition that the railroads had a valid claim in that they were being asked to relocate their own rights-of-way to accommodate a competing form of transportation. Where benefits accrue to the railroads they are required to contribute up to 10% of the cost of the project. 

OTHER ALLEGED INEQUITIES 

Pro-Reimbursement - There is discrimination by the state when it chooses to reimburse municipally-owned utilities and not privately-owned utilities. Some municipally-owned utilities are larger than private utilities and operate outside municipal boundaries, with substantial revenues and are better able to absorb relocation costs than many small privately-owned utilities. Among privately or publicly-owned utilities, the burden falls only on those in the path of highway construction, many of which are small companies which do not have the financial means of meeting such costs as do the large utilities.

[Feb. SENATE - 

No. 501. 

Anti-Reimbursement - The state, in choosing to reimburse municipally-owned utilities, does no more than transfer the burden from an inferior unit of government to the superior unit which is better able to bear it. Where there is an obvious case of inequity or when an extraordinary situation warrants governmental relief, the state, if it chooses to do so, can so provide relief for the private utility thus affected.

VARIED POINTS OF ISSUE 

Pro-Reimbursement - The state should not be hesitant about reimbursing utilities because the Federal Government assumes 90% of the costs on interstate projects, anyway. Such a policy would benefit the state's citizens who are consumers and utility ratepayers.

Relocation costs are obviously an integral element in highway construction; otherwise Congress would not have made possible such reimbursement when in accord with state law. 

States which refuse to reimburse utilities on the basis of diverting highway funds are adopting a position which penalizes their own taxpayers because the Federal Government uses motor vehicle revenues collected in such states to make reimbursements to other states which do reimburse utilities. Thus, in "non-reimbursable" states the local road users who may also be ratepayers, are contributing to road construction and utility relocation payments in other states.

In the past, utilities could bear these costs but the vast, modern highway program in many instances makes this the new burden of utility relocations almost prohibitive.

Anti-Reimbursement - The state should be most hesitant and reluctant about reimbursing utilities. Once a state is committed to reimbursement, the state will think twice about allowing future free locations on public rights-of-way and will probably deny free use as a protective move to cover possible future relocation costs. 

The utilities would in turn have to seek private rights-of-way at inflated prices or else pay a substantial rental fee. Thus, ratepayers would be further penalized. The argument that the Federal Government will pick up the major share of the check is misleading because that share is subtracted from funds made avail-

1959.] 

able for actual road construction, thus reducing total mileage to be built.

Congress provided reimbursement only where state laws are not in conflict with the federal offer. Most states, including Massachusetts, have constitutional provisions prohibiting diversion of highway funds for other than specific highway items. It is for a state to decide whether or not utility relocations are integral, incidental, or alien to highway construction costs.

States which do reimburse utilities from highway funds are penalizing those taxpayers who support the highway construction program through various highway user taxes by taking money intended for highway construction and using it to defray operational expenses of private enterprise. 

Most utilities can and should bear this cost as an obligation at common law. When the Federal Government made available such large sums for highway purposes, utilities wrote off their common law obligations and pressured the Federal Government to write the reimbursement feature into statutory law so as to obtain a large share of highway purpose money. 

PUBLIC CONVENIENCE AND UTILITIES 

Pro-Reimbursement - Public highways are used not only for transportation but also for various forms of communication which are in keeping with the public health, safety and welfare. Governments in the past offered utilities free occupancy to induce quick expansion of services. Utilities in order to accommodate this demand for service, located on public ways and thus unwisely, as it develops, forfeited the opportunity to buy private rights-of-way which at today's prices they do not have the means to acquire. If the utilities had expected their consumers to be the future victims of highway relocations, then they would have forsaken free occupancy of public ways even though consumers would have had to pay higher rates. At least the latter choice would have resulted in a compensable injury.

Anti-Reimbursement - Highway users do not question the right of utilities to occupy public rights-of-way free of charge, if the government so sanctions. They merely point out that such use is a permissive one, that it is secondary and subordinate to the primary

[Feb. 

use and purpose of highways which is for the traveling public in moving vehicles. It is because the utilities have had free use of the public highway rights-of-way that they have been able to grow and expand quickly and thus reward their investors and provide consumers with lower rates. Governments with jurisdiction over occupied public ways in the meantime have lost a sizeable revenue source which could provide taxpayers with some relief. The taxpayers, who have not demanded relief from this potential source, are now asked to shoulder the added cost of moving such facilities. 

Any utility which presently occupies public rights-of-way was either ignorant or poorly advised, if it originally did so under the impression that it was not subject to a reasonable exercise of the police power. Even if a utility had elected to buy private rightsof-way in the past, that would have been far more expensive to the company than to now bear the cost of a few relocations.

APPENDIX E JUDICIAL OPINION ON USE OF MASS. HIGHWAY FUND FOR UTILITY EXPENDITURES INCIDENT TO HIGHWAY PROJECTS SYNOPSIS: Opinion of the Justices, 324 Mass. 746 (1949) 

In 1949 a proposed bill would have amended Acts of 1947, c. 544, by declaring the subways, tunnels, viaducts, elevated structures and rapid transit extensions used by the Metropolitan Transit Authority to be public highways or bridges within the meaning of Article 78 of the Amendments to the Constitution. It further provided payments from the highway fund to the MTA for the maintenance and repair of the aforementioned facilities. The reasoning behind the proposed bill was that these facilities were constructed for the purpose of relieving traffic on the surface of streets and ways of communities served by the MTA and still materially contribute to the relief of such traffic. 

The General Court propounded three questions to the Supreme Judicial Court. The first question asked if it were constitutional to declare these facilities to be public highways or bridges within the meaning of Article 78. The second and third questions concerned the constitutionality of expending sums from the highway fund for maintaining and repairing these facilities. 

The Justices answered negatively to each of the three questions. 

In their Opinion, the Justices quoted Tax Commissioner v. Putnam 

227 Mass. 524 where it was said that the Constitution is to be interpreted in the light of the conditions under which it and its several parts were framed, the ends which it was designed to accomplish, the benefits which it was expected to confer, and the evils which it was hoped to remedy. 

In General Outdoor Advertising Co. Inc. v. Department of Public 

Works 289 Mass. 158, it was further said that an Amendment to the Constitution is to be construed in the light of its circumstances

[Feb. 

under which it was framed, the causes leading to its adoption, the imperfections hoped to be remedied, and the ends designed to be accomplished ... Its words are to be given their natural and obvious sense according to common and approved usage at the time of its adoption.

On diversion of highway fund revenue for non-highway purposes, the Justices wrote: "At the time of the adoption of that article (78) there existed the Highway Fund set up in accordance with G.L. (Ter. Ed.) c. 90, s. 34, as amended, and devoted by that section to certain specific purposes. These purposes were for the carrying out of the provisions of law relative to the use of motor vehicle fuels, for the maintenance and construction of town and county highways and of State highways and bridges, for engineering services and expenses, for the purchase and care of road building machinery, for snow removal, for the maintenance of signs and care of shrubs and trees on State highways, for expenses incidental to these purposes, to meet the requirements of highway and grade crossing bonds, to meet the requirements of certain metropolitan parks loans and payments 'out of receipts from motor vehicle fees for particular traffic routes now or hereafter authorized,' for the maintenance and construction of boulevards, and for the maintenance in part of the State police, the department of the State auditor, the department of the State treasurer, and the commission on administration and finance. It will be seen that the purposes to which the Highway Fund could be devoted under the general statute in force when art. 78 of the Amendments was adopted were with sufficient certainty intended to be limited to expenditure for purposes connected with roads which were available for use by motor vehicles and with the enforcement of laws affecting such roads and the partial support of state departments having to do in part with the administration and expenditure of the fund.... When art. 78 was adopted, under 

G.L. (Tel. Ed.) c. 90, s. 34, none of the fund could be used for purposes in which owners of motor vehicles as such were not interested.... The conclusion is irresistible that the people of the Commonwealth in adopting art. 78 of the Amendments in-

1959.] 107 

tended to make sure that the moneys exacted from owners of motor vehicles should be used solely for the purposes of highways and bridges for the use of such vehicles and could not have supposed that the highways referred to in the Amendment would include structures. ... of which motor vehicles could make no use. "Article 78 of the Amendments... must be interpreted to forbid the diversion of revenue.... The Legislature is bound by that article as adopted and cannot now by enacting a declaratory statute change the meaning of the article so as to permit such diversion."

APPENDIX F FEDERAL SHARING OF STATE REIMBURSEMENTS OF COSTS OF UTILITY RELOCATIONS (U. S. Code, Title 23, C. I, S. 123) 

General Eligibility. Under what general conditions can the state and the utility expect the relocation to qualify for federal funds? 

The law provides that federal funds may participate in an amount actually paid by a state or a political subdivision thereof for the costs of utility relocations made under one or more of the following conditions. (a) Where the utility occupies its existing location by reason of holding the fee, an easement or other property interest. (b) Where the utility occupies publicly owned land or a public right-of-way and the State certifies that payment for the utility relocation does not violate State law or any contract between the utility and the State. If the question arises as to the State's authority to pay for a relocation, then the State may be required to cite or establish such authority. (c) Where the utility which occupies publicly owned lands or a public right-of-way is owned by an agency or political subdivision of a State and the law or agreement does not require relocation of facilities at expense of said agency or subdivision.

With respect to (b) above, the Bureau of Public Roads is responsible, when there is any question of a State's authority to pay for relocation, to require a State to furnish complete and adequate evidence of its authority. This may lead to a formal opinion of the 

State's Attorney-General or even to a court decision. Further, each relocation is expected to qualify for federal funds on its own merits. 

Submission of Project Plans. After furnishing evidence of authority to pay, a state must submit its proposed programs and projects to the Bureau of Public Roads for approval. Where relo-

1959.109 

cation or adjustment of utility facilities are contemplated the detail furnished by the State in support of its program must include appropriate references to this work. This program is the first notice of record in the Bureau that a State intends to apply federal funds to highway project costs at certain locations. 

The Bureau recommends that at this point the State and utility conduct a preliminary field inspection of utility facilities which may be affected and to reach a preliminary agreement relative to work and the estimated cost thereof. Approval of a State's program by the Bureau reserves federal funds for program items. Federal funds are not obligated until the division engineer authorizes the State to proceed with any item of work included in the program submitted by the State. 

Authorization. The Bureau's division engineer may authorize the State to proceed with utility relocation: (a) On or after the date the utility relocation is included in an approved program as an item of right-of-way acquisition or construction. (b) At such time as the division engineer has been furnished and has reviewed plans and estimates which report adequately the utility work proposed and the location of the highway project and the utility relocation. (c) When the division engineer has been furnished and has reviewed the proposed agreement between the State and the utility.

In short, utility work may be authorized at such time after approval of the State's program as the State and the utility can give the division engineer evidence of the work to be done, the need for the work and their proposed agreement thereon. 

Formalizing Agreements. Formal agreement between the State and the utility must be reached and the final utility relocation must fall within the requirements of the approved project plans before federal funds can become operative. The formal agreement should specify the work to be done, cost accounting methods and should be supported by reliable cost estimates and relocation plans. 

The executed agreement between the State and the utility must be approved by the division engineer, and the work itself executed, before federal funds can be used to reimburse.

Operation of Plan. Unless otherwise provided, this is the utility's responsibility and it is expected that all of the work incident to the utility relocation will be by or under the immediate supervision of the utility company involved. The utility is also responsible for the maintenance of cost records. Each utility develops costs under its own prescribed accounting system, subject to audit examination to determine the representativeness of the system. 

With respect to replacing old structures during relocation operations, if a consistent pattern develops under audit examinations showing actual improvements in the capital structure of a utility which are out of balance with the cost borne with federal funds, it is certain that the Bureau of Public Roads will take a long second look at this provision.
