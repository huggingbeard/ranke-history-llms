India the Land 

A year ago India seemed a safe and quiet place. It lay 4,600 miles from Japan. It appeared to be well guarded. There is little point in showing how the Japanese changed that situation. It is more important for us to realize that by the spring 

India is big. Her 1,576,000 square miles of land would fill more than half of the continental United 

States. And the soil in general is fertile. India's 390,000,000 people are almost three times the number that our country supports, and more than the combined populations of 

South and North America. India has a fifth of all the people in the world. of 1942 they held Singapore, South Many Contrasts 

Burma, the Dutch East Indies, and islands in the Bay of Bengal. Thus they were at India's doorstep both by land and by water. 

Quite as important, we should understand what India means to Japan-why Japan should want to control her. We should also see clearly what chances Japan has for seizing India, or certain parts of India. But before we turn to such matters, let us pause a moment to get a picture in outline of the land we are talking about.

India has been called by many experts less a country than a continent. That is a good description. 

India, hanging from the mass of 

Asia, looks like a big pear turned upside down. She has water on two sides of her. On the third side she is shut off from the rest of Asia by the Himalayas the highest mountains in the world. Thus, geographically, she is a kind of complete unit in herself, as a continent should be. 

India has the contrasts you will find in most continents. On the whole, her climate is warm-hot even. She lies half in the tropical zone. But in the north, in her mountain valleys, the climate is mild. Again, much of India has heavy rains, but some of it, in the northwest, has almost none. And still again, you will find busy cities and carefully tended farms; and not far away you will find wild forests and jungles, with tigers, leopards, crocodiles, snakes, monkeys, and elephants.

India's 390,000,000 people themselves supply many contrasts. In physical appearance, they show more differences than do all the nations of Europe. There are some Indians who are black, with hair and features like Negroes. The majority are very dark, but with straight hair, thin lips and thin noses. There are also Indians with Chinese or 2

Mongolian appearance. There are many millions that are light-skinned, with features like the Europeans. Some look like Spaniards or 

Greeks or Serbians. Then there are mixtures of all these types. 

The Indians have their own costumes-turbans and flowing robes, embroidered jackets and loose trousers.

The laborers often wear only turbans and loin-cloths. The merchants and other well-to-do people wear full Indian costumes, or a mixture of Indian and European clothes.

Religions in India also vary. 

Hinduism is followed by the largest number. Mohammedanism comes second. There are also Christians, Jains, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis.

Both Hinduism and Mohammed anism are religions with rules for daily living.

Hinduism, for example, teaches that no animal life should be destroyed, and most Hindus are vegetarians. The idea of caste also comes from Hindu practice. People are divided into different social groups, which have special privileges and do not inter-marry. The Brahmans are the highest caste-they supply the priests, the teachers, the doctors, and the lawyers. The soldiers are next in rank, then the merchants. A number of Indians engage in occupations which are thought by the Hindus to be of base character. They can belong to no caste and are called the "depressed classes" or the "untouchables," since an Indian of regular caste who is touched by one of these persons must at once purify himself. Such are the fishermen, shoemakers (the first take life and the second use the skins of animals), the carpenters, scavengers, streetsweepers, and others. Caste is now breaking down considerably among educated Hindus. 

Mohammedanism permits the eating of meat, but not of any meat from the pig. It brought to India the idea of keeping women in the home, away from the eyes of strangers, or heavily veiled. This custom was adopted by many Hindus. 

There are 225 languages spoken in India, and there are almost 600 governments under the central British control. We shall come back to government later on. 

Finally, India is very rich-and she is bitterly poor.

That is, she has great resources, and produces greatly; but she does not house, feed, clothe, and educate most of her people well. So we can think of great rice fields, of plantations of tea, coffee, tobacco, cotton; of wheat and barley fields; of millions of cattle and sheep. We can think of mineral deposits, of fine lumber, of and gems and gums spices. We can picture to ourselves prosperous farmers, wealthy princes, rich merchants, alert lawyers and bankers. But at the same time

we must think of hundreds of millions of ill-clad, hungry people, living in crowded city tenements or in village huts without windows, existing on rice and a few vegetables, never getting the nourishment they should have. These masses work long hours for little pay, are often wasted with sickness. Practically none of them can read or write.

Biggest Prize 

India has meant a great deal to 

Britain. Prime Minister Churchill recently called her "that most truly bright and precious jewel in the crown of the King." Englishmen have invested money in India and got good returns. Many have had careers there as officials. Indian trade has been important to Britain. Indian cotton has gone to British textile mills; Indian jute, lac, linseed, and hides to other factories; Indian rice, tea, and wheat to English tables. 

The British have sold manufactured goods of many kinds to the Indians. 

Of the British colonies, India has al ways sent more products to Britain, and taken more products from her, than any other.

India would also mean much to Japan. She would be commercially valuable Japan could use India's iron deposits right now. She could use her cotton, mica, manganese, copper, leather. But there are other reasons why Japan would like to control India-many others. Let us look at them. They may help us understand why there will be a fight for India, and why it will be a bitter, desperate fight.

By taking India, Japan could rob England of great resources. For example, she could prevent Britain from getting Indian products. She could also prevent more Indians from swelling the British armies.

Indians in this war have fought for 

Britain in Egypt, Ethiopia, Syria, and Malaya. More than 200,000 of them have seen action. 

Holding India, Japan could also strike hard blows at the war effort of Britain and her allies. India holds a key position in the British Empire. 

She lies between Australia and Africa. From India Japan could pounce upon ships going from Egypt or

South Africa to Sydney or Mel bourne. She could also send out raiders to blast British and American ships coming around the southern tip of Africa from the west, and carrying war supplies north to 

Egypt or to Russia by way of Iran. (See map on pages 10 and 11 of this pamphlet.) The damage the Japanese could do to the United Nations from India is too clear to need description.

By winning India, Japan would shut off all war supplies to China. 

That is certain, of course, except for what China might get by way of Russia (which would be very

little, as roads are poor, and Russia has few if any war materials to ´spare). There are only two ways of feeding the Burma Road, China's supply route from the South. One is through Burma, and Japan now controls that. The other is through northern India. The map across pages 10 and 11 shows how aid to China could be cut off from India. 

To capture India would help Japan keep what she has already won in the Orient. This is also clear, for 

India is one of two bases in which Britain and the United States can prepare for an attack on Japan.

Take India out of the picture, and there is only Australia left. Having only one base to worry about instead of two would be a great help to the Japanese.

Winning India would extend Japan's control over most of Asia outside Russia. That is, it would bring a large territory and 420,000,000 more people (counting Ceylon and 

Burma) under Japan. With China,

Japan would rule more than one billion subjects-half the people of the world. She would truly have a "new order in Asia.”

Winning India would bring Japan valuable prestige. Japan likes prestige-glory, of course. But she surely would gain much more than glory from conquering India. She would gain other victories-many of them with no fighting, all with less fighting. India is the greatest of British possessions. Britain is one of the greatest world powers. If

Japan took India, people throughout 

Asia would say: "That is a great feat. Perhaps nobody can beat Japan." The peoples in lands held by

Japan, who might like to rebel, would be discouraged. Instead of fighting they might say: "What's the use? She conquered India. We can't fight her." That is the kind of talk Japan would like to hear. 

It is worth much to her. 

Can She Do It? 

There, then, are six good reasons why Japan should strike at India now. The Germans always knew that winning India would be of high importance. They have said for several years: "We shall come down through Turkey or Russia; 

Japan will come down by China and

Thailand. We shall meet in India, and the British Empire will collapse."

The Germans have not carried out their part of this dream, but Japan has almost completed hers. The only question is, Can she now take 

India, or control her? 

We say "take or control" because once the Japanese had Singapore, Sumatra, and lower Burma, they could determine what kind of war they would make on India. They might try to conquer the whole land. They might try to seize only

certain districts-the seaports and naval bases. To get Indian iron, to control the Indian Ocean, and to cut off aid to China are the things the Japanese want most to accomplish. Time only can tell just what they will attempt.

Japan has great advantages over the British and the Indians. Such advantages may be temporary. The 

British and the Indians may overcome them. But they are great.

Japan, for example, has more warships near India than the British have. More important, she has more airplanes of all types. She has more tanks and heavy artillery.

All these items are of the greatest importance in modern machine war. 

In fact, they are the four big "musts" of such war. To be sure, we don't know just how great a superiority Japan has in any one of them. We don't know how long her superiority may last. But while it lasts, it is important. 

Another factor that favors Japan is the remoteness of India from places that could send her supplies of aircraft, big guns, tanks, and ships. India is between 13,000 and 

14,000 miles by sea from New

York. She's almost as far from London. She is 3,600 miles even from 

Egypt. If India has to depend upon getting more war supplies from the outside, Japan may be able to win before enough of these supplies can arrive. "But," it will be said, "India makes war materials herself. She has great manpower. Why should she need help from Britain or America?"

India does make war materials. 

She has helped Britain greatly by manufacturing clothes, shoes, tools, drugs, rifles, machine guns, mortars, ammunition, and thousands of other items that are used in war. Of 

60,000 articles needed for a modern army, India makes 45,000. 

But she doesn't make aircraft engines, or motors for tanks, or large artillery. She makes few tanks and airplanes.

Of course, India has men enough. 

She has a million in training. More are joining up-60,000 a month. 

Japan can't transport and supply more than a quarter of a million soldiers for an attack on India. But 

India's men will need aircraft and big artillery and motor vehicles to beat off the Japanese.

There is one more factor on which the Japanese have counted—the Indian desire to be free of Britain, and the refusal of Indian patriots to support the war unless Britain promised them full independence and gave immediate responsibility for defense to Indian leaders. The Indian National Congress party, the most powerful political force in the country, protested against Britain's bringing India into the present war in 1939 without the consent of the

Indian people. For the next two and a half years Congress members refused to cooperate with the British in the war effort. "India can not associate herself with a war said to be for democratic freedom, when that very freedom is denied her," they said.

Japan hoped to fan such dissatisfaction into civil war. If she could, her job in India might have become an easy one.

Road to Freedom 

There is no doubt that the mood of the Indian patriots in 1941 was not a pleasant one. They had felt for some time that India should be free. They felt strongly, even bitterly, about it. 

To understand India today, and

India as she has been for the last twenty years, we should understand the growth of this passion for independence. We can give the story of that growth in outline only. Yet perhaps we can thus suggest its importance in the present crisis. 

The British took over political control of India from about 1750 on. At that time, the East India 

Company had some small possessions in India, and a keen interest in trade. The period was one of great disorder. The Company organized native soldiers and conquered one section of India after another. By 

1856 the entire land was under its authority. In 1858 the British government took over the rule from the Company.

During these years the British had established law and order. Their armies kept the land at peace. Their courts won a reputation for giving justice to rich and poor alike. India had never been one country before the British came. Now it was unified under a central government, although many native princes were still permitted to rule their own states.

Looking back, the British feel that they have done many good things for India. They have brought her peace, they say; stable government; better health and educational services; better farming methods, including extensive irrigation; and many new crops and industries. They feel that they have also helped India toward self-government.

As we shall see, this is not exactly the Indian patriot's view of what has happened. But let us look at that view later, and consider for a moment the way in which the idea of Indian freedom grew.

It began during the latter 1800's. 

Englishmen helped to get it started, and the Government of India was friendly to the program. It was a vague one at first. Indians were simply to get more opportunities to act as officials. They did. The Government appointed Indians to a growing number of posts. Later,

some native officials were elected. 

But the changes, although numerous, seemed not enough to the Indian patriots.

In the early 1900's the latter began to demand more power. They talked of India's becoming a dominion, like Canada. They felt that their civilization was old, and had given much to the world. They were now producing poets like Tagore and scientists like Jagadis Chandra Bose. Indians were doing good work as teachers, doctors, lawyers, merchants. In governmental work some of them had shown high ability. Why should they be a subject people any longer? 

Steps toward Self-rule 

When the first World War came,

Britain met these Indian demands for freedom with a promise. An announcement by the Secretary of

State for India issued on August 

20, 1917, declared that Britain would act on Indian hopes as soon as peace came. She would act to bring about "the gradual development of self-governing institutions with a view to the progressive realization of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire." This meant that in time India would become a dominion.

In 1919 the British moved to keep their promise. By act of the British parliament, the Indians were given a semi-democratic government in which they had a much greater share than in the old one. This government was to last for ten years. Then a commission was to see how it had worked, and make further changes. Perhaps these would be in the direction of fuller freedom. As a matter of fact, the Act of 1935, passed as a result of this commission's study, marked great advances. It gave full self-rule in most matters to the Indian provinces, and established a federal government of both provinces and states which had considerable powers. 

But the Indian patriots were satisfied with neither of these Acts. 

The India Congress party, which was supposed to be representative of all religions and social groups, has steadily demanded full freedomat first, as a dominion; later, as an independent nation. From 1919 on, under the world-known Mohandas 

K. Gandhi and other leaders, like 

Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru, the Congress has demanded that British rule in India cease at once.

Possibly had the Congress been able to speak for all India the British Government and the Indians would have agreed on some course of action. The British say so. They point out that there are many Indian groups that do not belong to the Congress, and do not support

its program. Among these are the 

India Moslem League, the "depressed classes" or "untouchables," the

Sikhs, the native princes, and others. 

Leaders of these various groups have urged different policies. The native princes would like things to remain much as they are. The Moslem 

League wants protection from the 

Hindus. The untouchables say that the Hindus must be checked and controlled. (There are 88,000,000 

Moslems and perhaps 40,000,000 untouchables. Hindus possibly number 256,000,000.) 

What's Wrong? 

The Congress leaders have charged that the British have encouraged discontent of this sort. They say that men like M. A. Jinnah of the 

Moslem League and B. R. Ambedkar, who claims to speak for the untouchables, have small followings. 

For example, they assert that the 

Congress contains more Mohammedans than the Moslem League. 

The Congress proposes to settle group differences by agreement, with possible arbitration by some outside tribunal like the World

Court if agreement is impossible. It stands for full religious freedom and social progress and justice for all Indians.

The Congress's attitude toward the British and British rule might be summed up as follows:

The British have shown a misunderstanding of Indian problems ever since they began to rule in India. Such misunderstandings have produced the Sepoy rebellion (1857) and the Amritsar massacre (1919). ¶ Britain has injured Indian industry and agriculture by making these serve British rather than 

Indian interests. 

She has encouraged bad feeling among Indian groups, seeking to "divide and rule."

By protecting native princes, she has held back the development of India. Most of these princes would long since have lost their thrones but for British support.

Britain has done little for Indian education. Only 10 per cent of 

Indians can read and write.

She has practised discrimination in many branches of government service. For example, only recently have Indians been trained to become commissioned officers in their own army.

Britain has shown by being grudging and slow that she does. not intend to give India full freedom. ¶ Britain has been doling out freedom to the Indians. This, the patriots say, is absurd. Indians themselves, they argue, and not the British, should be permitted to decide what kind of government they will have.

India's Resources 

But, as already pointed out, brave men have to have arms. In Burma and Malaya the Japanese have had superior equipment, and have worsted Indian soldiers. Will they repeat in India?

The situation will be different. 

Certainly the Japanese will not be superior in numbers of men. 

They may be superior in equipment. 

The question will be, How superior?

Unless the invaders come with overwhelming strength of aircraft and tanks and artillery, the Indians may be counted upon to make up with bravery what they lack in arms. 

One unfortunate fact is that most of India's coal and iron lie in the northeastern part of India. (See map on page 12.) Calcutta is the great heavy goods manufacturing center. from Calcutta. From airfields in that region the Japanese could easily bomb Calcutta. The region in which that city lies would be the most convenient in India for them to invade. 

Perhaps they will invade it. They may have invaded it before these words are printed. They may succeed in destroying many of India's young industries. They want iron, and they will also want to cripple India as an arms-producing country. 

It is possibilities like these that we must face. Of course, General

Wavell is in charge of India's defenses. He may have much better resources than he is supposed to have. If so, he will know how to use them.

Nor is Calcutta the only center of manufacturing. Madras, Karachi, Bombay, Lahore, Cawnpore-there are many others. All over India industries are starting up. India is vast, like China. If she should lose one valuable district, that will hurt but not cripple her. As a matter of fact, both coal and iron exist in quantities in many parts of India. (The quality, however, is best in 17

the northeast.) An American mission has gone to India to help India develop her industrial strength. 

Meanwhile, neither Britain nor the United States will sit by idly
