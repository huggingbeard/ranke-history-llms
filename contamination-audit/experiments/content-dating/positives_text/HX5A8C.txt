PREFACE 

FROM childhood all of us have struggled to free ourselves from the bondage of appearances. Years, to be sure, may have carried away from our memories details of the conflict.

Equipped today with superior data and digesting what we have learned, we may smile at a belief in Santa Claus, laugh at the superstitions of the savage, despise the slavery of the sensualist, and coldly pride ourselves in our ability to hold true to substantiated evidence, to distinguish between appearance and reality.

But for all our education and experience, how many of us can free ourselves from seeing the sun set instead of the horizon lift? To most of us is not the sky a vault and the earth a plane? As we look into the heavens at night are not all the stars at an equal distance over our heads? 

If then we cannot without difficulty free ourselves from the bondage of such appearances, how natural is it for the savage to trust all appearances and be their slave? That is, to judge the world as he sees it and attempt to understand it through his senses alone? 

As Professor Garman of Amherst used to say: "Everybody philosophizes; that is, we all start in ignorance in [7]

childhood and all attempt to get some conception of the world in which we live, and all make blunders."

To this the Algonquin Indian was no exception. Judging his world as he saw it, that is, trusting wholly to appearances as his basis for knowledge, he attempted to understand that world through his senses alone. Doing so, his philosophizing became solely a matter of dealing with effects his senses gave. 

Therefore when he found the white man in his forests he concluded the white man was there "because the wood was all gone in the white man's country." When he saw iron axes he wanted to know, "Where does the wood come from that is hard enough to make them?" When he picked up a piece of glass he mistook it for ice and could not understand why it would not melt in his hand. When he felt of cloth he wanted to know, "Of what kind of skin is it made?" And when he held a watch to his ear he asked, "Of what is it the child?"

Dealing thus only with effects his senses gave and encouraged by experiences of dreams, echoes, shadows, reflections, and other phenomena, to his Indian mind almost everything was imbued with life. Pointing, therefore, from one star to another in the cluster of the Pleiades, he would say, "When that star gets where that one is we will start." Stars, clouds, trees, water-falls, rapids, grasses, sun and moon were all alive. Dreams to him, likewise were sense perceptions, standards of certainty, just as real as a burn on his [8]

finger or an echo in his ears.

Discarding judgment, that is, substantiated evidence, as opposed to sense reality, in his eyes things were constantly taking on the invisible. Stars moved from one place to another and disappeared. At night-time there were lots of them in every pond of water, but during the day they were gone. 

Clouds came and went. Now you saw them, now you didn't. The water that was the dew drop on the marsh grass went away in the sunshine. The sun appeared and it was light. The sun disappeared and it was dark. Now the moon was visible, now it was invisible. 

Knowing nothing of what we call natural law and never doubting appearances as his standards of certainty, how did he explain these capricious [9] phenomena? Simply by falling back on his imagination and postulating a fairyland gained through his experience.

Dogmatically, therefore, he believed that all objects including himself had a visible and invisible existence. Dreaming then of going hunting, he did not dream. Rather his invisible or double actually went hunting. Suffering pain, as from a stomach ache, he was not sick. His pain was caused by the presence of some invisible enemy who tortured him. Hence the remedy was not medicine but noise-some hullabaloo that would frighten away his invisible foe. Hearing an echo as he passed some high bluff or bank, it was some invisible, some living spirit who mocked him. He made an offering of

ས་ ་ tobacco or something he prized. The mocking stopped. The spirit was ap peased and he passed the spot, troubled no more. Burying his child, he accordingly put a dog's head by the grave "because the soul of a dog can find its way anywhere and will show to the ignorant child the way to the land of souls."

Seeking thus to explain the unknown by an idea of life, the Algonquin believed in numerous Manitous and unseen powers, with one principal Manitou, however, dominant over all. In other words, having created his fairyland, he now peopled it attractively for himself. Doing so, just as he found in his own experience every factor in the creation of his fairyland, so in peopling it with Manitous and other creations of his imagination he gave such no more sense perceptions than he had in his everyday Indian life. 

You may say this is not real creation, but romance; that such philosophizing is pure fiction gained thru abstraction from one's own experience. 

But as "Manitou and the Animals" was told me by an Algonquin in northern Quebec, it lost nothing of its charm for this reason.

I hope it has lost no more of its charm from my attempt to put it into verse.

It is at least a true chapter in natural history.

THE LEGEND

IN Algonquin land, so long, long ago That moons are no means of measuring time, All things were managed by a Manitou 

And furry animals were in their prime.

Ad-jee-dah-mo, the squirrel, was not small. 

Mus-kwa, the black bear, took no winter's nap, But slept as he chose, curled up in a ball, With Wee-cha, the wash bear, held in his lap. 

The pine marten, Tha, had no orange throat; 

Seganku, the skunk, had no forked white back; 

Shing-gwus, the weasel, never changed his coat;

And Wah-kus, the fox, was blue, white, or black. 

There were twig-eater, whitetail, caribou,

May-hee-gan, the wolf, and the carcajou, Whose thick fur in winter did not turn dark

And who did not hide things just for a lark. 

Tha-cho, the fisher, would scream in his pride; 

Ah-misk, the beaver, would quietly glide 

Into pond and river from grey, green-eyed [IIa]

Pee-shoo, the lynx, or Ne-geek, the otter, As Sang-gwiss, the mink, swam in the water. 

Then Was-usk, the musk rat was big and fat And his tail, like Ah-misk's, was broad and flat. 

Thus it once was in the long, long ago, In Algonquin land under Manitou. 

Calling these creatures together one day,

The mighty Manitou had this to say: "Listen, my children, to a pleasant plan

I have decided on of making man From spruce-gum and honey and flour-dough, Thoroughly mixed and baked from top to toe; 

A living being with two hands and feet, Who in forest and water you must meet.

But before I bake this man in the fire From each one of you I wish to inquire 

How will you act towards him day after day,

If I make him and bake him in this way." [IIb]

To red squirrel then the Manitou said, "Now how will you treat him, oh, mighty red?" 

Very saucily red squirrel replied

With his travelling rug curled over one side, "I shall fight and bite him with all my might 

And eat him up at the very first sight!" "Oh, you will, will you?" said the Manitou, Gazing at red with an eye steely blue. 

Whereupon he made red squirrel so small 

You scarcely could see red squirrel at all.

But red squirrel's temper remained the same, This the Manitou did not try to tame. 

So that is why a red squirrel will cry

Whenever you happen to pass him by, "I hate you! I hate you! You are no good. 

I would eat you up if I only could." [12]
