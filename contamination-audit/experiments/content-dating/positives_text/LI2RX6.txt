FARMERS' COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS IN FLORIDA V. Citrus Cooperatives; Trends in Financial Structure and 

Services; and Factors Determining Success or Failure

By H. G. HAMILTON¹ and A. H. SPURLOCK INTRODUCTION 

The first bulletin in this series dealt with the status and legal phases; the second, organization and management; the third, business analysis of the Hastings Potato Growers' Association; the fourth, the Florida Citrus Exchange System.

This study on Florida citrus cooperative assocations deals particularly with the important changes of citrus associations; prices received for citrus fruit, the interrelationiship of prices and volume, and factors affecting prices received for fruit; grove caretaking services; sources of credit; cost of credit and factors affecting cost of credit; cost of packing citrus fruits and factors involved; and factors making for success or failure of cooperatives. The data are for the 15-season period 1925-26 to 1939-40, inclusive.

METHOD OF STUDY 

All charters issued to cooperatives by the Secretary of State of Florida were examined. From these charters names of associations, their financial structure, purposes for which formed and names of incorporating directors were recorded on prepared forms. This was done in 1930, 1937 and 1940. With these data in hand the managers of all active associations and a director or former employee of each inactive association were interviewed. 

In 1930-the first survey-the following data were secured from the assocations: Volume of business, balance sheets, profit and loss statements, and number of members for each of the 4 seasons 1926-27 to 1929-30, inclusive; and the same data for the first (or earliest available) season's operations. Data as to pooling practices, credit policies, method of voting, etc., also were secured. In 1937-the second survey-the same type of data were secured for the 1936-37 season and, in addition, data were secured relative to the associations' grove caretaking services.

In 1940-the last survey-the same data were secured for the 1939-40 season as had been obtained for the 1936-37 season. In addition, data for the 15 years on the f. o. b. price received for citrus fruit and prices paid growers according to types and varieties, together with cost of packing fruit, were obtained from many associations.

To better facilitate the analysis, 2 index numbers were prepared. One was an f. o. b. price index for citrus fruit computed to show the price received for citrus by each association for each season as compared with the weighted average prices received by 22 associations which operated continuously from the 192526 season to the 1939-40 season, inclusive. The other index number was a grower price index for citrus fruit computed to show the price paid growers by each association for each season as compared with the price paid growers by 22 successful associations. The method of constructing these indexes is given in Appendix A.

FIRMS MARKETING THE FLORIDA CITRUS CROP 

Since 1909 from 20 to 40 percent of the Florida fresh citrus crop has been marketed by cooperatives. Cooperative caretaking, which began in 1914 and expanded rapidly since 1930, now is an important service of Florida citrus cooperatives. Cooperative canning and marketing of canned citrus fruit has developed largely since 1930. In all 3 major fields of service, Florida cooperatives have had competition of a high class from the independent operators.

During the 1939-40 season the Citrus Inspection Bureau issued licenses to 543 operators. Of this number, 345 were issued to registered packinghouses, 27 to registered shippers who did not operate their own packinghouses, 25 to canning plants, and 146 to miscellaneous operators. Several firms operated both fresh fruit and canning plants. Of the 387 firms handling fresh fruit for out-of-state shipment, 54 were citrus cooperatives, 1 primarily a vegetable cooperative, and 332 were independent operators. The 146 miscellaneous operators were engaged primarily in the business of buying fruit from growers and selling to other operators, particularly to canners. These operators often own truck and field equipment and employ field crews but do not operate packing or canning plants.

TYPE OF ASSOCIATIONS 

There has been a tendency in recent years for local associations to perform the service of selling rather than to depend upon a central sales organization. Some of the large packing associations have withdrawn from a large federation and are performing both packing and selling services for their members. From 1930 to 1940 several cooperatives were organized which never affiliated with the federation but, from the beginning, performed both the packing and selling services. It should not be inferred that there are no large associations affiliated with the federation because some of the largest associations in the state are members. 5. In the 1926-27 season all except 1 of the citrus marketing associations were members of a federation; in the 1936-37 season

FINANCIAL POSITION 

While a few are in weak financial position, most associations, viewed in the light of changes in the services performed, have maintained or improved their financial position since 1927 (Table

9). Except for the year 1940, the amount of current assets was more than double the current liabilities. Members' equity was at all times better than 60 percent of the assets. 

It is significant to observe that fixed assets have increased nothing like as much as the increase in volume of fruit handled since the 1926-27 season. The percent of total assets represented by fixed assets has decreased from 60.2 percent for 1927 to 44.5 percent for 1940. This relative change represents a more complete use of the fixed assets and, in general, reflects favorably upon the management of cooperatives. 

During the period 1927 to 1940 there was a big increase in notes and accounts receivable. This was due to the growth in the caretaking services rendered by cooperatives, which was done to a large extent on credit. 

Not much change took place in the fixed liabilities of citrus marketing associations from 1930 to 1940. Although fixed liabilities were more than twice as great in 1930 as in 1927, by 1940 they were about $10,000 less than in 1930, as shown in Table 9. The most outstanting change in the financial position of citrus associations from 1927 to 1940 was in the kind of instruments representing members' equity. In 1927 and 1930 members' equity was either represented by certificates of indebtedness or by surplus and reserves. After 1930 a number of citrus associations were re-incorporated under the 1931 Cooperative Marketing Act which provided for farmers' cooperatives to organize on a capital stock basis. In 1927 about 3/5 of the members' equity was in the form of certificates of indebtedness and 2/5 in the form of reserves and surplus. In 1940 approximately 1/5 of members' equity was represented by certificates of indebtedness, about 1/3 by surplus and reserves, and the remainder by common stock, preferred stock and book credit in lieu of stock certificates.

CORPORATE STRUCTURE OF CITRUS COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS 

In 1909 Florida enacted the first cooperative marketing act, providing for the incorporation of associations without capital stock. In 1923 an act was passed which provided for the chartering of cooperative associations either with or without capital stock. In 1931 an act bearing the title of "The Agricultural Cooperative Act" amended and repealed the 1923 act. The Agricultural Cooperative Act provides for the chartering of associations either with or without capital stock.

The amount and distribution of the assets and liabilities of the 2 groups of associations are given in Table 12. The financial position of capital stock associations is somewhat better than that of the non-capital stock associations as shown by higher current ratio and average members' equity. It does not necessarily follow that the type of capital structure is the cause of the better position. Excellent financial position may be maintained with either capital stock or non-capital stock structures. 

To a considerable extent the nature of services to be performed by the association and the financial status of its members are important in determining whether an association should organize with or without capital stock.

The chief advantages of capital stock associations are: (1) Permanent financing-assets financed by capital stock do not have to be refinanced; and (2) financial stability-capital stock financing does not fluctuate from year to year as in the case of noncapital stock. The chief advantages of non-capital stock associations are (1) that financial contributions of members are in proportion to the patronage of members and (2) it is difficult for a few members to own or control the associations. 

By issuing preferred stock in proportion to patronage, some of the capital stock citrus associations have retained, in effect, one of the desirable elements of non-capital stock associations and at the same time preserved, to a large extent, the desirable feature of the capital stock associations. Because of the financial ability of citrus growers in Florida and the large amount of capital needed for adequately equipping citrus associations, capital stock is a desirable method of meeting the capital needs of citrus associations. One should not infer that good financing cannot be obtained with non-capital stock. 

Weak financing is the cause of failure of some associations. 

In general, when members of an association own less than half the assets, it is in an unsatisfactory financial position. A safe

membership equity is 60 percent or above, preferably 75 percent or above. Where there is considerable variation in the need for working capital, a sound policy to follow is one in which the members furnish the money needed for fixed assets and that part of the working capital needed throughout the year, then they depend upon borrowing to meet the peak load of working capital.

Possible explanations of higher prices received by large associations are: First, associations with large volume can have continuous supplies on the market; second, they can better meet customers' demands by filling orders for solid cars of 1 or 2 sizes or grades; and, third, associations with large volume perhaps can do a better job of packing because they can afford better equipment. Of course, some small associations receive more for fruit than some large associations, but this is not due, as some believe, to the ability of an association to rush its fruit to market when the price is good. In the first place, managers cannot judge markets so well and, even if they could, a small association would not be able to get any greater percent of its fruit on the market when prices are high than associations with large volume, unless the small association had a larger plant in proportion to its volume. than large associations.

INTERRELATIONSHIP OF PRICE AND VOLUME 

Since volume is so important in determining the grower price of fruit it is interesting to note that f. o. b. price and grower price influence the association's volume of fruit. Figure 7 was prepared to show the influence that f. o. b. price has on volume. 

Associations were divided into 4 groups. One group had an f. o. b. price index above 96 and a higher price index each succeeding year. The f. o. b. price index then was plotted with the following year's volume (volume lagged by 1 year), as shown at the left in Fig. 7. As the price rose in successive seasons from 103 to 

125 the volume index rose from 99 to 146. The second group of associations had an f. o. b. price index of above 108, but the index decreased from 108 for the base year to 87 the fourth year. 

Volume index for these associations decreased from 117 to 92. 

The third group of associations had a low price but had a rise in price for each of the next 3 years. The price rose from an index of 79 to 102 and the volume remained about constant for each of the 4 years, the indexes being 99, 104, 102, and 100. The fourth group of associations had a low f. o. b. price index and a still lower price each of the next 3 successive years. The price index decreased from 94 to 83 and the volume decreased from 

75 to 42.

It is clear from these data that price has an important influence on volume in succeeding years. The direction price is going seems to have slightly more effect on volume than whether the price is high or low. If an association is improving in price from year to year, even though the price to begin with is low, growers are less apt to withdraw than if the price to begin with is high but is getting poorer. VARIATIONS IN COST OF PACKING BETWEEN ASSOCIATIONS 

There were wide variations in cost of packing fruit each season for different associations (Fig. 9). In the 1925-26 season costs for packing oranges ranged from 50 cents to $1.29 per box, and in the 1935-36 season costs ranged from 45 cents to $1.54 per box. Such wide variation is evidence that many associations have ample opportunity for improving the efficiency of their operations.

Data were available for the cost of packing oranges for 24 associations for each season from 1925-26 to 1939-40 (Fig. 10).

The association with lowest costs for the 15 seasons averaged 53 cents per box, while the association with highest costs averaged 78 cents, a difference of 25 cents per box per season for the entire 15 seasons. To illustrate, if a grower having a crop of 

5,000 boxes of oranges per season had his oranges packed by the association with the lowest costs for the 15 seasons, the total cost for packing his crop of fruit would have been $39,750 (5,000 boxes x 15 years x 53 cents per box). But had the grower had his fruit packed by the association with the highest costs, his total cost for the 15 seasons would have been $58,500 (5,000 boxes x 15 years x 78 cents), or $18,750 more.

Cost of credit by types of lending agencies for the 1936-37 and 1939-40 seasons is shown for different terms of loans in Table 23. FACTORS AFFECTING COST OF CREDIT 

Where borrowings are as large as those for the citrus associations, cost of credit becomes an important item of expense. 

Size of Loan.-Size of the short-term loan affected average interest rates, as shown in Table 24. Associations borrowing less than $15,000 paid interest at the rate of 5.3 percent per year in the 1939-40 season for short-term credit, while associations borrowing over $50,000 paid only 2.9 percent. 

Amount of Members' Equity. The amount of members' 

equity in an association affected the cost of short-term credit. 

During the 1939-40 season 13 associations having members' equity of less than $40,000 paid interest at the rate of 5.1 percent per year for short-term credit. Eleven associations with more than $100,000 members' equity paid 3.3 percent (Table 25). 

Percent of Members' Equity. The percent of members' equity in total assets affected interest paid for short-term loans but little. Associations with less than 50 percent members' equity. paid 4.7 percent for money borrowed, those with more than 75 percent members' equity paid 4.1 percent (Table 26).

GROVE CARETAKING SERVICES RENDERED BY MARKETING ASSOCIATIONS 

Grove caretaking services (such as spraying and dusting) have been rendered by a few associations for many years. The service of spraying members' groves was done by 1 association in 1914, which is believed to be the first caretaking service rendered by cooperatives in Florida. In 1915, 1 association dusted groves for members. The increase in grove care service is shown in 

Table 28. In the 1925-26 season only 3 of 53 associations were rendering caretaking services. One association was rendering 2 caretaking services and 2 associations were rendering 1 caretaking service each. In 1926-27, 4 associations were rendering caretaking services; 2 were rendering 2 services each and 2 only 1 service each. In 1939-40, 4 associations were rendering 1 service each; 3 associations 2 services each; 2 associations 3 services each; 9 associations 4 services each; 3 associations 5 services each; and 4 associations 6 services each. Since 1925-26 more associations have performed caretaking services, with a tendency to increase the number of services performed. 

Cooperatives are not different from other institutions-the more successful they are the more the membership demands. 

As growers learn more about cooperation new avenues seem to be opened. In addition to the services regularly performed, associations render many special services when occasions arisesuch as eradication or control of pests. 

Detailed data were compiled for 1939-40 for 22 cooperatives performing caretaking services. Of these 22 associations 95 percent did spraying service for members, 77 percent dusting,

68 percent fertilizing, 36 percent pruning, 59 percent cultural, 41 percent irrigation and 82 percent tractor (Table 29). In

Table 30 is given the percent of the acreage in the associations that the various services covered and the year first offered. Because some members have large groves and are able to perform economically their own work and because some members own equipment that will be used until worn-out or until it is obsolete, less than half the acreage of fruit in the associations is serviced by the associations. The trend is not only to perform more services but also to service more acreage. Should the present trend continue for a number of years, caretaking services soon will be about as important as the marketing service.

The extent that an association's net assets might become involved is indicated by the amount of growers' accounts. Marketing charges are deducted from the sales of fruit before growers are paid and it is rare that sales are insufficient to take care of marketing charges. But, in the case of grove care services, if the members' fruit is lost by freeze or drought the association would have to carry the member's account over for another season in most instances. Should a number of poor years come in succession, the association's financial position might be weakened. In some cases where the association does not perform grove care services for members it either lends money or endorses a member's note to pay for such services. Where loans are made to the same extent as services are performed for credit, there is no difference in effect on the association's financial position. Because of failure of many associations to separate their accounts receivable of growers from other accounts receivable, data that would throw light on this particular problem are meager. It will be observed (Table 34) that growers' accounts receivable for associations performing caretaking services amount to 40.5 percent, 46.0 percent, and 52.0 percent of the total current assets for the years 1930, 1937 and 1940, respectively. But growers' accounts receivable for the 4 associations not rendering grove care services were only 19.7 percent, 8.3 percent and 17.3 percent of the current assets. 4 grove caretaking associations amounted to about 1/5 of the total assets in 1940, but growers' accounts receivable for noncaretaking associations amounted to about 1/16 of the total assets. On the whole the data available indicate that the caretaking service being done extensively on a credit basis has not yet involved the net assets of associations. However, conservatism in such operations is most desirable. By requiring a continuous crop lien and sometimes additional collateral, an association can protect itself against unusual losses resulting from extending caretaking service on a credit basis.

EFFECT OF PRECOOLING PLANTS ON CITRUS PRICES 

There is evidence that precooling plants have some effect on the price received for fruit (Table 36). Associations handling less than 50,000 boxes of fruit and having precooling plants had an f. o. b. price index of 92, while associations without precooling plants had an f. o. b. price index of 86. Large associations having over 300,000 boxes of fruit and having precooling plants had

an f. o. b. price index of 96 and associations without precooling plants had a price index of 95. Precooling plants enable associations to hold fruit until solid cars of 1 grade or size can be loaded, and in addition precooled fruit may arrive in better condition than fruit not precooled. Because of this slight difference in price it does not necessarily follow that the cost of installing a precooling plant is justified. Some associations have been able to cut their refrigeration cost by having precooling plants because they are able to order a less expensive type of refrigeration service and yet have their fruit arrive in the terminal markets in good condition.

POOLING PRACTICES OF CITRUS COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS 

Pooling of citrus fruit on the basis of variety, grades, and size has been almost universally adopted by Florida citrus associations. There has been wide variation in the length of pool used by Florida citrus associations (Table 37). It will be observed that there has been a distinct trend to the seasonal pool. In the 

1929-30 season only 10 associations, or 14 percent, used the seasonal pool, but in the 1936-37 season 53 percent and in the 193940 season 50 percent of the associations used the seasonal pool.

Many other lengths of pools were used in all seasons for which data have been compiled. The advantages given for a seasonal pool were: (1) The management can better meet the demand of the markets because it can pick fruit that is in demand; (2) a more uniform use of the association's equipment and personnel is possible, which may result in slightly lower cost; (3) the association can keep its fruit on the market at all times, which is important in auction markets; and (4) seasonal pools give the best protection against very low prices to an individual member. 

It may also be argued that a seasonal pool prevents the member from getting the highest price, which is true; but for most members it is a greater benefit to be protected from low prices than to be given a chance for high prices. Seasonal pools have the disadvantage (particularly important for inexperienced membership) of (1) encouraging members to break their contract with the association if the price rises toward the end of the season and (2) of being unfair because early fruit usually brings higher prices than midseason fruit. Members capable of producing early fruit should have the advantage of the early market which cannot be done with a seasonal pool. The mortality of citrus marketing firms is very high in Florida. 

Each year a large number of firms fail and another large number of new firms begin operations. Over a period of time not much change takes place in the number of firms operating.

The number of independent firms with packinghouses varied from 300 for the 1941-42 season to 359 for the 1936-37 season and averaged 326 for the 7 seasons 1935-36 to 1941-42, inclusive.

Volume of Business.-Associations with small volume of business have a much higher mortality rate than associations with large volume (Table 47). There were 15 associations which operated in the 1929-30 season with less than 25,000 boxes per association. In the 1936-37 season 12, or 80 percent, of these associations were inactive. Fifteen associations had a volume between 25,001 and 50,000 boxes in the 1929-30 season and by the 1936-37 season 5, or 1/3, of these associations had ceased to operate. There were 5 associations in the 1929-30 season with over 200,000 boxes of fruit. By the 1939-40 season 1 of these had failed; however, this association had lost volume before that season. Whatever the cause of small volume, associations with small volume are much more apt to fail than associations with large volume. 

Members' Equity.-Associations with low members' equity failed to a much greater extent than did associations with high members' equity (Table 48). There were 12 associations in 

1930 with a members' equity of less than 20 percent of the assets and by 1937 all of these associations had ceased to operate. Of the 34 associations having over 60 percent members' equity in 

1930, 24, or 71 percent, were operating in 1937 and 21, or 66 percent, were operating in 1940.

A poor financial position may be the result of small volume, too liberal credit policy, inadequate revenue for service performed, or any number of other things. Regardless of cause, the fact remains that when an association gets into a weak financial position it is soon forced out of business. Evidence that a weak financial position precedes failure is obtained from a study of the financial statements of associations at the close of their last season's operations (Table 49). The average balance sheet of 33 associations (for last year operated or last year available) shows that these associations were in a weak current position, the ratio of current assets to current liabilities being only 0.8 1.0. In addition to a weak current position, the amount of members' equity was less than 50 percent of total assets. 

It is doubtful if the assets, as given in Table 49, were worth face value, as there was a large item of accounts and notes receivable without provisions for bad debts. The investments were perhaps of doubtful value or they would have been liquidated and the proceeds used to reduce the debts of the associations. The f. o. b. price index of an association reflects its standing along with other associations relative to prices received. The grower price index reflects its standing among other associations relative to the prices paid members, being the result of not only the selling effectiveness but also the packing efficiency. An association with either low f. o. b. prices and average packing costs or average f. o. b. prices and high packing costs has low grower prices. Associations with low f. o. b. prices and high packing cost have very low grower prices. Most associations which have failed had during their last years of operation both low f. o. b. prices and high packing costs, which resulted in very low grower prices (Table 53). There are 2 primary factors affecting the grower price for fruit-price received by the association and cost of selling and packing. The first factor is affected largely by the initial quality of the fruit, preparation for market, and volume of fruit handled. The second factor is affected largely by volume of fruit handled, investment in plant, degree of labor-saving devices, and arrangement of the packing plant. All these factors are closely interrelated and eventually are worked out in both a cause and result relationship. That is, volume affects prices and prices affect volume the next year; volume affects cost and cost affects volume the following year; poor quality or preparation for market affects prices, which in turn affect volume the following year; and anything affecting costs affects the prices a grower receives, which in turn affect volume of fruit the following year. Anything that an association can do to improve its efficiency is cumulative in that it puts the association in a position to make further improvements in efficiency. Inefficiency is also cumulative; that is, high costs cause high costs and low prices cause low prices, so far as an association is concerned.

THE FLORIDA CITRUS EXCHANGE 

In Table 3 are shown 15 associations (known as sub-exchanges) affiliated with the Florida Citrus Exchange. These associations, at present, exist for the purpose of facilitating membership of the local associations in the Florida Citrus Exchange. One or more local citrus associations compose the subexchanges, which are the legal members of the Florida Citrus

Exchange. Originally and up to 1933, the sub-exchange association, in addition to affording membership in the Florida Citrus Exchange, acted as a clearinghouse between the local associations and the Florida Citrus Exchange. The sub-exchanges received the car manifests from the local associations and passed them on to the sales department of the Florida Citrus Exchange and, in addition to any suggestions of the local association, made suggestions as to the market or method of sale desired. The sub-exchanges received the proceeds from the sale of fruit from the Florida Citrus Exchange and after deducting charges for this service, usually from 12 to 2 cents per box, transmitted the proceeds on to the local associations. The subexchange received from the Exchange orders for fruit that were to be filled in its territory and equitably distributed them among its member associations. Since 1933 the associations have cleared all car manifests, received all funds from the sale of fruit, and all orders for fruit directly from the Exchange. 

The Florida Citrus Exchange is primarily a sales organization. In addition to its sales force in Tampa, Florida, it maintains salaried representatives on the principal terminal markets and brokers who act exclusively for the Exchange in handling Florida citrus fruit in the small terminal markets. Sales promotion work of the Exchange consists of maintaining dealer service men in the principal markets. Before the Florida grapefruit, orange and tangerine advertising acts were passed, the Florida Citrus Exchange carried on an aggressive advertising program. 

In addition to the sales service of the Florida Citrus Exchange, it has 2 auxiliary organizations, 1 of which supplies affiliated organizations with tissue wraps and other packing supplies, and the other furnishes production credit to growers affiliated with the Exchange System.

The Florida Citrus Exchange in recent years has handled approximately 25 percent of the Florida fresh citrus fruit sales.4

CANNING OF CITRUS FRUIT BY COOPERATIVES 

Canning of citrus fruit is done by both cooperatives that handle fresh fruit and those that handle canned fruit exclusively. Because canning of citrus fruit has been perfected so recently and so few associations handle canned fruit, it is not possible at this stage to determine whether the handling of canned fruit can best be done in connection with fresh fruit or with exclusive canners. Canned citrus moves to the retail stores through an entirely different set of middle men than fresh fruit and many of the services-such as transportation and storageare quite different. In normal times canned fruit moves to market largely by boat, while fresh fruit moves extensively by rail, truck and boat. Storage is an important factor for canned fruit but less so for fresh fruit.

EDUCATIONAL ASSOCIATIONS 

In 1940, 15 citrus educational associations had been chartered, 13 of which were active. These associations were mainly county units affiliated with the Florida Citrus Growers, Inc., a statewide association. These organizations were instrumental in the establishment of the American Farm Bureau Federation of Florida, as organized in 1941. These associations engage primarily in educational activities and sponsor legislation in behalf of the citrus industry. They handle no products and render no services of a marketing or production nature. 

MISCELLANEOUS ASSOCIATIONS 

Associations classified as miscellaneous consist of a cooperative crate association, a trade association, and 4 associations that perform business service for their members to a limited extent. 

The cooperative crate association manufactures containers for its member associations. For the season 1939-40 this association had 13 members. It received its original charter in 1920 but was re-incorporated in 1938 under the 1931 Cooperative Marketing Act. This association manufactured approximately 2,000,- 

000 boxes in the 1939-40 season.

In the 1939-40 season the 23 members of the trade association were cooperative and independent citrus marketing organizations. It is the function of this association to give its membership information on the current problems of the industry which affect them and to act as a unified voice for its members. 

Of the associations engaged in performing business services for their members, 1 purchases fertilizer and spray materials for its members, 1 performs general grove care services for its members, 1 performs irrigation service, and 1 purchases fertilizer and spray materials and inspects members' groves to determine the need for spraying and dusting.

One association ceased handling fruit for its members a number of years ago. Its only activity is leasing its well equipped packinghouse building and machinery. Because there is a feeling that the association may again enter the field of marketing its real estate is being retained.

SUMMARY 

On the average, Florida cooperatives market about 30 percent of the fresh citrus crop. Cooperative associations handle about twice as much fruit per association as independents handle per firm. 

From 1926-27 to 1939-40 the number of citrus marketing associations decreased from 61 to 55 but the volume of fruit handled per association increased from 82,301 boxes to 248,- 275 boxes. There was little change in the number of patrons per association. Originally, marketing associations performed only the service of marketing, but in 1939-40 most of them were performing a number of grove caretaking services. 

The tendency has been in recent years for the local association to perform both marketing services of packing and selling. 

Since 1930 a number of associations have changed from noncapital stock to capital stock. More of the associations organized in recent years have been capital stock than non-capital stock. 

It appears that adequate financing of assets can be obtained either with or without capital stock. 

Over a period of 15 seasons the f. o. b. price received for early grapefruit averaged $1.60 per box; Marsh Seedless grapefruit, $1.91; Parson Brown oranges $2.16; Pineapple oranges, $2.06; Valencia oranges, $2.50; and tangerines, $2.28.

The f. o. b. price received for citrus fruit varied greatly between associations. The volume of fruit handled by an association affected the price received. 

Associations with small volume had a much lower grower price index with wider variation than associations with large volume.

Prices received by an association affected the volume of fruit handled by the association. Associations with high prices increased their volume but associations with low prices had decreases in volume.

Cost of packing citrus fruit varied greatly between associations. The most important factors affecting cost of packing were total volume, volume per patron and investment in fixed assets. 

The number of associations borrowing for long-term purposes decreased from 34 associations in the 1934-35 season to 5 in the 1939-40 season. About the same number of associations borrowed for short-term purposes in 1939-40 as in 1936-37. Factors

affecting cost of credit were size of loan and amount of members' equity.

There has been a gradual and consistent growth in number of associations and number of services performed in grove caretaking for their members. The important grove caretaking services performed by associations in the 1939-40 season were spraying, tractor service, dusting, fertilizing, cultural, irrigation and pruning. There was no evidence that grove caretaking service rendered by the association affected adversely the financial position of associations or that it affected prices received for fruit. It is a service which evidently is satisfactory to growers or there would not be such a marked and consistent upward trend in grove caretaking services. It is probable that the association is able to perform this service at a lower cost than the grower.

Fruit prices were only slightly higher for associations with precooling plants than associations without precooling plants. 

Pooling by variety, grade and size of fruit is universally practiced by Florida citrus associations. However, much variation exists in length of pools. The most common pool length is for a season and the trend is definitely toward a seasonal pool. 

In the opinion of informants, lack of adequate volume of fruit for efficient operations was the most common cause of failure. 

Other reasons often given were poor management, financial difficulties, lack of cooperative spirit, and unsatisfactory prices. 

Statistical analyses reveal that Florida citrus marketing associations in the past have failed from 1 or more of 3 reasons: (1) lack of volume, (2) inadequate financing, and (3) unsatisfactory prices.

CONCLUSIONS 

While there are exceptions, a citrus marketing association needs at least 200,000 boxes of fruit for efficient operations. 

Financing of fixed assets can be done satisfactorily either through capital stock or by non-capital stock through the use of certificates of indebtedness.

Grove caretaking services have become an important function of citrus marketing associations. The tendency for strong associations to increase the number of grove caretaking services for members and the increase in the number of associations performing grove care service in recent years indicates that grove caretaking services are being rendered in a manner that is sat-

isfactory to the members. Grove caretaking service enables a marketing association to increase its size of business and to make fuller use of its personnel and equipment. 

High prices received for fruit, low cost of packing, and large volume of fruit are closely interrelated. These are the most important factors determining the success or failure of an association. High prices received for fruit and low cost of packing enables an association to pay high prices to members for fruit, which in turn increases the volume of business the following year, thereby making possible still higher prices and lower packing costs. Associations paying members high prices for fruit are characterized by an increasing volume of fruit to handle and an improved financial position. Associations paying members low prices for fruit are characterized by a decreasing volume of fruit and a weakening financial position. 

Associations usually do not fail because of 1 year of poor prices to growers. Some associations pay growers less than 90 percent of the prices paid by the average association for many years. 

For 3 or 4 years before failing associations usually pay their members only 60 to 75 percent of what successful associations return.

Large members' equity in the assets of associations enables associations to borrow at low rates of interest and affords needed strength in years of poor prices or crop failures.

Except for early fruit, the seasonal pool seems to be the most satisfactory.

APPENDIX B METHOD USED IN COMPUTING INDEX OF PRICES F. O. B. PRICE INDEX 

F. o. b. prices are those received (regardless of type of sale) at the association's packinghouse after charges for selling have been deducted, but not packing charges. Prices for miscellaneous varieties were excluded from the index. 

Simple price relatives were computed for each important variety of fruit, the base price being the weighted average price received for that variety by 22 successful associations which operated continuously from the 1925-26 season to the 1938-39 sea-

son, inclusive. Instead of using 1 base period for all seasons, each season was used as a separate base. 

The f. o. b. price index for oranges was obtained by multiplying the price relative for each variety by the volume, summing the products, and dividing by the total volume of oranges. The grapefruit f. o. b. price index was obtained similar to the orange index.

The f. o. b. price index for all fruit was obtained by multiplying the number of boxes of each kind (in oranges, grapefruit or tangerines) by its price index, by summing the products, and by dividing by the total volume of all fruit.
