CIVIL DEFENSE DEMOCRACY IN ACTION SECTION I 

Hitler's all-out warfare reverses the sequence of ancient wars. 

His new method attacks the populace first and deals with the armed forces afterwards. The waiting, the anguish, the fear for families, and the loss of hope are now imposed upon the armed forces to undermine their morale, to soften up and make easy the defeat of the army. Probably for an army under the aegis of dictatorship, it might have worked that way. But it appears that Hitler has missed his guess as to the effect of this type of attack against a democracy. The attack upon the populace behind the Army has given a free people what it has never had before in war: A fighting job for the civilian, a defense effort for every man and woman not in the armed forces, a place in an active organization of Civil Defense to defend their civil liberties, to protect their industries, to safeguard their homes, to help the homeless, and to afford shelter from destruction both from subversive elements from within and from air bombardment from without.

The heroic spirit with which the people of Britain have electrified the world is due less to hitherto hidden traits of racial character than to the fact that every individual is given a chance to do something concrete and actual in the defense of the nation during the nerve-tensing hours of the attack. The British people are glad to have a government that piles responsibility on them and sets them to work and to fight. The thoroughness of Britain's elastic organization of its Air Raid Precautions Service, in which everyone can have some job, is responsible for that democracy's united defense against a barbaric all-out assault that would have done credit to Attila's world-ravishers.

We Americans get flashes of Britain's defense through casual newspaper articles. We know of the air raid sirens, the rush to air raid shelters, the pounding of antiaircraft guns, the flashing searchlights, airplane dog-fights overhead, the burst of incendiary bombs and the crash of high explosives, crumbling walls, broken water mains, fire engines, rescue squads, ambulances, the all-clear siren, hospital service, the clearing of debris, and then,-business and work the next day. As each new article, with some new twist to this now heroic commonplace is read, we remark, "Stout fellows!

Brave people!" We scarcely give a thought to the organization that has made such defense possible. It should be comforting for all 

Americans to know that President Roosevelt has initiated a national program for civil defense and that it is in the process of complete organization.

Our organization starts with the Federal Government and is decentralized to the state and local governments, where, in a democracy, it belongs. At the apex of a vast defense pyramid, at whose 1

This office is charged with the development of the National Defense program, the aims of which are: first, that the nation have large, well-trained, thoroughly equipped military and naval forces; second, that the economic, physical, and social fitness of the people be insured; and third, that the people as a whole, not only the officials, must be given a clear understanding of the nation's problems and an opportunity to reaffirm their loyalty to the national interest by expressing their desire to serve. All this requires co-operative effort with unity of purpose. 

This program seems rather nebulous. But it has to be couched in large enough terms to cover the hundred-and-one activities connected with national defense-not just a military defense, nor a naval defense, nor an air defense, but a truly national defense, a defense encompassing every last member of the Republic, a defense of civilians by civilians, a defense in which the man in the street is as important as the man in uniform. 

State and Local Cooperation 

This requires the full cooperation of federal, state, and local governments, and public and private organizations. A free democracy can do this if it knows what to do. In order to provide facilities for state, local, and individual cooperation, there is in Washington, in the Office for Emergency Management, a Division of State and Local Cooperation. The Governor of the state is responsible for the protection of his civilian communities and of their essential utilities and essential industries. Naturally, therefore, the state is taken as the basis of Defense Council organization. This 

Division of State and Local Cooperation will assist the governor of each state in the creation of a State Council of Defense and in encouraging and guiding the creation of Local Councils of Defense. 

The activities of councils of defense include the following: agricultural resources and protection; health, welfare, and consumer interest; housing, works, and facilities; human resources and skills; industrial resources and production; and civil protection. 

Under Civil Protection is listed the protection of civil liberties, the maintenance of law and order, and measures to guard against sabotage and subversive activities, by means of the legally constituted authorities, through the enforcement of law and the exercise of regular and duly established police powers. 

That is a big order for a democracy. But America can put it over, if she has time. The organization for production is already showing results. Protection has many different fronts; social protection, moral protection, physical protection. Social protection is generally termed welfare. Physical protection is generally termed defense. For all persons, not in the Armed Forces, it is termed 

Civil Defense. 

To administer Civil Defense, the President has created the office of Director of Civilian Defense as a part of the Emergency 

Management Office. These activities will be decentralized to Regional Offices which are coterminous with the Corps Area organization under the War Department.

The Pattern of Attack 

In order to visualize just what Civil Defense must include, let us consider the pattern of the attacks upon the civilian population for which a defense must be provided. We must always expect variations from what has already been done, but if we have prepared for what this pattern has been in Europe, we will be a great way along.

The first phase of the attack is designed to create apprehension and doubt, divided counsels, and the sabotage of National Unity. 

The next step is the development of fear. This will come through sabotage of plants engaged in defense industry. Threats against the utilities, such as the water, electricity, telephone, and railway bring the danger nearer home. Nuisance attacks, such as damage to traffic arteries like the Hendrick Hudson Bridge, add to the confusion. Such an act would not be irreparable as long as the other crossings last, but damaging to the confidence of the people, and most annoying.

When confusion and doubt have softened up the people, some real fear is thrown in-a token raid the appearance of a bomber flight from an airplane carrier dropping pamphlets that threaten destruction from the air unless the government is repudiated or overthrown, or possibly the seizure of important points by Fifth Column agents (in Europe the favorite objectives were the City Hall, the telephone centrals, and the telegraph system). 

And then comes the panic of a population unaccustomed and unprepared that will come from a real air raid. Not only death and destruction from the effects of bombs, but fires, destruction to communications, water system, and transportation. An air raid may be one thing when it happens to spend its force against a residential district, but when it causes a city-wide conflagration, the destruction of an oil depot or munitions depot or arsenal, we have a disaster. 

In its wake inevitably follow the disorganization of family life,

Let us take up in order the defense necessary against each of these phases of the attack:

Sabotaging National Unity 

This is the most difficult attack to combat. It comes early in the game when there is no clear line between friend and subversive foe. It may appear in public print or in private conversation. It subtly plants doubts as to fact, doubts as to the correctness of our own ideas, doubts as to the honesty of the motives of our leaders, and doubts as to the wisdom of democracy itself. 

The best defense is leadership, mostly local leadership, the sort of leadership that arises and announces that the debate is over, the decision is made, it's time to get to work and organize for Civil 

Defense. 

Sabotaging Industry and the Utilities

Industrial plants and public installations such as water, gas, electric lighting, telephone, telegraph, power lines, bridges, tunnels, railways, warehouses—all necessities behind the scenes of our modern, comfortable life—our civilization-must be protected, and the planning therefor and the furnishing of protectors is a colossal job. 

Industrial plant and utility protection is primarily an inside job and the immediate responsibility of the management. However, the management may have to be assisted in its plant protection not by surrounding it with a cordon of sentries but first by the protection of vital interior key points. Surveys must be made to determine these critical points, the damage of which will affect the entire plant. Take our aqueducts for instance there are valve installations at certain points. If a saboteur is left alone with these for five minutes, tremendous damage can be inflicted. The guarding of these points may be simple-much simpler than the patrol of an aqueduct line.

The appearance of air raiders dropping pamphlets over Boston and New York City might probably be the signal for the intensification of sabotage and attempts to overthrow local governments. In the exuberant security of a cocktail tea anywhere in Manhattan, this idea gets a great laugh. But such a flight is not impossible. It is merely a question upon what point the flier is based. Stranger things have happened in this war already. In fact, they're made more possible in view of the strength of subversive organizations in our midst, organizations which are viewed with complacent amusement by many of our population. 

And then remember, it takes only six or eight determined, armed men to seize a radio station here, and another gang to put a power plant out of action there, and another to cripple a tunnel or a subway, and a few more squads to assassinate the municipal leaders. Hysterical mobs can be started milling. It is easy to suggest and start an exodus from our crowded centers of population which will rapidly block the highways and disrupt the movements of troops. And then what? Your imagination is as good as mine.

A properly organized civil defense is our best protection. Organization is now under way.

All questions concerning the use of troops immediately brings the query, why not federal troops? The troops are here and they may be available now. But there is no surety that they are going to be available here, or anywhere in this section of the country for any length of time. The Fifth Column may choose the time for action when they are absent on training or other duty. 

The preparation of armed units for defensive action against subversive activities is not the full solution. The best action will be to determine the members of the subversive organizations and arrest and confine them before they can stage their show. This procedure can only be handled by the National and State governments. For if it should get out of hand through the misguided efforts of local volunteer organizations, much mischief may result. 

It is very easy to start witch-hunting. 

Air Raids The Aircraft Warning Service: The first requisite to provide defense against an air raid is to insure warning of the approach of hostile aircraft. England has developed an Observers Corps. 

All of England is divided into a number of districts and each district is studded with Observer Posts. These posts observe the movements of aircraft, and telephone the information to Observer Corps Centers. These latter collect the messages, forward them to a central office, and also disseminate information which will be necessary for the proper sounding of air raid alarms. The warnings sent out by the Observer Corps Centers are given by telephone exchanges to certain key subscribers such as the Air Raid Precautions officials, siren operators, docks, railway, and oil installations. There are but four signals. The signal comes in on a call that takes priority over every other call or conversation. The operator's voice sounds "Mr. Jones, official call, yellow,” and then hurries to call Mr. Smith. "Yellow" is sent when the air raider's movements cannot be definitely forecast but when it is known that one is in the area. The purpose of this warning is to allow certain industries and services the extra time required to shut down or to get into a standby status. It is generally a confidential warning. As soon as the movements of the aircraft are definitely known, the danger signal "red" is given and only then are the whistles and sirens permitted to sound. "Purple" is for those factories that have special authority

to continue operation with modified lighting during raids. The Air 

Raid Warning signal is a trilling or warbling blast. The all-clear is a steady blast. It comes after the “white” or all-clear message. 

At first all personnel, within the area in which the air raid was sounding, were compelled to stop work and take cover. This re- sulted in many lost hours of work. Roof-spotters are now posted on the roofs of factories to watch the progress of raids and to sound the alarm only when danger is imminent.

Our own Aircraft Warning Service has been organized. It consists of a large number of observation posts based on the trunks of the commercial telephone service. Their organization is such as to give the Air Defense Command early warning of the approach of planes so that they can be met by aircraft and our antiaircraft batteries can be alerted in time. As soon as the probable paths of the raiders are determined, the appropriate civil defense agencies in each air raid warning area will be notified of the imminence of hostile air action in the area. The observation posts of our Aircraft 

Warning Service will be operated by citizen volunteers under the 

Army Air Defense Commands. 

The Air Raid Wardens' Service: Under the British system, when a hostile plane has arrived over its target and dropped its high explosives or incendiary bombs, The Air Raid Wardens' Service begins to functions. The Wardens' Service is controlled by a Chief 

Warden with Division Wardens, Head Wardens, and finally down to the Senior Warden, as the head of the local post. He has a Second 

Warden and four wardens assisting him. These six make up the crew of a Warden's Post. The basic organization is one Warden Post for each.500 in population. The posts are spaced about a quarter-mile apart in cities. It was originally intended that one warden could take care of 100 to 150 persons. He had two assistants so as to insure that one of them would be available at any time of the day or night. 

During long air raids it was found that one set of wardens was not enough. The periods of duty required at least two shifts of three men each, or six men to a post. These posts are organized into groups and the groups into divisions under a Chief Warden. 

Where large blocks of apartments and tenements exist, they have their own wardens recruited from residents in the apartments; this was especially so where the apartment houses had public air raid shelters.

The wardens act as a link between the local authority and the public. They are shepherds of their people. When first organized they distributed government or other leaflets of instruction to all householders in the district, checked up to see that each member of the household was fitted with a gas mask, and gave advice on fitting gas-proof rooms in public and private residences. During black-outs, they patrolled the streets in their sectors to check up on lights, but had no police powers and could merely draw the householder's attention to any infraction of lighting regulations. The only time they could enter the house against the owner's will was for the purpose of fire fighting—the same as a regular fireman. 

If the individual householder would not comply with the law, the warden's only recourse would be to report him to the police. Since the heavy raids began, the responsibility undertaken by the warden varies according to the district and, of course, according to the temperament of the warden.

CIVIL DEFENSE SECTION II 

The English have a genius for being casual. Nowhere does it show more lucidly than in their reports of what happens during their nights of terror under Hitler's savage air bombardments.

Although a city block had "happened" to be wiped out by bombs and fire, people killed, injured, and rescued from the smoldering basements of wrecked buildings, it will appear merely as "Incident 

316 of April 5th," with a record of time, place, and damage done.

An "Incident" With the brief picture of the air raid warden presented in Section I, let us follow through an “incident” caused by a raiding airplane that drops high explosive and incendiary bombs at night.

The Senior Air Warden is in the Warden Post. He is a full-time warden and the others, who are part-time wardens, have already reported at the sound of the siren. In fact, the Second Warden is just returning from the large air raid shelter which serves this post. Two noises are heard, one characteristic of an incendiary bomb, and the other, the detonation of a heavy high explosive bomb. 

A warden is sent out to locate the points at which these bombs have fallen and to make a report. He is barely out of the door when he is met by a member of the Fire Patrol who is coming to the Post to report. His report gives the locations of the damage, describing one of these points as a total demolition, and the other as a fire started by an incendiary bomb. 

The following action then takes place: The Senior Warden calls up a report center, reports the time, location, type of incident, and requests the report center to send a pump and an emergency squad. These come from the nearest auxiliary fire service depot. 

In his report, he designates an "incident headquarters." This incident headquarters is a place from which all of the operations which are necessitated by the fall of these two bombs or their consequences are directed. It is generally a place with a telephone close to the site of the incident. A special lamp is set up to indicate its position.

The Senior Warden designates one of his wardens to proceed to this incident headquarters and take charge until the arrival of an "incident officer," who will be sent out from one of the higher posts or control centers. The Incident Officer directs the operations and gives the necessary instruction for the employment of the various "parties" who will report to the scene.

If there are casualties, the Senior Warden will put in a request to a local depot for a First Aid Party. Until this Party arrives, the wardens will render first aid and other necessary assistance. Upon the establishment of the incident headquarters, the Senior Warden waits for the first report upon which he will base a request for an ambulance unit, either from the depot, if available, or from the report center. In case of a demolished building he requests a Rescue Party. 

The incident officer takes over all operations upon his arrival. 

He sends word also to notify the First Aid Post of the dispatch of the ambulance and of the number of injured that may be expected

from this incident. From further reports, requests may be sent in, either direct to depots, or to the report center for a Demolition Party to knock down walls which are threatening to crumble and which may block the street, or for repair parties to repair doorways or passages so as to insure the escape of persons in local air raid shelters, or to repair broken water mains, gas mains, electric conduits, or telephone cables. About this time we may expect the arrival of an auxiliary fire pump with its crew, and, if the nearby water mains are broken, the water grid will have to be closed so as to shut off the broken main. If the nearest intact main were at a distance, water would have to be pumped in by series pumping, that is, one engine pumping from a hydrant to another engine at the limit of its serviceable pressure and this engine pumping into another until finally sufficient water can be pumped to the scene of action. In some cases, broken mains have been so general that the pumping had to be from open water at long distances. Incendiary bombs, that have not fallen in a building where they cannot be reached, are smothered with sand. The above is one incident. 

In one night, there were 1530 such incidents in London. 

Now, with these Air Warden Posts about a distance of a quarter of a mile from each other as a basis for their organization, let us look at the British system. These Air Warden Posts are organized into groups of twelve to twenty posts under a Head Warden-the original limit being sixteen posts for about 8000 population. These form a district or group. Ten of these groups are under a Divisional 

Warden, forming a Central Division for a population of about 80,000. 

Three such Central Divisions operate under a Chief Warden. This would be the organization in a city of 250,000 population. With the genius that the English have for organizing according to conditions, this fixed organization is subject to change. Large blocks of apartments and tenements have their own wardens, recruited from the residents. In districts which are thinly settled there is a Warden's Post for far less than 500. 

In the case of industrial or commercial buildings, with say, more than 100 workers, factory or office wardens are appointed for duty within the premises during working hours. The main thing is, that Warden Posts are spaced at convenient intervals in the streets irrespective of the number of persons in the sector. 

THE AIR RAID PROTECTION SERVICES 

The Warden Service is the backbone of the A.R.P. system as the infantry is in an army. It is closely supported by the fire and police services as the artillery and the air service support the infantry. The effects of high-explosive and incendiary bombs spread injury, fire, and destruction in large areas. The existing civil agencies and facilities are unable to meet such emergency demands, hence the need for newly created services. Just as the infantry has the medical department and quartermaster corps, the A.R.P. has the fire, casualty, maintenance, rescue, and repair services. 

Fire Protection Service 

This is a force trained in fire fighting and equipped with auxiliary apparatus consisting of light portable pumpers, hose lines, and sufficient other equipment to meet emergency requirements. It maintains a system of patrols to detect and extinguish fires, enlist-

9 ing, where necessary, the help of all available citizens. It is designed, of course, to relieve the fire department of all fire fighting which is within its power to handle. 

In buildings housing more than 100 persons, such as apartment houses, hotels, office buildings, factory, loft buildings, et cetera, the licensed building superintendents and the sprinkler and standpipe engineers organize interior fire brigades and carry out their own fire protection service.

Casualty 

First Aid Posts: These are fixed establishments required in cities of 3,000 or more population. They should be situated close enough to each other, so that an injured person will not have to walk more than one mile for help, and should be reasonably well protected against the effects of bombing. They should provide for the reception, emergency treatment and evacuation of casualties requiring hospitalization and should have washing facilities for the decontamination of those who have been exposed to gas. They should afford facilities for segregating the serious casualties from those requiring only emergency treatment. First Aid Posts are classified as -- large, 110, in cities of over 30,000, medium, 90, in cities of from 20,000 to 30,000 population and small, 70, in cities under 20,000 population. The personnel is composed of doctors, nurses, nurses aids, and men and women trained in first aid work as well as persons who perform duties not requiring any special training.

First Aid Parties: A party comprises four trained men with equipment and a stretcher squad, with two stretchers and four bearers. There should be at least 12 such parties to 80,000 in population, with a reserve of 25% in large cities. Two ambulance units (hereinafter described) are attached to each party. These parties are mobile units operating from control and report center depots. 

They are dispatched at the request of senior post wardens to the scene of an occurrence to attend the injured and arrange for the transportation of serious casualties to first aid posts or field hospitals for treatment and evacuation. 

Ambulance Units: A unit comprises one ambulance with two drivers and two attendants who may be women, and a small truck or station wagon with driver and attendant (both may be women) for the transportation of sitting_wounded. Two such units are attached to each first aid party. Each first aid post should have sufficient units attached or available for its needs. Women personnel are particularly adapted to this work and should be used whenever possible in order to release men for heavier and more dangerous work.

Field Hospitals: The maximum facilities of existing hospitals are hardly sufficient to care for the great influx of casualties in war time. It will, therefore, be necessary to have a mobile field hospital for each 50,000 population to be used as an evacuating medium to base hospitals situated in less dangerous and less vulnerable areas. 

Rescue, Repair, and Maintenance 

An air raid upon a densely populated town may be expected to cause casualties and damage over a large area. Consequently, it is necessary to make provision on a considerable scale for the personnel required to deal with such a situation. Work of various kinds will be required, and in almost every case speed will be an

CIVIL DEFENSE 

important factor. In case of damaged buildings, it will be necessary to rescue living persons entrapped in the debris, to recover the dead, and to take any immediate steps necessary for the temporary repair or demolition of buildings, the collapse of which is likely to endanger life or obstruct traffic. For this purpose it is necessary to organize rescue parties which will be of two types-light and heavy, the latter being held in reserve under the control center reserve depots to deal with the more formidable types of rescue work.

Light Rescue Parties: These will consist of ten men, excluding such drivers as may be required, and will include one foreman and three skilled men. The remaining six will be responsible primarily for general assistance in the work of rescue, but four of them will also be trained or equipped to render first aid in case the first aid party is not immediately available. 

Heavy Rescue Parties:. These will normally consist of eight men, excluding such drivers as may be required, and will include one foreman and three skilled men. They will be provided with heavier equipment than the light rescue parties. They will undertake the heavier tasks of rescue and take such action as may be necessary to prevent further collapse or to effect such clearance of debris as may be required to enable urgent activities to be resumed.

It is desirable that each type of rescue party should be available and prepared to turn off the gas and electric services if the need arises. In war time, both types of parties will be maintained on a full time pay basis and arranged on a shift system so that throughout the 24 hours parties will always be available. 

Demolition Parties: A party will consist of a foreman and from 5 to 7 men. The personnel should be recruited from workmen skilled in the building trades, especially housewrecking. 

The parties are provided with sufficient heavy equipment to carry out large demolitions. They are based at the reserve service depots at the control centers, and, because of the nature of their work, are not essentially integral parts of the A.R.P. System. 

Decontamination Parties: There are six to each 100,000 in population. A party comprises a foreman with from 5 to 7 men and equipment. The personnel may well be recruited from city departments of gas, electricity, and water supply and should be specially trained in measures to rid buildings and areas of persistent gases, such as mustard.

Control Centers 

The control centers are established according to population, there being one control center for an area not exceeding 500,000. 

Certain areas will require the establishment of sub-control centers. 

In the London region, there are nine districts, each with its control center and four sub-districts. A control center receives reports of damage from the report centers in its districts. During heavy air attacks, it exercises general control of all available resources and arranges for mutual support of the report center areas. It is under the direction of the Air Raid Precaution Controller, who corresponds to the general in command. He has an officer-in-charge or chief of staff, incident officers, plotting clerks, chart writers, message clerks, record clerks, and messengers. The heads of services

REPORT CENTERS 

A report center is usually set up for about each 100,000 population. It has an organization closely resembling that of the Control 

Center. Its function is to receive, evaluate, and transmit information and to take executive action in ordering out Air Raid Precaution Parties, from the depots assigned to the Center, for action on the scene of damage. It acts upon the Warden's reports of damage and passes along the needed information to the services concerned and forwards the damage reports to the Control Center. Ordinarily, the sending of information and orders to the depots where the First Aid, Stretcher, Mobile Hospital, Rescue, Demolition, and Decontamination squads are housed is the function of the Report Center, but in exceptional cases the depots may deal directly with requests received from the wardens, informing the Report Center of the parties dispatched and the point to which they are sent. The Report 

Center maintains the most detailed information on the status of all the services and detailed particulars of damage and the current disposition of effectives. Its control map shows the location of all bombs dropped as well as the damage done. 

So much for what can be called the Fourth Line Fighting

Forces. Behind these, just as in the Army, are many other installations that carry on other operations.

EVACUATION 

One of the most dramatic, as well as the most difficult, was London's evacuation operation. As the possibility of intensive airraiding was anticipated at the outbreak of the war, it was decided an unnecessary cruelty to subject the helpless to such attack. Precautions were made to evacuate children over five, children under five with their mothers, pregnant women, the crippled, the blind, and those confined to hospitals, to quiet areas at a distance from 

London. The Ministry of Health and the Board of Education made surveys of available accommodations for these people and prepared

plans for their billeting and the payment of rentals therefor. By the end of August, 1939, all the plans were ready and the evacuation began. First, patients from the hospitals in the big cities were sent out. They were met in the billeting areas by fleets of ambulances and cars, then sorted and transported to the hospitals and nursing homes previously cleared for them. Then came the children, the mothers, the blind, and the crippled. Each train-load had to be met, fed and cared for, taken to the appropriate billets and settled. 

In three days 1,500,000 were evacuated and settled. 

However, difficulties were encountered. In the first place, the evacuation was not compulsory. Parents had to be willing to send their children away. Next, the evacuees had to be made comfortable and happy. This produced many difficult problems of human relationship. The hostess had to be paid. Parents had to be persuaded to advance something toward the keep of their children, and clothing had to be obtained. Two great difficulties had to be met; first, providing additional health facilities for the evacuees and second, providing for the schooling of their children. There were no immediate heavy air raids and soon the evacuees began to return. By January 1940, over 675,000 had trickled back. Then, when the military situation became acute, another evacuation was carried out in June, 1940, in which 120,000 school children were again sent out leaving some 300,000 in London.

Schools 

The educational problem in London is of interest. Many school buildings were destroyed. There were no adequate bomb shelters in those that remained and the question was presented as to whether it would be better to have children study at home and submit their work to teachers for correction, or whether children would be encouraged to assemble in small groups in the homes of some of them and have the teachers visit and give personal attention. This latter was found to be the best and may be the percursor of a modification of English school methods.

Public Health 

In preparing for evacuation, the Public Health Service was brought into relief. The evacuation of the sick required the expansion of hospitals outside of London and the construction of temporary hospitals totaling 40,000 beds. Transportation for the large number of sick had to be improvised. A system was devised during the air raid periods much like the medical system in an Army—that is, a steady evacuation of injured from the battle front to areas outside the attacked region. A new classification of bed patients was made and those who were classed "home patients" were sent home. An additional 100,000 beds were cleared in this way.

With the influx of a large population into parts of the country which were previously rural and suburban, local authorities had to make increased provision for water, drainage, infectious disease accommodations, maternity hospital accommodations, midwives, and the nursing service.

Food Inspection 

Through the measures taken by the National Government to control food supplies and the collection of larger reserves of food, an added drain was placed upon the food inspection services. Food

WELFARE 

The sum of all the bombs-the fact of bombing—will create disturbances in the delicate social organism of cities which will be both severe and manifold. All citizens will be affected in some degree. There will be "social" as well as physical casualties. Some will accustom themselves to sleeping nightly in shelters, some will have to eat communally, and some will have to be evacuated to less populated districts.

The society of London has been slowly adapted and modified in response to the pressure of new needs. Shelters are provided so that people can sleep in safety, but the congregation of large numbers of people in unhealthy and cramped surroundings gives rise to the danger of epidemics. It is no longer possible for members of many families to eat together, and they, therefore, use communal facilities. Both as a preventive and as a remedy, evacuation of as many people as possible from crowded centers is plainly desirable. 

Welfare Wardens: A person who emerges from a bombed house may be suffering from mental shock but is rightly not considered as a fit subject for the normal first-aid services. And the existence of a rest center, possibly within half a mile, is no guarantee that such a person would find his or her way there. Even when victims of bombing find their way into the houses of friends or neighbors, their problems are by no means at an end. Therefore, Welfare Wardens are needed who will be on the spot as soon as possible for the purpose of directing the homeless to rest centers and looking after the needs of those who take temporary shelter elsewhere. They are a mobile corps of case workers who can apply the principles of individual case-work to the problems appearing at the scene of the bombing.

Rest Centers: As a rule, homeless people who fail to make arrangements with relatives or friends will find their way to a rest center. This is a place where they can stay until they are rebilleted, evacuated, or until their homes are repaired. 

In London, the rest center organization has shown steady development since September, 1939. Rest centers have increased from forty-three (43), established in March 1939, to five hundred ninetyeight (598) in September, 1940, comprising fifty (50) "first line" centers equipped, stocked and ready for use, forty-eight (48) "second line" centers usable but not equipped to "first line" standard, and five hundred (500) "third line" centers, which were to be staffed by voluntary workers, and only opened in the case of extreme emergency. When the test came, it was not so much the number of centers as their equipment and staffs which were deficient.

Mistakes were quickly recognized. A separate rest center service department was established, more premises were taken over and equipped, and teams of teachers, voluntary workers, and paid staffs were organized. There were in the middle of January, 1941, ninety-six (96) "first line" rest centers open and fully staffed in the London area alone. Billeting: The bottleneck of the rest centers is at the rehousing 

In England the metropolitan borough councils are the local billeting authorities. The original policy that homeless people should be rehoused in their own boroughs met with difficulty as the supply of billets, in boroughs most severely affected by bombing, was rapidly exhausted and there was no compulsion on people in rest centers to move into billets, however suitable they appeared to be. Billeting in strange districts and communal billeting have been far from successful. The real answers are outright evacuation or the use of hotels and boarding houses, publicly operated where necessary, and located near the place where the homes used to be. 

One of the reasons why women object to evacuation is their unwillingness to leave the working members of the family without, what wives and mothers consider, adequate provision for meals and care. 

House Repairs: Rest centers are essentially half-way houses to some more permanent provision. People will return to their houses, if that is possible, once there has ceased to be any danger from delayed-action bombs, or when vital services have been restored. Temporarily homeless people may stay in the rest centers until their homes are repaired. It is the business of city engineers to make all repairable houses wind and weathertight by temporary repairs, and, later, in suitable cases, to see that more permanent repairs are made. In London, local authorities are being assisted in this work, which is in progress on a considerable scale throughout the city, by 3,000 soldiers of the Royal Engineers.

There will often be sound furniture in homes which are themselves uninhabitable. The city is the authority entrusted with the job of protecting furniture and property left in damaged houses, and, when necessary, removing and storing it. The British have found that anxiety about furniture has been a common reason for not evacuating, and that it is important to provide facilities for its removal and storage.

Financial Aid: People who are bombed out, plainly need housing; this has been dealt with. But in many cases, they also need financial aid if their lives are to be restored to normal. Furniture, clothing, tools, cash savings may have been lost or serious injuries may have been sustained. The piles of broken furniture in the streets, or the clothes hanging on the only remaining walls of bombed houses in England, are common testimony to the necessity for financial compensation. 

London has an organization known as the Assistance Board, which grants compensation to "Social Casualties." The Board, with offices throughout London, makes advances to persons with small incomes to enable them to replace essential clothing and furniture; pays the cost of removing furniture if the local authority has not removed it; grants amounts not exceeding 50 pounds for the replacement of damaged tools; and, on behalf of the Minister of Pensions, pays temporary injury allowances to persons who are incapacitated from work as a result of being bombed. Quite apart from the Assistance Board, numerous voluntary agencies have

Salvage 

Salvage of paper, metals, bones, and rags was made a patriotic duty, carried out by school children. The articles are brought to the schools and are collected by municipal authorities from there. 

A clear income from the salvage campaign netted $7,500,000 a year. Recreation A recreational scheme was found necessary, not only for the population, but for the personnel of the civilian defense forces. The necessity for recreation fields and for garden plots used up all of the unbuilt space in all of the city areas. 

Vegetable Gardens 

Attempts were made to provide garden plots for all persons applying for them. Arrangements were made for the supply of seeds at cheap rates, and, in some localities, the lawns and parks were plowed under to afford space for growing vegetables. Power was given to municipal and county governments to seize unoccupied land and allocate it to individual families for food production. 

Black-Outs 

In the early days of the war, street lighting was completely extinguished. A dim light was permitted public vehicles at night but it was too faint to eliminate traffic hazards. Lights in public buildings were dimmed and screened and air raid wardens patrolled the streets to detect even the glow of low-powered bulbs from gaps in the shading of windows. Local authorities painted fresh white lines on the streets and along the curbs and placed screened red lanterns at danger spots. Traffic lights were ordered covered, with an opaque disk leaving only a narrow aperture for the emission of light. But, under these conditions, casualties from night traffic accidents arose at an alarming rate. Then came a modification of the first precautions. A low light is now allowed that is blacked out from the top with shades, with the light cast directly downward. 

The new lighting, though not giving anything approaching the illumination of pre-war days, affords a maximum of assistance to pedestrians by giving them confidence and the ability to see moving traffic. This light is on an intensity of four lumens which aerial observation has shown is the limit for lighting that will not reveal vital information to the enemy. Factories, hospitals, and certain key points are allowed to have more light until the siren sounds when their lights must conform to the four lumen measure.

Air Raid Shelters 

It was the aim in Britain to provide bomb shelters so placed, that every civilian in a locality considered vulnerable, could be sheltered within seven minutes from the sounding of an alarm. 

Municipal engineers provided for shelter for the First Aid posts or warden control centers and for lateral or sandbag protection for public buildings.

Public shelters were provided, and private shelters were designed. These latter shelters were of a type suitable for mass production and could be erected in private gardens. They were

given without charge to householders who earned less than $1,200 a year and who were not located near a public shelter. Families with a higher yearly income were urged to purchase these shelters. 

In June, 1940, it was estimated that there was shelter for twenty million people.

The British have found from experience that large mass shelters have not been very satisfactory. It is impossible to construct shelters far enough below the surface of the ground to make them impervious to the direct hits of heavy bombs. The subways are too near the surface to afford absolute protection. It has been found advisable, however, to add fragment-proof construction in existing buildings and to provide therefor in new structures. In the last analysis, there is no known protection against a direct hit of a heavy bomb. If protection is afforded against fragments and splinters, the maximum result has been effected. 

Training of the Public 

The emergency councils with their volunteer and professional personnel were confronted with the task of teaching the public how to protect themselves from the effects of war in the bombed areas. 

The public was instructed as to the effect of bombs and gas, what was necessary for their protection, and the rules of conduct during air raids. The councils also carried out the technical training that was necessary in the various volunteer services. In working on this, the classification of personnel was found to be of prime importance. 

Such classification resulted in the selection of volunteers and of applicants for full-time positions from technicians or men with experience. The ideal toward which the British are working is for paid municipal employees to be used as the trainers of volunteers and the volunteers to be employed on a part-time basis. Another interesting feature which has helped in the cutting down of the number of men engaged in this rear area work is the increasing employment of women in municipal positions and in the volunteer auxiliary services.

SECTION III British Organization 

In the organization of England's defense, which serves as a pattern for our own Civil Defense program, the British started with three principles-first, to make use of the existing structure of the local government as befitting the fundamental idea of democratic government; second, to use emergency air raid precaution organizations based upon a full-time and volunteer organization; and, third, to expand the police, fire and civil utilities departments in accordance with the needs of the emergency.

Each municipal government responsible for an Air Raid Precautions Service appointed an Emergency Committee of three to five men and an Air Raid Precautions Controller. If the local government was a well-organized town or city government, the head of the emergency committee was the Mayor and the active members generally members of the City Council or of the town government. 

The Air Raid Precautions Controller was generally the Chief of 

Police. He was the executive of the Council in carrying out its air raid and emergency measures. He also had direct communication with the Regional Commissioner, a home government official, in matters pertaining to air raid precautions. 

In county districts, the Emergency Committee was composed of volunteers entirely. The Executive Staff of this committee con sisted of an Air Raid Protection Controller, generally the Chief 

Constable, a health officer, a supervisor, and one of the local Fire Service Chiefs. These councils and their executive staffs were in charge of all emergency measures in their district during war and also in charge of the organization and training of the people for 

Air Raid Precautions Service. 

Great Britain was divided into thirteen defense regions. In each of these, there is a Regional Commissioner. These commissioners took their posts on the outbreak of the war. Each has a staff of governmental department representatives. No specific powers are delegated to the Commissioner. He represents the Home 

Government and at all times is under its authority. He is prepared in case of a breakdown of communications to take over the authority of the home office over the entire region. Thus, each of the thirteen regions would function should it be cut off from the counterpart or from other sections.

A Plan for Organization in the United States 

From the pattern of attacks on England and the description of the defense organization which she has developed, we may draw up a general plan for our own civil defense. Although such a plan must be based on the experience of others, we should be prepared for variations in the pattern of attack and for possible surprises in the methods.

Our National Government has charged the Office of Civilian Defense with developing civil defense plans and the War Department has directed the Commanding Generals of the nine corps areas to advise and assist the state and local governments in the preparation of their plans.

The War Department proposes to coordinate civil defense measures with other War Department activities, to authorize the use of military personnel to assist local authorities, to prepare essential instructional material, and to exercise general supervision of civil defense measures within the corps areas. The War Department will organize an Air Raid Warning System to be used by the Air Defense Forces and for giving warning of hostile attack to local civil defense areas. 

In the Second Corps Area, which comprises the states of New York, New Jersey, and Delaware, the Governors have created State Defense Councils and the legislatures have enacted permissive, and, in some cases mandatory legislation, to legalize organization for civil defense. Heads of local defense councils have been appointed and their organizations are in various stages of development. 

In connection with the problem of organization, we should consider what can be learned from our sister democracy. We should hold to our democratic structure and make use of the existing facilities of local government. Civil Defense is, after all, a problem of the state. The state must be responsible to its citizens for an adequate defense organization. This requires that a council be set up in each county, city, and incorporated community. The 

County Council will coordinate the organization of local civil defense councils of the cities, townships, and incorporated communities therein and the employment of defense forces of such communities. Large municipalities within such counties may be exempt from control and jurisdiction of the county civil defense council if so authorized by properly constituted authority. 

In well organized municipalities, the Mayor of the city should be the executive head of the civil defense organization. On the council, there should be the heads of those municipal departments whose duties are linked with civil defense activities. The council will include representative citizens who enjoy the confidence of the people. These men may be used as advisors, planners, organizers, and specialists in those technical subjects which will present themselves in the development and administration of civil defense. 

City departments will be expected to meet the emergency by the addition of paid full-time personnel and of part-time volunteers. 

In rural communities, the entire defense council may consist of volunteers. County officers whose official functions can be expanded to conform to specific defense activities should be exofficio members of the council or should be left in administrative or executive positions with the Defense Forces.

Each council, whether large or small, should have an executive staff headed by a Chief of Staff. This staff comprises an administrative section, an intelligence and liaison section, a finance, equipment and supply section, and a defense personnel section. The function of the staff is to coordinate the work of the main divisions or committees during the planning phase and, in the operations phase, to enable the council to function efficiently as a unit when an emergency requires the use of the defense forces. The work of planning and organizing can be divided among divisions or committees. Since the mission of civil defense is to provide for the protection of essential things and of people, there should be three main divisions: The first, Engineering; charged with preparing plans for the protection of essential utilities and industrial plants; the second, Protection of the Civilian Population, charged with preparing plans for the physical protection and welfare of personnel; and the third, Training, charged with plans for the education and training of the civilian population. This last division, in addition to its planning, will also carry on the educational work.

Let us now consider the work of these divisions in turn. 

The Engineering Division: The work of this division can be divided into three sub-committees, one for public utilities, a second for industrial plants, and a third for city and public maintenance, clearance and repair.

Public Utilities: The Sub-Committee on Utilities will conduct a survey with special attention paid to the key points most vulnerable to sabotage, and will prepare instructions as to the best methods of protecting such points with a minimum use of man power. 

Members of this committee should include, wherever possible, local engineers whose technical advice will be essential. 

Industrial Plants: This sub-committee will plan for the or ganized protection of all local industry. Some industries may be engaged in National Defense work. Others, though not so engaged, are still of vital importance to the community. All plants in the area which have not conducted a plant protection survey, or have not had one conducted by the F B I, should be listed and the work of surveying begun. Plant protection is a private matter, but the continued operation of the plant is a matter of vital concern to the community and to the nation. It is obvious that utilities and industrial plants will have to provide self-help and interior protection, and they cannot do this unless they have made proper plans. 

Public Maintenance, Clearance, and Repair: This sub-commit tee will prepare plans for the expansion of the personnel and the equipment of existing city and public maintenance departments for emergency use. Provision must also be made for substitute services when regular services are damaged or destroyed. Plans must be made for by-passing broken water and gas mains, insuring the continuance of telegraph and telephone communication where cables and conduits are damaged, providing continuance of electric power despite destroyed power lines and damaged plants, and maintaining the regular operation of transportation facilities. This sub-committee will plan for the organization of rescue, repair, demolition, clearance, and maintenance parties or squads which will function as units of the ARP Services, which have been described in Sec tion II.

Stoppage of work, whether caused by the attack of hostile forces or Fifth Columnists, would be a serious blow to National Defense.

Plans of the Division of Engineering will provide for the physical protection of utility and industrial plants. It will also be necessary

Air Raid Protection System: This sub-committee has the all important job of planning and organizing an Air Raid Protection System. This system lends itself to the needs of Civil Defense because it not only protects the civilians, their property and vital institutions from air raids, but also furnishes protection from other disasters and an adequate control of the population. Since many communities are skeptical of the probability of air raids, it might probably be well to call this the Civil Defense Protection organization. The basis of the organization is the Air Raid Warden Post 

System. This again could better be called Defense Warden System. 

When this sub-committee considers its plans, it must decide on the method of establishing Warden Posts. There are three main methods. The first is to have arbitrary areas and increase the size of the posts to conform to the population ratio. In uniformly settled suburban areas, this would be the best method. The second is to base it on the police precincts. The objection to this is that the police headquarters are going to be very busy on their own police protection duties. The third is to base it on election districts. The advantage of this basis for organization is that there already exists a sufficient number of leaders in the district who know all the people and for this reason would be very efficient in this type of work. 

After deciding upon Warden Posts, the ratio of wardens to the population should be determined. This will vary according to the type of population of each area. The next is the method of selection of wardens. Whatever method is used, whether by voluntary enrollment or by the selection and drafting of proper persons, care must be taken that the wardens reside in the area covered by their Warden Post.

Plans must then be made for the selection of the Report Centers and the Control Centers. Whether public buildings, police headquarters, or fire headquarters are selected will depend entirely upon the communication system. The plan must assure duplicate lines of communication as well as duplicate systems of communication from the Warden Post to the Report and Control Centers. 

There will be a tendency to concentrate the activities through the use of public buildings in order to avoid rental. This must be carefully guarded against because protection from any form of attack will depend upon the dispersion of the various defense installations, so that one direct hit will not wipe out more than one facility.

The Air Raid Protection Services must be carefully planned, not only for the expansion of existing services, but also for the creation of emergency services described in Section II.

Protective Construction: This sub-committee will consider the shelter necessary for its district. Protective construction is now the subject of study by the American Engineer. The War Depart ment has issued Bulletin No. 1 on Protective Construction. In the light of England's experience, mass shelters are not entirely satisfactory. It is not practicable to construct shelters which are impervious to direct hits of heavy high explosive bombs. In case of direct hits, such shelters may prove to be death traps. Subways are inadequate because they are too near the surface of the ground. 

Living conditions in mass shelters are often deplorable. Dispersion is the probable answer. In metropolitan centers, cellars and basements, reinforced with fragment proof construction, have proven to be adequate protection. In suburban communities, types of family shelters will probably be the solution.

Evacuation: This sub-committee will have a difficult problem. 

Three types of evacuation must be considered: First, a forced evacuation following a disaster, such as a sudden destruction of an industrial plant with a loss of the homes of the workmen; second, a panic flight of the citizens of the community; third, the deliberate removal of surplus population from a danger area. Evacuation plans must provide for temporary shelter in public buildings, churches, and schools, for emergency food, clothing, and medical care, and for the necessary transportation for the movement. Also, long periods of occupancy by evacuees must be considered and this will require a billeting system. The sub-committee should consider plans for types of evacuation most applicable to their particular locality. All communities must consider the first type, and most of them should consider the second type. Some localities must plan

CIVIL DEFENSE 

to be on the receiving end in such an emergency, while others must plan to prevent or to minimize the dangers of such an evacuation by controlling hysteria or panic at the source. The third type of evacuation is applicable only to the larger congested industrial areas. This type of evacuation must be carefully planned in cooperation with the areas considered suitable for the reception of evacuees. This cooperation must be effected through the State Council in each case. When the portion of the population which is to be moved, and an area which is to receive them has been determined, a survey should be made of this area to determine the facilities existing there for the reception of evacuees. This survey should list available individual shelter, community shelter, medical establishments, food supply, schools, churches, and water and sewage disposal. It must consider the system of payment for board and should set up a classification system to determine the type of evacuees who can be best taken care of in each locality or in each shelter. This plan will require a great variety of operating per sonnel. The Red Cross, Salvation Army, Women's Voluntary Ser vice Corps, Local Welfare Corps, and regularly constituted state and municipal agencies all must cooperate in this plan and in its operation. The test of its success will be the welfare and contentment of the evacuees after they have arrived in their new area. 

Health Service: This sub-committee is responsible for plans for the establishment of emergency medical and surgical services, care of the destitute and refugees, establishment of food kitchens and canteens, and control of food supply including the creation of communal markets.

Division for Civil Defense Training 

The great problem in Civil Defense is educating and training a people who have never considered the possibility of attack by air or land from without, nor by well-organized enemy sabotage units from within. In order that hysteria and panic may be avoided, the public should be constantly impressed with the fact that there is a Civil Defense organization, and that in any kind of an emergency, no matter how dire, their safety and welfare has been provided for. 

Thorough training must be given them as to what to do in air raids, hostile invasion, and other disasters, and how to do it. They must be prepared to adjust themselves to an unnatural and dangerous existence with a minimum of discomfort, and impressed with the fact that on their coolness, industry, and energy, the well-being of the community, and, therefore the nation, rests. 

Newspapers, radio broadcasts, periodicals, moving pictures, lectures, instructional pamphlets, and the more personal efforts of trained workers must be used to educate the people and to maintain public morale. When the defense organization is well under way, blackouts, simulated air raids, and other practical demonstrations should be conducted. 

Special training will be required for instructors and those actively engaged in Civil Defense work by means of schools and specialist courses. Student bodies in the public schools should receive proper training, not only to prepare them for their own roles in Civil Defense but to help in educating foreign-born or illiterate parents.

The Defense Personnel Section of the Executive Staff will pro ceed with the enrollment and classification of personnel to augment the regular city forces. The Police and Fire Departments must be increased either by deputies, student police, or student firemen, by the Department Reserves or by volunteers organized by the Mayor so that they can be trained or called out under service conditions. With regard to this augmenting of personnel, the main consideration is training. They must be trained for the emergency and not by it. As a supporting force for the local police, stands the State Police, and, as a final reserve, the State Guard. The 

State Police and the State Guard can be used in case of subversive activities that the local forces cannot handle or to defend strategic key points in the state's communication or utility system. The 

State Guard can be released only on the Governor's order. 

Community and disaster relief forces are composed of existing agencies and in addition auxiliary units which are being organized, such as the Women's Service Corps, Veterans' Service Units, Medical Units, and Motor Corps. These will establish food kitchens, rest centers, and other welfare services as may be necessary. 

The Water Department of the city will have to expand so as to be ready to repair broken mains and to switch water supply around damaged sections by means of the grid system. The installation of the necessary valves and by-passes for such a system is an expensive operation and will consume considerable time to install. The Highway and Street Departments will organize Rescue,

Demolition, and Street Cleaning Squads. Special preparation will have to be made to keep open telegraph and telephone communications so that the reports of conflagrations or of subversive activities can be promptly reported to the Control Center and The Air Raid Protection Forces and the Police can be sent to the threatened points.
