OCCUPATION, BUSINESS, AND AMUSEMENT FEES AND TAXES IN OREGON CITIES, 1949-50 

Oregon cities are looking increasingly to occupation or business license taxes and amusement taxes for revenue to supplement the property tax. These taxes are an outgrowth of the long-time practice of many Oregon cities of licensing businesses, occupations and amusements for regulation. Business, franchise, privilege and amusement fees, levied to cover the costs of inspection or regulation in the interests of health, safety, or morals, have recently been broadened to yield revenue in many Oregon cities. In the fiscal year 1949-50, at least 22 cities had comprehensive occupation or business license taxes, while in 1946 only 17 cities taxed occupations or businesses within the city in addition to those which required special regulation or inspection. Revenues from occupation, business and amusement fees and taxes have increased from $928,535, or 5.8 per cent of the general revenues of all cities, for the fiscal year 1943-44 to an estimated $1,784,417, or 7.9 per cent, in the fiscal year 1949-50. Portland, in 

1949, became the first city in Oregon to adopt a business license tax based in large part upon the gross receipts of specified classes of business. 

Eugene, in 1948, was the first Oregon city to adopt an admissions tax. 

The occupation and business license fees and taxes levied during the 1949-50 fiscal year by the Oregon cities responding to a recent questionnaire sent to all cities in the state by the Bureau in order to revise its 1946 bulletin on the same subject are tabulated in Part I of this bulletin. 

The amusement fees and taxes are tabulated in Part II. 

Of the 104 cities which completed and returned the questionnaire, 49 are levying both business and amusement license fees and taxes; only 1 city levies on businesses and occupations exclusively; 42 cities tax amusements only, and 12 of the cities replying do not tax either business or amusement. Of the 65 cities with more than 1,000 population reporting, 5 do not levy either a business or amusement tax. In 1946 of the 65 cities in the same population group reporting, only one levied neither of the taxes. 

Of the cities in the population group of 500 to 1,000, there has been an increase of nine in the number of cities using both types of tax. Five cities levied taxes on both business and amusements in 1946; in 1950, 14 cities levied on both classifications.

Occupation and Business Fees and Taxes Rate Basis 

Most of the business and occupation taxes now being levied by Oregon cities are on a flat rate basis for various business classifications. This is partly because the occupation and business license tax is an extension of the practice of licensing occupations and businesses for regulation, wherefor a flat fee was imposed. In a few cities, however, the rates on selected businesses are based on some measure of capacity or on the amount of business done.

The city of Milwaukie bases its license fees on businesses upon the number of employees including the owner or owners which it takes to carry on the business. License fees on trucks coming into Milwaukie are based on the number working in or out of the truck. The license fees as authorized are:

License fees in Portland are based upon gross sales and gross income. 

The fee for food or beverage processing, packaging, bottling, or canning and for manufacturing is $15 annually plus $1 on each $1,000 of gross sales of such business in excess of $15,000 and up to $115,000 plus 50 cents on each $1,000 of gross sales of such business in excess of $115,000 during the calendar year preceding the year for which the license is issued. 

The fee for professional persons and businesses, retail merchants, and service businesses is $15 annually plus 31 on each $1,000 of gross sales or gross income of such business in excess of $15,000.

The fee for wholesale merchants is $25 annually plus 75 cents on each $1,000 of gross sales of such business in excess of $30,000 during the calendar year preceding the year for which the license is issued.

Flat business and occupation taxes of fixed dollar amounts, such as are commonly imposed by Oregon cities, produce less revenue than rate schedules which vary with the volume of business transacted. Where the flat rate tax is used, the rate for each classification can be no higher than reasonable -2-

af

for the smallest volume of business in the classification. The flat rate schedule fails to produce increased city revenues during periods of price and population increases which expand the volume of business transactions. 

A flat rate schedule is regressive in that it places the greatest relative burden on the smallest business in any particular classification. 

The need for increased revenues to meet increased costs and to provide services to an increased population has in the past decade caused many of the larger cities in the United States to abandon their flat rate business and occupation tax schedules and to substitute a schedule of rates that varies according to some measure of the volume of business. Thus, some cities base their business taxes on gross receipts, the number of employees engaged in the business, the area of space occupied by the business, or on seating capacity. Gross receipts is the basis most generally employed. 

The use of such measures, though better adapted for the larger cities because of the added administrative complexities, tends to increase the yield over the flat rate tax and tends to make more equitable the distribution of the tax burden among the taxpayers according to ability to pay. In 1949 the city of Portland, conforming to this trend, adopted a schedule of rates founded in part upon the gross receipts of specified classes of business. 

A business tax based on net income--that is, on gross income less expenses incurred to create that income-would undoubtedly provide the most equitable type of business and occupation tax. Because this type of tax is difficult to administer at the municipal level, and especially so for smaller cities, many cities have adopted business taxes based on gross receipts, but have differentiated the rate schedules roughly according to the ratio of net income to gross receipts. Thus, wholesale businesses are taxed at a low rate and retail businesses and professions at higher rates. Similarly, since the margin of profits varies considerably among different retail businesses, these businesses are sometimes subclassified and taxed at different rates. These principles are illustrated in the Portland gross receipts tax schedule. Over a period of years the cities of Oregon have largely substituted this method in levying franchise fees to replace the flat rate franchise fee that was formerly imposed on public utilities under private ownership, but they are still largely using flat rate schedules in taxing businesses and occupations. Purpose

Every city has authority under its police powers to license businesses and occupations for regulatory purposes. But in the exercise of the licensing power for regulation, the authority of the city is limited to such a charge for a license as will bear some reasonable relation to the additional bur dens imposed upon the city by the business or occupation licensed and the necessary expense involved in police supervision. 3 McQuillin, Municipal 

Corporations, (2nd Ed. rev. 1943) Sec. 1091 (991). It should be noted carefully, in this connection, that some of the charges, particularly the larger amounts, listed in the accompanying tables include amounts for revenue as well as, or instead of, amounts for regulation. 

Express charter authority is necessary before an Oregon city may levy a -3-

business or occupation tax for revenue purposes. The Oregon supreme court has ruled that such taxes may be levied, for instance, under charter authorization "to license and regulate all such callings, trades, and employments as the public good may require to be licensed and regulated and as are not prohibited by law." Abraham v. Roseburg, 55 Or. 359, 105 P. 

401 (1910). See also Lent v. Portland, 12 Or. 488, 71 P. 645 (1903), Coos 

Bay v. Eagles, 179 Or. 83, 170 P. 2d 389 (1946), and Garbade and Boynton v. Portland, 50 Or. Sup. Ct. Advance Sheets 173, 214 P. 2d 1000 (1950).

While the question has not been litigated in Oregon, the courts of other states, recognizing that express powers can be stated in general terms, have held general grants of power in city charters sufficient authority to impose business and occupation taxes for revenue purposes. See Etter, General Grants of Municipal Power in Oregon, 26 Or. L. Rev. 141 (1947). 

It is assumed that the Oregon courts would follow these rulings and hold that a general grant of powers in a charter or incorporation statute of Oregon provides the express authority necessary to impose business and occupation taxes for revenue purposes.

In addition to statutory exemptions, city councils may decide to exclude, on grounds of public policy, certain businesses or activities from the coverage of an occupation license tax. For example, organizations carried on for the benefit of charitable purposes might be so exempted. Such policy exemptions are valid if there is a reasonable basis for the creation of the favored class. People v. Jordan, 172, Cal. 391, 156 P. 451 (1916). Classification

The nonexempt businesses and occupations may be classified for licensing for revenue. However, the following rules must be observed. All those businesses and persons exercising the same privileges in the same manner within the same jurisdiction must be placed in the same class for tax purposes and must receive the same tax treatment. The city may fix different charges for the separate classes by a standard or rule which is fair, reasonable and not confiscatory. Thus, wholesale businesses might be distinguished for taxing purposes from retail businesses; service businesses, professions, crafts and trades could justifiably receive different tax treatment than businesses producing or selling merchandise. See Garbade and Boynton v. Portland, 50 Or. Sup. Ct. Advance Sheets 173, 214 P. 2d 

1000 (1950), and Barnard Motors Corp. v. Portland, 50 Or. Sup. Ct. Advance 

Sheets 279 (1950).*

Peddlers, Solicitors, and Transient Merchants Special problems arise in connection with the regulation or taxation of business transacted in the city by peddlers, solicitors, and transient merchants. They are generally defined as follows: (1) a peddler travels from place to place, and sells and delivers at one and the same time; (2) a solicitor usually travels from place to place, not carrying his goods with him, but taking orders for future delivery; and (3) a transient merchant occupies a temporary fixed location, selling and delivering from stock on hand, doing business in much the same manner as a permanent business--the principal difference being in the temporary nature of the business. If a city lacks charter authority to impose a license tax, it can only license these traveling sellers for regulation. In this case, only such fee may be charged as is reasonably necessary to cover the actual cost of such regulation. Otherwise, such fee will be regarded as a tax in disguise and, therefore, beyond the city's powers. But if a city * The Oregon supreme court also held in these Portland cases that the state constitutional 6 per cent limitation did not apply to non-property taxes. -5-

has charter authority to impose a license tax, it may classify these traveling sellers for the purpose of licensing for revenue. The city may impose a higher license tax upon these traveling sellers than upon other classes of business or occupation taxed, so long as the rate of tax imposed is not arbitrary, unreasonable, nor has as its purpose the creation of a protective tariff for the benefit of local business. The justification for the higher tax on peddlers, solicitors, and transient merchants is equalization of the tax burden, since most of them do not have personal and real property used in their business or occupation on the local tax rolls.

Another way of dealing with traveling sellers for tax purposes without specifically classifying them would be to require under the occupation or business license tax that persons who do not own real or personal business property appearing on the local tax rolls pay a determined higher percentage than that paid by local property taxpaying businesses. Here, too, the amount of the differential in the tax could not be arbitrary, unreasonable, nor have as its purpose the creation of a protective tariff for the benefit of local business.

In taxing solicitors who are engaged in interstate commerce by virtue of orders taken for goods to be shipped or for work to be done out of state, a flat, dollar-amount tax has been held to be inherently discriminatory. 

Nippert v. Richmond, 327 U.S. 416, 90 L. Ed. 760, 66 S. Ct. 586 (1946).

Apparently the city may tax such solicitors engaged in interstate commerce, but the problem is to devise a fair method of tax computation under which interstate trade bears the same proportion of its share of the city's tax obligations as the local merchants, bearing in mind the value of the goods sold or offered for sale within the city by interstate trade in relation to the total goods sold or offered for sale within the city by interstate trade in relation to the total goods sold or offered for sale within the city by all merchants.

Note: Athena and Oakridge have comprehensive ordinances levying occupation taxes on all businesses, professions, occupations, or pursuits, and in each city the fee is $10. They were not listed in the tabulation in Part I because the returned questionnaires did not show the businesses upon which the tax was actually levied. 

The questionnaires for Beaverton, Burns, and Garibaldi state that for businesses not specifically enumerated the annual fee in Beaverton is $12, and the annual fee in Burns and Garibaldi is $10.

Jacksonville levies a $10 annual tax on all business houses. Only those businesses listed on the questionnaire are included in the tabulation. 

Medford and Redmond levy with a few exceptions taxes of $20 and $10 respectively on all businesses. The exceptions are tabulated in Part I since the questionnaires listed only those businesses in which the tax was different from the usual tax. 

Myrtle Creek levies a tax of $15 on most businesses, but was not included in the tabulation because determination could not be made of those businesses upon which the tax was actually being levied. Only businesses taxed at a rate other than the usual rate are included in the tabulation. -28-
