INTRODUCTION The Small Business Administration was established by the Small Business Act of 1953 (Public Law 163, 83rd Congress). 

Enacted on July 30, 1953, the law was signed the same day by President Eisenhower. This Administration is the first independent Government agency created to serve and represent all small business both in peacetime and in periods of national emergency. 

SBA has been given a far more extensive scope of operations than its immediate predecessor, the Small Defense Plants Administration, a great many of whose functions it has also acquired. In addition to being charged with SDPA's broad powers and responsibilities with respect to procurement and various other activities designed to assist small business and protect its interests, SBA has been given the authority to make business loans to small business concerns, as well as loans to assist victims of floods and other disasters. These latter two powers formerly were lodged with the Reconstruction Finance Corporation.

In addition to a broadened scope of powers and responsibilities, as compared with those of SDPA, SBA also has been given a greatly enlarged range of operations. From an area primarily confined to defense manufacturing and essential civilian industry under a mobilization economy, Congress has expanded the range of our responsibilities to include all types of small business enterprises, such as mining, construction, and transportation firms, and those engaged in distribution, retailing, and numerous service trades and industries.

Although SBA succeeded to certain functions and programs of SDPA, the problem of organization was not one, so to speak, of moving into an already established structure. A new structure had to be built and certain facilities, as it were, transferred from the old structure to the new. In the process a solid foundation had to be laid. Foundation laying is laborious, timeconsuming, unspectacular work. But the effort expended in this direction is already being reflected in the increasing momentum of our programs, old and new. 1

SBA in the first six months of its existence has been in the process of organization against a backdrop of changing economic factors. There is a minor contraction in the economy. A sharply reduced scale of Government procurement has added to the difficulties of numerous small contractors and subcontractors and has increased the number of concerns which must turn to civilian markets. These problems of our new "armed-peace" type of economy have required a flexible approach to SBA's responsibilities, with changing emphasis on various programs as the necessities of the situation warrant. 

Small business problems fall into three areas of major need and concern: obtaining a fair share of Government procurement; gaining access to adequate capital and credit; and obtaining competent management, technical and production counsel. 

In the discharge of its responsibilities, SBA believes that one of its fundamental tasks is to strengthen and expand the position of small producers of defense items. Their productive facilities are vital in times of national emergency and the procurement policy which spreads defense work over as wide an area as possible builds a strong, prepared network of small plants ready to spring into action at any time. It should be noted, also, that frequently in the process of obtaining the widest use of the facilities of small plants, SBA at the same time has saved the Government and the taxpayers many thousands of dollars.

Despite the steep reduction in Government procurement, SBA's efforts have succeeded in maintaining a fair proportion of purchases for small business or at least in staving off any further decline. An agreement recently negotiated with the Department of 

Defense may result in a much stronger joint determination program that will lead to earmarking an even greater percentage of suitable procurements for small business, particularly through dropping the minimum amount for purchases subject to joint determination from $25,000 to $10,000. The deeper cuts in procurement which loom ahead will necessitate the most vigilant and energetic action on our part. 

In the all-important area of finance, SBA conceives that its task is to obtain readier access for small business to adequate sources of capital, as well as credit, on reasonable terms. If credit for working capital is the life blood of business, capital is its structure and sinew. Business must have both types of financing not only to 2

survive but to operate successfully and expand. Small business is gaining increasing access to credit, but the problem of intermediate and long-term capital continues to plague the small firm, frequently from size alone, as much as the institutional environment in which it operates.

SBA has taken determined action at the community level to widen the scope of bank lending to small firms. Through our bank participation program, we have expanded substantially many a small bank's lending power, as well as its willingness to lend to small firms. 

This program and our program of direct lending, operating when required and within the confines of our statutory limitations, serve to meet an increasing part of the financial needs of small concerns. In many instances, our financial counseling, both in Washington and the field, has obviated the necessity of Government financing, showing the applicant how to obtain financing from private sources. 

During the four months in which our loan program has been in operation, despite the time required for processing applications, the difficulties in staffing, and the time required for making the business community conscious of our lending role, SBA has made 

55 business loans amounting to $3, 755, 900.

There still remains, however, an unfilled amount of capital requirements. SBA's statutory authority extends only to loans "of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment". We cannot supply equity money, which is the need of many small firms.

The recommendations of President Eisenhower and proposals in Congress for changes in the tax laws will benefit small concerns in many ways. These proposals, if enacted into law, will permit retention of a greater amount of cash in a business, particularly in the crucial, formative years of a company's operations. The elimination of the excess profits tax will also help to alleviate the financial stringency of many a small company. Even so, however, the tax burden must necessarily remain a heavy one on most small businesses, adding to their financial problems.

For many small businesses, the lack of many necessary managerial skills and technical and production "know-how" are serious impediments to progress.. In the growing complexity of business operations, businessmen can rely less and less on native talent and trial and error to make a success of their businesses. They must be alert, well

informed, and conversant with daily changes in their particular fields, if they are to survive. And yet all too frequently small concerns cannot afford to engage expert counsel to set up, for example, efficient accounting systems, or to advise them as to the most advanced business methods. 

Often, too, small firms cannot afford to hire engineers or other technicians to help them solve their technical problems. 

It is in this area of demonstrated need that SBA believes it is required by its mandate to make one of its most important contributions to the free enterprise competitive system and the preservation of small business as the wellspring of new ideas, new services, and new products. 

Through expert advice in Washington and our 31 field offices, we are furnishing engineering and production help to many small firms, as well as management and technical assistance. We are ublishing and distributing a wide range of publications in the management-aid field. We have launched an experimental program of extension courses in business management. We have recently established a new-product unit which will give small businesses help and guidance in producing new lines whether they are now in civilian production or in military work and face the necessity of reconversion. 

The test of the vitality of any free enterprise economy is the health of its small business concerns. The success of SBA's efforts in the areas just described will promote the success of our free enterprise economy.

As broad and challenging as is our role, it is only proper to point out frontiers for SBA activity.

In the first place, although we are the champion of small business, it goes without saying that we cannot and should not seek to underwrite the success of all small businesses. True, we must keep open wide the doors of opportunity so that we, as a country, can continue to profit from the ingenuity, industry and inventive genius that have produced our economy of abundance. However, free ingress into the business world does not necessarily constitute an open sesame to success. It would appear from statistics that many are foredoomed to failure, or at least retirement from the lists voluntarily or involuntarily after a few jousts in the economic arena. "Discontinuances" from business are for many causes: retirement, dislike of business, poor health, family break-ups, lack of sufficient capital, lack of experience, and many other causes. We do not believe that it was the intention of

Congress, nor do we propose, to underwrite incompetence, or lend money to stave off inevitable failure. Free ingress conversely implies free egress.

In the second place, a small business agency while being the advocate of small business, cannot be single-minded in its dedication to small business since the claims of small business must be woven in with other strands of public policy. Tax relief over and beyond the 

Administration's proposals, for example, would benefit small business greatly, but on the other hand SBA is cognizant of defense needs, which require high Government revenues. This problem is an illustration of the fact, however, that there are inequities besetting small businesses as compared with large ones which cannot readily be cleared away. 

In the third place, salvation for small business in the long run depends less on a Government agency than on local self-help and community effort and upon institutions existing within industry and the free enterprise system. By our assistance and counseling we are filling in the gaps and hollows that exist today. But at the same time we are developing cooperative efforts at the "grass-roots", such as by development credit corporations in the financial area, and we are teaching small business to use instruments on hand for managerial and technical assistance within the free enterprise system, such as trade associations and the universities and colleges. To nurture and develop further these mechanisms within the realm of private rather than Government responsibility is a frontier that lies ahead.

SBA DEFINES SMALL BUSINESS 

The  is attempting to solve one of the perennial problems of the small business field: "What is a small business?" Unless small business is successfully identified, the benefits that are designed for small firms may actually be obtained by firms which Congress intended to exclude. An accurate definition of small business therefore is essential. 

For the purposes of the Small Business Act of 1953, Congress provided that "a small-business concern shall be deemed to be one which is independently owned and operated and which is not dominant in its field of operation." The responsibility for making a more refined and detailed definition was delegated to the Small Business Administration. 

Experience has proved that effective administration of Government small business programs requires variable size standards by kind of business activity; thus a single size standard is unrealistic. 

SBA has worked out a definition of small business based on the variation in the size structure of manufacturing industries and other types of business activities. It is intended that these size standards will be used only as a guide until their adequacy has been demonstrated. They will be given a practical test by our field offices for a trial period. 

Only after trial will the standards be treated as conclusive. 

The Small Business Population: 

The actual number of small businesses changes daily. 

However, Chart 1 shows the estimated number of small business concerns as of June 1953, on the basis of U. S. Department of Commerce estimates of the total business population and SBA criteria used to define small business. 

About 96 percent of an estimated 4,212,400 operating businesses, or more than 4 million of them, are small as defined by the SBA size standards. They account for approximately one-half of total value of economic activity. 

The small business share of total business does not remain constant and is difficult to measure at any time. However, in the field of manufacturing, an estimated 310,000 small manufacturers

The account for a little over 30 percent of total output, whereas fewer than 20,000 large manufacturers account for nearly 70 percent. percentage of total business accounted for by small wholesalers is about 45 percent; retailers, 60 percent; service trades, more than 75 percent. In other categories of business activity, the aggregate proportion is nearly one-half. These include mining, quarrying, transportation, communications and other public utilities, finance, insurance, real estate, and contract construction.

Size Standards: 

In a bulletin which the Acting Administrator signed on January 19, 1954, these tentative size standards were formulated: 

1. For the present, the definition of small business used by the Government procurement agencies shall continue to be used for the purpose of procurement assistance programs. That is, any concern which employs 500 or fewer persons, including affiliates, shall be classified as small. 

2. Whenever an SBA loan is involved in the issuance of a certificate of competency, the same 500-employee standard shall be used in determining a company's eligibility for such loan. 3.

For all other purposes, any manufacturing concern shall be classified: (a) As small if it has 100 or fewer employees; (b) As large if it has more than 1,000 employees; (c) Either as small or large, depending on its industry and in accordance with industry employment size standards, if it has more than 100 but not more than 1,000 employees.

For example, concerns in the following industries are considered small if they have 100 or fewer employees: canvas products, cleaning and polishing preparations, bread and other bakery products, mattresses and bedsprings, luggage, and envelopes. 

Concerns in the following industries are considered small if they have 250 or fewer employees: textile bags, canning and preserving (except fish), pickles and sauces, wood office furniture, scientific instruments, lead pencils and crayons.

Concerns in the following industries are considered small if they have 500 or fewer employees: explosives, salt, storage batteries, insulated wire and cable, watches and clocks, footwear (except rubber), and machine tools.

Concerns in the following industries are considered small if they have 1,000 or fewer employees: medicinal chemicals, soap and glycerin, motors and generators, radios and related products, sugar, and photographic equipment.

When a manufacturing concern is engaged in the production of a number of products which are classified into different industries, the standard to be used is that for the industry in which it is primarily engaged.

Because employment may vary widely during the year for some lines of business activity, consideration will be given to the average employment of a concern during the preceding year. 

4. Any wholesale concern whose annual sales are $2,000,000 or less shall be classified as small. In addition, any of the following types of wholesale concerns whose annual sales are $5,000,000 or less shall be classified as small: Coal and coke (in yards); coffee, tea, spices; cotton; drugs (general line); dry goods (general line); electrical appliances and equipment; electrical goods (general line); farm-dairy machinery and equipment; general merchandise; grain; groceries (general line); hardware, general line, with industrial department; meat, meat products; non-ferrous metals and metal work; piece goods, converters; refrigerator equipment (household); tires and tubes; tobacco (leaf); wool (mohair). Any wholesale concern also engaged in manufacturing shall not be designated as a "small business concern" unless it so qualifies under both manufacturing and wholesaling standards. 

In addition, 5. Any retail or service trades concern whose annual sales are $300,000 or less shall be classified as small. any of the following types of retail concerns whose annual sales are $500,000 or less shall be classified as small: farm equipment dealers, lumber yards and building materials dealers. In the case of new and used motor vehicles dealers and department stores the standard shall be $1,000,000 or less.

Variations From Standards: 

SBA is ready to recognize any revisions in these standards that may be necessary in the light of experience and new statistical evidence. It cannot be emphasized too strongly that these standards are not meant to be inflexible. They are designed only as a basic guide from which SBA may deviate in a particular case. The uniform definition may fail to take into full account size differences of concerns competing for business within a particular product line or in different geographical regions or metropolitan areas. For some industries a different index of size, such as number of units produced or net worth, might be more suitable. In such cases, the SBA Administrator, with the advice of his Office of Economic Adviser, will apply the more suitable standard. Despite the fact that the present size standards cannot fit all cases, they are a long step forward to a more realistic answer to the question: "What is small business?" 10

ECONOMIC POSITION OF SMALL BUSINESS 

The huge growth in the economy, the increase in personal income, and the expansion in consumer wants open wide opportunities for small business in the years ahead. Our gross national product in 1953 prices has increased from $175, 900, 000, 000 in 1929 to $367,000,000,000 in 1953; personal consumption expenditures have increased from $1 23, 200, 000, 000 to $229,900,000,000. Even if the present year should witness a 5-percent drop in the gross national product by virtue of the readjustment now taking place, this would leave the output of goods and services at $349,000,000,000, which would still be higher than the 1952 output of $348,000,000,000. 

This is an expanding economy. Our technology, as in electronics, atomic energy and aviation, is opening up new vistas; our population is increasing by leaps and bounds. In such an environment, there will be opportunity and reward for the new, resourceful entrepreneur.

Looking to the immediate period, the minor contraction in the economy has not had a serious impact on most small businesses. 

The failure rate is not significantly high and the number of business incorporations was much greater in 1953 than in the preceding year. 

The total business population continues to increase although the rate has slowed up since the mushrooming of new businesses right after 

World War II. 

The latest available sales and earnings statistics do not show too bright a competitive situation for small business as a whole. The impetus given by the Korean boom has faded, leaving small business in a somewhat less favorable position than in the pre- Korea period. Large businesses, on the other hand, are definitely better off in sales and earnings than they were before Korea. The deep slash in procurement as we have moved from the emergency into the "armed-peace" economy has been disastrous for many small businesses formerly in defense production. Further cutbacks and cancellations of Government contracts are to be expected in the next fiscal year. 

There is little escape from the conclusion that, if the interests of small business are to be adequately protected and promoted in the period ahead, existing procedures within the Government will have to be drastically overhauled. 11

The rate of growth of the business population has slowed during the past three or four years. The rate of increase in the first half of 1953 was only 33, 600, compared with 54, 100 in the first half of 1952. This slowing up should not lead to the conclusion that small business is on a decline. The extremely rapid growth of the business population after World War II, when thousands of ex-GI's went into business, had resulted in a total business population which is somewhat higher in relation to the total human population of the country than was true back in 1929; thus a slower rate of increase is not surprising. Another point is the fact that since World War II business firms have increased by 35 percent as against a 14 percent increase in population.

As is evidenced by Chart 2, growth of the business population was dealt a body blow by the circumstances of depression and war. 

There were a quarter of a million fewer businesses in 1933 than in 1929. 

The historical relationship between the number of businesses and the 13

total population was not restored until 1937. 

Again, during the first two years of U. S. participation in World War II, 1941 to 1943, there was a decline in our business population of more than 350,000. After World 

War II, the number of new businesses increased rapidly. 

From one aspect, these figures give testimony to the remarkable stamina of small businesses in the face of competition with big business. From another viewpoint, however, there is little comfort in the bare fact that small business has survived. The period since 1929 has been one in which there has been a spectacular increase in living standards and a big rise in disposable personal income. Contemporary observers have recorded the shift from a "producing" to a "consuming" economy. The President has recently referred to "the new horizons that have recently opened up for the consumer." Since 

1929, in terms of 1953 prices, per capita disposable income has increased from $1,059 to $1,553.

In view of the increased ability and desire to spend and the number of new products on the market, an increase in the business population of 39.1 percent since 1929, compared with a growth in human population from 121, 770,000 to 159,696,000, or 31.1 percent may not be considered a very encouraging growth record for small business.

Noteworthy is the fact that for the entire period 1929 to 1953, the growth was relatively greater in the wholesale trades, contract construction, transportation, communication and other public utilities, retail trades, and manufacturing, than in mining and quarrying, finance, insurance and real estate.

The number of new business charters reached a peak during March 1953; in that month, 9,659 stock corporations received charters. While this is serious for the individual small firms affected, it does not justify taking a pessimistic view of the future for all small businesses.

Small Business Growth Dependent on Retention of Earnings 

There has been a great deal of discussion about the inability of small business concerns to finance expansion of facilities out of earnings. This situation is made obvious in Chart 9, which shows changes in the level of earnings after taxes beginning with 1947. 

In the case of large corporations, earnings after taxes were slightly higher during the latter part of 1952 and 1953 than during the base period. On the other hand, the earnings of small corporations were only two-thirds as great as during the base period. 23

Tax Factors: 

Students of the small business problem agree that the oppressive tax burdens on small business are a major obstruction to survival and growth. Retained earnings are a prime source of financing for corporations. Up to 65 percent of their financing needs in 1953 came from internal financing including retained earnings and depreciation allowances, but the tax burden on small, growing firms has been so great as to prevent them from plowing back earnings to any great extent.

Small business should obtain considerable tax relief in 1954. The biggest boon is the death of the excess profits tax, which crippled new and struggling small businesses. Under that tax, a new business became subject to the 82-percent levy on every additional dollar of income as soon as it passed the $25,000 mark. On the other hand, a corporation with a million dollars of earnings might be subject only to the 52-percent rate.

Under the National Administration's new tax proposals, many of which have been adopted by the House Ways and Means Committee, small business would obtain substantial relief. Major proposals include: (1) Depreciation allowances 

The taxpayer would be allowed to step up depreciation deductions in the early years of an asset's life either by allowing more leeway in determining the life expectancy of the capital asset or by the use of the declining-balance method of depreciation. This would enlarge the amount of funds that small business could spend on capital assets. 

(2) Double taxation of dividends The Administration's proposal would seek to mitigate the present situation under which business income is taxed both to the corporation as it is earned and to stockholders as it is paid out in dividends. Under the House Ways and Means Committee's proposal, taxes on dividends would be eased step by step through a period of three years. 

(3) Research and development expenses Unless a company has an established research or development program, these expenses now must be treated as capital expenditures and are not deductible. This is especially burdensome to small concerns because large companies with established research laboratories can usually 24

get immediate deductions. The Administration's proposal to allow such expenses to be currently deductible will be an incentive to small businesses to engage in research and development work. Under the 

House Ways and Means Committee proposal the company can elect to write off the expenses over a five-year period. 

(4) Accumulation of earnings (Section 102 of the Internal 

Revenue Code) - This would provide for a slight easing for small companies which have to depend on retained earnings for expansion. 

The Government rather than the business would have to assume the burden of proof that earnings were unreasonably retained. (5) Optional tax treatment for corporations and partnerships Corporations with a small number of active stockholders would be given an option to be taxed as partnerships and certain partnerships be given an option to be taxed as corporations. The privilege of being treated as a partnership will be valuable to a corporation, relieving it of the double tax load. (6) Corporate reorganizations 

Estate taxes often force the sale of independent companies to larger corporations. The tax law would permit tax-free rearrangements of stockholders' interest in corporations to secure new capital or to make stock available for a new management group so long as no corporate earnings are withdrawn. (7) Loss carry-back 

The one-year carry-back of losses often is insufficient for a company which experiences a loss year. 

The present one-year period would therefore be extended to two years. The five-year carry forward would be continued to permit new businesses to offset their early losses against later profits. 

Undoubtedly, the two most important provisions are those relating to depreciation and the double taxation of dividends. The increased depreciation deductions would help small businesses which are unable currently to expand. It must not be overlooked, however, that big business would also enjoy the same privilege, so that in many instances it is debatable as to how far the competitive position of small business would be improved. Study should be given to the possibility of extending to small businesses in the future, a preferential privilege which would be denied to larger businesses. 

The easing of double taxation of corporate dividends would initially give a healthy boost to shares of big, well-established companies. It would in turn, however, be a big boon to smaller companies, 25

whether incorporated, or unincorporated, whether new or established. 

Present high returns attract available risk capital to old established companies rather than to new companies with an uncertain future. A loosening of risk capital would divert a greater share of it to new small companies which investors now pass up. 

Some advocates of small business have urged further tax relief in the form of differential treatment. They have urged, for example, that the $25,000 limit for the basic 30-percent rate be lifted to $100,000 or that the basic rate of 30 percent should be lowered while the surtax is raised. 

Small companies have complained volubly about the hardship caused by the Mills plan for acceleration of tax payments. 

In 1954, corporations will have to pay 45 percent of their 1953 tax liability in each of the first two quarters, and in 1955, 50 percent. 

Small companies have had difficulty accumulating funds for tax payments and often have been forced to borrow. The National Adminis tration has recommended that beginning in the fall of 1955 a start be made in smoothing out corporation income tax payments by requiring advance payments in September and December before the end of the taxable year. In a transition to such a pay-as-you-go basis, small corporations may be hard hit by the necessity to make payments for the current as well as the previous year's taxes. 

Financing for Small Firms: 

External factors operating in the general economic environment during this period enlarged the availability of credit for business from private sources, thus diminishing the demand for credit from a 

Government agency: (1) The money supply hit a new peak during this period, reflecting in part the Government's shift to an easier money policy. 

Total deposits and currency amounted to $205, 500, 000, 000 in December 1953. (2) The minor contraction in general business activity reduced the demand for credit. (3) The slow-up in inventory accumulation by early fall was succeeded in November by inventory liquidation, which reached an annual rate of $6,000, 000, 000 by the end of November 1953. 26

(4) The reduction in Government procurement reduced the demand for loans to finance Government contracts, a type of demand which had been very great when SDPA was in operation. (5) The imminent demise of the excess profits tax diminished the demand for loans. Corporations which had borrowed huge sums to broaden their invested capital base, thus creating additional exemption from the EPT, prepared to repay these loans or use the loans for future needs. 

As a result of these factors, the private banking system had a growing surplus of funds which it could employ. This brought about healthy competition for loans by banks and finance companies; interest rates declined; rates on commercial paper were reduced month by month to the lowest level since 1951. For example, on 

January 22, General Motors Acceptance Corporation reduced the interest rate for money it borrows on commercial paper its fifth such reduction in four months. Loans to business by banks in the 

New York Federal Reserve district dropped for the week ending 

January 6, 1954, by $231,000,000, the largest weekly decrease in banking history. By January 29, loans outstanding had dropped by $440,000,000 for 1954 compared with a decline of $130, 000, 000 a year ago. Loans outstanding by all Federal Reserve banks by the week ended January 27 had dropped by $500, 000, 000 less than a year ago. A study by the Federal Reserve Board of new bank loans in the first two weeks of December showed a reduction of 5 percent. 

Talk of a cutback in the prime rate from 3 1/4 percent to 3 percent was widespread.

Private Credit for Small Business: 

Private lenders are becoming increasingly conscious of the financial needs of small business. Banks continue to establish departments for small loans. The National City Bank, reporting on the plan it adopted in May 1950 to make business loans of $10,000 for as long as 60 months to qualified borrowers, stated that as of the end of 1953, 38,000 such loans, totaling $46, 000, 000, were outstanding and that its loss experience last year was less than one-fourth of 1 percent.

Insurance companies, such as Prudential and Metropolitan, also have small business loan programs in operation. Life insurance companies have been able to invest in small business primarily through 27

the mortgage loan route. A large New England company made 231 business loans in 1952 of which 21 percent were in amounts of $50,000 or less. In recent years life insurance companies have found it possible to meet the requirements of small borrowers via direct placements, that is, bond debenture issues directly negotiated with borrowers. "The 1953 Year Book of Private Placement Financing, a publication of E. V. Hale & Company of Chicago, shows many $25,000 to $200,000 loans.

Security Issues: 

One of the difficult financing problems facing small business is the reluctance of security houses to market issues for small companies and the prohibitive expense of such flotation. Investment banking houses ordinarily pass up any flotation of less than a million dollars as unprofitable. Considerable relief to small business would be effected under a bill now pending in the Senate which was introduced by Senator Capehart of Indiana. It would exempt offerings of $500,000 or less from registration requirements of the Securities Act of 1934. Proponents of the change describe the present $300,000 limit as onerous, since it does not take into consideration the change in the value of the dollar since the limit was set in 1945.

GOVERNMENT PROCUREMENT 

As pointed out in the chapter dealing with the economic position of small business, the steep decline in Government procurement during the first half of fiscal 1954 had a much sharper impact on small business than on big business. This made more difficult the responsibility of the  to see to it that small business receives a fair share of Government contracts. Despite the factors during this period tending to concentrate prime contracts in large business, the drop in the small business share has been stopped through the efforts of SBA in cooperation with the Depart ment of Defense. 

SBA has secured informal agreement from the Department of Defense on a number of important changes in the joint determination program which should serve to increase substantially the volume of military prime contracts for small firms. SBA also is intensifying its efforts to obtain all possible subcontracting for small firms in the areas of both defense and civilian production. In this way the agency hopes to offset the normally great reduction in subcontract awards to small firms during a period of declining Government purchasing. While SBA's joint determination program has been limited to date to the military departments, the agency also has taken steps with other large purchasing agencies, such as the General Services Administration, to increase the small business share of their purchases. 

THE JOINT DETERMINATION PROGRAM 

Nature and Purpose of Program

SBA's joint determination program is designed to increase the small business share of Government procurement by restricting for competitive bidding among small firms all or a percentage of the military procurements suitable for award to them. 

To accomplish this objective, SBA has strategically located representatives where they may screen unclassified purchases in 35 major military purchasing offices. 

Criteria used in determining which procurement offices shall be covered by the SBA representatives are (1) volume of purchases, and (2) suitability of items purchased for manufacture by small 35

business. Thus, SBA representatives are stationed in procurement centers on the basis that a center's purchases must total at least $50,000,000 for each man committed and that an adequate percentage of its total procurement must be suitable for award to small firms. 

In some instances, one SBA representative covers two or more offices whose combined yearly purchases exceed $50, 000, 000. 

The 35 military procurement offices covered by SBA representatives are shown in Appendix E. Present plans provide for coverage of the following additional offices: Rock Island Arsenal, Rock Island, Illinois; Picatinny Arsenal, Dover, New Jersey: Raritan 

Arsenal, Metuchen, New Jersey; 832nd Air Force Depot, Topeka, Kansas; and Gadsden Air Force Base, Gadsden, Alabama.

SBA representatives at procurement centers initiate joint determinations on purchases determined to be suitable for award to small business. Each joint determination earmarking all or part of a procurement for small business is reviewed by military procurement officials and either agreed to, modified, or totally rejected. If modified or rejected, the SBA representative has the right of appeal to the chief procurement official of the installation, whose decision is final, except in the case of Army Quartermaster installations. There the appeal may be made to the Office, Assistant Chief of Staff, G-4 Logistics, through SBA's Washington office.

If a joint determination is made for 100 percent of a proposed procurement, the contracting officer proceeds to buy in the normal manner, either through advertising or negotiation, notifying all concerned that the procurement is restricted to small business. 

If only a partial joint determination is made, the portion not restricted to small firms is advertised to any and all who are interested and the award is made to the lowest competent bidder. The portion reserved for small business is then negotiated with small firms based on the price established by the advertised portion.

Proposals For Improving Joint Determination Program 

The first 11 of the following SBA proposals have been informally agreed to by officials of the Defense Department. The last proposal is being given further consideration by the Department. Proposals 1 and 2, it was agreed, would be included in a new Department of Defense directive. Proposals 3 through 11 will not be included in this basic directive, however, since they represent detailed operating procedures. It was agreed, instead, that they would be included in directives to be issued to personnel at the operating level by each military department. It also has been agreed that the implementing procedures of each of the military services will be checked to insure inclusion of the intent and purpose of the basic agreement with the Department of Defense.

Joint determinations shall not be made with respect to general classes of items, but will be limited to individual procurement actions which are expected to involve an expenditure of $10,000 or more. (The minimum amount is now $25,000) 2. When the bids are restricted to small business, bids received from firms not classified as small business will be considered as non-responsive.

COUNSELLING AND ASSISTING SMALL BUSINESS IN SELLING TO THE GOVERNMENT 

The purpose of this program is to aid and assist small business concerns by making available all possible information regarding military and civilian Government procurement opportunities. SBA has no authority to place prime contracts with small companies. Instead, the agency must seek out procurement opportunities for small business wherever they may be, to insure that a fair proportion of the total purchases and contracts for supplies for the Government shall be placed with small business enterprises. 

Through its Washington Headquarters, its 31 field offices, and its representatives in the principal military procurement centers, SBA disseminates a vast amount of information to help small business compete for Government contracts.

In providing such assistance, it is necessary for SBA to determine what type of products or services the businessman can supply and then to furnish him with the names and locations of Government installations which normally buy the items or services. 

Once a firm has learned which agencies buy the products and services which it is capable of supplying, it is then necessary for it to obtain listing on the agencies' appropriate bidders lists. 

In addition to comprehensive counselling on selling to the Government, SBA, through its regional and branch offices and its representatives in procurement offices, renders many other types of assistance to small business. For example, SBA: 1. 

Intercedes for small firms having difficulties with procurement offices. 

2. Advises small firms of the necessary procedure for getting their products on qualified products lists. 41

3. 

Instructs small firms as to bidding technicalities in both advertised and negotiated procurements. 

4. Assists small firms in obtaining prints and specifications. 

5. Initiates action on certificates of competency to assure that competent small low bidders whose financial or production ability is questioned do receive the award. 

6. Furnishes information regarding major prime contractors for possible subcontract work. 

Provides a library of packaging specifications in SBA regional and branch offices for the convenience of small firms. 

8. Advises small firms of purchasing offices which need additional suppliers for specifically described items. 

9. Screens previous bid and award data in order to help new suppliers quote on future procurements. 

10. Instructs interested firms on the method of securing samples of items up for purchase. 11.

Makes pre-award surveys of small firms which are low bidders when asked to do so by procurement officials. 

As an indication of the work load necessary to provide the above types of assistance. SBA representatives in procurement offices interviewed 1,007 representatives of small firms during the first six months of SBA operation, handled 8, 267 pieces of correspondence and 10, 169 phone calls. They were also instrumental in having 1, 228 small firms placed on bidders lists. A far greater number of requests for procurement assistance were handled by the SBA regional and branch offices and the Washington Headquarters.

In the course of counselling small business firms, SBA's procurement assistance personnel find it necessary to consult with contracting officers and procurement policy officials in many Government agencies. It is frequently necessary 42

for SBA to analyze and develop recommendations on procurement policies of these agencies which will result in small concerns being awarded a greater proportionate share of their prime contracts. follow:

A few examples of specific assistance to small firms Through negotiations initiated by an SBA representative, a St. Paul, Minnesota, concern received considerable price relief on a prime contract that had required several Engineering Change Orders. These Change Orders had increased production costs and the procuring activity agreed to an increase in the contract value of approximately $59, 000. 

A small firm in Marion, Indiana, was low bidder on a military contract, but the procuring installation's regional office reported unfavorably on the company, stating that it had been delinquent on several contracts. Investigation by SBA disclosed that the report concerning the small company was biased because of unfavorable personal relations between a member of the military installation's regional office and the president of the small firm. It was further learned that the company had been delinquent on only one contract, which had then been finished, and that the company generally was considered a satisfactory supplier. 

When the results of this investigation by SBA were brought to the attention of procurement officials, the small firm, rather than the next lowest bidder, was awarded the contract. This saved the purchasing office $8,800.

SBA was asked by a small firm in Chicago to help it obtain the opportunity to bid on a product that was considered a sole source item. SBA established that the small firm was licensed under patents to produce the item and arranged for the firm to be supplied with bid sets. It also arranged for extension of the time limit for bid opening so that the small firm could submit a proposal. SBA's action resulted in the award going to the small firm at almost $60, 000 less than the amount bid by the former sole source, which is a large company. 

A small firm in New York City notified SBA that money due the company was being held by the purchasing office pending accounting and settlement of costs resulting from com- 43

pletion of several contracts. As a result of SBA representations to the purchasing office, approximately $35,000 was released to the contractor and only the estimated amount involved in the settlement of costs was withheld.

A small firm in Monticello, Illinois, had made several appeals for an extension of time on its product delivery date, but each time had been refused. When this was brought to the attention of SBA, the agency successfully interceded with the purchasing office for an extension of time which enabled the small firm to complete its Government contract. 

A small California firm complained that a $1,500,000 procurement for asphalt by the Corps of Engineers was discriminatory to small business.

The discriminatory feature of the invitation for bid was that delivery could be made (1) in Saigon, Indo-China, or (2) at dockside. If delivery were made to dockside, the Government would pay 100 percent of invoice at time of delivery, but transportation costs of $30.69 per ton based on MSTS (Military Sea Transport Service) rates had to be added to the price for evaluation of the bid. If a firm bid on a delivered to Saigon basis, actual costs of $18.75 per ton would be included in the bid price, $11.94 less per ton than dockside. However, Saigon delivery required that only 65 percent of total payment would be made upon delivery at dockside, and the other 35 percent, amounting to approximately $500,000, would be withheld for several months until the material arrived at Saigon. 

Because small firms could not afford to finance a $500,000 hold-back, they were forced to bid for delivery at dockside, with the higher freight rate, as compared with larger bidders who could finance the hold-back. 

After discussions with officials of the small firm, the Congressman from its District and a member of the House Select Committee on Small Business, SBA interceded with the Corps of Engineers and the Foreign Operations Administration, which had placed the procurement with the Corps of Engineers for purchase. 

After considerable negotiation it was agreed that the evidence presented was sufficient to warrant cancellation of the procurement. 

It is anticipated that the asphalt procurement will now be made on the basis of delivery at dockside, which will be fair to all bidders. 44

FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE 

SBA's program of financial assistance is designed to stimulate and preserve the initiative, independence and enterprise of small firms. The basic consideration in this program is that small business concerns generally are at a competitive disadvantage compared with large businesses because: (a) they are frequently unable to afford specialized financial management; and (b) they are often unable to obtain from private sources the intermediate- and long-term credit required for their general purposes and normal growth. Paramount in SBA's functions is our financial counseling assistance, which we have given to hundreds of small firms, directing them to available private sources of credit and thus minimizing the need for Government credit. Our lending activities are a secondary aid after all other possibilities for assistance have been exhausted. 

No loan applications were accepted by SBA until October 1, 1953, in order to allow time for development of procedures and necessary staffing. SBA'S LOAN AUTHORITY 

The Small Business Act of 1953 empowered SBA "to make loans to enable small business concerns to finance plant construction, conversion, or expansion, including the aoquisition of land; or to finance the acquisition of equipment, facilities, machinery, supplies, or materials; or to supply such concerns with working capital to be used in the manufacture of articles, equipment, supplies, or materials for war, defense, or essential civilian production or as may be necessary to insure a well-balanced national economy." 54

The Act provided that no loan can be made for more than $150,000 or for longer than 10 years, except in the case of construction loans where the period may be for such additional time as is required to complete the construction. All loans must be of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment. 

Congress also empowered SBA to make loans for the relief of individuals, businesses, or institutions suffering losses because of floods or other disasters.

The Reconstruction Finance Corporation, which formerly exercised a direct loan function, is in process of liquidation, to be completed by June 30, 1954. There are important differences between the new and the old system: 

(1) The former Small Defense Plants Administration could recommend to the RFC that a loan be made to a small business. The 

SBA is now empowered to make its own loans. (2) SBA is limited by law to lending no more than $150,000 to any one firm. In contrast to the almost unlimited lending power of 

RFC, SBA has been granted by Congress a revolving fund of $55, 000, 000. 

Since it has been estimated that disaster loans will run around $5,000,000, the SBA revolving fund will have available about $50, 000, 000 for other types of loans and for SBA's prime contract function. (3) A fundamental difference between SBA and its predeces sor agencies in the small business field, the Smaller War Plants Corporation and the Small Defense Plants Administration, is that SBA is empowered by law to help all small business firms, not merely those engaged in the manufacture of military, defense, or essential civilian items.

THE FORMULATION OF LOAN POLICY 

The first task confronting the  was to work out the appropriate policies, methods and procedures for effectuating the mandate given it by Congress. The direct lending authority of SBA does not permit grants or subsidies to business concerns but requires that the loans made be of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment. Accordingly, in administering the direct lending program. SBA must be sure to apply sound credit principles to promote the welfare of small business enterprises and to protect the general interest in accordance with the intent of Congress. 55

The general policies under which SBA authorizes both business and disaster loans are determined by a 3-member Loan Policy Board composed of the SBA Administrator, as Chairman, and the Secretaries of Treasury and Commerce. This Board made a decision in August to defer the acceptance of loan applications until after September 28, 1953 when the lending authority of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation expired. In the interim all applications were referred to that agency.

Policy Statement on Loans: 

The first policy statement issued by the Loan Policy Board on September 16, 1953 gave preference to loans to Manufacturers or processors of materials and products for military, and essential civilian purposes. A revised statement dated November 16, 1953 eliminated the preference formerly given to manufacturers and made possible loans to all kinds of business concerns on an equal basis. 

A later amendment dated December 21, 1953 permitted use of funds to refinance indebtedness to a Government instrumentality, which would enable a person owing money to RFC to refinance the loan as a corollary purpose of the loan. 

Appendix A is a copy of the SBA loan policy statement. 

It fixes the interest rate for a direct SBA loan at 6 percent per annum. 

In the case of participation loans, the rate of interest may be fixed by the bank or other lending institution provided the interest rate cannot be less than 5 percent on the portion of the loan which SBA is obligated to purchase or, in the case of an immediate participation loan, on the SBA share in the loan. The loan policy statement lists purposes for which financial assistance will not be granted. For example, a loan cannot be made if the purpose is to finance recreational or amusement facilities; or if the direct or indirect purpose is to pay off creditors who are inadequately secured or in a position to sustain a loss; or if the purpose is to effect a change in the ownership of a business; or if the purpose is to provide capital to an enterprise primarily engaged in the business of lending or investment. 

On December 21, 1953 the Loan Policy Board issued a public statement of loan policy and an explanation of loan procedures. 

It contained a list of practical credit requirements such as "applicant must be of good character;" "there must be evidence applicant has the ability to operate his business successfully;" "the applicant must have enough capital in the business so that with loan assistance from SBA it will be possible to operate on a sound financial basis." 56

Bank Participation Plan: 

In accordance with the Small Business Act of 1953, SBA has worked out procedures for entering into participation agreements with banks and other lending institutions. This plan has sound reasons and objectives. It ensures that private credit facilities are used to the utmost; it minimizes the drain on the reservoir of public credit funds by sharing the burden with private credit; it obtains a local evaluation of the applicant's credit problem by the financial specialists of the local bank. 

A direct loan by the  is authorized only as a last resort. These are the steps which must be followed: (1) The applicant must show that he has been refused private financing on reasonable terms. (2) The applicant must seek deferred participation in which the bank or other lending institution provides the entire amount but has an agreement with SBA under which it will purchase part of the loan on demand at any time. (3) The applicant must seek an immediate participation in which SBA agrees to take a portion of the loan from the start. (4) SBA grants a direct loan.

In the case of a deferred participation, SBA is limited to the purchase of not more than 90 percent of the outstanding balance of the loan, which is payable on demand at any time. The bank or lending institution originates the application to SBA. The borrower makes application for a participation loan with SBA. at the desk of his own banker. According to the SBA loan policy, in return for SBA participation in the loan the bank pays the agency a small fee, which varies with the extent of SBA participation. SBA fees, which are designed to encourage maximum participation by private lending institutions, are as follows: 57

percent per annum on SBA participation up to and including 50 percent 

1 1/2 % percent per annum on SBA participation from 51 percent to and including 75 percent 2 percent per annum on SBA participation from 76 percent to and including 90 percent

The yield to the bank on a 6 percent loan under this plan ranges from about 4 percent to 5 percent, less cost of servicing and administration.

In the case of an immediate participation, the borrower must file his application with SBA with a letter from the bank stating its willingness to participate. SBA gets the full interest on its share. 

SBA and the Private Banking System: 

The  is not competing with private banks. Before getting SBA help, the applicant must prove that he has been refused credit by his own bank of account. If the amount of the loan applied for is in excess of the legal lending limit of the applicant's bank or in excess of the amount that the bank normally lends to any one borrower, then proof of refusal must be furnished from a correspondent bank, or from any other lending institution whose lending capacity is adequate to cover the loan applied for. 

The applicant must additionally show that he cannot raise the money from other known sources of capital in his community, nor through an offering of securities, nor through disposal at a fair price of assets not required in the conduct of the business, nor "through utilization of personal credit and/or resources of the owner, partners, management or principal shareholders of the applicant"--with due regard being taken for undue hardship--nor through use of a V-loan or other applicable Government financing.

Far from competing with private banks, SBA is encouraging banks to lend by sharing in the risk and participating in the loan. 

To further this objective, it is SBA's policy to encourage banks or other lending institutions to take over any loans made by this Administration on a direct or an immediate participation basis. 58

Banks generally have shown considerable interest in the authority of SBA to participate in loans to small business concerns. 

These participation loans permit a bank to accommodate its small business depositors, often by providing loans in excess of its lending limit. This is made possible by holding the bank participation to the bank's loan limit and obtaining an agreement from SBA to purchase the balance of the loan either immediately or deferred to some future date determined by the bank. In a deferred participation loan, the SBA portion does not affect the bank's reserve requirements and the bank receives the full interest return less a small commitment fee ranging from 1 to 2 percent of the SBA share. 

It is conceived to be a function of SBA to fill in interstices -- "chinks"--where commercial banks feel that they cannot or should not make a loan. Cases such as these were reported in a survey made by 

RFC in the last half of 1946. This survey which covered direct loans. 

RFC had made that had been declined by banks, showed the following reasons for bank rejection of the loans: (1) The amount of the individual loan exceeded the bank's legal limitation. (2) The term of repayment was longer than the bank was willing to approve. (3) Necessary diversification of lending portfolios sometimes made it unwise for a bank to extend credit further within a particular industry.

There is undoubtedly a clear recognition in the banking community that there is an unequal distribution of capital--geographic ally speaking--over the country, with definite localized shortages of credit. Although the number of such areas has decreased during the past 20 to 30 years, reflecting the increased mobility of credit that has accompanied improved transport, communication, and banking facilities, the Federal Reserve Board in a study issued in May, 1953 declared--"there are undoubtedly some communities where the shortand intermediate -term requirements of small business concerns have outgrown existing local sources of such credit. The SBA representative, together with the applicant, re-analyzed the situation to make his application more attractive to the banks. It was suggested that the businessman re-apply to the banks, offering again his fixed assets to secure the $5,000 loan, together with the assignment of his contract which it was estimated would net him in excess of the $5,000 needed. 

It was also proposed that the loan should not exceed the duration of the contract. This new proposal was acceptable to a local bank. (11) A sawmill company, with assets in excess of $450,000, was in financial difficulties. This is due in part to the excess costs over estimates of erecting the saw and planning mill, installing roads, purchase of machinery, etc. Acting on SBA's suggestion, the manage ment contacted a lumber company which needed substantial amounts of pine to supply the needs of its retail outlets. This resulted in cash orders sufficient to relieve temporarily the company's distressed condition. (12) A World War 11 veteran, who is a veterinarian, had established a lucrative business after the war. He constructed and equipped a veterinary hospital in 1952 at considerable expense, obtaining a mortgage on the real estate and purchasing considerable equipment on conditional sales contracts. His earnings enabled him to meet his fixed obligations until he was recalled to the service in 1953. He therefore sought an SBA loan to consolidate all his debts. 

Upon exploring the details of the indebtedness, SBA arranged (with the consent of the veteran, who is stationed at a distant base) with the local mortgagee of the real property for a moratorium on the mortgage payments. Staffing Problems:

Difficulties experienced in staffing the regional and branch offices and the time required for interviewing the many individuals who seek information in person have resulted in a considerable backlog of applications in these offices. 

To reduce the backlog of applications, procedures are being further simplified within prudent limits and completion of field staffing is being expedited. A reasonable balance between applications received and applications processed is anticipated within the next 30 days. Thereafter, efforts will be directed to reducing processing time to a minimum and to providing a speedy decision on all loan applications. 70

Present regional and branch office staffing authorizations provide for 71 financial specialists. This provides for at least one financial employee in each branch office and from two to six financial specialists in each regional office, depending upon estimated application volume. Staffing of these offices is being completed. 

In the last months of its lending activities, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation had approximately 500 loan examiners in its field offices. SBA, with a substantially smaller staff, has had to select its financial specialists very carefully to assure maximum output per employee and to provide the highest possible quality of service to small business concerns. These SBA financial specialists must be more than loan examiners. Besides having a background in credit and banking, they must understand Government lending as well as the problems of small business generally. They must be resourceful in suggesting solutions to these problems and be able to inspire the confidence of small businessmen. Men with the qualities desired are not readily available. Consequently, staffing of the field offices has been slow. Due to difficulties in staffing, it was necessary for SBA to call on the Reconstruction Finance Corporation for assistance to the greatest extent possible in processing some of the many applications being received.

As SBA field staffing progresses the agency will be able to give faster consideration to the problems of small business concerns and to provide needed assistance more effectively.

DEVELOPMENT CREDIT CORPORATIONS 

As a longer-range objective, SBA wishes to foster the development of State-wide and local pools of private capital, often called development credit corporations, in which banks and other financial institutions, business and civic leaders and individual citizens can participate.

Since the end of World War II, State-wide development credit corporations have been set up in each of the six New England States, in each case obtaining a special charter from the State legislature. Under these charters, capital stock is sold to obtain funds with which to start operations and to provide a reserve against bad loans. Lending funds of the corporation are obtained chiefly through loans to it by commercial banks, savings banks, trust companies, building and loan associations and insurance companies. 

Development credit corporations have wide latitude in their approach. PRODUCTION AND MANAGEMENT ASSISTANCE 

Small business is at a competitive disadvantage in the area of technical and managerial know-how. The small firm is unable to bid for engineering talent, which is now at a premium in the job market; it cannot compete for highly trained specialists graduating from business schools during periods of shortage; it cannot send expediters into the field to search for scarce materials and equipment; it cannot set up research laboratories to develop and test new products and to engage in other research which may cost hundreds of thousands of dollars. 

The easing of the materials and equipment situation in recent months has enabled the  to expand its efforts in other directions. Production specialists in SBA field offices have concentrated on helping small businesses iron out their production problems. The list of publications informing small firms of the latest management methods has been lengthened considerably. 

In cooperation with colleges and universities, an educational program to bring the small business in touch with the latest management policies and procedures has been placed into operation. Most important, a service has been established to give small business concerns assistance, advice and guidance in identifying new products which they can produce or distribute.

MATERIALS AND EQUIPMENT ASSISTANCE 

A basic objective of the Small Business Administration is to insure that small business concerns receive adequate materials and equipment for defense production. Materials and equipment shortages, which plagued small business after Korea and throughout the operation of the Controlled Materials Plan, are not the problem today that they were a year or two ago. There has been an increase in productive capacity of the three principal metals needed for defense production-steel, copper and aluminum; there has been a decline in military requirements, and there has been a slight let-down in the general level of industrial operations.

These three factors have combined to make most materials readily available through normal sources of supply. However, there still are occasional instances where individual firms are unable to obtain needed materials or equipment in certain industries or in certain local areas. It is in instances of this type that SBA's services are useful. 75

Through close working relationships with suppliers of materials and equipment, SBA is able to locate promptly materials or production equipment needed by the small manufacturer. 

At the policy level, SBA acts as spokesman for small business before Government agencies which are developing future materials and equipment plans or which have adopted policies that are causing hardship to small firms. As an example of SBA's assistance in this area, SBA jointed forces with the now terminated National Production Authority to urge removal of restrictions on the use of nickel-bearing materials. This recommendation, which was adopted, was made in behalf of several thousand small business concerns comprising the electroplating industry, suppliers in large part, of electroplating service in the production of automotive parts, hospital and surgical supplies, houseware, etc. The restrictions on use of nickelbearing materials had seriously hurt this industry, which had lost much of its business to foreign competitors, who were not similarly restricted in the uses which they could make of nickel. 

Machine tools are of primary concern to small manufacturers. Expensive at all times, they are subject to critical shortages in emergency periods. The objective of SBA has been to assist small defense contractors in obtaining needed equipment. SBA representatives have consulted with officials of the Office of Defense Mobilization, the Business and Defense Services Administration, of the Department of Commerce, the General Services Administration, and the Department of Defense to assure full consideration of the interests of small firms in the distribution of Government-owned machine tools. 

In April 1952, SDPA began a program through which its field offices provided small firms with information on the number and kind of Government-owned tools available from educational institutions over the United States. During the past six months, SBA has obtained from over 400 colleges, high schools and other institutions the latest information on their holdings, and SBA's present listings of machine tools available from such institutions can be regarded as current. This program has been a boon to small business. Through it many small manufacturers have been able to obtain tools that could not be obtained in any other way, since the backlogs of all machine -tool builders have been large. Also, SBA's program has made it possible for a small firm to obtain machine tools at prices considerably below those of new machine tools. 76

During the period August 1, through December 15, 1953, a number of requests for assistance with machine tool problems came to SBA's Washington office through its field offices. In 27 cases assistance was given in obtaining needed machine tools from used-machine dealers; in 17 cases, assistance was extended in acquiring machine tools held by educational institutions; and in 35 cases, assistance was given in leasing Government-owned machine tools controlled by the Defense Department and BDSA. Five invita tions-to-bid on machine tools owned by the Government and by educational institutions were sent from the Washington office to nearby regional offices, which in turn informed small firms of the opportunity to purchase the tools.

In order to safeguard the interests of small business in materials planning and policies, SBA has sought and obtained representation on Government committees responsible for the plans and policies or has been promised representation as committees are formed. For example, SBA staff members serve as members or observers on important committees of both the Office of Defense 

Mobilization and the Business and Defense Services Administration. 

SBA also is studying çarefully the materials control measures previously employed, as the basis for steps to insure for small firms a fair share of materials and equipment in any future defense emergency.

PRODUCTION POOLS 

SBA gives advice and assistance in the formation of small business production pools and formally approves the pools' proposed operations in accordance with Section 217 of the Small Business Act of 1953. This approval is subject to the concurrence of the Attorney General and the Chairman of the Federal Trade 

Commission.

Production pools consist primarily of groups of small concerns voluntarily associated together to obtain and perform jointly contracts for the production of articles, equipment, supplies, and materials, or for the furnishing of services needed for the defense effort. The firms have a mutual agreement as to their organization, relationship, and procedure.

When a group of small firms becomes interested in pooling their facilities and bidding as a group on defense production, their first step is to consult the nearest SBA regional office. SBA gives information and advice to the group concerning the procedure to be followed and the rules and regulations pertaining to the formation of a production pool. If, after considering this information, the group desires to proceed further, it asks the SBA office to arrange an initial meeting of representatives of all interested firms. If this request, which lists the firms interested, indicates the eligibility of the prospective group, SBA arranges such a meeting at a place that is convenient to the firms and has a representative of the agency preside. After the proposal has been thoroughly examined, if the group decides to seek Government approval of a pool, it then files a formal application, with SBA, together with a description of the proposed plan of operation. 79

By organizing as a pool, a group of small firms usually is able to perform larger and more complex contracts than the firms would be able to handle individually. Experience has indicated, however, that pools are less likely to be successful in peace-time than in a period of war. Of the pools now approved, at least a third are inactive and are maintaining their organizations on a standby basis. 

However, this technique is now being studied for applications in labor distress areas.

Authority to approve pools under the Small Business Act of 1953 was delegated by the President to the Administrator of the 

 by Executive Order No. 10493, signed October 14, 1953. On October 30, 1953 the Administrator of SBA 

resigned and a new Administrator had not been appointed by January 30, 1954. The Attorney General ruled that a pooling order could not be signed by an Acting Administrator. Therefore, SBA has had only 

12 working days during which it has been able to approve pools. No new pools were approved during that time, although since then the necessary steps have been completed for the approval of three new pools. Some new applicants for membership in existing pools were approved during the period, bringing the total number of companies in the 26 approved pools to 581 with approximately 28, 000 employees. 

FACILITIES INVENTORY 

Section 210(a) of the Small Business Act empowers the Administration "whenever it determines such action is necessary to make a complete inventory of all productive facilities of small-business concerns which can be used for war or defense production, or to arrange for such inventory to be made by any other governmental agency which has the facilities." This section also provides that the 

States may be requested to furnish such State-wide inventories as are available or in prospect. Exploratory investigations of the nature of the undertaking have indicated that facilities inventories would best be compiled and maintained by the States. Moreover, this is in keeping with the Administration's desire to decentralize its operations and to utilize as fully as possible local means and resources in all phases of assistance to small businesses. 80

As a stepping stone toward a Nation-wide inventory, and in order to provide currently needed information as rapidly as possible, SBA is accumulating small business productive inventory information in each of its regional offices. Under the present arrangement, small firms desiring assistance in obtaining prime contracts or subcontracts furnish SBA regional offices with information as to products normally manufactured, products capable of being manufactured for defense and essential civilian use, estimates of productive capacity and lists of equipment and machinery. A basic information file is maintained alphabetically by company names; a secondary file gives the industry classification of each company. As regional offices receive information on proposed procurements or need for specialized production facilities, they use the inventory records as a source of ready information on firms that can qualify for the production involved. 

By January 15, 1954, approximately 20,000 small firms had listed their facilities with SBA. 

Although this constitutes only a small percentage of the manufacturing plants in the country, it nevertheless is a basis upon which a Nation-wide small business facilities inventory can be build whenever the Administration determines such action is necessary.

NEW PRODUCTS PROGRAM 

The  has established a program of technical and managerial assistance to small business in locating and developing products which they can manufacture, including new lines and new types of products. The need for new-product assistance to small business has become increasingly important because of recent economic trends. Cutbacks or cancellations of

Government prime contracts and subcontracts have left many small plants with idle facilities, and these firms need advice and help in reconverting to suitable civilian lines. Aside from the effects of 

Government procurement policies, there also has been a minor contraction in economic activity which has created distress among some small plants; many of these firms are seeking additional product lines in order to tap new markets.

The need for helping small firms to find new products is a continuous one which prevails independently of temporary economic adjustments. All business concerns are searching continuously for new products. Markets change and there must be a shift to new production. There is an increasing tendency in big business to diversify product lines in order to obtain insulation against market declines which may affect a single product. Small business has been grievously handicapped in the search for new products, however, because of the lack of funds to hire sufficient engineering and technical help for this specialized branch of "know-how". An SBA products assistance program has been established within the Office of Produc tion Assistance. This office will:

(1) Provide a "clearinghouse" to which small firms may come for information on redesign, improvement or further development of present product lines and development of new lines and new types of product. (2) Provide assistance in finding private and Government research facilities which are available to small concerns. (3) Counsel with small firms with respect to problems related to new products and with respect to ways and means of keeping abreast of technical developments and maintaining a profitable operation. (4) Provide counseling assistance in locating and apprais ing new lines and new types of product and new-product ideas; counsel in using patent office facilities, and in obtaining new product ideas through organized searching of national and international patent information available through Government channels; working cooperation with other Government agencies such as the Department of Commerce, Department of Agriculture, FOA and the Government Patents Board to develop information on products and markets. (5) Maintain lists of specialists in product design. 

A company seeking SBA assistance in redesign improvements and further development of present products and in locating new lines and new types of product will make initial contact with the SBA regional or branch office in its area. The regional or branch office will survey the company's plant or business facilities and refer the case to SBA's Washington office for analysis and recommendation by the products assistance division. The Washington office will direct and coordinate the program to insure that its execution at the Washington, regional or branch office level will follow a systematic and uniform pattern. 

Liaison with Schools of Engineering: 

The SBA intends to develop close working relationships with schools of engineering to bring the services offered by the laboratories and other facilities of these schools within the reach of small businessmen. Schools of engineering are particularly eager to help small businesses test and develop new products, but despite this persistent 85

interest on the part of the schools and the continuing need for such help on the part of small businessmen, no close working relationship has yet been perfected. SBA is now setting up a series of conferences with deans of schools of engineering to work out a suitable program. 

For example, on December 8, 1953, the representatives of SBA's Minneapolis Regional Office met with representatives of the engineering department of the University of Minnesota and 15 small plant owners. The purpose of the meeting was to explore and discuss ideas for the development of new products which could be manufactured with existing facilities and marketed profitably by small manufacturers in that area. The meeting resulted in: (a) A more comprehensive recognition by the University of the service it could render to small plants; (b) a belief that the University can conduct studies relative to the scope of products which could be made in the State and marketed profitably; (c) the possibility of the use of small plants for part-time employment of student engineers; (d) the possibility of the University setting up a service bureau for manufacturers which could be a source of information or reference. 

Mr. Robert H. Ramsey, head of the Industrial Reference

Division of the School of Engineering, Pennsylvania State College, has advised SBA that his office is prepared to: (a) Assist the industries of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, principally the smaller ones, with their technical engineering problems; (b) bring to the attention of industries the services of engineering and research organizations of the State including professional engineers, research organizations, and commercial laboratories in the fields of research and development; (c) bring to smaller industries sources of information concerning engineering research and development activities; (d) create interest in organized research and development programs so that the indus tries of Pennsylvania can produce new products, better products, and more products at less expense; (e) bring current industrial problems to the attention of members of the School of Engineering faculty so that they and graduate students may keep in better touch with industrial activities.

Publications: 

SBA is preparing a number of publications which should prove useful in the new-product field. For example, under a contract 86

with SBA, a leading management consulting firm is preparing a rounded guide which can be used by small firms to solve technical problems in the new-product field. This publication will advise small firms of private research facilities which are available to them, including profit scientific institutes, nonprofit scientific institutes, such as Battelle Memorial Institute and Armour Institute, engineering schools, and the facilities of large corporations. The publication will also advise on ways and means of setting up a technical division within a small plant. SBA also is preparing a listing of leading technical publications and trade association bulletins which investigate and list new products. This SBA Guide will indicate the type of product that is covered in each publication. In that way a small manufacturer, by reading or subscribing to publications in his own or related fields, will have a ready reference guide to current new technical processes and new products.

EDUCATIONAL COURSES FOR SMALL BUSINESSMEN 

The  has inaugurated a program to help the owners of small manufacturing and distributing firms to help themselves by bringing them in touch with the latest management policies and procedures. This policy is based on the belief that independent operators will adapt the new management ideas -if exposed to them--to their own needs. 

This program has been launched in cooperation with educational institutions throughout the country. A small business education conference arranged by SBA was held in Washington on December 4, 1953. Among the participants were: Dr. Robert Love, Director, Evening and Extension Division, City College School of 

Business, New York City; Dr. Raymond Coleman, Dean, College of 90

Commerce, University of West Virginia; Dr. William Hoad, School of Business Administration, University of Michigan; Dr. James E.

Georgia; Dr. Nathan A. Baily, Chairman of Business Administration;

Bankers Association, Washington Office. 

On December 29, the SBA Acting Administrator announced to the Executive Committee of the American Association of Collegiate Schools of Business an SBA program for management courses for owners of small businesses to be held throughout the country. SBA does not intend to carry out this program of extension courses with its own staff, but will work primarily with the colleges and universities of the country which have highly trained and experienced staffs. 

SBA will assist in organizing the sources and act as a clearing house in their development.

The major problem which the owner of a small business faces is the lack of management experience and knowledge of sound management procedures. He has no staff of experts; he cannot afford to retain a staff of consultants. Through experiment and investigation, SBA is determining what problems small operators are facing and what kind of courses will provide the best answers in the easiest, most graphic manner.

The SBA will use university schools of business, colleges of engineering, evening colleges, and university extension departments. 

SBA has arranged with the University of Richmond, at Richmond, Virginia; the University of Vermont at Burlington; University of

Wisconsin at Milwaukee; and one other university at Cleveland, Ohio, to co-sponsor experimental courses for owners and managers of smaller firms. Different methods will be used in these four courses. 

Nationally known speakers will lecture and answer questions; conference leaders will conduct discussions among owners on important management problems; technicians will answer questions and explain procedures. Many of the latest devices of visual education will also be employed.

The businessmen will meet one evening a week for 8 to 10 weeks; listen to and take part in discussions on their management problems; and develop ideas which they can apply the next day to their own operations. At the conclusion of the course, they will receive a 

Certificate indicating their attendance and interest. 91

Upon the completion of these courses, every "student" will be asked to evaluate the work in terms of substance, procedure, and speakers. Then, based upon experience and the comments of these experienced, practical business operators, a manual will be prepared, setting forth the best of the courses for the use of educational institutions, associations and other organizations interested in training or aiding the smaller operator in helping himself. 

A typical course will be that which the University of Wisconsin is inaugurating in cooperation with SBA. The fee will be about $25 for an 8-week course. The meeting will be two hours each night once a week, for eight weeks. The group will be limited to approximately 25 and conducted on the conference-discussion basis. 

In the first hour, the conference leader will present questions and lead discussion among the businessmen on questions which have been raised. In the second hour, the conference leader will introduce a management expert who will comment on the discussion and answer questions raised. Initially, members will be limited to small manufacturers. Interviews will be held in the Milwaukee area to determine what problems small businessmen are facing today about which they would like more information and advice. 

The experience gained from this test course, like the other test courses, will serve as a guide to SBA as it expands the program to other parts of the country. 92

INTRODUCTION This General Policy Statement is established by the Loan Policy Board of the  pursuant to Section 204(d) of Public Law 163, 83rd Congress (Small Business Act of 1953). 

It sets forth the principles and policies which will be followed by the Small Business Administration in the granting and denial of applications by small-business concerns for financial assistance. It is not intended that this General Policy Statement provide answers to all questions which may arise in connection with specific applications. 

II. AUTHORITY AND PURPOSES Under Section 207 of the Small Business Act of 1953 the 

Small Business Administration is empowered, among other things, 93

"(a) to make loans to enable small-business concerns to finance plant construction, conversion, or expansion, including the acquisition of land; or to finance the acquisition of equipment, facilities, machinery, supplies or materials; or to supply such concerns with working capital to be used in the manufacture of articles, equipment, supplies, or materials for war, defense, or essential civilian production or as may be necessary to insure a well-balanced national economy; *** "(b) to make such loans as the Administration may determine to be necessary or appropriate because of floods or other catastrophes ***"1

All financial assistance granted by the  must be for one or more of the purposes set forth above. 

III. DEFINITION OF A SMALL BUSINESS 

Section 203 of the Small Business Act of 1953 provides that a small-business concern shall be deemed to be one that is independently owned and operated and which is not dominant in its field of operation. 

In addition to the foregoing criteria, the Small Business Administration, in determining whether a given firm is a "small-business concern" (pursuant to Section 212(c) of the Act), may also use the number of employees and the dollar volume of business of the firm. 

IV. BASIC PRINCIPLES GOVERNING THE GRANTING AND DENIAL OF APPLICATIONS FOR FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE 

A.

Applications for financial assistance may be considered only when there is evidence that the desired credit is not otherwise 94

available on reasonable terms. The financial assistance applied for shall be deemed to be otherwise available on reasonable terms unless it is satisfactorily demonstrated that: 

1. Proof of refusal of the required credit has been obtained from: (a) the applicant's bank of account, and (b) if the amount of the loan applied for is in excess of the legal lending limit of the applicant's bank or in excess of the amount that the bank normally lends to any one borrower, then a refusal from a correspondent bank, or from any other lending institution whose lending capacity is adequate to cover the loan applied for. 

2. The funds required do not appear to be obtainable from other known sources of capital serving or operating in the business community concerned. 

3. The funds required do not appear to be obtainable on reasonable terms through the public offering or private placing of securities of the applicant. 

4. The funds required do not appear to be obtainable through the disposal at a fair price of assets not required by the applicant in the conduct of its existing business or not reasonably necessary to its potential healthy growth. 

5. The funds required do not appear to be obtainable without undue hardship, through utilization of the personal credit and/or resources of the owner, partners, management or principal shareholders of the applicant. 

6. V-loan, or other applicable Government financing, is not available to the applicant. 

B. All loans made shall be of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment. No loan shall be made unless there exists reasonable assurance that it can and will be repaid pursuant to its 95

terms.

Reasonable assurance of repayment will exist only where the loan is of sound value, or is adequately secured in the judgment of the . It will be deemed not to exist in all cases where the proposed loan is to accomplish an expansion which is unwarranted in the light of the applicant's past experience and management ability, or where the effect of making the loan is to subsidize inferior management. 

C. It is the policy to stimulate and encourage loans by banks and other lending institutions. 

1. An applicant for a loan must show that a participation by a bank or other lending institution is not available; no immediate participation may be purchased unless it is shown that a deferred participation is not available. In 

2. 3. No agreement to participate shall establish any preferences in favor of the bank or other lending institution participating in the loan with the Small 

Business Administration.

V. TERMS AND CONDITIONS OF LOANS TO SMALL BUSINESS CONCERNS UNDER SECTION 207(a) OF THE SMALL BUSINESS ACT 

A. Amount. No loan shall be made if the total amount outstanding and committed (by participation or otherwise) by the  to the borrower would exceed $150,000. 

B. Maturities. No loan, including renewals or extensions thereof, may be made for a period or periods exceeding ten years, except that any loan made for the purpose of constructing industrial facilities may have a maturity of ten years plus such estimated additional period as may be required to complete such construction. 

It shall be the policy, however, to restrict the maturity of each loan to such minimum as is consistent with sound business practice. 

C. Charges and Interest. 

1. Charges: In deferred participation loans (those made by a bank in which Small Business Adminis tration has entered into an agreement with the bank to purchase thereafter a participating share in the loan), a participation charge shall be payable by the bank to Small Business Administration for the latter's agreement to purchase a share in the loan. The participation charges shall be on a sliding scale, depending upon the percentage of the loan which Small Business Administration is obligated to purchase. Such charges shall be as follows: 

For a loan in which Small Business Administration is obligated to purchase (i) an amount not in excess of fifty percent (50%) of the loan, the participation charge shall be one percent (1%) per annum on the portion of loan which Small Business Administration is obligated to purchase; 98

(ii) an amount in excess of fifty percent (50%) of the loan, but not in excess of seventyfive percent (75%) of the loan, the participation charge shall be one and a half percent (1-1/2%) per annum on the portion of the loan which Small Business Adminis tration is obligated to purchase; and (iii) an amount in excess of seventy-five percent (75%) of the loan but not in excess of ninety percent (90%) of the loan, the participation charge shall be two percent (2%) on the portion of the loan which  is obligated to purchase. 

Small Business Administration is prohibited by law from agreeing to purchase more than ninety percent (90%) of the loan. 

2. Interest: (a) Deferred and Immediate Participation Loans.

In all loans in which the Small Business Administration participates with a bank or other lending institution, whether the participation is on a deferred or immediate basis, and whether in the first instance the bank or the Small Business Administration makes the loan in which the other purchases an immediate participation, the rate of interest may be fixed by the bank or other lending institution, provided that the interest rate shall not be less than five percent (5%) per annum on the portion of the loan which the Small Business Administration is obligated to purchase or, in the case of an immediate participation loan, on the Small Business Administration's share in the loan. 60 99

(b) Direct Loans. 

Interest on all direct loans which may be made by the  shall be at the rate of six percent (6%) per annum.

VI. 

DISASTER LOANS UNDER SECTION 207(b) OF THE SMALL BUSINESS ACT 

Disaster loans will be considered on an individual basis in the light of the circumstances of the particular flood or other catastrophe. Such loans will be made to relieve the distress and hardships attendant upon the disasters. Accordingly, such loans will not be required to meet standards which would otherwise be applicable.

Interest on disaster loans for the purpose of the acquisition, construction or restoring of homes or personal effects shall be at the rate of three percent (3%) per annum; and interest on disaster loans for the purpose of the acquisition, construction or restoring of business facilities and inventories 100

shall be at the rate of five percent (5%) per annum.
