INTRODUCTION 

Over 2,500 years ago, in a period of similar cultural crisis where basic values were being examined and discussed, Plato recorded the quality of student discussion at Athens. Basically, Plato, and the educators who followed him were attempting to improve the quality of thought in their students minds, as they, together, sought for truth.

In a somewhat similar spirit, but using more concrete evidence, I have endeavored to record the quality of thought of a group of students who have been trained under my direction during the academic year, 1956-1957. As the world knows, leadership has been thrust upon these young men, and I feel that all teachers have the deep obligation of preparing these students for a responsible role in a changing world.

This is a second, and final, report on the twelve boys who have constituted a Third Form (ninth grade) Honors Section, selected by the school on the basis of ability and interest in History. The previous one, "Report on Roman Studies", issued in January, 1957, demanded that the students compare various types of studies of Roman history with the chapters on Rome in the book I have written for them, "From Mythical to Mediaeval Man." For this, they did library research, wrote a long paper (minimum: 1,000 words) and defended their views in class discussion. They were then told that, by the end of the year, each would be asked to define, and to defend, a philosophy of history. 

Since January, then, these students have made the attempt to put their ideas into order, and they have searched for interpretations of history to clarify their thinking. Individual and group conferences have been held, but the resultant papers are the students own.

The class discussion, as recorded by a Soundscriber machine, is slightly edited for the sake of clarity and length, but I trust its essentially frank and responsive quality has been preserved. It is my hope that these dialogues tend to keep these keen minds alive. 

Dr. Sumner C. Powell

History Department The Choate School

Reinhold Niebuhr:

Dr. Reinhold Niebuhr was born at Wright City, Missouri, June 21, 1892. He was ordained into the ministry as an Evengelist in 1915. Over the years he has received a number of degrees: D.D., B.D., and L.L.D. In 1950 he became dean of the

Part I WHY STUDY HISTORY? 

Why do we study history? Many history students have been asked that question and many more will be. The wise guy always answers, "Because it's part of my curriculum. But there are always a few who will stop to ponder on this question and try to come up with a reasonable answer. Why is it a part of one's curriculum? To make a start on this question, we must first know what history is. It is, in lofty terms, "a systematic written account of event, particularly of those affecting a nation, institution, science, or art, usually connected with a philosophical explanation of their causes. "1 To study history, we must read one of these "systematic accounts" to learn of the "events". This we do. Now we know many facts, which may someday win us a great deal of money on a quiz show. But I am sure that no one will agree with the man who says that the reason for studying history is to build up a reserve supply of knowledge for a quiz.. So why, then, do we study our texts? I feel that my thoughts were put on paper when it was said, "History is studied and taught in order that people may learn the lessons of the past and thereby gain knowledge for dealing more thoughtfully with the problems of the present and future." #2 This is why I wish to study history Our forefathers had some knowledge of history when they wrote the Constitution, and drew up the Bill of Rights. They had studied the democracy of ancient Greece and knew the pitfalls and the high-points of their system of government. Therefore, when I study history I try to grasp the general outline of a given situation or crisis, so as to know its "symtoms" when I see them again. Even if the only reason for doing this is to recognize a forthcoming crisis when I read the daily newspaper, it will have an effect on me. This ability to recognize various crises is very valuable to any kind of leader. The Russians of today have leaders who do not appreciate that they are falling into the pattern set by the Roman 

Empire when it fell.

The Madison Avenue advertisers must have this quality in order to succeed. When they realize that the United States has its mind primarily on beautiful, well-built women, they immediately set to work decorating magazines, billboards, and signs with pictures of these attractive women. 

What good will this ability to recognize patterns of history do me? If I became a great political leader, it would be of incalulable value. On the contrary, however, I don't plan to have anything to do with politics. My heart is set on becoming a wholesale hardware dealer. Even in this business, my outlook on history 1

Part II ARNOLD TOYNBEE--DOES HE HELP? 

Arnold Toynbee is generally acknowledged to be one of the foremost historians of our time. He has undoubtedly spent the greater part of his life towards the enlightening of man concerning history. His monumental "A Study of History", fourteen volumes in length, is one of the truly great efforts of all time. 

Whether or not it is a great work is a matter of opinion. "The His argument can be broken down into one sentence. nature of a breakdown can be summed up in three points: a failure of creative power in the creative minority, which henceforth becomes a merely 'dominant' minority; an answering withdrawal of allegiance and mimesis3 on the part of the majority; a consequent loss of social unity in the society as a whole."4 Mr. Toynbee examines twenty-six civilizations, of which sixteen are dead, nine broken down, and only one, our own, is still"alive".

Mr. Toynbee says that once a society fails to respond to one of the crises to which it must react, it is destined to fall. The only civilization for which there is hope, he says, is our own. 

I think he realizes that he has been trapped by his system. For all the other civilizations, he says that they are through, dead. 

He has stated his theory, making doom for a civilization certain once it has failed to respond. He offers no salvation except for our own, and I think that these "remedies" are not entirely foolproof. He says that world leaders must forget the thoughts of world domination, because he feels that domination by one power spells the end. But what happened to the Roman Empire in the time of Hadrian (117 A.D.-138 A.D.)? He set the ultimate boundaries for the Empire, because he realized that the Romans had gone too far. But this did not save the Roman civilization from complete downfall.

I feel that Mr. Toynbee's work, being a large scale one helps in a small way my reasons for studying history. He stresses challenge and response, but he does not give any method by which one mimesis--imitation, mimicry.

Part III HISTORY AH--HAS IT HELPED? 

The basis for the History AH course at Choate this year has been an as yet unpublished book, From Mythical to Mediaeval Man,, written by the course's instructor, Dr. Sumner C. Powell. He concentrates heavily upon the Roman and Greek Empires, because, I believe, he feels that they will have the greatest effect on what he is trying to say throughout. his book. And what is he trying to say? "It was designed to accomplish these three things: 1. 

Allow you to realize that a vigorous interpretation by one historian can provoke a vigorous response. 

2. Convince you that one interpretation is never enough. 

3. Stimulate you to make a study of history part of your whole life."5

This book definitely is helpful to my method of looking at history.

It is filled with questions concerning the content of the book, but they are not only applicable to the period under discussion, but also to almost any civilization. Most of these questions remain unanswered, leaving the answers to be determined by the reader. In this way, the question, rather than the history itself, sticks in the reader's mind. In later life, the se questions may arise in one's mind again, alerting him to some crisis in his life. In this way, Dr. Powell's book is a definite asset to my way of thinking.

There are two other phases of the course, the weekly classes and the period in the workshop each week. The se, I will discuss separately.

The weekly classes are devoted to discussion, which are conducted in the manner of a lecture, followed by a question and answer period. At least once every two weeks there is a test concerning the chapter that the class is currently reading. The class itself cannot be criticized for not being helpful to me, as it is only teaching what the book says, and, as I said previously, I like the book. 5. 

From Mythical to Mediaeval Man, Introduction, p. ii.

The period in the workshop is another matter. Dr. Powell and the class are making a great contribution to the school in presenting it with models such as the one of Chartres Cathedral constructed this year. But, as far as I am concerned, model building does not improve my outlook on history. In trying to learn lessons from the past one must know how people thought, and building models of their structures doesn't help me to learn how. Some people may argue that a civilization's creativeness is reflected in their architecture. Yes, this is true, but is it necessary to build them in order to know their true creativeness? 

This matter of model building is minor, and it rests largely on personal taste. I have never enjoyed building models of anything; this may be another reason why this phase of the course does not appeal to me.

The course's greatest aid to me in learning about past problems of man which can be compared to other events is the book, "From Mythical to Mediaeval Man.. I am glad that I have had an opportunity to read it. The course itself has stuck to the central theme of the book and has been a great aid in study. The only criticism I have is for the hours spent in the workshop. Some boys have enjoyed this time spent in model-making, however, and one cannot please everybody. I should be glad that this is the only phase of History AH that does not appeal to me.

To learn the lessons of the past in order to deal more thoughtfully and wisely with the problems of the present and future. That is why I study history. I.. A report and general criticism of the philosophy of history outlined in much detail in Arnold J. Toynbee's book, A Study of 

History: 

Toynbee upholds the theory that the unit of historical measurement should be the civilization, not the nation. He lists 21 civilizations since man emerged from a completely primitive society 6000 years ago; he then states that 12 of the said civilizations are dead, and most of the rest are moribund. Mr. Toynbee's structure is a dramatic one that is based on a pattern of growth and disintegration that he says has not changed. The growth of a civilization takes place until, after successfully meeting many challenges, the civilization meets one that it does not respond to; that, Mr. Toynbee says, is the moment of the beginning of breakdown. However, the disintegration is not steadily downward, but has lapses of growth or rallies; none the less, Mr. Toynbee hints that once the breakdown has begun, the society is doomed. As an example of Mr. Toynbee's theory of civilization, one may take the Hellenic civilization. Gibbon places the moment of the beginning of the fall of the Hellenic civilization in the second century A.D., after the age of the Antonines, but Toynbee says that the civilization failed to respond to an important challenge as early as 431 B.C., when the Atheno-Peloponnesian war was fought. Mr. Toynbee attributes the failure to respond to the "idolization of an ephemeral institution. In other words, the 

Athenians were so pleased with their city-state, an institution that

had been a notable achievement in its time, that they did not understand how economic forces had changed the Hellenic world and did not appreciate the need for a change in political institution. For four centuries, the members of the Hellenic civilization struggled along until one of them, Rome, established itself as ruler, and universal state. The Antonine age was but an Indian Summer, for even the great administrative power of Rome could not arrest the progress of degeneration. 

The universal state is usually, says Mr. Toynbee, preceeded by a "time of troubles" and the state often remains as the only means of salvation, if only temporary, from the rapid downfall which the scholar in point dismisses as inevitable. Behind the advent of that universal state is a crucial failure to respond to a certain challenge usually in the beginning of the life of the civilization. II. The statement of my philosophy of history: 

Mistakes are evident in all phases of man's activities and will always be, as God allowed none of us to be perfect, and few of us to even approach perfection in any given field. I feel that history is a medium of enlightenment, and that the enlightenment from his former mistakes is what man needs most. With past mistakes out of the way, man can concentrate on a life with only spontaneous errors to correct, and can then do more good for himself and the other people around him. In my opinion, history should then be a means by which man can live a more useful life. 

I feel that the way to use history in the pre-mentioned manner would be to analyze man's reaction to certain challenges, document his mistakes and successes in reacting and compile this material for distribution. For instances, through extensive research, historians could note all the ways in which the previous ages reacted to famine or plague. Then, if we today had a plague, we could consult the successful or unsuccessful attempts of the former ages to avert plague, and, taking into consideration the advancement of medicine, discover what would be unfavorable to do, or, with luck, what would be favorable. While one might think this is a negative approach to civilized man, telling him what not to do instead of what to do, I feel modern man, if given a list of many of the things he should not do in relation to a certain problem, unless the problem is extremely complex, will shortly discover what would be most useful to him, his family, and his countrymen. So, I think that history should be a collection of the studies of the reactions by different races, nations, etc., and should be written in a way grouping history together through this analysis and by challenges not in the standard chronological

A Preface to History 

The writer, Carl G. Gustavson, is an Associate Professor of 

History at Ohio University, but there is no further information on him in the D.A.B., Who's Who, Twentieth Century Authors, Encyclo pedia Americana, or Encyclopedia Brittanica.

His book was copyrighted in 1955. The principle purpose of the book is: "to present an outline of the nature of historicalmindedness suitable for first year college students. "1

Mr. Gustavson states that historical-mindedness has seven characteristics: 1. A curiosity as to what is behind any historical event; 2. One must seek the origins and comparisons of any event; 3. One must discover the shapes which are dynamic in society; 4. We are only a small part of a never ending cycle-society continues always; 5. Societies nevertheless change continually--nothing can prevent the process of gradual transformation; 

6. One must approach the subject with humility--also one should have a good idea of what ought to have been done; 7. One must realize that each event and situation is unique--history is not a science--one can't be sure of what will happen next. 

He further says that in order to remember facts in history, one must have a "frame of reference" in relation to which one can assemble facts in their proper "slots"... If history is learned in this way, it should not be a dreary process of memorization. If these seven points are memorized and become habitual, "historicalmindedness will become a form of reasoning, and it is obvious that a historian must know how to reason properly.

In my first serious study of ancient history I would like a general idea of what has happened before me. In other words, obtain an outline of facts that gives the student an accurate picture of history.

His book gives an overall picture of history in two volumes of about 800 pages each, and he calls it the whole story of man. A PHILOSOPHY OF HISTORY 

Why do we make a philosophy of history? For myself, there are two reasons: One, is that I must satisy my curiosity as to the fate of the world, by predicting the future from lessons of the past; and two, is that I must find some pattern which will fit the civilizations of the world today, and also have an equal suitability to the civilizations of the past. The reason for a pattern is to gain security in predicting the future, which, I feel, would be lacking, if one had to think up a new, yet basically same theory, for every civilization. 

Also, the idea that there is perhaps a Divine Pattern, a never-changing constitution which governs the present civilization and every other, since the beginning of time, makes me feel that we can at least try to find a similar pattern. The idea of a Divine Pattern brings up the question of what kind of a history I am going to write. Of Mr. 

Powell's six categories, mine falls into that of Philosophical, and perhaps that of Religious. I feel that this must be so, because religion has a certain, varying relationship with philosophy which must be included in the writer's interpretation of history. As the values of a religion change, its codes of ethics, morals, and principles, the interpretation that the writer gives to history is apt to change accordingly. Thus, with the intent of finding a theory which can be applied to all the civilizations of the world, throughout the whole period of recorded history, I will now go on to the philosophy.

The theory of P. A. Sorokin, with two exceptions, and modifications by the writer, is my philosophy. Mr. Sorokin's theory is as follows:

History falls into three distinct categories: 

1. A religiously-centered civilization. One which is governed almost wholly by the principles and values of a distinct religion. 

2. A materialistically-centered civilization. One that is governed by the senses, one in which the satisfaction of the senses is the main purpose of the people of the civilization. Mr. Sorokin gives the present Western civilization as an example of this type of civilization. (Since this type of civilization directly contradicts the principles of the Christian religion, perhaps this use of the West as an example of a materialistic civilization will give Americans something to think about when they start the flag-waving routine). 

3. The third type of civilization is actually a cross between a materialistic civilization, and a religiously centered one. Mr. 

Sorokin aptly terms this type: idealistic. 

My two major objections are these: 

1. That Mr. Sorokin does not seem to realize that there is a definite pattern of "swing" through these various types of civilizations, as the people of the areas making up the civilization demand a different type. And that the three categories of his theory are not really three, but one, and that the "swing" of the civilization through these three patterns is just one pattern in varying degrees. In my opinion, the religiously-centered civilization is the best stage, because it offers the most security to the people of the civilization, because it offers the best set of principles for the people of the civilization to follow, and because it is the farthest from the potential downfall of the civilization. 

The idealistic stage has the advantage of bringing the civilization closer to a rational way of thinking, and hence closer to reality, but this reality also brings with it, materialism.
