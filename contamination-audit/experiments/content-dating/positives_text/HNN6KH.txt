THE CODE-LETTER MYSTERY CHAPTER I THE ruling vice of Mr. Sheridan Orford is curiosity.

The complaint is one with which he was born, and the warnings and exhortations of nurses, parents and subsequently pastors and masters, have been utterly ineffectual to prevent its development. Sheridan himself early abandoned all efforts to curb his passion. He employs, without apparent loss of self-respect, the most unscrupulous methods of acquiring information and his appetite is equally voracious and catholic. If, at some remote period, he can be said to have had a conscience, he squared it by making a rule never to part with or to make any use of knowledge dishonestly come by. On these terms his Curiosity was given a completely free rein and his conscience quietly died.

This, perhaps, explains why, when an utter stranger came on board the Harwich boat at 

Amsterdam and thrust into his hand an envelope bearing initials which were not his, Mr. Orford did not call the messenger back to point out his mistake, nor yet turn the letter over to the purser, but deliberately broke the seal. There would be 5

plenty of time to rectify the mistake of the initials after he had read the missive thus thrust upon him.

The envelope was a bulky one. Orford was not surprised to find that it contained a second envelope, folded inside a single sheet of paper on which a brief instruction was typed: "Wear a buttonhole on the wrong side of your coat and give the enclosed to the man who asks for it at Liverpool Street."

Mr. Orford weighed the enclosed packet thoughtfully in his hand. It was a thick, square envelope without superscription of any kind.

Since this was Amsterdam, the natural home of stolen jewellery-or so Orford had always understood the packet might well contain notes in payment for same. This would be interesting but not particularly exciting. "If I am to be a mere District Messenger, there should be fees," he remarked to himself.

Nevertheless he put his hand to the lapel of his coat and leisurely transferred the carnation he was wearing to the other side. A man on the quay was watching him—as Mr. Orford was perfectly well aware, although he did not so much as glance in that direction. He had a notion that it was the same man as had given him the packet. 

When he had arranged the carnation to his satis-

faction, he crossed over to the rail of the steamer and scanned the faces on the quay side. The man he was looking for had gone. "Satisfied that I am 'M.L.K.,"" Mr. Orford remarked, referring to the outer envelope's superscription. "Well, well, well."

His Curiosity was suffering from a divided mind. 

It often does. He wanted to know the contents of the inner envelope; he also wanted to pursue it on its journey. He fancied that the man who was to ask for it at Liverpool Street would expect to receive it in its present virginal condition. An envelope that has been tampered with, even by an expert, if that expert has no special appliances at hand, commonly betrays the fact. Mr. Orford's

Curiosity is unscrupulous but not barefaced. He deliberated for some minutes and finally, in a secluded corner of the boat, tore the envelope with as little ceremony as if it had been one of his own letters. He argued that one plain envelope was much like another and that the man at Liverpool Street-probably another messenger-was unlikely to scrutinize the stationery with any particular care. Mr. Orford had plain envelopes in his own dispatch-case.

If it is proper to speak of rewards in so immoral a connection, Mr. Orford's curiosity was amply rewarded. The envelope contained nothing su dull as treasury notes. It appeared, on the con-

trary, to be a long and chatty letter from an 

English schoolgirl in Holland to someone whom she addressed as "Dear Old Thing." Orford read it through twice with sensations of the keenest pleasure. It was so wonderfully innocent a letter that its transmission by other means than the ordinary post filled him with delight. This was really worth following up.

Naturally he thought of a code, but this missive had none of the marks of a code letter. Not a phrase, from beginning to end, was unnaturally turned; not a blot nor a misspelt word drew attention to any particular sentence. For a time he wondered whether the thing were really a children's game, an elaborate form of post devised by some boy and girl to satisfy their craving for romance. He put the possibility aside to be reconsidered when he had followed the letter to its ultimate destination. Against it he set the fact that the round schoolgirl hand was, in his opinion, not natural to the writer. There were indications, here and there, of a more vigorous, individual caligraphy. 

Orford read the letter through a third time and put it away. He knew it by heart now. Learning by rote is a trick which he early cultivated; only when he is particularly anxious to be certain of the position of every comma and every capital letter does he read anything more than twice.

This was holiday time and the writer of the letter had been sight-seeing in company with an aunt-a religious aunt, apparently, for the niece had been forced to accompany the aunt to the English Presbyterian Church on the previous 

Sunday, much against her inclinations. "I wrote the text-Joshua 19. 4—on the corner of an old envelope and went to sleep, at least very nearly, during the sermon, and then she caught me out with silly questions about what the old josser had said—as if that mattered. So she made it an excuse for taking me again in the evening-bit thick when I knew the text, which is all any decent person expects you to know about a sermon." 

That "said "was the only underlined word in the whole letter and Sheridan could not make it a key to anything, try as he would. He understood the principles of a great many codes, but he applied them in vain. However, the search for a key very pleasantly beguiled his journey.
