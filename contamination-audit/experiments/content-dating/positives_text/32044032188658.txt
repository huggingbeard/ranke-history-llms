General Assembly convened: 

(a) There is created a commission of eleven members to study the judicial systems of other states. The governor shall appoint five of said members, including the president of the state bar association, the speaker of the house of representatives shall appoint three representatives as members and the president of the senate shall appoint three senators as members. 

Of each group so appointed, not more than a bare majority shall be members of any one political party. (b) The legislative council shall, on request, assist the commission in the performance of its duties hereunder. (c) The commission shall inquire as to the administration of the judicial departments of the other states, to determine the most efficient mehods of insuring prompt and proper administration of justice consistent with a free and independent judiciary. OBSERVATIONS 

Connecticut, one of the nation's most progressive, populous, sophisticated, and economically developed states, suffers acutely from court congestion. The delay from commencement of action to final conclusion severely impairs our citizens' rights to speedy and impartial justice. We fast are approaching a break-down of the judicial process.

Connecticut is not alone. Sister states, however, have or are taking measures to remedy the problem. Now it is our turn to act boldly and wisely.

Litigation grows with any nation's progress and development. Methods, court organization, and legal efficiency must keep pace with this growth. In Connecticut, the executive branch of our government, with its administrative agencies, has followed the trend toward modernization. Our courts have not. 

When litigants must wait six years for their cases to be tried, justice is impaired. Connecticut should not suffer from a plodding and creaking judicial system.

We rightfully are proud of the high calibre of our judges, clerks, court personnel, and lawyers. This is no self-serving statement. This is a cold matter of fact. 

No one person or group is at fault. An immediate remedy is the duty of all.

FINDINGS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

A sufficient number of qualified trial and appellate judges sitting in efficiently designed and maintained court rooms will provide a judicial system adequate to handle Connecticut's needs. This judicial system may be made more efficient by adopting certain practices and procedures presently used in other states.

The number of judges in Connecticut's over-all judicial system should be determined by the state's population. Connecticut has far too few Superior and Common Pleas Court judges for its population. In Florida, legislation requires one trial judge per 50,000 residents. The committee recommends legislation by which the number of trial court judges would relate directly to Connecticut's population. Consideration should be given to a Constitutional provision guaranteeing this requirement.

In several states the authority appointing judges is required to select appointees from a list of candidates whose qualifications were approved by the state bar association. In Missouri, the 

Governor may appoint only those persons whose qualifications were determined by the state bar association. During recent years Connecticut Governors, although not so required by law, have obtained the approval of the state bar association before making judicial appointments. Accordingly, the committee does not recommend any change in Connecticut's present method of appointment.

Court-room arrangement has been studied by several legal organizations. Complaints by jurors and judges of the inability to hear witnesses and see exhibits prompted the re-designing of traditional court-room arrangement. Modernization of facilities available to judges during trial has been recommended. Placement of jury boxes, attorneys' tables, witness stands and the like have been altered. The committee recommends that the results of studies conducted by such organizations as The American Judicature Society be followed in future courtroom construction and arrangement in Connecticut.

The moving pictures, involving typical courtroom scenes and narrated by a Connecticut judge, would acquaint new jurors with their duties and obligations, thereby eliminating the use of judicial time for this purpose. The use of moving pictures for jurors is recommended by the committee. 

Two jury judges sitting together in one courthouse can achieve far more than twice the accomplishments of one judge sitting alone. The ability to transfer matters between them and the division of work on a day-to-day basis promotes greatest efficiency.

The jurisdiction of the Circuit Court should be raised to $5,000 in civil matters. Superior Court judges should be able to transfer cases to the Court of Common Pleas or to the Circuit Court, without prejudice to the litigants, and Common Pleas judges also should have the right to transfer cases to the Circuit Court.

As in New York, jurors should be selected during a voir dire conducted by counsel only. If necessary, questions or disputes may be referred to a judge for his decision, but the routine of the voir dire would be handled by counsel, thereby freeing the judge from this time-consuming task. 

The arbitration system used in Philadelphia successfully could be employed for a faster determination of relatively minor civil matters. Special masters selected from prominent members of the bar would hear such matters in their offices. 

Pre-trial conferences in Connecticut generally serve little purpose. Such conferences should be held just before trial, and not several months before trial. Under our present system, the aim of the pre-trial conference is not achieved because of the enormous time difference between the conference and trial. 

Dean Howard Oleck of the Cleveland-Marshall Law 

School advised the committee that studies conducted in several states demonstrate the advisability of having the pre-trial conference immediately before trial.

Careful consideration should be given to the possibility of separating the issue of liability from the question of damages in civil suits. Such a theory has successfully been followed in the United States District Court for the Northern District of Illinois.

The defense of contributory negligence should be eliminated in civil accident cases, and the doctrine of comparative negligence adopted.

An interim court of Administrative Appeals should be created. The court would sit in Hartford, New Haven, and 

Bridgeport. Sitting in each area would be three judges from the

Court of Common Pleas selected from that court's regular membership.

The court would hear all appeals from administrative bodies including zoning and planning commissions, the Liquor 

Control Commission, Motor Vehicle Department, Public Util ities Commission, and boards of tax review. The decision of this court would be final in each case except for the following: 

1. A divided opinion from the court; 2. 

A constitutional issue; or 

3. Certification by the Connecticut Supreme Court of 

Errors that the matter involves a substantial issue which should be ruled upon by it.

Superior Court judges should be paid the same salaries as judges of the United States District Courts. 

As in several states, law clerks should be provided for the justices of the Supreme Court of Errors and the Superior Court. 

Extensive and exhaustive research is mandatory if accurate judicial decisions are to be reached. Time spent by judges in proof-reading decisions, checking citations, and the like would be spent more profitably if clerks were assigned to these tasks. 

Serious consideration should be given to the elimination of claiming a specific sum as damages in negligence cases. Listing an arbitrary figure, often inflated, tends to make settlement more difficult, and serves no real purpose. On the other hand, if the present method of claiming specific damages continues, an additional entry fee should be charged for each thousand dollars claimed above $50,000.

In addition to eliminating the "ad damnum" in negligence complaints, the return day also should be eliminated. No useful purpose is served by maintaining the present system.
