LEAGUE publishes "Financing the

Defense Program" by its Consulting Economist, Professor Harley L. 

Lutz of Princeton University, with the conviction that the author has devised a practicable method of raising taxes sufficient to meet the greater part, possibly all, of the cost of extraordinary defense on the pay-as-we-arm principle. To imagine that by forcing the federal government to "borrow" the necessary billions, America defers the cost by spreading it over a longer period of time is a cruel illusion. Actually the lenders to the government pay for whatever the government buys in labor, services and goods. But if we borrow, the lenders have paid once for the arms and later we shall all pay again-double, triple or many times over in higher prices for all goods and services, and we shall pay by reducing our level of common living for many, many years. If we pay now, in cash and without borrowing, our standard of life need be shaved only during the abnormal period of producing these needed war goods.

The League endorses this thesis of Professor Lutz as the most effective and democratic method yet advanced to preserve the financial basis of American economy during the present war years and the fearsome period of later readjustment. Objections will be found, criticisms voiced; some better method may be presented. The League as a body will welcome and endorse any improvement upon the thesis here presented. 

In the meantime, we commend to thoughtful consideration the analogy of the Lutz plan to the method of collecting the Social Security taxes applied in this case not only to wages and salaries but to dividends and interest. We emphasize the futility of hoping that any increase of income tax rates or "broadening the base" of the income tax can yield the equal of the cost for which we have rightly engaged our resources. We would deplore the more cumbersome and inadequate general sales tax. 

Through the ages men gave their "tithe" to the support of the works of the Lord. Shall we not willingly give our tithe to defeat the works of evil?

FINANCING THE DEFENSE PROGRAM¹ By HARLEY L. LUTZ Professor of Public Finance, Princeton University 

I. 

INTRODUCTION 

HE AMERICAN PEOPLE are being drawn into the present European war by a process quite similar in character (though different in detail) to that by which they were drawn into the first world war. In each case they have approached the conflict from the same background—namely, a strong preference for peace, limited and inadequate military equipment and organization, and a naive belief in the validity of a neutrality concept developed long ago.

While the psychological aspects of the two situations have been much the same, the practical aspects, especially on the fiscal side, are vastly different. 

In 1916 the federal debt was $1,225 millions. Today it is $45,000 millions. 

In 1916 the federal expenditures were $734 millions, but for 1941 they will approximate $8,500 millions, entirely apart from special defense spending. 

In 1916 federal taxes amounted to $726 millions, while in 1941 they will exceed $7,000 millions. Furthermore, the attitude of government toward business in 1916 was benevolent, but today that attitude is critical, even hostile, despite a thin veneer of patriotic cooperation and unity. 

It is obvious that the defense program is being undertaken in the face of serious fiscal handicaps. An immense ballyhoo will be required to conceal these handicaps, and to preserve national morale by diverting public attention. to other matters than the financial chaos resulting from the "boondoggling" years. Under the circumstances now prevailing, it will not be easy, by any devices, to pay for defense, or to pay the cost of war, if that should come.

II. LOANS VERSUS TAXES HE AVAILABLE METHODS of defense financing are, obviously, (1) by borrowing, (2) by taxation, or (3) by a combination of loans and taxes. 

The job will be difficult, under any method of financing. The huge debt will make taxation seem more attractive, while the existing heavy taxes will make borrowing appear the easier way.

A loan program may be handled in one of two ways: bonds may be sold to banks, as heretofore; or they may be sold directly to individuals in such a manner as to absorb a portion of private income or savings. The sale of defense bonds to the banks will have a credit inflationary tendency which will become stronger with the further expansion of the volume of new purchasing power thus created. To prevent price increase under this growing inflationary stimulus will require price control. But price control will involve wage control, priorities, and, very likely, consumer rationing on a substantial scale. 

Thus, the efforts to stave off the logical effects of bank borrowing for defense would lead to an enormous degree of bureaucratic control over, and interference with, the affairs and the activities of individuals. 

It is generally assumed that sale of defense bonds to individuals, thereby absorbing not only income destined for saving but also income which might otherwise be devoted to consumption, would not be inflationary. Such sales would merely divert purchasing power from private to public use without increasing the aggregate. To assure this result, defense savings bonds must be non-negotiable. They must be ineligible, both for bank ownership and as collateral for bank loans.

These restrictions would definitely handicap the sale of large amounts of defense savings bonds. To offset this handicap, one of two steps might be taken: (1) a rate of interest could be paid which would be sufficiently attractive to induce large purchases by individuals; or (2) compulsory purchase could be adopted.

The payment of a really attractive rate of interest would probably be resisted by the Treasury because of the interest cost, and also because of the threat thus presented to the price and yield structure of the Treasury bonds. 

According to 1937 income returns, the amount of partially exempt federal bonds reported by individuals with net incomes of $5,000 and over, and by fiduciaries with balance incomes of $5,000 and over, exceeded $1,250,000,000. 

These bonds would probably be sold to banks on a substantial scale, if the • 6 •

opportunity to buy higher yield defense savings bonds were presented. If insurance companies were allowed to buy such bonds, the volume of investment switching could easily become such as to damage the present price and yield basis of the Treasury bonds, with serious effects on the banks themselves. 

No definite plans have been announced which would indicate the degree of consideration being given to sale of defense bonds on the basis of interest yield. Some form of compulsion is not unlikely, eventually, for two reasons: it would protect all of the vested interests in cheap money which the Treasury has created, and it would be more consistent with the whole ideological pattern of the current administrative and fiscal policy.2 

This brings up the compulsory savings plan, further discussion of which is deferred until some other matters have been presented. 

III. GENERAL APATHY TOWARD THE QUESTION OF WAYS AND MEANS 

HERE HAS BEEN NO EVIDENCE of great interest, thus far, in the methods of financing the defense costs. This subject was actively discussed in 1916 and 1917, and a large number of economists joined in a memorial to Congress, advocating a cash basis war. The present apathy may be due, in part, to the failure of the earlier cash basis proposal, and to the conclusion, based on world war experience, to the effect that a cash basis program of defense—or of war—is impossible.

It is true that the tax program advocated by the economists in 1917 would not have paid for the war. This program followed the conventional federal

It proposed complete absorption of all special war profits, heavier income taxes and high consumption taxes on luxuries. 

Professor Seligman, writing in 1921, concluded that the cost of the war could not have been met out of taxes alone. But Seligman was then looking at a war cost which, as Professor Bogart has pointed out, was increased by many billions by the inflationary borrowing methods employed. That cost was increased further by the planless and often witless methods of procurement used. No one can really say, now, that the expense of the world war could or could not have been met out of taxes, because no one can now determine just what the cost would have been if it had been on a cash rather than so largely on a credit basis. 

Like the economists of 1917, Seligman was also handicapped in considering taxation. He mentioned only the conventional taxes, and proved, from the record of their actual yield during the war years, that the entire inflated war cost could not have been met by taxation. If we must be limited, now, by convention in taxation, as the economists were on the eve of the first world war, it would be useless to talk about a cash basis defense program. 

It is significant to note, however, that while Seligman concluded that a combination of loans and taxes is permissable in war finance, he laid down a very definite condition, namely, that the debt incurred for one war must be paid off before there is any probability of another war. 

Circumstances have rudely violated his argument and its conclusion. 

Approximately $15 billions of the present federal debt consists of unpaid world war debt. This is three-fifths of the total borrowing on account of that war. Under these conditions, we cannot now cite Professor Seligman as an authority in support of defense borrowing. The indifference towards ways and means on the part of Congress and the Administration is evident, for a total of $18 billions has already been voted in appropriations and contract authorizations, with no adequate provision, as yet, for the financing of this sum. It is true that the statutory debt limit has been raised by $4 billions, and that two tax acts have been passed. The first of these acts said something about defense taxes. Revenue need was not stressed as much in preparing the second tax act as was the bogy of new war millionaires.'

On this passage the following comments are submitted: 

1. The restriction of new taxes to those of the progressive type will fail now, as it did before, to provide an adequate revenue. 

2. Taxation does not curtail over-all consumption. The reduced consuming power of taxpayers is offset by the transfer of funds to public employees, persons working on government contracts and public beneficiaries. 

3. The idea of the full use of our productive capacities as an ultimate goal, is dim, remote and hazy. The expression is in common use, but it has not been defined. Does it mean employment of all labor on a 40-hour week or a 60-hour week? Does it mean present plant capacity, or the capacity of all the new plants that are to be built in addition to existing capacity? The essence of the private capitalist system of enterprise is to plan and to build in excess of the current market demand. Reliance on such a vague and indefinite test to indicate when the tax system should be broadened sufficiently to balance the budget means that this will never be done. There may always be an empty factory or an idle mill somewhere. Elsewhere in the budget message occurs this statement: "We must face the fact that even with what we call 'full employment' there will remain a large number of persons who cannot be adjusted to our industrial life.” 

In other words, full employment except in a limited, technical sense, is impossible. It is obvious that the situation requires bolder and stronger taxation measures than the President has in mind.

SOME OBJECTIONS CONSIDERED 

SUBSTANTIAL TAX on individual gross incomes is not a conventional tax. It will no doubt be vigorously opposed, as would be any other departure from the conventional pattern of thinking and behavior. 

1. It will be said that such a tax violates the accepted notions about regression, progression and ability to pay.

It is true that this is not a progressive tax. It cannot be if it is to be collected at the source, which is the only feasible administrative procedure. 

In the writer's opinion, there has been too much uncritical acceptance of the kind of tax progression now used. It should be noted, however, that the existing progressive taxes, bad as they are, are not to be affected by this tax. 

Furthermore, the introduction of a new tax, levied at a proportional rate, will not affect the progressivity of existing taxes. 

The proposed tax would reduce somewhat the yield of the present progressive tax on net income, but no effort has been made, in preparing this paper, to estimate the amount of that reduction. This result would be produced by the collection of the tax on gross income at source, and the consequent shrinkage of the total gross income received by the individual as defined by the present federal tax law. 

A tax on individual gross incomes, at a proportional rate, is not regressive. 

No proportional tax is really regressive. It has become customary to use an artificial indicator of regression, whereby all taxes are referred to a base called "personal net income." This is doubly artificial, because it is bad mathematics and because the concept of a personal net income is a pure fiction. It is the result of transferring an accounting concept from the business unit to the individual, a transfer which has no valid economic basis, and which produces a grotesque statutory definition of personal net income. 

A tax on individual gross incomes, collected at the source, that is, collected in installments as the several installments of wages, salary, dividends or interest are paid to the recipient thereof, is the closest approximation to ability taxation that can be devised. Such a tax falls on the individual at the only time when he has any ability to bear taxation. The ability to pay taxes differs in no way from the ability to pay rent or grocery bills or interest on debt. It exists at the moment of receiving income, and it disappears as

In this respect a tax which is deducted from an item of income payment as that payment passes to the recipient is a vastly better levy in accordance with "ability" than one which is based upon, and presumably is to be paid out of, income a year after that income has been spent. It is quite likely that Adam Smith had such a tax in mind when he said that citizens should contribute to the state "in proportion to their respective abilities, that is, in proportion to the revenues which they respectively enjoy under the protection of the state." It is most unlikely that Smith had in mind, as a measure of ability, the absurdly artificial thing which modern tax laws call "personal net income." It is certain that his intelligence would have been outraged at the thought of retroactive personal income taxation, although such a practice is completely consistent with the current theory of net income and ability. 

2. It will also be urged that such a tax involves too heavy a burden on the low income group. But it is made clear in the paragraphs that follow that any other method of financing the defense program will involve an even greater burden on this group.

It has been customary in the history of the income tax to assume that there is a subsistence minimum which should not be touched by taxation. 

However, if we review the record of the federal income tax we find that the exempt minimum, or personal credit, is a conventionalized concept. The allowance to a married couple has ranged from $4,000 to $2,000. It will probably be reduced still further. Apparently this allowance is purely a matter of administrative convenience or of revenue urgency. The successive changes in this allowance, its wide range, and the fact that its further reduction is always proposed as a means of increasing the yield, alike indicate no great respect for the concept, and no concern whatever for a sound statistical determination of what it should be.

The question of burdening the low income group raises two issues: one is the issue whether any part or group of the people should be shielded from a share of the defense burden. At bottom this is an ethical question of the utmost importance, but it is passed over here. The other aspect of the question of burdening the low income group is practical—can any part of the people be shielded from this burden?

The writer's answer is that this cannot be done, however the defense program is financed. The diversion of national product for defense purposes, on the scale contemplated, will involve deprivations which will affect all • 14 •

citizens in some manner. The great advantage of a tax on individual gross incomes is that it merely parallels, on the fiscal side, the actual deprivation which must, in any case, occur with respect to goods. That it universalizes this deprivation as no other method of financing can, is a strong argument in its favor.

The situation may be illustrated as follows. In this illustration the operation of the current fiscal system is taken for granted. 

Assume that the annual value of the national product is $75 billions. 

Extraordinary spending for defense at the rate of $7 to $8 billions a year means, at current prices, the diversion of approximately 10 per cent of the national product to defense uses. Hence there would remain for civilian use $67.5 billions, or 90 per cent of that product. Assume also that the total income paid out to individuals is $75 billions. Then a tax of 10 per cent on that income would pay for the goods diverted to defense, and leave $67.5 billions of income paid out for exchange against an equivalent value of national product. In other words, the people will be paid for producing a certain quantity of goods which they will not be permitted to buy and consume. As the defense program expands further, they will be paid for producing still more that they cannot buy for their own use. 

Suppose, however, that the purchasing power sufficient to pay for defense materials, etc., is created by new bank loans. The product available for civilian use is $67.5 billions, but the people are in receipt of $75 billions of income paid out. In order to equate the income and the available goods, the prices of goods must rise. As this process of financing goes on, prices will rise further, and it is well known that in such a movement the low income groups are pinched, usually severely pinched. Price increase is not only as effective a method of deprivation as the gross income tax would be, it is also more erratic, and very likely more severe, in its effects, than such a tax would be.12 As indicated above, resort to controls to prevent price increase means control of wages, and of consumption through rationing, as well as control of prices. Low income groups cannot be exempted from these controls. 

Furthermore, a gross income tax would tend to limit and determine the

But suppose that instead of bank loans, defense savings loans should be used. In order to pay for the program in this way and at low interest cost, compulsion will be necessary. Such compulsion will be universal, and any proposal to exempt low income groups from its scope will involve both grave administrative difficulties and sharp animosities among the people. Universal compulsory purchase of defense bonds will involve quotas for states, districts and individuals, and in fixing individual quotas, the amount of income received is very likely to be given major consideration. 

Borrowing on this basis approaches the compulsory savings plan suggested in England by J. M. Keynes. That plan involves precisely the same sort of deprivation of current income as is involved in the gross income tax plan, although there is a small income minimum, under the Keynes plan, below which the compulsion is not applied. Under either plan, the fiscal operation simply parallels the diversion of goods to the military use. That is, as the goods are taken for armament, a portion of current income is taken to pay for them. Under such conditions neither the savings loan nor the gross income tax would be an added deprivation, since it is assumed that the remainder of the individual's income (after deducting his tax or his compulsory saving) will buy all of the goods that he is entitled to receive. In view of the reduced volume of goods available for civilian use, because of the diversion, the relative position of individuals in buying the available goods is not changed, either by a proportionate gross income tax or by another proportional levy which may be called compulsory saving. The relative position of individuals with respect to consumption will be changed if the consumer deprivation is to be accomplished through price increases (resulting from inflationary borrowing), or through drastic taxes on certain commodities which, by law or decree, have been declared to be luxuries. 

The serious flaw in the Keynes plan is the fact that it creates debt which must be repaid. Keynes proposes that repayment be made to the low income "savers" during the post-war or post-emergency period, when the redemption would increase the purchasing power of the low income group. 

If this redemption is to be an honest liquidation of debt, and not a mere refunding, the funds for repayment, particularly in the depression which will follow the war boom, will be drawn from all ranks of taxpayers. The low • 16 •

income groups will bear their full share of these taxes, and they will, in effect, thus redeem their own savings bonds. Such a process will certainly not increase their aggregate purchasing power. 

To be sure, Keynes sees the matter differently. He expects to redeem the savings bonds held by the low income groups by levying still heavier "soak the rich" taxes. Were this possible, his reasoning as to the increase of low incomes in the post-war period, through the redemption of savings bonds, would be valid. Such an expectation is wholly illusory. The war is destroying 

English fortunes, English wealth, and the large English incomes, and when it ends, there will be no great reservoir of concentrated income from which to draw such taxes. Large-scale debt repudiation is a far more likely outcome for England after the war.

This conclusion applies to the United States, if the debt is allowed to increase. Because it is illusory to suppose that defense savings bonds forced into the possession of the low income groups can or will be redeemed by taxes from which these groups will be immune, the savings bond plan will prolong the period of deprivation for all citizens. To that necessary current deprivation caused by the defense program will be added a second period of deprivation caused by the taxation for debt redemption. If this country is forced to repudiate its debt, low income holders of savings bonds cannot be kept immune from the consequences. 

The last budget message takes an optimistic view regarding debt increase. 

It is said that the main fiscal problem is not the rise of the debt but the rise of debt charges in relation to the development of our resources. Although interest charges will increase, this fact is said not to present a serious fiscal problem so long as a high level of national income can be maintained. 

But there is no assurance that a high level of national income can be maintained. With the increase of the debt to fantastic heights, and with no beginning of debt reduction in sight, an indefinite future of high national income will be required to escape the burdensome consequences. During the 

1920's the economic soothsayers predicted a "new era" of indefinite prosperity.

A large part of the political capital of the past decade rests on these erroneous predictions and the folly of the people in believing them. To proceed with debt increase on the theory that the measure of its burden is the relation of interest charges to national income in the most prosperous years is like justifying the post-war prices of mid-west farm land on the basis of a famine price of $3.00 per bushel for wheat. • 17.

The issue of paramount importance is that further large increase in the public debt should be avoided. It involves no concession as to the merits of the plan here proposed to admit that in the event of a prolonged and costly war, government may be obliged to resort to financial devices which are unsound and dangerous. But that stage has not been reached. Even if sufficient industrial capacity should be developed in time to make the country an adequate arsenal and supply depot for the British, all of that aid, as well as all of our own war matériel, must be provided out of current production. It must all be a diversion of current product from civilian to military use. This is what the economists mean when they say that the real burden of a war must be borne by the war generation. Borrowing does not defer the burden. It merely prolongs the period of sacrifice. 

Some have suggested that the defensive equipment now being made be regarded as capital, and that its cost be treated as a capital investment, to be amortized during the useful life of the equipment. Under the stress of war conditions, all military equipment has only a current usefulness. A battleship is good for twenty years of peace time patrol, but it may last only fifteen minutes in action. An airplane only three months old may be merely a death trap for the men who take it up. It is the worst form of self-delusion to persuade ourselves that in building our defense, or in making supplies for Britain, capital investment is being accumulated which can be leisurely written off out of future income as the benefits of this investment are enjoyed. 

3. Another objection to a gross income tax may be that it will be unnecessary, because the increase of national income will so greatly expand the yield of the existing taxes. The latest budget message contains evidence of this viewpoint. The rate at which national income will grow and the level to which it will rise are alike problematical. It is necessary, also, to distinguish between an actual increase of national product, and a higher aggregate valuation of a given product which is caused by the inflation of prices. By marking up prices we could easily persuade ourselves that national income had risen to $90 or $100 billions, but it is altogether likely that the low income groups would lose rather than gain thereby. 

As to the rate of increase of total physical product, greater doubt is legitimate, because of the scarcity of skilled labor, the defense priority demands for tools and materials, and the profit hazards in production not protected by government contracts. The factor of opportunity cost will be operative, also. Steel used for munitions is steel diverted from automobiles and construction. The opportunity to build battleships is a lost opportunity • 18 •

to make trucks, tractors and buildings for civilian use. It is unfortunate that the new index of production should be so unacceptable to competent statisticians, for this means that there is no thoroughly reliable indicator of relative progress in the expansion of the national income. 

Predictions as to the future high level of national income tend to involve wishful thinking. They are based on deductions derived from the current and prospective rate of production in armament factories. But they ignore the dead-end character of production for war use. Goods that are made into armament are permanently withdrawn from the cycle of economic processes. They do not thereafter contribute to further production, either as capital goods or as durable consumer goods. The process of expanding national product (as distinguished from national income paid out) to the volume of $90 or $100 billions at current prices will very likely be slow and difficult, the more so as the prime conditions of such expansion, namely, immense and widespread capital investment and the induction of substantial additions to the skilled and semi-skilled labor forces, are not in evidence. 

The argument that an enormous increase of the national product may confidently be anticipated, even if valid, does not constitute a proper objection to the proposed gross income tax. This tax should be introduced, if for no other purpose than to equalize the revenue and the total expenses, including defense. The larger the yield from the conventional sources of taxation, which are more acceptable and satisfactory than the gross income tax on current ideological grounds, the lower the rate of the latter tax need be. 

The fiscal inadequacy of the present type of income tax is often neglected. 

There is a wide lag between the increased levy and the flow of receipts into the Treasury. This provides a good reason for concluding that defense costs can never be financed out of the kind of income tax now used. The expenditures will be made in one year, and the income tax payments will drift in a year or so later. Furthermore, a tax on net income is exposed, as a revenue producer, to all of the hazards of the profit system, and the factors which cause variability of profit cause variability and uncertainty of income tax yield. Obviously, there will be no such lag in the collection of a tax on income paid out.

It is possible that some may propose, as an alternative, a federal sales tax. Only a few years ago the sales tax was being condemned on all sides in the strongest possible terms. It has now established itself in many states, and an erstwhile opponent of a federal sales tax, the Mayor of New York City, now finds that his city cannot operate without one. • 19

Notwithstanding all the good qualities of the sales tax, it is not adequate for the defense job. It will apply to a much smaller base than income paid out; it will not distribute the burden of defense costs as widely as will a tax on all individual gross incomes (since it will apply only to such income as is transferred in taxable sales); and if high rates of sales tax are imposed, as on luxury goods, it may defeat its revenue ends in the same manner as does a protective tariff. Despite the relative inelasticity of demand for liquors and tobacco, taxes on these articles can be increased to a point at which revenue loss will result.

A sales tax, or a series of specific commodity or excise taxes, will tend to produce price dislocations through the process of tax shifting. Every price change is a means of influencing the direction and the volume of consumption. But to undertake the curtailment of consumption through fortuitous, erratic or unpredictable price increases is a far less desirable method than to gain this end through a gross income tax. The latter method tends to leave all individuals in the same relative position, as regards consumption, after the tax is collected as they were before. It does not directly affect prices, and there is every prospect that each individual can do a better job of arranging for his consumption needs under such circumstances, even with a somewhat smaller amount to spend, than will be possible if various prices have been thrown out of line under the impact of a miscellaneous lot of excise taxes, or even by a sales tax. 

4. It will also be said that a general tax on gross income will lead to curtailed consumption of articles not essential to national defense. The agricultural surpluses will be specifically referred to as examples. 

This argument assumes, first, that we shall continue to have agricultural surpluses, and second, that the effect of the defense program and of the tax on gross income, in combination, will be a pro rata curtailment of all customary civilian consumption. The second assumption would mean the same proportionate reduction of food consumption as of gasoline, liquor and dog racing.

Neither of these assumptions is wholly tenable. If the war is prolonged, this country faces the prospect of supplying a substantial part of the subsistence of the British population, in which case there would soon be no surpluses. While no one can forecast the precise extent to which the defense program will involve diversion of the various stocks of commodities, it is clear that to some extent this diversion will be general, although of variable degree. Defense may absorb the entire output of some things, and to this • 20

extent curtailment of civilian consumption will be complete. All of the income formerly spent for such things will be available for other things. 

Until the system is in full operation and its ramifications are clearer than at present, it would be rash to assert that the gross income tax, per se, will result in a diminished consumption of those commodities of which there may be now an embarrassing surplus. 

5. Another objection may be that such a tax as is here proposed should not be used while there remains, in the hands of those who receive larger incomes, any portion of so-called net income not appropriated by the present income taxes. This is evidently the thought underlying the suggestion, in the budget message, that chief reliance should be placed on progressive taxes collected out of a higher level of national income. 

It has been pointed out above that the proposed tax is to be in addition to the present taxes, and this means that the effect of the new tax would be to reduce by so much the remainder of income not now being absorbed by existing taxes. The suggestion that government should take all or a substantial part of that remainder ignores two things: 

First, the statutory definition of personal net income is a pure fiction, and such residue of income as would remain in the hands of a large proportion of the present group of income taxpayers is in no sense a true net income. 

Any attempt to absorb all of that remainder would defeat its purpose because it would destroy a large part of that which was being levied upon.¹³ 

Second, there is in sight today no workable and acceptable substitute for the system of private property and private enterprise. There is, however, a belief, widely held even among those who are professed adherents of private enterprise, that this system can survive and flourish without any roots at all. 

The drastic taxation which, according to this hypothesis, can and should be levied on the group that must provide the main support of the enterprise system would, in fact, cut its tap root. Instead of doing that, individual investment and the restoration of the risk-bearing function should be promoted by allowing credits for investment in the computation of so-called taxable net incomes, just as such credits are now allowed for gifts to education and philanthropy.

Government Finance, Lecture V. The central idea is that a certain part of what is now designated as "net income" is really a cost of earning or getting the income, though not so recognized by the tax law. When taxation cuts too deeply into this cost, the individual can no longer maintain the station or position upon which his income depends. • 21 •

6. It may be objected, further, in line with the President's budgetary statement, that such a tax as is here proposed will curtail production. This is connected with the thought that no step should be taken that might in any way interfere with our early arrival at the full use of resources. 

But the defense program, like a war, provides a special and unique dynamic force which drives the economy toward expansion. This force is the urgent need of government for more and more war material and equipment. As long as the pressure from this source continues, and as long as huge appropriation acts are passed, there is no reason to fear for a slacking of the productive drive. 

7. Finally, there is the question of the effect of the financing methods used upon morale. A gross income tax will have its effects here, and they could be quite adverse by skillful use of the sort of propaganda by which the masses have been persuaded that the government owes them a living. 

But morale will be adversely affected, also, by any method of borrowing. 

As the debt grows, the prospect of redeeming it will fade and the prospect of some kind of sweeping readjustment through partial or complete repudiation will grow. Savings depositors, insurance policy holders, and even the prospective beneficiaries of public programs of social security will become increasingly alarmed as further debt increase threatens more and more the certainty of their expectations.

As between these two sets of demoralizing influences, the writer would prefer to take the risks of dealing with those arising out of taxation than with those arising out of inflationary borrowing. Adam Smith said that the practice of funding had gradually enfeebled every state that had adopted it. 

This is even more true in the 20th century than it was in the 18th century. 

It is a question of fact, not merely one of opinion or psychological reactions. 

The sole purpose of the defense program is to make the nation strong. Bad fiscal methods will weaken it, and the more such methods are used in the struggle to become stronger, the weaker will the nation be in the end. 

The problem of the method of financing defense is like the problem of an aching tooth. Will it hurt more to have the tooth pulled or to let the ache continue until it wears itself out? Will it hurt us more to pay cash for defense than to borrow and then keep on paying indefinitely for debt service? The foregoing argument is based on the assumption that it will hurt less to pull the tooth than to let the ache continue. It will hurt to pay cash for defense, but it will hurt worse, and for a much longer time, to borrow for defense. • 22 •
