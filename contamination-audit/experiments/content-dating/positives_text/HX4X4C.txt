LIBRARY The Author FOREWORD 

One hundred years ago, on May 20, 1834, there died in

Paris, at the end of a singularly eventful life, a man whose place in American history will always be secure. Seventyseven years earlier he had been born the Marquis de Lafayette, but he had willingly exchanged the life of a French aristocrat for the title of Major-General in the ragged and ill-fed army of Washington. His actual share in the winning of our independence was no greater than that of many others; but his "grand gesture," followed by his unswerving devotion to his "paternal friend," was of incalculable worth to the rebel cause. More than that, Americans liked him, and every one of them who ever had any contact with him seems to have treasured the association, no matter how ephemeral.

The following unrhymed verses, first read before the students of Union College at the centennial exercises arranged in co-operation with the American Friends of Lafayette, are intended merely as a small contribution to local history. 

They are based on authentic records, and sources could be cited for nearly every line. 

Union College, May, 1934

LAFAYETTE AT SCHENECTADY 

"The world has never witnessed an exhibition like this a private individual, of foreign birth, enjoying a splendid triumph throughout the whole nation... awarded to him by the spontaneous feelings of a grateful people. 

Without power, he has a nation at his command; without authority, he controls all hearts."

UTICA SENTINEL and GAZETTE, 1825 

"When Lafayette is forgotten our enthusiasm in the cause of liberty will have departed, and the hour of our slavery will have arrived." 

ILLINOIS INTELLIGENCER, 1825 

TOW BREED fresh heroes in Schenectady HO 

If we forget the men of just renown 

Whose feet have trod the streets we daily tread 

Whose eyes have seen this selfsame Mohawk vale 

Beneath the Helderbergs, that we call home?

Our city has a goodly heritage Of close to three full centuries of life 

Since venturesome Van Curler came to buy

The fertile river flats. Here men of mark 

Have grown and labored: Yates and Arthur, Payne 

And Seward, Doctor Nott and Edison,

Then later-Steinmetz, Whitney, Langmuir, these We know and honor, names which stir our blood; 

Here other great ones have been hailed with pomp 

And fervor: thrice the noble Washington 

Was proudly welcomed; thrice his bosom friend 

From France, "America's Adopted Son,"

Passed through our borders,-twice in zealous youth, A slim knight-errant clad in buff and blue, Then, after almost half a century, Fatigued in body, tireless in mind,

He came again, this time the Nation's Guest 

Amid such plaudits as few kings have known 

Symbol of Freedom-General LAFAYETTE!

UT HEROES are outmoded, you reply, And LIBERTY? A legend on our coins (As if enough of them could set us free?) "No, Liberty is dead!" the tyrants cry, "And so are Reason and Democracy." 

Such idols do not suit the present age, Which worships bluster, scorns the liberal. 

How longer venerate past leaders when 

Our modern portraits show them "warts and all"

In such perspectives that all souls seem small, Exalting every ugliness with glee 

To mock the simple? Far too well we know 

The mechanism of idolatry,

The adulation of a crowd gone mad 

Seems childish. 

Who believes in chivalry

Today between the peoples, or in aught But greed and stark self-interest? Man befouls 

His nest with hatred, "friendly" nations arm 

And clutch their gold like misers, haggle long

About their debts, as if the millions dead Had shed their blood for money in a deal 

Ignoble and gigantic?

So Romance 

Must hide in books, and Glamour is a name 

To lure the toothpaste-buyer, Charm is taught

By mail, or merchandised in jars, and Fame? 

Fame is the greatest racket of them all! 

T WAS not so in eighteen twenty-five,

IT Existence was less soft, more rugged, yet Men's hopes were high, their humble heartstrings out 

To one who'd shown himself their fathers' friend

In time of need, and ever Liberty's.

Since Washington had died, he seemed to them 

The greatest man alive. What mattered it 

That Europe turned him down? America 

Knew better. Let the hated Bourbons rage! 

Perhaps these men were fools, and we are wise?

Perhaps instead they kept what we have lost- 

A faith, a dream . . . of freemen's government 

Unsullied by injustice . . . dream of us? 

Say not that we alone have played them false!

CHENECTADY had been alert for days 

That week in June of eighteen twenty-five;

For once the stolid burghers of Old Dorp Were torn from trade, and more-from politics! 

At first they said: "On Thursday he'll be here,"

Then word kept coming down the new canal (That laughed-at "Clinton's Ditch," a joke no more 

And like to ruin shipping in the town) 

Of fresh delays, uncertainties; one thing

Alone seemed sure, that with five thousand miles 

Of travel in the wilderness of South 

And West behind him, he would soon arrive, So all must be in readiness: each man 

Who owned a uniform refurbished it;

Each vrouw laid out with care her Sunday best 

For instant donning at the festal hour; 

Before the City Hall the carpenters

Still worked on a Pavilion; flags and greens 

Were everywhere; and Benson's heavy guns 

Stood ready for saluting on the bridge.

So Friday passed, no General; till at last 

Their patience sorely tried, three officers 

Set out by carriage, swearing they would meet

The tardy boat, and send back an express 

To warn of its approach. On Saturday

The hubbub had redoubled in the streets, Since daybreak, streams of people had poured in 

From all the countryside: the very old,

The very young, alike in festive mood 

Sought vantage-points to see and cheer. All day 

They waited, eager, tense with hope until 

At five o'clock a breathless messenger 

Came headlong down the highway with the news: "The General will arrive at seven . . . sure!"

EANWHILE the packet "Governor Clinton" made 

Unwonted speed behind its spanking team 

Of four superb white horses, all festooned 

With sprigs and streamers; but the great canal (Not opened till October of that year)

Was new, and at the crossings of the creeks Spring freshets interfered, and time was lost. 

Not wholly lost, for people always came 

To greet their hero, every lock and bridge

Was lined with cheering crowds; some veteran 

Of '76, all bent and dewy-eyed

Would be received with princely courtesy As if all hours could be whiled away In reminiscence. But in four short days 

The road to Bunker Hill must be traversed 

And Boston reached, "the polar star" of all

His travels through a round two-dozen states. 

Schoharie Creek once crossed, late afternoon

Was spent in following the Mohawk's bends In placid waters bordered by green farms. 

In that rare quietude his mind reviewed 

The continent's parade: that endless maze

Of arches,thrones,illuminations,balls, Salutes,Masonic rites,collations,toasts, Addresses,laurel crowns,degrees,parades, Dramatics,christenings,and barbecues,

Enormous cheeses, presents by the ton, Old comrades to remember, babes to kiss,

Reviews to hold, and cornerstones to lay 

And in his heart the thought of Adrienne.

He lived for glory, loved attention, yet A grateful people seemingly were bent On proving that republics don't forget, On killing him with kindness, stuffing full 

For once, what Jefferson, his friend, had called 

His "canine appetite for public praise."

ND YET, how truly kind they were, and proud 

To show him everything, as if to say: "You see, this country which you helped to free 

Is thriving; we have justified your pains 

And hopes by rapid progress." 

It was true

He hardly recognized this Mohawk vale 

So prosperous and ordered, as the place 

Where he had come in March of "78,

A stripling Major-General in command 

Of that ill-fated Canada campaign

To which he had been lured from Valley Forge By Gates's plot to break the tie that bound "The Boy" in loyalty to Washington.

Arrived in Albany, he'd found no troops, No guns, no money-end of all his dreams 

Of glory at the gates of Montreal. 

A bitter pill, at twenty, to be made 

To look a failure; but he carried on-

By sled went westward through the giant pines

To meet the rude frontier, still firmly locked In grip of winter, at Schenectady, "Door of the Long House of the Iroquois," The river port, a Dutch town garrisoned Against swift raids, he found a regiment Of regulars (with staunch Oneida scouts) Encamped, but also signs of Tory plots . . 

One soldier he examined as a spy 

But got no hanging proof. 

Then, taken ill,

The young commander had the best of care From every hand; and soon pressed on to try 

His charm (and that of good French gold) upon 

The Indians at Johnstown. Readily 

They took his Louis d'or, and christened him 

Kayewla, “father," smoked the pipe of peace

And made the wanted treaty. Always he 

Got on with Indians, as well as blacks 

Whose slavery he hated. 

Back once more

In Albany, he headed south to hear 

The joyful tidings that his country, France

Had followed his alliance by her own. ONG YEARS of war were then ahead of him; 

But after they had ended in the master-stroke Of Yorktown, and the long-sought Peace was signed, The Marquis, still but twenty-seven, came 

Again along the Mohawk Valley, quick 

To help his second-fatherland once more

At that great council with the Indians Which gathered at Fort Schuyler (later Rome)

In fall of '84. Then "everything," 

He said, "recalled the War," the Butler raids

Had left grim scars; but settler families 

With "twelve small children sleeping in one bed"

Gave signs of reconstruction. Roads were few 

And wretched; mostly they were forced to ride 

Through silent forests reddened by the keen 

October frosts. The Indians rejoiced,

As children will, to see Kayewla, signed Another pact which made a step more sure 

Their ultimate extinction ... 

So he mused- 

Why, just the day before, at Utica 

Three old Oneida chiefs had come to greet 

Kayowla, talk of better days, and take 

From him with smiles . . . a dollar each!

HOUGH REDSKIN appetites had seen no change, All else was different, save perhaps the hills 

Now gilded by the swift-declining sun.

The last lock passed, the packet slid along The curve from Rotterdam, and to the east There loomed no puny frontier settlement With its stockade, but wharves and warehouses (That had escaped the fire six years before),

Tall churches, banks, a massive wooden bridge Across the river, with its fleet of boats, And crowning all, upon the distant slope

The spacious buildings, French in every line (Ramee's fine brick and stucco symphony), Of some new college shone in sunset hues. 

How little did the city then suspect

That just such a canal-boat, four months hence 

Would drop the curtain on an era, prove 

It could no longer stay the terminus 

Of river traffic, though a silly crank

Had talked about a railroad run by steam 

To Albany! 

Just as we today are blind 

To what the future holds, and what the past 

Has taken from us, never to return.

LREADY RHYTHMIC thunders spelled the end 

Of that long wait; the roar of "WELCOME LAFAYETTE!” 

Began, as every throat took up the shout 

With pent-up fervor, lest their bosoms ache

From unexpressed emotion. Borne on waves 

Of joy almost delirious, the craft

Passed under State Street bridge to land At Union Street before the City Hall. 

Amid a din of cheers and pealing bells,

A portly, blue-clad figure, with a limp (Supposedly acquired at Brandywine), Stepped forth, his flashing hazel eyes alight 

With pleasure, with his son George Washington

Beside him, as he stood and gravely heard 

That same old speech of welcome from the Mayor, Met Doctor Nott, remembered every man 

Who claimed participation in the War, In short, took everyone by storm. . . . 

Then through the streets up to the Givens House

A long procession moved: three escort troops 

Of soldiers, several bands, the local Grays,

The Union College Guards (and Faculty), 

The Masons, Judges, Lawyers, Doctors, then

Just "Citizens and Strangers," plus small boys. 

Masonic greetings followed; everyone 

Must shake the General's hand; small chance

For him to reach the table for the "elegant repast" 

Long since prepared. 

At last the many toasts: 

"OUR NATION'S GUEST-All Honor to the Brave, 

And Glory to the Patriot" was pledged; 

The Guest of Honor, rising, gracefully returned

The toast: "THE CITY OF SCHENECTADY- 

Most kind in times of danger to a young commander, who comes, after a lapse of forty-seven years, to offer her his affectionate devotions, good wishes, and the tribute of his old and new feelings of respectful gratitude."

The day's enthusiasm reached its crest, Yet ran still higher, for at ten o'clock 

The hour had come to say a last good-bye.

His war-time comrades knew too well that they 

Would never see the chief they loved again

And wept; the young rejoiced they had been born 

In time to greet this kindly sage to whom They owed so much,-reminder of the days When Liberty and Glory could conspire 

To bring a Marquis halfway round the world. 

To stand beside their fathers under fire.

Then, thoughtful to the last, the General paid 

A call of courtesy on Governor Yates; 

Took coach, in doubtful hopes of sleep, And rumbled up the hill toward Albany.
