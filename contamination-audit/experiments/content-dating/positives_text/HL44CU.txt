STATUTE CREATING JUDICIAL COUNCIL 

Extract from Oregon Revised Statutes 

1.610 Judicial council; membership. There hereby is created a

Judicial Council of the State of Oregon, consisting of three persons who are active members of the Oregon State Bar, five persons who are not attorneys, one circuit judge, one district court judge, one municipal court judge and, ex officio, during their respective terms of office, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, the Chairman of the Senate Committee on Judiciary and the Chairman of the House Committee on Judiciary of the Legislative Assembly and the Attorney General. Except for the chairmen of the committees on judiciary of the Legislative Assembly, any member who ceases to maintain the qualifications prescribed by this section shall thereupon cease to be a member of the council. 

(1965, c. 494, §1; 1967. c. 270, §1.) 1.620 Functions of council. The council shall have the powers and shall be charged with the following duties: (1) To make a continuous survey and study of the organization, jurisdiction, procedure, practice, rules and methods of administration and operation of each of the courts within the state, the volume and condition of business in the courts, the work accomplished and the results obtained. (2) To receive, consider and, in its discretion, investigate criticisms and suggestions from any person pertaining to the administration of justice and to make recommendations in reference thereto. (3) To keep advised concerning the decisions of the courts relating to procedure and practice therein and concerning pending legislation affecting the organization, jurisdiction, operation, procedure and practice of the courts. (4) To recommend from time to time to the legislature any changes in the organization, jurisdiction, operation, procedure and methods. of conducting the business in the courts which can be put in effect only by legislative action or constitutional amendment and to recommend to any court any changes in the rules and practice of the court or the method of administering judicial business therein which, in the judgment of the council, would simplify and expedite or otherwise improve the administration of justice therein. (1965, c. 494 §8.) 

1.630 Appointment; term; qualifications. (1) The attorney members shall be appointed by the Board of Governors of the Oregon 

State Bar. (2) The circuit court judge, the district court judge and the municipal court judge shall be appointed in such manner as may

be determined by their respective Oregon judicial associations. 

If such associations shall fail to make such appointments, they shall be made by a majority of the other members of the council. (3) The Governor shall appoint the non-attorney members of the council from the state at large, one member representative of commerce and industry, one of education, one of labor, one of agriculture and one from the professions not otherwise represented. (4) Each member, other than ex officio members, shall be appointed for a term of five years and shall hold office until his successor is appointed and has qualified. The terms of members first appointed by the Governor shall expire as designated by the Governor at the time of appointment, one at the end of the year, one at the end of two years, one at the end of three years, one at the end of four years and one at the end of five years after July 1, 1965. The terms of members first appointed by the Board of Governors of the Oregon State Bar shall expire as designated by the board, one at the end of three years, one at the end of four years and one at the end of five years. (5) No member of the council appointed by the Governor may hold any national or state office in a political party. Not more than three of the members of the council appointed by the Governor may be members of the same political party. Each member of the council shall be a citizen of the United States, a resident of the State of Oregon and over the age of 21 years. Before entering upon the duties of the council, each member shall take and subscribe to an oath or affirmation to support the Constitution of the United States and of this state and to faithfully and honestly discharge the duties of his membership. 

(1965, c. 494 §2.) 

1.640 Vacancies; effect of membership. (1) If there is a vacancy in the office of the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, or if the Chief Justice is unable to perform the duties of his office, during the period of such vacancy or inability, the majority of the justices of the Supreme Court shall designate an associate justice of that court to act in his stead and to serve as chairman of the council. (2) If there is a vacancy in the office of Attorney General, or if the Attorney General is unable to perform the duties of his office, during the period of such vacancy or inability, the Deputy Attorney General or the acting Attorney General shall serve instead of the Attorney General as a member of the council. (3) Other vacancies shall be filled in the same manner as the person whose membership has become vacant was appointed, and an appointment to fill any such vacancy shall be for the remainder of the unserved term.

(4) Membership on the council by any justice, judge or the Attorney General, including any acting or Deputy Attorney General, who may become a member, shall be, in the case of any judge or justice, deemed to be one of his judicial functions and, in the case of the Attorney General or any acting or Deputy Attorney 

General or the Chairman of the Senate Committee on Judiciary or the Chairman of the House Committee on Judiciary of the 

Legislative Assembly, one of his official functions, and shall not constitute holding a public office. (1965, c. 494, §3; 1967, c. 270, §2.) 

1.650 Officers; employes; rules. The Chief Justice of the Supreme

Court shall be the chairman of the council. The council shall appoint from its members a vice-chairman who, in the absence or disability of the chairman, or in the case of a vacancy, shall act as chairman. The council shall appoint, and may at pleasure relieve, such employes, including persons qualified for legal research, investigating and statistical work, as may be deemed necessary to enable the council to perform its duties and administer its affairs, and from time to time to fix their salaries or compensation within the appropriation therefor made by the Legislative Assembly. The council may make and modify from time to time rules and regulations for the conduct of its proceedings and the exercise of its powers, including provisions: (1) For regular meetings and for the method of calling special meetings of the council; (2) Defining the number of members of the council which shall be necessary to constitute a quorum for the transactions of its business; (3) Prescribing the duties of its executive secretary and other employes of the council; and (4) For the conduct of hearings and investigations authorized to be conducted by the council or any committee of the council. 

The council may appoint regular and special committees of its members and advisory committees of nonmembers to investigate and report upon any matter relating to its duties. (1965, c. 494, §4.) 

1.660 Council offices. The council shall maintain its office at the capitol and shall be provided by the Secretary of State with suitable office quarters. (1965, c. 494, §5; 1967, c. 270, §3.) 

1.670 Executive secretary; term; salary. The council shall have the authority to appoint an executive secretary, without regard to the State Civil Service Law, to manage the business of the council when the council is not in session. The executive secretary shall serve the council for such periods as may be designated

by the council. The executive secretary may be removed at the discretion of the council, and such discretion shall not be reviewable in any court of law. The salary of the executive secretary shall be fixed by the council, within the appropriation made therefor by the legislature. (1965, c. 494, §6.) 

1.680 Expenses of members. Each member of the council and of advisory committees shall serve without compensation but shall be allowed and paid his actual and necessary expenses incurred in the performance of his duties. (1965, c. 494, §7.) 

1.690 Reports to legislature. The council shall, 30 days before the legislature meets in regular session, make a report to the legislature of its proceedings and of the work of the various courts from the date since the last legislative session, the condition of business therein and any recommendations which require action by the Legislative Assembly to improve the administration of justice. It may make such further and supplemental reports as it deems proper. Copies of all such reports shall concurrently be transmitted to the Governor and to the Board of Governors of the 

Oregon State Bar. (1965, c. 494, §9.) 

1.700 Meetings. The council shall by rule make provision for at least four regular meetings each year, to be held to the extent practicable, so as to accommodate the persons resident in each of the several judicial districts. Such regular meetings shall include at least one session to which the general public shall be invited to submit any complaint, observation or recommendation concerning the administration of justice in the courts within the State of Oregon. (1965, c. 494, §10.) 

1.710 Judicial Council Account; gifts; contracts; employes. (1)

An account designated as the Judicial Council Account hereby is established in the General Fund of the State Treasury. All moneys received by the Judicial Council shall be paid into the 

State Treasury and credited to the Judicial Council Account. All moneys in the Judicial Council Account hereby are appropriated continuously for and shall be used by the Judicial Council in the discharge of the duties imposed in ORS 1.610 to 1.710 on the

Judicial Council. 

(2) The Judicial Council may accept gifts, grants or services, may contract with nonprofit organizations, educational institutions, state and federal agencies, and retain such experts as in its judgment it finds necessary to effectively carry out the purposes of ORS 1.610 to 1.710. 

(1965, c. 494, §11; 1967, c. 270, §4.) 10

Action Taken by The Judicial Council 

1967-1968 

The special session of the Fifty-Fourth Legislative Assembly which was called in the fall of 1967 revoked the appropriation of money previously granted to the Judicial Council by the regular session, thereby leaving the Council without funds to continue the studies and research relating to matters concerning judicial administration upon which it intended to base its recommendations to the Fifty-Fifth Legislative Assembly in 1969. The Council was convinced so sincerely of the value of these proposed recommendations that it requested funds from the Emergency Board and received $25,000 to carry out its proposed program. While this sum enabled the Council to continue to function, it limited the extent of research and study to which the Council could avail itself.

The original program of the Council was revised after the grant of the $25,000, and it was the decision of the Council to concentrate on three major recommendations, to-wit: (1) an intermediate court of appeals; (2) consolidation of the justice, municipal and district courts; (3) defense at public expense of indigents. 

Inasmuch as all three of these proposals directly and seriously affected the public, it was decided to hold public hearings in different sections of the state and to invite local officials and members of the legislature residing in the general area in which each hearing would be held to appear and offer such comments, suggestions and criticism as they wished. The general public was also invited through the press. Substantial publicity was given well in advance of each public hearing, the first of which was held February 23rd at Portland, Oregon, and at which some 26 witnesses were heard. Some expressed their opinion with respect to all three topics, some with respect to a particular topic. In addition to the verbal testimony received, written statements were submitted and made a part of the record of that hearing. Subsequently public hearings were held in June at Bend, in July at Coos Bay, and in September at Medford. While the attendance at these hearings was not as large as at the Portland hearing the response was exceedingly satisfactory and the comments, suggestions and criticisms received were very helpful to the Council. 

The Council heard from an extremely good cross-section of the population with respect to the cost of providing defense for 11

indigents, whether it be a continuation of the court-appointed counsel system or the utilization of a local public defender system. 

Many appeared, or submitted written statements in lieu of appearance, and spoke both for and against the consolidation of the lower courts. Most of the speakers at all of the public hearings favored the creation of an intermediate court of appeals, primarily because of the growing backlog of cases in the Supreme Court. 

The Judicial Council believes that by holding these public hearings and receiving the opinions of the participants it has relieved to a great extent the work of the legislative committees in considering the legislation which the Council will propose and which will be based upon the testimony received at the public hearings and the discussion and study of such testimony by the individual members of the Council and its part-time staff. 

In addition to the four public hearings, there were meetings held in Salem in April, May and October, at which time reports were received from sub-committees appointed to carefully review the three major proposals, and studying the actions taken in other states with similar problems. 

In October the first Citizens' Conference on Oregon Courts was held at Salem, and the Judicial Council takes special pride in its co-sponsorship of this conference. Many other states have held these conferences but this was the first time in Oregon that lay people from all sections of the state and from every walk of life were called together and asked to look at the problems of their courts and to comment and suggest as they felt necessary upon possible improvements which could be made. The Conference lasted two days and it is quite probable that there will be a permanent citizens' committee established; at the time of this report such steps were being taken. The Judicial Council will work in close cooperation with this committee. 12

Recommendations of The Judicial Council I Court Structure and Administration 

(A) The creation of a court of appeals. (B) Further study and research regarding the ultimate establishment of a one-trial court level in the State of Oregon, which will utilize commissioners or magistrates to handle preliminary matters requiring immediate attention. 

II Defense of Indigents 

(A) Employment of one public defender for each judicial district, to be appointed by the Public Defender Committee from a recommended list submitted by the presiding circuit court judge of that judicial district.

III The Judiciary 

(A) Judicial salaries be increased in conformance with the recommendations made by the Oregon State Bar Committee on Judicial Administration and adopted by the Bar at its annual meeting in October 1968. (B) That ORS 3.560 and ORS 4.642 be amended to increase the compensation for circuit judge pro tempore from $50 to $90 and for district judge pro tempore from $35 to $60. 13

I. Court Structure and Administration 

(A) The creation of a court of appeals.

1. Introduction: 

Man's contempt for delayed justice is as strong today as it was when Shakespeare, Dickens, Chekhov, Moliere, and Gilbert and Sullivan compared the delay with death, disease and condemnation. A more recent commentary noted: 

Delay in the courts is unqualifiedly bad. It is bad because it deprives citizens of basic public service . it is bad because delay may cause severe hardship to some parties . . and it is bad because it brings the entire court system a loss of public confidence, respect and pride.2

Oregon's trial courts are among the best in the country at preventing a backlog at the trial court level.3 Unfortunately this fine record is not found in the Supreme Court where the number of appeals has risen sharply. Appellate congestion and delay is a problem which has become serious in many jurisdictions long before it has appeared in Oregon. A number of states, both in smaller as well as in larger and more populous states, have established Courts of Appeals to cope with this situation.4 

Oregon must deal with the problem of an increase in the number of appeals and the growing backlog of cases at the Supreme Court level before it swamps our entire judicial system. If parties are not afforded a prompt appeal, the pressure for a prompt trial will dissolve and the whole judicial machinery will bog down. The victim of this is the public, which is hurt by not getting prompt adjudication of its civil grievances and prompt determination of innocence or guilt in criminal matters.

The causes of this increase in appeals today are varied. Among the broad causes are the population increase, industrial growth, and urbanization. Our society has become more complex, more mobile and today is subjected to more governing bodies. The increase of governmental regulations, the growing number of automobiles, the abrasion of human relationships-particularly in dense urban living-all contribute to increased litigation and pro-

The problem in Oregon of appellate congestion is not new. At its 1956 meeting, the Oregon State Bar adopted a resolution stating that "the problem of delay on appeals to the Oregon Supreme Court is one of the most serious problems confronting the administration of justice in Oregon at the present time." The 1959 Legislative Interim Committee on Judicial Administration gave several reasons for the growing backlog, many of which still are true today. One section concluded with the comment '. . . the rate of appeals now exceeds 260 a year and will probably rise in the future. In 1959, a Constitutional Amendment was passed to allow the use of circuit court judges and retired judges as pro tempore justices. Since 1960, the Supreme Court has used pro tem justices to cope with the problem. Although it worked at first, even this has not solved the backlog problem permanently.

3. Past Action Taken: 

The Supreme Court has attempted to cope with the delay. In addition to the use of pro tem justices since 1960, the Court now hears less important cases in five man departments or three man panels. The Supreme Court justices are working as hard as possible and still maintain a high degree of excellence in their opinions.' 

In 1966, each justice averaged 35 opinions; in 1967, each averaged 

34. If no remedial action is taken and the Supreme Court feels the pressure to increase the number of opinions each justice writes to cope with the backlog, the quality necessarily will fall. Less time for research and study means poorer opinions which will give less guidance to the public. An examination of a set of law student casebooks shows a high percentage of cases quoted from the Oregon Supreme Court. This is a reflection of the esteem in which the nation's professors and legal scholars hold this court. 

Also, more and more opinions to be written mean that a justice can spend less time studying the drafts of opinions by the other justices, with the result that the opinions will become "one judge" opinions. This is to be avoided. 

4. Possible Alternatives: 

Various alternatives to cope with this backlog have been discussed: enlarging the size of the present Supreme Court, having the Supreme Court sit in departments of three, creating a Court. of Appeals, or allowing the Supreme Court to exercise certiorari by denying some appeals.

At first, enlarging the Supreme Court appears to be the logical way to cope with the problem-adding a justice or two when the caseload demands. Presently the Supreme Court caseload would require a twelve judge court to keep current. However, this would not be a panacea because all justices must read the drafts of opinions that their brethren write. Enlarging the court would mean that many more opinions for each justice to read, consider and discuss. 12 Eventually some provision will have to be made for a court of appeals, for the number of appeals will grow. If an appellate court then were established, the problem would be what to do with the added justices when the Supreme Court shrank back to a workable seven members.

In 1956, the Supreme Court wrote 179 opinions which filled 2,164 pages in the Oregon Reports, for an average length of 12.2 pages per opinion. In 1966, the Court wrote 306 opinions filling 2,018 pages, paring the average length to 6.6 pages. 

12To keep current in 1972, a 15 judge court would be needed. If California had adopted this approach instead of a court of appeals, today it would need 47 judges on the Supreme Court to handle the appeals. 17

The second alternative for coping with appellate congestion is for the Supreme Court to hear all cases in departments of three justices. In evaluating this proposal it is necessary to analyze the purpose of a Supreme Court. It serves two purposes: it determines finally the disputes between litigants; it establishes the law so people can conduct their affairs in reliance on this law. The preponderance of private law (regulating business and property dealings and private grievances) in the United States and England is established not by legislation but rather by courts through decisions in individual cases. Courts continually are re-evaluating past decisions in the light of changing conditions and establishing new law to meet new situations. While hearing cases in three judge panels would accomplish the first purpose of a Supreme Court, it would fail in the second. Inconsistent decisions could result from the different departments of the same court. The result in any appeal might be determined by which department heard it. This would be an intolerable solution in a court of last resort. ¹4 

In addition, there is a limit to how many opinions a judge can write, regardless of the size of the department in which he sits. 

One other possible way to cope with appellate congestion would be to give the Supreme Court the right to refuse appeals. 

It would mean that in those cases in which the Supreme Court refused a request of litigant for permission to appeal, there could be no appeal of any kind from the circuit court. The public's conception of justice seems to contemplate at least one appeal on the law as a matter of right. Nearly all cases which end in the Supreme Court start in circuit court-very few start in the lower courts. In addition, serious doubts exist whether in criminal matters any abolition of the right to appeal would meet the due process test of the United States Constitution.

The disadvantages of these alternatives outweigh any advantages. The proposal approved by the Judicial Council, the Oregon 

State Bar, and the Supreme Court offers the best solution. It would be capable of deciding large numbers of cases, yet would leave the making of major policy decisions to one group of manageable size. It would provide for the establishment of a court of appeals. 

5. Proposed Recommendation: 

A court of appeals should be established which would have jurisdiction over certain types of cases and would relieve the press of cases now in the Supreme Court.

A seven man Supreme Court, coupled with a five man Court of Appeals, could handle the present appellate load. The Court of Appeals could sit in departments of three, with the Chief Judge sitting in both. This Court of Appeals could relieve the Supreme 

Court of 40-45% of its current workload. 

The jurisdiction of the Court of Appeals is of great importance. 

Cases heard in this court should be of clear, specific classes, to avoid jurisdictional quarrels. 15 Therefore, only cases which fit easily into distinct classes should be included. The Supreme Court should have authority to determine all jurisdictional disputes in a summary manner without formal briefs, oral argument, or written opinion. Ninety to ninety-five per cent of all cases would be final after disposition by the Court of Appeals. Double appeals are to be avoided. This court would be worthless if all petitions to the Supreme Court were granted-it would gain nothing. A losing party in the Court of Appeals could petition the Supreme Court to rehear his case, but petitions seldom would be granted. 

Petitions would be granted and appeals would be heard where the Supreme Court felt that an important policy decision may have been decided erroneously or a conflict in decisions is seen between the departments of the Court of Appeals. Statistics in states having courts of appeals show only seven or eight per cent of the petitions are allowed. As large a percentage as possible of cases in this court should be those where the law is settled and not changing rapidly because this would cut down on the number of petitions to the Supreme Court. 

Exclusive appellate jurisdiction in the following types of cases would be vested in the Court of Appeals: 

1. Criminal, post-conviction, and habeas corpus. 

2. Probate, including guardianships. 

3. Domestic relations, adoptions, and juvenile matters. 

4. Appeals from the decisions of state agencies (excluding the tax court, where a record for direct appeal is made). 

A statistical study for the last seven years shows that the classes of cases listed above composed 42.3 per cent of the Supreme 

Court's business. The criminal cases took up 26 per cent; probate cases took 3 per cent; domestic relations took 6 per cent; administrative agency cases took 8 per cent. When the backlog of cases with original jurisdiction in the Supreme Court grows in the future, another three judge department could be created in the Court of Appeals, and jurisdiction over another class or two of cases would be transferred to it. Oregon eventually will need a court of appeals for all cases, and this plan would enlarge it gradually as the need develops and thus avoid the expense of a full scale court of appeals until one is needed.

This Court of Appeals should be headquartered in Salem. The cost of maintaining a separate law library is reason enough to demand this. This court could travel to Pendleton, Medford or even Portland once or twice a year to hear cases from those areas. 

In addition to the five judges, five secretaries and five law clerks, a bailiff, a deputy clerk and a secretary for the clerk's office would be needed. 

6. Estimated Cost: 

Naturally, the cost of this Court of Appeals must be considered. 

At the outset it must be remembered that out of total General Fund expenditures of 522 million dollars (1967-69) the entire state court system is allotted approximately one per cent of this total.16 When service to the public and respect for an efficient court system are balanced against the cost of this court, the expense certainly is justified.

The estimated expense of operating the new court for a biennium would be at a minimum $533,072.

In addition, the initial capital outlay for establishing the court would be near $10,000, including office equipment, furnishings and initial supplies. This figure would be increased if quarters were not available in the present Supreme Court Building, and it were necessary to rent office space until a future date when the new and larger Supreme Court building is constructed. 

These costs would include the salaries of the five judges, five secretaries, five law clerks, a bailiff, a deputy clerk and a secretary for the clerk's office. 

7. Conclusion: 

The 1969 Legislature will ask several questions regarding this proposed Court of Appeals. The Judicial Council has done this at its public hearings and meetings. 

16In 1966, the percentage of Oregon's budget expended on the judiciary was third lowest in the United States-only Washington and Pennsylvania were lower. 20

Is this court needed now? Many more statistics to show the need today and the consequences tomorrow could be thrust at the Legislature. Only enough statistics to illustrate the need to attack this problem now have been presented. 

Is this court the best way to solve the problem? This report covers the alternatives and their shortcomings. The nuances of the proposed Court of Appeals cause some disagreement, for any plan like this is a compromise. However this proposed court embodies the best ideas with the least complications. 

Is the cost justified? When the entire judicial budget only amounts to one per cent of the expenditures of the General Fund, the amount needed for this court is not large. The cost must be balanced against service to the public and respect for efficient operation of the courts.

Does this proposed court have wide support? At the public hearings the Judicial Council received a great deal of endorsement and very little opposition for this court. The Council heard from local bar associations, newspapermen, attorneys, and interested citizens--and there was near unanimous support. The Court of Appeals was recommended by the 1968 Judicial Administration 

Committee of the Oregon State Bar, and was endorsed by the bar membership at the annual meeting. The consensus statement of the Citizens' Conference on the Oregon Courts endorsed the Court of Appeals: 

"The current caseload and resultant congestion in the Supreme Court causes a substantial delay and some remedy is required to promote the efficient administration of justice. "This conference feels the solution is best found in an Intermediate Appeals Court. Not all cases should be heard by this court, but only those which can reasonably be expected to be concluded with the hearing by the Intermediate Appeals Court."""

(B) Ultimate establishment of a one-trial court level in the State of Oregon which will utilize commissioners or magistrates to handle preliminary matters requiring immediate attention. 

The conversion from the present multi-trial court system to a one-trial court level represents a major change; one which involves many problems and, in all probability, cannot be accomplished in one sweeping action. Therefore, the Council believes further study and research is necessary. It may require, in addition to the work done by the staff, employment of specialists to do an "in depth" study of the entire matter. This change-over may appear drastic at first but Oregon is not the first state to work for this type of a judicial system: i.e., a single trial court, a court of appeals and a supreme court. This has been experimented with and adopted by many other states and works very satisfactorily. 

Oregon should move in this direction.

The Council recommends, however, serious consideration of a proposed bill which may be introduced to merge the justice, municipal and district courts. If such a bill is introduced the 

Judicial Council will give support and endorsement to it. This proposed bill provides basically the following: (1) A district court in every judicial district of the state (for the purpose of district courts only the 8th and 10th Judicial Districts will be combined and considered as one and the 13th and 14th Judicial Districts combined and considered as one). (2) Three new district judgeships will be created, one for the combined 8th and 10th Judicial District, one for the 9th Judicial

District and an additional judge for the present 11th Judicial District which is composed of six counties. (3) The district court judge will have the authority to appoint commissioners to perform certain functions provided and limited by law; the commissioner need not be a member of the bar, and in most instances, will not be a full time commissioner. He will be paid on a per diem basis and will be required to make a monthly report and accounting to the district court judge of the time spent performing the functions provided by law. (4) The commissioner will be given the power of a magistrate; e.g., bail, arraignment and guilty pleas; his civil duties will be restricted to accepting and filing papers in any action before the court, issuing writs of attachment and other miscellaneous duties presently given to a clerk of the district court. (5) The justice of the peace courts will be abolished and their civil and criminal jurisdiction transferred to the district courts and 22

the municipal courts will be given authority to enter into cooperative agreements with the state whereby district courts may handle violations of municipal ordinances. (6) Fines and forfeitures received in traffic cases will be allocated on the basis of who is the arresting officer (this is the present system); i.e., if a state officer makes the arrest the money will go to the General Fund, if a county sheriff or deputy makes the arrest the money goes to the county and if a city law officer makes the arrest the city receives the money. With respect to all other revenues obtained in the district court one-half will go to the county and one-half to the state. At the present time all of these revenues go to the county, however, after the merging of the courts the counties will not have to pay the salaries of the justices of the peace. The salaries will be paid from the General Fund and the counties will have only the expense of the court personnel and costs of operation of the courts. It is presumed that there will be provisions for allocations to municipalities which avail themselves. of their contractural right to have the district court handle violations of municipal ordinances.

Substantial testimony was received at the public hearings in Portland and Bend that because of the distances involved the counties east of the Cascade mountains would be deprived of the availability and convenience of local courts if the justice of peace courts were abolished. There was also considerable testimony received from the Justice of Peace Association and from individual members of that association that the justices of peace would like to have their courts upgraded to be a part of the statewide court system. Many said that there were insufficient funds in the individual counties to provide adequately for proper courtroom facilities. 

In many instances the salary of the justice of the peace is so low it becomes an absolute necessity for him to rely upon his primary occupation to the detriment of his responsibilities and obligations. as a judge. This they believed could be cured if the justice courts were made a part of the statewide system. 

If the legislation relating to the merging of the justice, municipal and district courts is enacted the objections to abolition of the justice courts would be overcome because the people would not lose the availability of their local courts. In most instances the commissioner serving under the district court judge would assume many of the duties and powers now vested in the justice of peace. 

Many justices of the peace realize that in order to upgrade their courts some of the present sixty-one justice districts should be eliminated. In fact, in a letter to the Judicial Council, the 1968 executive committee of the Justice of Peace Association recommended 23

the abolition of twenty-one of the present sixty-one justice courts. 

The remaining forty judicial districts could be supplied with adequate and available justice through the use of commissioners. 

In most instances the justice of peace in that area would be appointed to serve as a commissioner. His interest in upgrading the court under the present system indicates a desire to improve the entire judicial system of the state. Therefore, under the proposed merger the people of this state would not lose the convenience of "local justice" and would gain a higher level of justice. The use of commissioners acting under the direction and supervision of a district judge who is a full time law-trained jurist would insure a higher quality of justice and a greater uniformity of procedures. 

The commissioner would be removed only with approval of the 

Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. 

Although the justice of peace courts would be abolished, the only justification for their continuance, i.e., convenience and immediate availability to the local communities, would be retained through the use of commissioners who would be a part of the state court system. The expense of operating the district courts would not be as great as at present where the large number of justice courts in many instances impose an extreme hardship upon the county budget.

If this proposed legislation becomes law then Oregon is halfway toward its goal of achieving a compact and efficient statewide judicial system offering three levels, trial courts with original general trial jurisdiction of all cases including misdemeanors, a court of appeals to which the majority of cases tried in the trial courts can be appealed and a supreme court which will try cases involving constitutional questions, questions of substantial and statewide importance and, upon certiorari, some cases which have been heard by the court of appeals. This is the ideal judicial system recommended by nearly every organization interested in the improvement of justice. More important, it would guarantee to the people of Oregon the most effective administration of justice. 

There are twenty-nine district court judges in Oregon, there are sixty-one justices of the peace and in nearly 225 municipalities there is a person designated as a municipal judge. In this latter category the word "designated" was used purposely because in many of the smaller communities this person has multiple duties and responsibilities of the municipal judge, the city assessor and the city recorder and his duties are primarily fiscal. Evidence received at the hearings indicates that many would be very happy to be relieved of the judicial functions where they have been "tacked on" to their main responsibilities. In some larger 24

municipalities this is not true, but only in Portland is there a municipal court where the judges are full time and are law trained. 

In many of the medium size cities the municipal judge is a lawyer and does excellent work on a part time basis, however, it may often cause a conflict of interest when a client appears as a defendant or as an accused before the judge who is also his attorney.

The workload of the municipal courts and the justice of peace courts consists mainly of traffic cases; better than 95 per cent is traffic violations. In the district courts the percentage of the caseload properly allocated to traffic is approximately 50 per cent. 

In most instances the workload of these courts could be transferred to the district courts without any substantial impact upon their present workload and would not require many additional district court judges at the present time.

Most of the people who appear in court do so in a traffic court and therefor the image of the entire judiciary of the state is based upon what these people observe when they appear in these courts, often called "lesser" courts. They are "lesser" only in their limitation of jurisdiction and they are often "greater" because the largest percentage of the people are aware only of their operations, not those of the circuit courts or Supreme Court. Therefore, it is most important that these courts be conducted in a dignified manner and that the people who appear there understand fully all of their rights guaranteed under the Constitution and that the judge has the knowledge to advise them of these rights. It is important that adequate and proper courtroom facilities are provided which make it apparent that both parties are present to give their evidence to a judge who sits impartially to hear evidence from both sides. This is not always true at the present time in some of our justice courts. Usually, through no fault of the justice of peace, the courtroom may be the back room of a real estate office or an insurance office or a grocery store and the construc tion of the space set aside for the courtroom does not lend itself to the air of impartiality that must exist so that all parties involved leave that courtroom believing they have had their day in court. 

The justices of peace in this state have provided great service in the past but these services are no longer adequate and sufficient to meet the needs of the communities and most of the justices of peace recognize this fact and are trying to upgrade their courts but they have limited means with which to do so. Although the evidence received at the public hearings held throughout the state this last year was rather evenly divided with respect to the proposed merging of the justice of peace courts with the district courts 25

it would appear that the main objection was that the people would be deprived of immediate access to the courts if the justice courts were abolished. This objection is met and overcome through the proposal to use commissioners under the direction and supervision of the district judge in all areas where such use is warranted. 

A more uniform quality of justice would result from the proposed arrangement.

The proposed legislation makes optional the merging of the municipal courts with the district court. If they wish to contract with the state to utilize the services of the district court they may do so, if they do not so choose they may continue under their present system. It is the consensus of the Judicial Council that many cities will enter into such contracts and that as these cities prove that such contractural agreements lessen their costs of operation without substantially diminishing their revenues other cities will follow.

The courts of this state are not to be considered as producers of revenue. In too many instances both cities and counties in the preparation of their budgets take into consideration the revenue received from their courts the preceding year and apply thereto an arbitrary increase to meet anticipated higher costs of operation and expect these courts to produce this revenue. This is not desirable and must be eliminated. 26

II. Defense of Indigents (A) Local Public Defenders 

The Judicial Council recommends employment of one public defender for each judicial district who would be appointed by the Public Defender Committee from a recommended list submitted by the presiding circuit judge of that judicial district. 

In its 1966 report to the Fifty-fourth Legislative Assembly the Judicial Council made several recommendations with respect to defense of indigents and the recommendations which it now make are not inconsistent therewith. However, since that first report an opinion of a judge of the circuit court for Multnomah County in the case of Stevenson v. Holzman held in effect that in any case where the defendant is faced with a possible loss of liberty, if he is unable to pay for counsel, he is entitled to have a lawyer appointed to act for him at governmental expense. In the Stevenson case the accused had been arrested for violation of a Portland municipal ordinance, he was tried, convicted and sentenced in the municipal court for a jail term of six months. This appears to be the first time that a circuit court in Oregon has held that a person accused of a misdemeanor is entitled to court appointed counsel at governmental expense.

Because the number of misdemeanor complaints filed is so large the Judicial Council is firmly convinced that a substantial problem is presented if indigent misdemeanants are entitled to court-appointed counsel. This is so not only because of the volume of judicial business which must be undertaken by members of the bar but also because in many of the smaller counties and municipalities there are not funds sufficient to pay even a nominal fee to such court-appointed counsel. 

There is also the question which arises in many of the smaller communities where there are no lawyers: does one city have the right to require an attorney residing outside that city or community to defend a person accused of violation of an ordinance of that city? There are some counties in which the only attorney residing therein is the district attorney. What happens then? Can that county require an attorney residing outside the county to come in and defend an accused? Does the county have funds which it can use to pay for such defense, and if it refuses to pay because the attorney is not a resident of that county can it be compelled to pay? 27

These are all important questions that must be resolved and these are the questions which were presented to the people appearing at the public hearings held throughout the state this year. 

Many competent and helpful comments and suggestions were received. Much evidence was given supporting the continuation of the court-appointed counsel system; many counties and quite a few cities testified that they had sufficient funds to pay fees to such court-appointed counsel and preferred that method. On the other hand, there were many communities which stated that while at the present time they were getting complete cooperation from their local bar, many of whom were serving gratuitously to assist the court, they felt in good conscience that it was an imposition to ask the attorneys to continue to assume this burden which is not necessarily a part of their responsibility as members of the bar. 

Also, substantial evidence was received with respect to distinguishing between the quality of defense offered to the accused. 

Under the court-appointed counsel system in many instances counsel appointed by the court would have had very little, if any, experience in criminal law yet it would create an inequitable situation if the list from which the court made the appointments was restricted to attorneys specializing in criminal practice. In some of the smaller communities the bar is composed of but a few lawyers, in some instances one is the municipal judge of the community, another the district attorney, leaving only one or two in general practice. This imposes a terrific burden in such situations. On the other hand, if a public defender could be appointed to serve the accused would be afforded defense by a person thoroughly qualified in the practice of criminal law. There would be specialists on both sides, the district attorney for the prosecution and the public defender for the accused. 

The Judicial Council studied the oral and written testimony submitted at the public hearings. It considered the increase in the volume of judicial business if all indigents, whether accused of a felony or misdemeanor, are to be provided counsel at public expense and the burden upon the local bar to provide such counsel. It also considered the burden upon the counties and the municipalities to provide funds to reimburse counsel for such services and the quality of the defense to be afforded the accused. 

Based upon this the Judicial Council recommends the appointment of local public defenders throughout the state. 

The Council recommends that there be one public defender for each judicial district and that he have the authority to appoint as many assistants as justified by the statistics on the ratio of crimes to population. In the more populous districts the local 28

public defender would have a full-time job; in the more remote areas, a part-time job. 

It is also recommended that the local public defender be appointed by the Public Defender Committee from a list nominated by the presiding circuit judge in the area he will serve; that the appointment be for a four year term renewable at the pleasure of the committee and that the public defender be removed for cause only. It is further contemplated that, while not participating in the appointment or reappointment, the state public defender in Salem should have the opportunity to be heard by the committee if he objects to any particular appointment. 

The local public defender would be answerable only to the committee but, under circumstances where warranted, he could seek advice and assistance from the state public defender in Salem. In any situation where the local defender desires assistance the committtee may assign a local defender from another district to be co-counsel at a trial or to participate in proceedings. The local courts should therefore appoint the local public defender for all indigent persons charged with any crime for which the accused is faced with a possible loss of liberty; the courts should not be precluded, however, from appointing local counsel if the situation so requires.

The local public defender would represent indigent defendants at all stages of the criminal proceedings, including re-trial, except direct appeal to the Oregon Supreme Court. The state public defender would continue his representation on the Supreme Court level and in post-conviction proceedings.

Each local public defender should have the authority to appoint as many investigators as needed.

The local public defender would be a state officer and funds should, in part, be appropriated by the legislature with the provision that a portion of the funds be contributed by the individual counties or municipalities.

It would be impracticable to have a local public defender in each county; on the other hand if a local public defender were assigned to a region larger than a judicial district, in eastern Oregon particularly, he would be required to travel large distances at a moment's notice and lose valuable time during the initial period following arrest when the presence of counsel is most vital. Therefore, on a judicial district distribution there will be some approximation of the service to both population and number of cases. Any discrepancy could be regulated by permitting the 29

local public defender to appoint assistants in populous areas and by allowing the part-time local public defenders in the remote areas to engage in private practice. 

If the local public defender is appointed by the Public Defender Committee it sufficiently insulates him from all potential influences; also it would be consistent with the present state public defender system.

It is very important and necessary that the courts have the latitude of appointing local counsel if the situation warrants it and that the courts not be limited to the exclusive use of the local public defender. 30

III. The Judiciary (A) Judicial Salaries 

In its initial report to the Fifty-fourth Legislative Assembly the Judicial Council recommended an increase in the judicial salaries in this state; again in this report it recommends appropriate increases. 

It is necessary to insure the most efficient and effective administration of justice in this state that the judges of the courts of this state be men of the highest caliber and with the best legal training. It is exceedingly difficult to attract such men away from the private practice of today when the judicial salaries are low. 

The Committee on Judicial Administration of the Oregon State 

Bar in 1966 recommended substantial increases in the judicial salaries and such salaries were increased to a certain extent but not yet have they reached the sums originally recommended. In the meantime the cost of living has increased substantially. 

At the present time Oregon is very fortunate in having men of excellent character and ability serving as judges in the courts of this state. The question is: will Oregon be able to retain these men? These men have a right to earn an income commensurate with the responsibilities and duties that they have assumed and sufficient to enable them to meet obligations they owe to their families.

(B) Increased per diem for circuit judges pro tempore and district court judges pro tempore. 

Chapter 549, Oregon Laws 1959, provided for the compensation of circuit judges pro tempore. This compensation was set at $50 for each day actually engaged in the performance of judicial duties. Chapter 377, Oregon Laws 1965, provided that a district court judge pro tempore shall receive as compensation for his services $35 for each day he is actually engaged in the performance of his duties.

While the provision for the use of judges pro tempore in the district courts is fairly new it has been used quite frequently. 

The services rendered by the attorneys willing to assist during emergencies at this low rate of compensation has been of great assistance in maintaining the currency of dockets of the district court. The use of attorneys as circuit judges pro tempore in the circuit court has been in effect since 1959 and has been proven to be a valuable asset when assistance was required to maintain the expeditious handling of cases. Not since its original enactment has there been any change in the compensation. 

At the present time a duly elected or appointed circuit judge will make, based upon a twenty-two judicial working day month, approximately $85 to $90 a day for his services and, in addition to this, he has the benefits provided by the Judges' Retirement Act, annual vacations and many other fringe advantages. It is equally true that the salary of a district court judge when reduced to a judicial working day basis far exceeds that of the $35 per diem allowed the person serving as a district judge pro tempore. 

Circuit and district judges pro tempore are used as sparingly as possible. They do, however, provide a very necessary service and it is becoming exceedingly difficult to induce competent attorneys to leave their practice for a few days or perhaps one or two weeks to serve for this low compensation. Therefore, in order to bring it more in line, although not completely equal to the salary of the circuit judge and of the district judge, the Judicial Council recommends that the per diem allowed the circuit judge pro tempore be increased to $90 for each day he is actually engaged in the performance of his judicial duties and that the per diem allowed a district judge pro tempore be increased to $60 for each day he is actually engaged in the performance of his judicial duties. 32
