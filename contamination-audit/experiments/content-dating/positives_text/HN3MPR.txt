CHAPTER I  I. GUIDANCE NEEDED 

There probably never was a time when youth had greater difficulty in choosing a career than now. This is trebly true for girls.

The frontier boy a century ago had few vocations among which to choose, and his sister had fewer still. 

The preparation required for success in vocations then was not great, but in our complex modern civilization there are thousands of vocations, and their requirements have been increased and so narrowly defined that youth stands mystified in their presence. 

The fact that rapid changes are taking place in vocations still further complicates the problem. Choice is becoming not only more difficult but more important. 

The chances for success and for failure have been multiplied, and the degree of success or of failure has been correspondingly increased. 

1. Too Few Choose Early

The narrowing of the lines calling more and more for specialists in all vocations increases the demand for special training which in turn increases the importance of early choice, but youth seems dazed and unable to choose. 

The writer found in one high school of seven hundred and fifty students that one hundred had given no thought at all to a vocational choice, fifty had thought upon the subject but not seriously, while only forty had made a definite decision. Thus only one in eighteen in this high school had any definite objective. The others were at sea, drifting, or else were trying to find their bearings in a fog. This is perhaps typical for high schools throughout the country. [14]

The situation is better in colleges, but it is a lamentable fact that even in junior and senior classes in college a large per cent of the members are still in doubt. In 

North Carolina State College, a few years ago, thirtyfour and six-tenths per cent of the students were undecided about their future occupation. In Yale a large per cent of the seniors were undecided according to a report of the president. In Leland Stanford thirty-six per cent of the seniors were undecided a few weeks before graduation. In the University of Chicago twentynine and five-tenths per cent of the freshmen had no choice. These cases fairly represent the conditions in colleges generally. 

2. Many Grope Their Way 

A study of Who's Who in America reveals the fact that most of the men listed there had tried from one to four occupations before they found the one in which they succeeded. This reveals a sad state of uncertainty on the part of young men entering their careers, and emphasizes a great need of wise guidance. There is no way of estimating the loss these men sustained in the years spent in groping to find their proper fields, nor of knowing the number of equally gifted men who did not succeed because they never found themselves. 

3. The Cost of Failure in Choice 

Writers generally have estimated that seventy-five per cent of the workers of America are misfits. Some have placed the number as high as eighty per cent. In the surveys made by the author in small towns and country communities in West Tennessee, he found sixty per cent dissatisfied with their choice. The number is no doubt much greater in the more cosmopolitan sections. 

What this great number of misfits means to society in a monetary way may be estimated by multiplying threefourths of the forty-nine million workers by the increase in the earnings that would accrue to them from being put into their right places. If this increase should be only one hundred dollars a year each, which is ultra-

conservative, it would amount to more than one hundred billion dollars each generation. All of this is lost to the individuals and to society because the workers make the wrong vocational choice. But the monetary loss sustained is the least of the losses. No estimate can be placed upon the ill effects this mischoice has upon the development of society and upon its peace and spiritual prosperity, nor can any value be set upon the loss of contentment and happiness it causes the individuals. 

Youthful feet stepping across the threshold into the maze of twenty-two thousand occupations need the wise guidance of their more experienced elders. But they need more than mere guidance. They need instruction in the processes of choosing, because every one must choose for himself in the last analysis, no matter how much guidance he may have. 

4. Fundamental Processes 

The four fundamental processes of wise choosing are: 

First, the gathering and classifying of information on the occupations in which one is interested. Second, a thorough study and analysis of one's self. Third, a proper course of reasoning with regard to one's information and his self-analysis. Fourth, the best possible counsel and guidance, both human and divine. 

5. Self-Guidance 

It is clear from the nature of the fundamental processes that the problem of vocational choice is a personal one and that it must be solved through selfguidance. It is a lifelong problem. It may be settled today and be unsettled tomorrow. One may wake up any morning to find his vocation gone or its doors closed to him while he must walk the streets in search of a new one. In youth one must choose, and often he must continue to choose as conditions and age change. Only a thorough understanding of the principles underlying this whole question can help one to meet the vocational chances that come through life.

6. A Place for the Counselor 

While vocational choice is largely a matter for selfguidance, still the vocational counselor and the friendly adviser can offer much help. They can give information about the vocations and help one in his self-analysis and in making the application of his analysis to the vocations. They may often help young people to discover themselves. It is here that pastors and teachers can often render a very great service. The wise ones will be constantly on the lookout for God's hand in the lives of their young people.

From childhood we have quoted Gray's beautiful lines: 

Full many a gem of purest ray serene 

The dark unfathomed caves of ocean bear: 

Full many a flower is born to blush unseen, And waste its sweetness on the desert air.

It is the pastor's and the teacher's duty and joy to discover these gems and flowers. 

7. Examples 

(1) Singer discovered-Had it not been for an alert and wise pastor, Marian Talley's voice probably would not have thrilled the world. The author had this story from a friend who was her pastor and discoverer. He said: 

Marian was singing in my choir at twelve years of age.

I was attracted by her voice and observed her for several months. One day I said to her, "Marian, God has given you a voice to charm the world. I want you to dedicate it to him and develop it the best you can. I will help you. 

Now, as much as I want you in my choir, it is not best for you. Our director will ruin your voice. I want you to go to the First Church in this city where you will have a better director and greater opportunities."

This pastor enlisted the interest of a wealthy friend in that First Church, who financed Marian's education and saved this flower from "wasting its sweetness on the desert air."

(2) Artist discovered-Benjamin West was discovered when a wise teacher caught him drawing the teacher's picture on a blackboard for the amusement of the other students in "time of books." The lad stood trembling while he and the school expected the ruler to be applied in customary fashion, but they were astonished when the teacher, admiring the boy's work, said: "Why, Bennie, I didn't know you could do that. You have talent, my boy, and if you will work hard you can be a great artist some day."

This aroused the lad like an electric thrill and he became America's greatest portrait painter.

II. GOD'S BLUEPRINT 

Some one has said that God has a blueprint for each life. If so, every one should find his own blueprint and build according to its specifications. In what more exciting search may one engage than in the search for 

God's will in his own life? What more delightful experience may a pastor, teacher, or parent have than in assisting one in that search?

Generally, we change the figure and speak of this search for God's will as life-planning. But, whatever the terminology, it cannot be too strongly emphasized that every one should search his life to see what God has done for and in him, and then to plan and build his career in accordance with the divine handiwork. 

1. Objectives

There must be a starting point for every plan and, paradoxically, in all wise planning the starting point is the objective. One must determine where he wants to go and then devise ways and means of getting there. He must decide what he wants to build and then proceed with the plans. This is eminently true in planning a life. One must first decide whether he will be a producer or a parasite. If the former, he must serve.

2. A Life Outline 

If one is to lead a life of service, he must answer five great questions, which may well form the outline of a life. First, Whom shall I serve? Second, What service shall I render? Third, Who shall help me in the service? 

Fourth, Where shall I serve? Fifth, How can I improve in the service? When one answers these questions either in the right or wrong way, his life is planned. 

E. Stanley Jones says, "Each life is immovably centered somewhere, or it is at the mercy of the winds, is swept by notions, is swayed by minor influences." 

The one we serve is this "immovable center." Around this our whole life revolves. The nature of one's life, his character, his choice of work, the product of his lifeeverything is determined by this choice. If one chooses to serve self, to put self first, to build around self, to lead a self-centered life, then his chief aim or purpose in life will be to advance self. Soon this will lead him, first, to forget the interests and rights of others, and then deliberately to trample upon them in order that he may gain. To gain by such procedure eventually is to lose. 

Jesus said, "Ye cannot serve (be a slave to) God and mammon" (Matt. 6: 24b). This is the significant hint that he gives in regard to vocational choice. It is not possible to serve both vocationally. One represents all that is good, all that is helpful and upbuilding in society; the other represents all that is bad, all that harms, hurts, and tends to destroy man and society. In serving self for self only, one serves mammon; in serving God, he helps humanity. 

3. Serving Mammon

Mammon, as referred to by Jesus, is represented as the god of riches, of unrighteous gain-that master who would tempt one to barter conscience and character for profit. He, or that base nature, is the one that leads men to sell human life, or human rights, for gold; to traffic in property, social or political station, for unrighteous gain. To follow any vocation that does any of

these things that hurts self or man in conscience, in character, property, or rights, is to serve mammon. 

The dope-peddler or bootlegger who traffics in poison; the maker or seller of intoxicating liquor, either legally or illegally, profiting from that which debauches man, wrecks homes, and hurts society, is serving mammon. 

The loan shark who preys upon the unfortunate, taking advantage of their necessities for gain; the robber, the gambler, the counterfeiter; the man who profits by crooked dealing and cheating; the debauchee, the whiteslaver; the one who gives low or base entertainment for profit; the one who preys upon the credulity, ignorance, weakness, or depravity of men, is serving mammon. Men do all of these things in the vain effort to serve self at the expense of others. This is serving mammon. 

Any one facing the choice of a vocation does well to try it by these tests. Anything that hurts man is against self, against God, and is wrong. No man can afford to sell conscience or character for any kind of gain, financial, political, or social. The old question, "What profit hath one in unrighteousness though he get him gain?" still remains unanswered. 

4. Serving God 

What is it to serve God? Suppose one has a silver dollar that he would give to God. How can he do it? 

Can he flip it up to him? No, neither can he literally break the alabaster box and pour the soothing oil upon his thorn-pierced brow. He cannot, as the Cyrenian disciple, take the heavy cross of wood and relieve the Master on his sorrowful way to Golgotha. One may go alone into the closet or into a solitary place and praise God. 

He may adore him and worship him in the secret chambers of the heart unknown to men, but when one would render him a service it must be done to one he loves. 

The cup of cold water must be drunk by another; the loaf of bread must feed the hungry. Would you work, teach, preach, sing, or give to God? Then you must do it for the profit of one he loves. "Inasmuch as ye have

done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me" (Matt. 25: 40b). And thus only may we serve him.

Any work that our hands may find to do that helps man, that makes him happier, better, or wiser, serves God. In this only is there profit. But the profit is far greater if it is done in his name, and for the purpose of serving him.

Therefore, it would appear that Jesus, in calling attention to whom we serve, has also in a most impressive way emphasized the kind of service we should render. 

It is settled by the simple question, Does it help or hurt men? It is not to be decided by the questions: Does it pay? Does it offer material gain? 

5. The Pay

Sometimes society pays rich rewards to those who serve it well, but often it lets them die in poverty, or nails its greatest benefactor to a cross. If so, angels attend their death, and succeeding generations build their monuments.

Mammon sometimes pays in gold, and often allures with a golden glitter, but in the end he takes his toll in blood. To have a self-centered life is to lose it, and, "For what doth it profit a man, to gain the whole world, and forfeit his life?" (Mark 8: 36-ASV).

Again, the choice of a vocation is not always to be determined by the question of social position, as to whether it is high or low. King David answered this question when he said, "I had rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God, than to dwell in the tents (high places) of wickedness" (Psalm 84: 10b). The consciousness of rendering society the service which one feels most called upon to render, and the knowledge that he is doing it in the spirit of service to God, gives a quiet satisfaction which surpasses any joy that material profit or social station can give. 

6. Exalting the Vocations

Rotary International has done much to dignify all use-

ful work through one of its principles, "I see in my vocation an opportunity to render a worthy service to my fellow man," and through the slogan, "He profits most who serves best." One may dignify a lowly occupation with a noble purpose.

Two millionaries were walking down a street in Boston when one twitted the other upon his lowly beginnings: "Bill, I remember when you used to make shoes in that little joint across the street."

W. L. Douglas, the great shoe king, lifting himself with pride, answered, "Yes, sir; and every shoe I made was fit for the foot of a king."

The shoemaker who sees nothing before him but old shoes, some scraps of leather, and the few pennies that may come to him for putting the pieces together, gets little out of life in comparison with what another one gets who sees in these things an opportunity to make it a little easier for his fellow man to walk the path of life. 

That farmer who sees nothing in the ripening fields but the golden shekels that roll in gets little out of his work when compared with what the other farmer gets who sees in the waving fields the smiles upon hungry faces as the harvest turns to bread and who hears in the rustle of the fodder blades the happy voices of millions as they are fed.

Pleasure, profit, and eternal promotion come only from a life of service, usually sacrificial service. Even as 

Jesus said, "Whosoever will be great among you, let him be your minister (servant); and whosoever will be chief among you, let him be your servant (slave)" (Matt. 20: 

26, 27).

The world crowns no heroes from among the servants of mammon. It remembers only in scorn those who have lived for self, while they who have served the God of righteousness shall shine "as the stars for ever and ever" (Daniel 12: 3). 

When E. Stanley Jones was asked to say a word to the thousands of college students in the South, he said,

"First of all, settle your relationship with Jesus Christ definitely until he is Master."

When this is done, and the whole life is planned with regard to this relationship, one will hardly go far wrong. 

III. HOW TO PLAN YOUR LIFE 

The general principles and the argument for a planned life have been presented. Let us now concentrate upon the process of planning. It is assumed that you are serious, that you have come to a full realization that your life is now in your hands to make of it what you will. 

Go out alone. Let the solemn fact that you are now living overwhelm you. Living! Charged with the responsibilities of a life! What may this life be? What lives may it touch? What eternal consequences may hang upon it? Allow yourself to realize how blind you are as you try to look down the years to see the coming changes, the pitfalls, and the opportunities. In that solemn serious state settle some things. 

1. Questions to Be Settled

Settle, first, the objective you will have in life. This, in effect, will settle many other questions; such as, whether your life shall be good or bad, or whether you will go upward or downward, whether you will throw the spiral inward or outward. Settle, second, the question of your life's center. Submerge self with its appetites, desires, ambitions, forever in complete submission to the will of the One who said, "I will guide you." Then search for his will in your life. 

2. Seeking the Divine Will

No life can approximate its best that is not planned and built in harmony with the divine will for that life. 

By this we mean that one's work must be such as will call for the exercise and development of one's strongest natural abilities, and that the objectives be such as will enable these gifts most efficiently to serve and honor God.

How may one find this divine will? Three methods are presented here, all of which should be used to the uttermost: First, by approaching this question with an open mind, earnestly seeking divine guidance and confidently expecting to receive it. He who created man has promised to give wisdom for guidance to those who diligently seek it, and the experience of the best of men confirms our faith in the promise. There is profit in prayer.

Second, by a wise use of information about vocations. 

To illustrate: I was playing one day with a boy who had some tuning forks. A friend watching me said, "Gene, you can find the keys on the piano with those forks." 

I held a C-fork in my hand. "Now, strike that fork and hold it over the piano, and the C-strings will answer it," he suggested. 

I tried, but it didn't work. "Oh, you must press the peddle, lift the dampers, and set the strings free."

I did as he said and all of the C-strings answered. I moved the other forks over the piano and each string vibrated in response to its key.

Now, in like manner, set all of your mind and heartstrings free (by an open, receptive mind-a surrendered will) and then pass over them information of the various vocations. The strings, if free, will respond when the right information strikes them. 

Third, use your best judgment in analyzing yourself and in making decisions, and at the same time soulfully trust to receive divine impressions for guidance.

1. Find the number in your high school or class in college who have made vocational decisions. How many decisions appear to be based on adequate information? 

2. Interview a number of adults. Find their age at the time of making their vocational decision. Are they contented with their choice? Why? How many made their choice a matter of serious prayer? Are those who sought divine guidance any better satisfied on the average than those who did not so seek? (See Number 5, Exercises at close of

Chapter VIII.) 

3. List any vocations that serve mammon which are not included among those given in this chapter. 

4. Do you know any who are rated as valuable citizens who are engaged in any of the vocations listed as serving mammon? 

5. What do you know of the homes of such workers? Are they happy? Do the children from these homes turn out as well on the average as those from other homes on the same financial and intellectual levels? 

6. Can you name any who are living sacrificial lives? Are they happy and successful in the best sense? How about their homes and their children?

Note-If an entire class will make the above studies and combine the results, they will be much more valuable for conclusions. 

7. Are you making any serious effort to plan your life? Have you talked it over with your pastor? Teacher? Father? 

Mother? Have you talked to God about it?

CHAPTER II MAKING VOCATIONAL AND PERSONAL ANALYSES I. ANALYZING THE VOCATIONS 

Information is the cornerstone of self-guidance. No one can choose wisely among the things he knows nothing about. One may not hope to know anything of all of the many thousands of vocations, nor all of any of them, but he can and should know much of many of them. 

Every one who may read this will already have much information about vocations, and will have developed some vocational interests. Some will have made decisions, but this should not close the door to additional information. Such interests and decisions should be treated as tentative and be subject to revision. New information may bring new interests or may strengthen, modify, or displace old ones. 

1. Vocational Information

No attempt will be made to give information about the vocations in this book. The space is too limited. But there is given in the Appendix a list of the best books that discuss vocations in general. In these books will be found references to special books and articles giving additional information on vocations in which one may be interested.

One may get much information from observation, and from interviews with those engaged in various occupations. Many young people try themselves out in different vocations in vacation times, and thus get much information by experience. 

2. Classifying the Vocations 

The process of gathering and organizing vocational in- [ 28 ]

formation is simplified somewhat by first classifying the vocations. All of them may be grouped into a few fields or classes. There is an indefinite number of bases for such classification. One basis, that of service, was discussed in the previous chapter. Some vocations may be classified on the basis of exposure, whether most of the work is done indoors or out of doors. They may be classified on the basis of location. For example, they may be domestic or foreign, land or marine, urban or rural vocations, or those that may be followed at home, and those that must be engaged in away from home. They may be classified on the basis of the mental capacity required for success in them. For such classification see page 49.

Many scientific classifications have been attempted. 

Roger Babson puts all vocations into four groups: Merchandising, manufacturing, the professions, and agriculture. Douglas Fryer makes four groups on the basis of the labor required as follows: Professional labor, the professions; distributive labor, the commercial occupations; maintaining labor, the maintaining occupations; and productive labor, the crafts. Rutherford H. Platt, in his Book of Opportunities, offers six main divisions of occupations on the basis of the kind of work done. 

They are industry, transportation, business, the professions, personal service, and public service. All of these groups may be subdivided many times. 

3. Organizing the Information 

It is important to gather much information about the vocations in which one is interested, but a mass of information is of little value to the mind until it is organized and classified. It is much better, much more economical of time and of effort, to classify the material as it is gathered. To do this, some plan must be prepared in advance. The following outline is offered as a suggestion for studying a vocation:

(1) Introduction (general remarks, and so forth) (2) Duties and nature of the work (3) Advantages (4) Disadvantages (5) Social status (6) Native abilities (those required and those merely desirable) 

(7) Educational requirements 

(8) Preparation (nature, time, and cost) (9) Supply and demand (10) Opportunities for promotions (11) Financial and other rewards 

4. How to Use the Outline

Place the topics of the outline at the top of cards, sheets of paper, or on different pages of a loose-leaf notebook, one upon each sheet. Then, as you get information, write it in brief form under the appropriate heads. In this way your information will be classified at all times. It also will be easy for you to see whether you have secured information on all of the topics, and to determine whether it is sufficient and satisfactory on every topic.

The outline as given will serve as a basis. Each one should modify it to suit his own case. One may have physical defects, may be tied at home, may have property connections, or have special likes or dislikes, which must be considered in choosing. A place in the outline should be made for information bearing upon all points of special interest. One should thus outline all of the vocations in which he has a special interest. These outlines of vocations should be placed beside the outlines you shall later make of your likes and dislikes, your talents, your opportunities, and other vocational attributes. Your problem of choice will then be greatly simplified. 

II. MAKING A PERSONAL EVALUATION 

It is vital for one to take his measure and to take it early. His whole life's plan depends upon it. His edu-

cation, the amount and kind, his choice of vocation, where he shall work and at what level; his plans for a home, its location, and the kind it shall be, together with his choice of a companion, all depend upon the estimate he makes of his abilities and opportunities. 

1. Those Who Overestimate

There are some who overestimate their abilities, and undertake what for them is impossible. They try to hitch their wagon to a star when it should be tied to a mule. However, the author's observation leads him to believe that most of those who seriously overshoot themselves do so at the suggestion of others rather than from their own feelings and judgment. Ambitious parents and close relatives want family traditions maintained, or the family name honored, and insist upon the effort to satisfy their desires at any cost. 

It is a lamentable fact that when parents or close relatives definitely choose a vocation for one, they usually choose what they want him to be, rather than what they think his talents fit him to be. They make no scientific analysis. They choose social station, monetary rewards, family convenience, or family tradition. 

The wise parent does not decide for his child. He studies him, helps him to analyze himself, assists him in gathering information, and tries to guide him in the use of it, but he leaves the final choice to the child and does not urge him to a premature decision.

The discreet parent who sees his child about to make a serious mistake will use diplomacy to prevent it. He will advise delay so as to gain time. He will then counsel him as wisely as he can, get others to advise him, and will furnish all the information he can that may lead to a wiser choice. The author bears personal testimony to the wisdom of this course. A wise father thus guided him away from a bad choice in his own immature boyhood. 

2. Those Who Underestimate

Some overestimate their possibilities, but by far the

greater number underestimate them. One sees Jones and 

Smith, successful men, and fancies that they are possessed of rare genius, while he, John Dumb, is poor in gifts. He has difficulties with his arithmetic while they are solving the problems of the universe. He judges himself by comparing his own awkward beginnings with the finished work of age and experience. When it is too late to begin again, he will find that the degree of success among men is wholly out of proportion to the difference in their native ability. 

3. Measuring by Comparison 

If you would know your possibilities for success in a vocation, select a number of persons who have succeeded in it and measure yourself by them. Study their lives. or biographies, and learn what you can of their youth and beginnings. How do their school grades, their difficulties, their failures and successes compare with yours at the same age? What opportunities did they have, what jobs, and what did they do with them? How do their experiences compare with yours? What were their ideals and prospects, their outlook upon life at your age? 

What handicaps or what special advantages did they have? Did they have doubts, fears, and heartaches such as yours?

In comparing one's self with others, due allowance must be made for prejudices and proximity. Even a prophet is without honor among his neighbors, because they see but common clay in his weaknesses which proximity magnifies, while at a distance only his greatness appears and men crown him with a halo. 

4. Evaluating Training and Preparation 

In taking your measure by comparing yourself with other men, due consideration must be given to the effect of training or circumstances upon their lives and upon yours. It is as common for men to undervalue training and environment as it is for them to underestimate their abilities.

Multitudes of parents underestimate what training and preparation will do for their children. The children themselves make the same mistake. Many drop out of grammar school in the grades. A large per cent of those who enter high school do not finish, and only forty per cent of those who finish high school enter college, while fewer than sixteen per cent of those who enter college graduate. Still fewer go on and take special training to fit themselves more fully for their careers. 

It would appear from this universal neglect of preparation that the majority of young people aim at the lower rather than at the higher levels in their vocations. Many are not capacitated to reach the higher levels, but the thorough training of any individual for any vocation will greatly raise the level and success he may attain in his vocation. The proper training of all will return dividends on the investment. 

5. Evaluating Opportunities

It is quite as important for one to take the true measure of his opportunities as it is for him properly to evaluate his talents and training. Yet he is a rare individual who recognizes the full value of his opportunities as they come to him. We depreciate those we have and magnify the ones we want. We see Anderson and Rogers doing great things, but we think they had greater opportunities and superior advantages. Ours are insignificant and trivial.

How happy are those who early take their true measure, who choose wisely their vocations and make careful and sufficient preparation for them; who recognize their opportunities, improve them, and chart a true course! 

From the ranks of this chosen few come the successful and the great. This is the mountain range of humanity from which rise the peaks that are seen across the ages.

In a very few occupations are they absolutely necessary. A blind man could never be a taxi-driver; neither could a deaf woman be a telephone operator, but in both kinds of business there are related jobs that may well be filled by the blind or deaf.

It may require time, effort, strength, and means that could be spent more profitably upon something else. 

8. Changing the Physical Characteristics 

Nearly every point in the physical chart is subject to modification at the will of the individual. Even the two

apparent exceptions, age and size, can be affected to a limited extent. One may age prematurely, or extend his youth in appearance and spirit several years. Size can be increased or diminished as to weight, while any disadvantage of stature can be overcome in a degree by cultivating other qualities as strength, activity, personal appearance, and personality.

All of the physical characteristics can be weakened, even destroyed, by neglect or abuse. Bill Jackson, an old sot, is an example. Through dissipation he reduced his size, aged prematurely, lost his strength, wrecked his health, slowed down his activity, became unsteady in action and nerve, weakened his endurance, eyesight and hearing, thickened his tongue, and marred his personal appearance. He lowered his profile at every point on the chart.

In contrast with Bill Jackson, consider Theodore Roosevelt. As a youth he was so weak in body and health that his parents put him under a private tutor, thinking him too frail to attend the public schools. He was small in size, weak in health and strength, weak in endurance and eyesight, and of a delicate, nervous constitution, yet with grit and persistence he overcame his weaknesses and became remarkably strong and robust. 

He raised his physical profile at nearly all of the points on the chart.

The smooth, natural functioning of every part of the body is far more important to one's success and happiness than are some special physical characteristics as size, strength, and muscular activity. One takes his car to the garage for the mechanic to examine frequently. Likewise one should take his hu-

I. MEASURING ONE'S Bow 

There are two very important questions, among others, that each one must answer in making a personal analysis. For what vocation do my talents best fit me? To what level in that vocation am I capacitated to rise?

The first question calls for an analysis of one's aptitudes, the last for the measurement of one's strength. 

The last question is the more easily answered and will be discussed first. No detailed psychological study nor critical analysis of the mind will be attempted. Such would be out of place here. We can give only a few broad hints on a few of those factors that most vitally affect one's success in the vocations. 

1. Intelligence and the Emotions

By intelligence we mean one's power to think, to learn. It is common sense, mother wit, the sum total of one's inherited powers for perceiving relationships and forming judgments. By the emotions we mean that group of mental qualities or personality factors which furnish the motivity, the driving power in life. Intelli- [ 40 ]

gence is the engine, the emotional qualities are the steam which drives it; or the intellectual capacity is the motor, and the emotions are the electric current. A pony engine with a high steam pressure can accomplish much, while a mogul with little pressure can do nothing. 

2. Amount of Intelligence 

Intelligence is described by such terms as much, little, high, low, inferior, superior, and intermediate expressions. 

We classify individuals on the basis of intelligence, as idiots, imbeciles, morons, feeble-minded, normal, superior, very superior, and geniuses. Occupations vary in the amount of intelligence required for success in them. 

Furthermore, most vocations have divisions or levels that require different degrees of intelligence. For example, take a machine shop. A moron may mop the floors. One of low average intelligence can be a handy man to move material. The average man may be a helper, the man of high average may be a mechanic, while superior intelligence is required to build and manage the business. 

3. Measuring Intelligence

All of us want to know exactly how smart we are, but unfortunately there is no way of finding out with certainty. There are no absolute standards of quantities or degrees. The measurements are all of a relative or comparative nature; that is, we must say that John is as intelligent as James, more intelligent than Henry, but not so smart as William. But how smart are Henry, James, and William? They are measured in comparison with somebody else who in turn may be measured by John. In addition to the fact that standards are of a relative nature, there is also a large element of error and uncertainty in all of the methods of measurement. 

4. Methods of Measurement 

Four methods of measurement are being used, all of which have value, but no one taken by itself gives conclusive results. The four methods are: (1) Intelligence tests; (2) School grades, and other performance tests

(experience); (3) Interest in vocations; (4) Opinion of acquaintances. (1) Intelligence tests-The general intelligence tests had their beginning with a French psychologist, Alfred Binet, in 1905. He conceived the idea of measuring intelligence by what it does. Others had been trying to find out what it is and to measure it by analytical processes. 

Other psychologists have followed Binet's methods and have improved them until they are now supposed to have a fair degree of reliability. They are all based upon the idea that two persons of equal intelligence will meet a practical problem in a similar way; that a certain amount of intelligence is required to solve one problem and another degree of intelligence is required to solve a more complicated one.

Intelligence tests have a very limited use in vocational self-guidance, so that little space is given to the subject here. For fuller discussions, the reader is referred to any good modern psychology. (2) Performance-Performance is the most conclusive evidence of one's ability to achieve. You ask, "Can John do this?" It is answered, "He has done it," or "He has done something like it," and there is no further question. Performance of difficult tasks is an evidence of superior ability.

The youth who uniformly does all tasks well with little prompting and a minimum of instruction gives evidence of superior ability, while the one who requires much instruction, much prompting and guidance, and yet uniformly does poor work, perhaps lacks ability. Such one who manifests ability but who cannot be trusted to do good work without constant oversight and goading lacks character, energy, ambition, or self-directive ability, and is headed toward failure in life. It is from observing our performance that our friends and enemies judge us. 

The great difficulty of differentiating general ability and performance is the lack of standards of measurement. 

School grades-School work constitutes a performance

333335 test. The grades are supposed to show what one has really done under known conditions in competition with others. They, therefore, offer a basis of comparison with a large group and over definite periods of time. 

They have long been considered an important measure of intellectual ability and a valuable basis for forecasting success in life. The chief difficulty in using school grades as a standard of measurement is the fact that they may be influenced by so many other factors. The supposedly "known conditions" are not always known, therefore they may mean much or little as to intelligence. 

When one would measure himself by his school record he should consider his rank in all classes to see if there is a fairly constant tendency for him to be in the same position in his classes as in upper third, lower third, or middle third, throughout his school career. If there is such a tendency, the presumption is very strong that he will maintain this rank in society when he leaves school. 

One should study the factors that influence his grades, and make proper allowance for them. He should look ahead and judge the probability of these factors being changed for better or for worse in the future. (3) Interest in vocations-One's interest in vocations is a measure of intelligence. This seems strange at first thought. Yet an abiding passionate interest in any vocation is a fair indication of one's ability to achieve some degree of success in it, provided this interest is based upon a knowledge of the nature of the work. The mind can be interested only in what it is able to grasp. Also, interest in a subject usually requires that there shall be some effort required in the grasping. Thus a moron cannot be interested in law or medicine, nor a superior man in doing the moron's work. Neither can a man of low average intelligence be interested in engineering except in doing some of the mechanical work connected with it. The probabilities are about five to one that the boy who has a passionate desire to be a physician, for example, and whose interest deepens as his knowledge

of the profession increases, is capacitated for success in that profession. (4) The opinion of acquaintances-We intuitively pass some judgment upon those we meet, and they in turn judge us. These individual judgments are often wrong, but the combined judgment of a group is fairly reliable. A few acquaintances may think we are inferior, or very superior, and miss the mark; but if our acquaintances in general agree in putting us in a class, the probabilities are very great that we belong there. 

Our chief difficulty is in finding out how they class us. 

One way of interpreting the judgment of our friends is in observing the tasks they set for us. In our infancy and youth we are stimulated to greater efforts by the encouragement of parents and friends. Our confidence in ourselves is strengthened by their faith in us, or weakened by their discouraging remarks. Thus it is that through life the public asks us to do only what it thinks we can do well. It thus reveals its judgment of our ability. An interesting exercise may be had in any class by securing group judgment by means of question cards. 

5. Summary of the Methods of Measurement 

Each of the four methods of measuring intelligence has some value and a large element of uncertainty. It is not very significant for one to make a high or low score in any one of them, but when two of these agree it is significant, and when three agree there is little probability of error. For example, if a student should rate low on intelligence tests but high in grades, in interests, and in the judgment of friends, the probabilities are that the intelligence test failed in his case. If one should make low grades but rate high in the other tests, one would conclude that the low grades are not due to lack of ability and would look for other causes. If one should rate low on intelligence tests, low in grades, but fair to high in interests and in the opinion of friends, one might conclude that the intelligence of such one is low but that he rates high in personality. If one's intelligence

quotient is high, grades high, interests high, and is highly rated by his friends, there is no room for doubt. The student is of superior intelligence and should make his life plans large.

II. FINDING ONE'S SPECIAL APTITUDES 

Old writers on vocational choice had much to say about "square pegs in round holes." It was thought that each boy was created with certain characteristics and aptitudes that qualified him specially for some one vocation and if he failed to locate in it that his life was doomed to failure. This old idea has been greatly modified.

It is now believed that the mind functions as a unit and that it may be so directed by will and interest as to succeed in many kinds of work, some of them differing greatly from others in character. However, the truth still remains that most of us can succeed better in some vocations than in others, are better adapted to them, have more interest in them, and will be happier in them than in others. This leaves ample room and incentive for choice.

Searching for one's special vocational aptitudes presents an interesting exercise. It is uncertain because, as in measuring intelligence, there are no fixed standards or methods of testing that eliminate all elements of error and doubt. Some aptitude tests have been developed, but they are mostly mechanical and are still in the experimental stage. A few "clues" are offered here with the hope that they may be suggestive and give some help in this great and interesting search. 

1. Likes and Dislikes of Work as Clues 

Take a look at your inner feelings-a personal introspection. What are your likes and dislikes of work in general? Do you get more joy in dealing with people or with materials? Do you prefer to spend most of your time with people, or do you seem to get more satisfaction

in working with things either alone or in company with others who are also working with materials? 

Are you mechanically inclined, interested in making things, in watching construction work, in studying the structure of buildings and furniture, and in the mechanism of machinery, or do such things give you only a passing interest with little desire to engage in such work? 

If the former, follow up the clue by making a study of the many vocations listed in books under the heads of mechanics, crafts, and maintaining occupations. 

Are you commercially inclined? Do you like to trade and traffic? Do you especially enjoy meeting and dealing with strangers? Are you fond of studying things with reference to the use others can make of them, and do you have pleasure in explaining them to people? If so, follow up this clue by studying the various commercial vocations that you may know or can find discussed in vocational books. 

Do you like to impress the minds of folks and bring them to your way of thinking? Are you philosophical and do you love to dwell in the realm of thought, either in philosophical fields or in science? Do you get your greatest intellectual pleasure in searching for truths and in making their application to man's physical, spiritual, social, or industrial needs? If so, follow up this clue by studying all of the professions you may find discussed. 

However, it should be remembered that success in most of the professions demands more than average intelligence. 

2. Pleasure in Work a Clue 

One gets pleasure in exercising any strong talent, capacity, or gift. This fact presents a clue to assist one in self-analysis, also in choosing a vocation. The one with constructive talent loves to build or create and, all else being equal, would be happy in a vocation which constantly uses this talent. The one with artistic talent delights in exercising it and, to be happy, must have that opportunity, either in vocation or in avocation. The one

strongly practical by nature delights in occupations that are full of emergencies, that have many changes in conditions, different people or things to be dealt with, or variety in environment. He chafes under rules, customs, and precedents. On the other hand, the one in whom the mathematical or mechanical type is dominant is happier in vocations that are most characterized by rules, customs, and precedents. He is annoyed by changes, interruptions, and emergencies, and so tries to avoid those occupations that are subject to many irregularities.

The one in whom the reflective characteristic is strongest loves to meditate and to reason, consequently is happier in those vocations which require the exercise of these processes, as law, ministry, research, and so forth. He would be irritated in a vocation which constantly demands decision without time for reflection. 

3. School Subjects as Clues 

One's preference for school subjects has long been considered an indication of vocational ability. Our modern courses of study represent the composite thinking and experience of school men in their effort to provide the training young people need for success in life. If a student possesses interest and aptitude for the studies required in a certain vocation, it should follow that he has some aptitude for that vocation. But one's interest in a school subject may be influenced by many other things, as the teacher, the classmates, the hour of recitation, former prejudices, the room in which the class meets, or one's previous preparation for the study. All of these must be discounted. Furthermore, one needs experience in more than one branch of a subject to enable him to judge his aptitudes in that field. The fact that one does not like English grammar is no evidence that he likes literary talent. The fact that he likes practical arithmetic is not much proof that he will like higher mathematics or that he has talent for engineering. But these are clues, however slight, and if followed up may

lead to something decisive. They are conclusive in extreme cases only. (1) Mathematics-The knowledge of some branches of mathematics, or the principles and methods of thinking in mathematics, are required in a major degree in the following vocations: 

Engineering, mechanics, manufacturing, accounting, surveying, astronomy, architecture, and all vocations requiring logical processes of reasoning and the knowledge of mathematical principles. (2) Literature and languages-The knowledge of literature and of languages, together with the mental processes of mastering them, are needed to some extent in every profession, especially in all vocations at the higher levels, but they are essential in a high degree in: 

Editorial and other literary pursuits, teaching, ministry, law, salesmanship, advertising, and so forth. (3) Fine arts-The school courses and the vocations in the fine arts are so closely associated that most of the studies are merely the elementary stages of the work in the vocations so that success in one is success in the other. (4) Sociological group (Religion, history, psychology sociology, education, and ethics). A liberal knowledge of the large body of information represented by the subjects in this group is required in a major degree in: 

The ministry, all religious work, teaching, law, social work, political vocations, medicine, journalism, statesmanship, library, editorial and other literary work, homemaking, and so forth. (5) Sciences-A knowledge of some of the sciences and of the scientific method of approach to truth is required in a major degree in:

Medicine, agriculture, stock-raising, and all of the scientific pursuits, all technical vocations such as engineering, mechanics, certain manufacturing, technology, and so forth.

4. Qualities of Mind 

The mind must manifest itself in some way, and in the discussion which follows, these manifestations are called qualities. They might be called traits, types, temperaments, or characteristics. Some of these qualities are so distinct and positive as to be important in qualifying for certain vocations. There are many mental qualities, but only a few are discussed here: (1) The artistic quality-One in whom this quality of mind is dominant more than the average person is sensitive to the delicate and the refined. Such one naturally appreciates and craves music, beauty, harmony, and refinement. In school this one would find delight in music and art studies, literature, dramatics, languages, and kindred subjects.

The artistic mind is required in a high degree or as a major in the following vocations:

Music, art, poetry, oratory, sculpture, decoration, costume designing, landscape gardening, architecture, certain businesses as art vendors, florists, milliners, jewelers, and so forth.

The presence of the artistic quality is required but in a lesser degree, or as a minor in all vocations that require a fine sense of the beautiful, good taste, refinement, and culture, as: 

Horticulture, dry goods, drugs, orcharding, ministry, teaching, law, homemaking, fancy poultry-raising, dressmaking, tailoring, and so forth. (2) The constructive quality-The constructive mind is imaginative. It is one that creates new forms, new plans. Those who possess this quality in a high degree together with superior intelligence are the builders, the creators, the pioneers in all lines of human endeavor. 

However, this and all other qualities of mind may be possessed in all degrees at all levels of intelligence, so that the possession of any quality in a high degree does

not qualify one for large success unless it is accompanied by a high degree of intelligence. 

The constructive mind is required in a major degree in: 

Manufacturing, carpentry, law, surveying, architecture, agriculture, some teaching, music in the higher levels, especially composing, art and painting (except as copyists), costume designing and dressmaking, dentistry, surgery, poetry, oratory, all mechanical and engineering pursuits, and all occupations requiring imaginative and creative thinking. It is a requirement of all successful leadership. 

The occupations that do not require construction in a high degree are those on the lower levels in which the workers follow established rules, follow orders, copy models, or do the same exercises over and over in the same way. This group includes:

The blind alley jobs, clerkships, ordinary bookkeeping, minor secretarial jobs, minor mechanical, machine operating, and labor jobs. (3) The scientific quality-The one in whom this quality predominates is curious, inquiring, searching, exacting in disposition. He craves new facts and new thoughts. He wants to know the why and how of things. 

He is not bound by precedent and is ready to break with the past. He looks to the future and when not well balanced may easily become radical. The occupations requiring the scientific mind in a major degree are: 

Scientific research, medicine, pharmacy, science-teaching, all scientific pursuits, detective and police work, engineering, agriculture, stock-raising, homemaking, exploring, and all vocations in which gathering and classifying information is an important factor of success.

The scientific mind is not required in a very great degree in those vocations that are more or less static, that are governed by rules, precedents, and customs, such as: 

Government, municipal, county, and other official positions, most civil service positions, small banking, small busi-

nesses, and all occupations listed above as requiring little constructive ability. (4) The practical quality-The practical is the resourceful mind. It adjusts to situations, it readily invents ways of meeting emergencies. It follows rules when they serve, and breaks them when circumstances seem to require it. It is the perceptive mind, which means it is quick to see the facts of a situation, and quick in forming a judgment from them. It is required as a major in:

Larger businesses, medicine, agriculture, housekeeping, engineering, salesmanship on the higher levels, all executive positions, exploring, purchasing agents, speculators, pastor of larger churches, farming, inventing, and all positions calling for much initiative.

This quality is required in varying degrees in all vocations in which one has any responsibility to meet sudden changes in conditions. (5) The reflective mind-This is the logical, reasoning, meditative type of mind. It may be contrasted with the practical mind; one, the practical, seems to see the facts at a glance; the other must reach them by reasoning processes. One sees the point and reaches a conclusion from a few details; the other, the reflective, must have more time and lots of details. Some vocations require that one be strong in both, that he must take in a situation quickly, gather facts rapidly, and then deal with them carefully and logically. The reflective mind is required as a major in:

Law, philosophy, ministry, all engineering pursuits, medicine, surgery, scientific pursuits, poetry, and prose literature, college teaching, astronomy, and other mechanical pursuits.

This quality is required in some degree in every occupation that involves responsibility for any important independent thinking.

8. Put the results of all of the tests together and judge the results. Are you satisfied with the rating?

It is assumed that the student at this point has already gathered a fund of information about the vocations in which he is interested and is now ready to choose among them. It is also assumed that he has studied himself and has a fairly well-defined idea of his capacities and aptitudes. He has also, it is assumed, chosen his master and fixed his ideals. This being true, he is now ready to study the application of his self-analysis to the analyses of the vocations before him. 

I. SEEKING DIVINE GUIDANCE 

The earnest, serious-minded student hesitates when he comes face to face with making a definite choice of vocation or of a partner. It is too much like making a long leap in the dark. There is a sense of fear. A mistake can be costly. This recognized need of help should impel one to seek assistance from God. The servant should go to his master for orders or counsel. Now, if one has decided to make God his master and to serve him as suggested in the beginning of this book, he should go to God for guidance in the selection of the service to be rendered, of the place for service, and of the partners in that service.

II. SOME GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF CHOOSING 

The following is a list of some of the more important general principles that should guide one in making his final choice of a vocation: 

1. A Fundamental Principle 

One's vocation should give him pleasure as well as profit. His choice is a failure if he is not happy in it. [57]

2. Important Factors 

The following factors contribute to one's happiness in his vocation: (1) Aptitudes for the labor involved-One gets pleasure in exercising his strongest qualities. (2) The character and quality of the service rendered -One cannot have lasting satisfaction in doing work that he feels is not worth while, no matter what the remuneration may be. One's greatest joy and happiness come from the consciousness of doing a fine quality of work that renders a real service. (3) Personal development-One wants to grow. He recoils from any occupation that deadens or dwarfs. The attraction of many poorly paying jobs is that they offer opportunity of personal development. (4) Personal enjoyment-Personal satisfaction through social standing, the companionship and esteem of others, pleasant environment, congenial atmosphere, and material comforts; healthful surroundings; financial rewards; political, social, or business advancement. 

3. Motivation

In making a choice, one will be motivated by one or more of the above-named factors. The importance one attaches to the different factors will often determine his choice. If he is moved most strongly by the desire to make money, he will reject those vocations that do not offer such reward. If he is moved by the desire to render a sacrificial service, he may do as did a young man who, refusing an offer of ten thousand dollars a year, accepted a bare living in China as a medical missionary. 

It is at this point that great souls emerge from the common level and rise to sublime heights, while selfish ones sink and live low, sordid lives. It was here that Jesus rebuked the devil who offered him conquered worlds, while Pilate, to gain the applause of a small multitude, condemned the Christ and crucified his own conscience. 

It was here that Washington refused to be king and was

crowned the father of his country, while Napoleon sought the crown of Europe and died a prisoner in St. Helena. 

4. Build upon Strong Points

One should choose with reference to his dominant characteristics and his opportunities for preparation and for entering the vocation. Why should one spend his energy, time, or means building up his weaknesses for foundations when nature or previous training has already given him strong foundations upon which to build? 

5. Consider Handicaps

In choosing a vocation, one should carefully consider the effect of any handicaps he may have. Why should a stammering man try to lecture, a cripple seek to be a policeman, or a man color-blind strive to be a locomotive engineer? 

6. Be Deliberate

One's choice should be made deliberately. It is important enough for careful, thorough investigation, even for considerable expense in gathering information. 

7. Decide Early 

One's vocational decision should be made early in life, but it can be made too early. One may choose from impulse, or upon the suggestion of another, and go wrong. 

He may be over-persuaded in his choice by parents or friends and end in disaster. He may choose before mental or physical maturity and later develop superior capacity for a definite vocation. But after making proper allowance for all rashness and undue influence, the fact remains that the choice should be made long before the period for education is ended, certainly by the close of the freshman year in college and preferably by the close of the junior or senior year in high school. It is wasteful of time and money for one to secure a college education aimlessly, to choose afterward, and then have to go back and take subjects that were omitted, or else have to enter the vocation handicapped for lack of special training

that might just as easily and cheaply have been acquired while the aimless process was going on.

An early choice makes it possible for one to plan his education for his vocation. It gives motivation and pur- pose to the study and mastery of the required school subjects. One will be much more interested in chemistry if he knows he will need it in the study of medicine or in some industrial laboratory. He will have delight in his mathematics if he knows he will need it in calculating the wind pressure on a skyscraper that he may be called upon to plan or to build. 

The Bible becomes a different study when one chooses a religious vocation. Sociology has a new interest after one decides to do social work, and logic is no longer impossible when one realizes he will need the mastery of these mental processes in the solution of a great law problem later.

Thus, an early definite vocational objective will add zest and inspiration to one's school work, and will give point, purpose, and interest to much in early life that otherwise would be irksome. It is better to adjust one's course of study to his chosen vocation than to adjust one's vocation in after life to his former course of study. 

Caution-Any one with intelligence rating above average makes a mistake to begin specializing in school courses too early. Such persons (there is doubt about this rule for those below high average rating) should secure a liberal education first, and specialize afterward, for three reasons: First, for the breadth of culture and the foundation such training gives for larger success in one's vocation. Second, for the ease with which one may make any adjustments that time and circumstances may require. Third, for the larger social service one will be enabled to render his community. 

8. The Instability of Vocations 

Vocations are not static but are ever changing. The wise man considers this in making a choice. Is there a future for the vocation? If it should go out of existence

or cease to be desirable, would the experience in it prepare you for another, or would it leave you stranded? 

9. Possibilities for Promotion

One wants to make progress. If he has above-average ambition and ability, he cannot be satisfied to remain upon the entrance level of his vocation. Is promotion possible in the vocation, or may it be secured through changing to higher allied vocations as from clerk to manager, to partnership; or, as from helper to mechanic, to foreman, to superintendent; or, as a teacher to principal, to superintendent; or, as a lawyer to law-maker, to judgeship, to an executive position? 

10. Possibilities of Changing to Another Vocation 

This is the "stepping-stone" idea, and is closely related to numbers eight and nine above. One may enter one vocation as a means of getting into another, as becoming a private secretary to the president of a corporation with the idea of advancing to a managerial or departmental position. Sometimes it may be a process of training as was the case of John W. Thomas, Jr., the son of a former president of the Nashville, Chattanooga, and St. Louis

Railroad. He joined a section gang as a day laborer to familiarize himself with road-building. He went into the shops, designed and built a locomotive and ran it on the road as an engineer. Then he served as assistant in his father's office. He did all of this to prepare himself for the presidency of the road, and succeeded both in securing the presidency and in executing the duties of his office.

III. METHODS OF PROCEDURE IN CHOOSING 

The mental processes in choosing are simple and natural. They are spoken of here as steps. First, elimination; second, comparison; third, experience. 

1. First Step in Making the Choice-Elimination 

(1) Elimination on basis of interest-It is assumed that the student has studied the broad divisions of oc-

cupations sufficiently to know in what field his choice will probably lie. He should know whether to consider the trades or the professions, or whether he would prefer manufacturing or merchandising.

To illustrate the process of elimination on the basis of interest, let one start with some outline of vocations. 

That given in the Book of Opportunities (see Bibliography in the Appendix) is recommended. One looks over the list in the first division, Industries, and sees nothing that attracts him. He has already made a study of a few representatives of this group, let us say fishing, oil drilling, and mining. He has had a little experience in some others-farming, logging, and carpentering-but he has found no interest in any of them. In fact, he feels an aversion to many in the group. 

Next, he studies the list under Transportation. He finds he has some interest in Air and Highways. He reads several articles on these subjects and talks with men engaged in these vocations. He finds his interest confirmed, so he enters these two on his list of temporary choices for further investigation. He deals with all of the other groups in a similar manner. At the conclusion of this part of the study, he will have eliminated all but a small number of scattered vocations. We shall call this 

List A. He will now proceed to narrow this list by the other processes. (2) Elimination on the basis of physical capacities a. Complete handicaps (a) Some handicaps close the door-There are some occupations that have certain absolute physical requirements. These are automatically eliminated from consideration by those who cannot meet the requirements. 

For example, many vocations are positively closed to blind people; others are closed to the deaf, and some are impossible for those who cannot walk. If these handicaps eliminate any on List A, check them off and you will now have Revised List A. (b) Interest may be capitalized-A vocation may re-

main a source of interest and inspiration to one, even though it be closed to him. The author knew a small boy who was passionately fond of baseball and whose one ambition was to be a ball-player. Infantile paralysis hopelessly crippled him so that he could never play ball, but in a measure he satisfied his passion for the game by becoming a great fan and a successful sports-writer. (c) Handicaps may be capitalized-Many a man has been elected to a county office because he was a cripple. 

Edison considered his deafness an advantage to him. 

Henry Ford, it is reported, made a survey of his factories to find work for handicapped people. Many such jobs were found and were filled by cripples with profit to the organization. For instance, he found that a blind person was more efficient in sorting bolts, nuts, and screws than one who could see, and that a man with no legs would do more work at sitting tasks than one who could walk. b. Partial or doubtful elimination-One is often in doubt as to whether his handicap is sufficient to defeat him in his chosen work. In such cases he should seek all possible help from reading and from interviews. Seek information and counsel from those with similar handicaps and from those engaged in the desired occupations who are not so handicapped. Weigh all of the facts and advice, count the cost, balance the cost against the advantages of some other vocation in which the handicaps do not count so heavily, and then decide. 

Consider possibilities for preparation-Having chosen a vocation, one should make the best preparation possible for entering it. The possibilities and opportunity for making such preparation are important factors that must be considered in making a choice. 

It is not sufficiently known that the United States Government in co-operation with the Departments of Education in the various states is offering material assistance to physically handicapped adults in training for vocations. These departments have also gathered much in-

formation on vocations that are open to cripples. A letter to your state department of education should put you in touch with this information. (3) Elimination on the basis of mental capacity- Mental capacity automatically shuts out many vocations. 

The moron, or one of low average intelligence, should not be encouraged to prepare for a vocation in which much intelligence is necessary, while the one with a superior mind should not be content with a job that requires only low mental activity. a. Vocations classified on the basis of capacity required -Based upon the mental capacity required, vocations may be classified as follows: (a) Those which one of very limited mentality may perform when directed by those of more intelligence 

Hoeing, ditch-digging, handling lumber, running errands, driving teams, and the simpler common labor jobs. (b) Those which may be performed by persons of only low average intelligence if properly directed The simpler forms of manuel labor, truck-driving, domestic service, minor clerking, delivery, agricultural labor, lumbering labor, standardized factory work, cleaning, polishing, loading, petty shop-keeping, and so forth.

The workers who are restricted to work on this level; that is, those who are unable to do work above this level and need guidance here, constitute the group whose capacity lies between the moron and the average mind. 

Only a few of this group do as much as two years of work in high school. Most of them fail to finish the eighth grade. They must always work under the direction of those who will think for them. A few will succeed in managing a small business of their own, but the vast majority must work at wage and blind alley jobs that do not lead to promotion or to anything higher. 

They can succeed in such jobs, can render valuable service to humanity, and should be encouraged to be

content and to find happiness in such work. These and those of the preceding group are often found upon government relief rolls. (c) Those requiring at least average intelligence- The workers who are restricted to vocations on this level range from a little below to a little above what we call average intelligence. One of average intelligence should make average grades on intelligence tests and low to fair grades in grammar and high school. Some of those on this level go to college, but most who go fail or at best make only low passing grades.

The workers classified in the lower half of this group level may succeed in jobs ranging from semi-skilled to the lower skilled labor. They may succeed as:

Small business men, minor clerks, tenant farmers, laborers in standardized factory work, street car conductors, locomotive firemen, brakemen, lumbermen, barbers, printers, carpenters, butchers, common bricklayers, common painters, linemen, seamstresses, hairdressers, cooks, truck drivers, and so forth.

Of course, many of superior intelligence are engaged in these occupations and ordinarily do a much better quality of work in any of them than those who are restricted to this level.

Those workers classified in the upper half of this intelligence group can succeed:

In skilled mechanical trades (less technical), in small businesses, in millinery, elementary teaching, and as small farmers, salesmen, policemen, foremen, musicians, photographers, postal clerks, shoemakers, plumbers, tailors, boilermakers, station agents, express agents, auto mechanics, assistant librarians; in fact as assistants in most vocations except requiring high professional qualifications.

Other factors-One must keep in mind that other factors besides mental capacity are highly important in determining one's success or failure in a vocation. For

example, one a little below average intelligence but full of enthusiasm, interest, and energy, with ambition and self-reliance will succeed above those of higher intelligence who are not so well endowed with these qualities. (d) Those requiring superior intelligence-The workers belonging to this group range from average to superior mental ability. Most of this group, in this generation, will finish high school and college. They make fair to excellent grades in their school work and many of them are found in the upper half of their classes. 

On the whole, they constitute the middle third of their classes in high school and college, and the great middle class in society. They may succeed as:

Lawyers, doctors, farmers, writers, business men, high school teachers, managers, minor executives, preachers, the better homemakers, dentists, insurance men, salesmen, librarians, chemists, auto mechanics, airmen, private secretaries, nurses, social workers, draftsmen, bankers, journalists-middle-class leaders in all lines. (e) Those requiring very superior ability―This is the high professional level. The workers in this group range from superior to the very highest intellectual ability. 

They make A-grades in college. They are the honor students and constitute the Who's Who class. This is the group having intelligence for research, original and creative work; or, in a word, this group includes those who work at the top of their vocations and includes the world's thinkers. However, not all in the group are marked by all of these abilities, nor can all who succeed in some of the vocations listed on this level be said to have this very superior intelligence. The exceptions are accounted for by the influence of other factors such as emotional elements, opportunities, and special circumstances.

The following workers may be listed as requiring intelligence on this level:

Editors, college professors, college executives, city superintendents, railroad and other business executives, accountants, industrial and other engineers, technical experts, big contractors, diplomats, statesmen, judges of the higher courts, army officers from colonel upward, higher naval officers, preachers in the larger pulpits, corporation lawyers, surgeons, and experts at all higher levels. (4) Elimination on the basis of opportunity- After a small group of vocations has successfully run the gauntlet of all the above processes of elimination, each still must pass the test of opportunity. It is quite possible for one to be physically and mentally capacitated to be successful in the vocation in which he is most interested, and yet have no possible chance of following it. a. Wisdom and faith required-It takes a great deal of faith and wisdom properly to eliminate vocations on the basis of opportunity. Who has no opportunity, or, having none, cannot make one? When and under what conditions must a vocation be eliminated for lack of opportunity to enter it? Many boys, for example, have wanted to study medicine, who were in every way fitted for the profession, but on account of the cost, threw up their hands and said "impossible," and thus eliminated it, while a host of other boys with less money pressed their preparation to completion, entered the profession, and succeeded in it.

History is so replete with the stories of boys with no chance of boys with apparently impossible handicaps who overcame them, who literally created opportunities where none existed, that one is almost tempted to say there is no necessity of eliminating any vocation for lack of opportunity for entering it. b. There are limits-However, after full allowance is made for the possibility of making opportunities, limits may still remain beyond which the individual cannot go, or at least should not try to go in considering a vocation.

c. Counting the cost-Before one undertakes the expensive task of creating opportunities, or of overcoming impossible handicaps, it is well to count the cost, weigh the probable chances of success, and estimate the value of victory. Are one's capacities and other advantages for this vocation such as to make success certain when the handicaps are overcome? Is success in the vocation worth the cost of time, effort, and sacrifice necessary to attain it? Is this the only vocation he can enter, and is this his only hope of a successful life? d. Who should attempt the impossible?-Hannibal crossed the Alps because he was blocked from crossing the sea. The prize was Rome and Italy, and there was no other way of reaching it. Furthermore, he could afford the hazardous attempt because of his surpassing genius for achieving the impossible. One of lesser ability would have failed as hosts of others have failed. 

Only a Hannibal can cross the Alps; only a Lindbergh can fly to Paris; only the eagle should attempt to soar to the sun; only a genius should hitch his wagon to a star.

It is doubtful if any except those of superior ability should be encouraged to undertake a vocation with any very serious handicap unless the circumstances are very unusual, and then it should be undertaken only after much thought and investigation, after consulting the best counsel available, and after much prayer. 

2. Second Step in Making the Choice-Comparison 

If, after one has applied all of the processes of elimination, he still has a few vocations left on his list, he must now choose from these. The very fact that they have survived the process of elimination is evidence that he is fitted for a measure of success in each, but which one is best for him is his question now. This calls for a critical and comparative restudy of all of them. There is probably no better way to do this than to place complete outlines of the vocations side by side and compare them point by point with one's personal outlines. Ex-

pert advice may be needed on some points. Really it is at this stage that the counselor can render his best service. 

3. Third Step in Making the Choice-Experience 

The final and supreme test of one's fitness for a vocation is experience in it. This is the trial and error plan. 

It is old and sometimes wasteful, but it must be resorted to when all other efforts fail. However, it is often possible for one to try out different vocations on week-ends, summer vacations, as a supply, or as a temporary proposition. This is a wise course to pursue, provided one does it seriously and studies each vocation earnestly while engaged in it. Otherwise, it may encourage drifting.

Oftentimes when one has difficulty in choosing among a few vocations that have passed all the elimination and comparison tests, he should enter the vocation that seems most convenient and promising, and indulge in one or more of the others as avocations, and then let experience, time, and opportunity settle the matter for him.

CHAPTER V CHOOSING A BUSINESS PARTNER INTRODUCTION 

This book is an attempt, the reader is reminded, to help young people answer four great questions: Whom shall I serve? What service shall I render? Who shall help me in that service? Where shall I serve?

The first two questions have been discussed, and we now come to the third: the choice of a partner. Two kinds of partners may be chosen: a life mate and a business or professional partner. Not every one will choose either. There are some who will venture alone. Most people will or should choose a mate, and many will face the question of choosing a partner in business. 

The author was teaching a class in vocational selfguidance, having to feel his way, making his own course, because none had been worked out for him, and no suitable text had been written. In the development of that course, the problem of choosing a business partner arose. Many members of the class would soon face this question, and they needed some lessons on the fundamentals involved just as they needed them in the choice of a vocation.

On looking for help and being unable to find any that was satisfactory, except on the legal phases, the author turned to his friends in Rotary International, who were much interested in boys. 

A large number of business and professional men in various parts of the United States and in Canada were addressed concerning the problem. Each was asked to imagine his son coming to him with this question of a partner, and that he write the boy a good heart-to- [72]

heart letter, giving him his very best counsel, and then to send the letter to the author.

The response was splendid. This chapter is largely a digest of the replies. It therefore represents the best thought of some of America's most practical business and professional men. 

1. Definition of a Partnership 

A partnership is an association of two or more persons for the purpose of carrying on a business or profession for profit. It is entered into voluntarily by each of the partners and presumably for their mutual advantage. 

This forms a company which will deal with others whose interests are affected, and a group of laws has grown up to protect them. Thus the actions, duties, and responsibilities of partners are regulated, and the rights and interests of the partners themselves are protected. 

For the legal aspects of the subject, the reader is referred to books on business law and encyclopedias that may be found in all public libraries, or to the books on partnership that may be found in the libraries of lawyer friends. 

2. Responsibilities of Partners

In the absence of an agreement to the contrary, partners share equally among themselves the duties, responsibilities, and profits or losses of the business. In common law they are each personally responsible to others for all of the debts and other liabilities of the firm. These duties and responsibilities may be legally modified when desired as may be learned from the legal references mentioned. The wise person will inform himself on all of the legal aspects of a partnership before forming one and will then follow Benjamin Franklin's advice: 

When you make a bargain with a friend, Let it be clear and well penned 

That you remain friends to the end.

When one enters into a partnership he assumes new obligations and must surrender some of his liberties, being bound by certain moral and legal restrictions both within and without his partnership. It is, therefore, a relation- 

Iship not to be entered into lightly. This fact was emphasized by several Rotarians in their replies. One said, "Except in a few instances where a young man has an opportunity to become a partner in a well-established business he should proceed with extreme caution."

A business man in Canada wrote: "Having had experience extending over two generations in this connection my advice would be-don't choose a partner if you can avoid it." 

Certainly one should ponder the problem seriously before entering into a partnership, and should become thoroughly convinced that he needs a partner and that this particular partnership would be advantageous to him. The devout Christian will pray over this just as he prays over other problems and should receive the same divine assistance. 

3. When Does One Need a Partner?

One prominent lumberman in East Tennessee says: "A man needs a partner when his business has attained such proportions that he is unable to give it the amount of time and supervision necessary to secure maximum results." 

A business man in Winnepeg, Canada, says: "A partner is valuable to any man when he has found out or decided that his qualifications do not cover all branches of the business he intends to engage in. For instance, if he is a producer, mechanically inclined, he would do well to enter partnership with a man familiar with finance and office management.

A young man starting out in life usually lacks capital, experience, and opportunity. Sometimes he lacks courage and confidence. It is possible to secure all of these through partnerships. Two timid young people may become courageous when joined together. One having

experience and skill may lack the capital that another can offer. Family or political connections will sometimes open up opportunities.

Profit of some kind is the motive for every partnership. This profit may be financial, social, political, or increased efficiency. Every partner must contribute something capital, experience, skill, influence, and the desirable qualities discussed below. Any one seriously lacking any, or certainly if lacking many of the things referred to above, needs a partner to supply them if that appears to be the only or the best way to get them, "one supplying what the other lacks." 

4. Some Desirable Qualities in a Partner 

When one realizes the duties, responsibilities, and the intimate relationships of a partnership, the importance of the personal qualities and qualifications of the partners becomes more apparent. One business man emphasized the fact that the choosing of a partner is of sufficient importance to warrant one's taking plenty of time and going to some expense to secure the information necessary for one to make a thorough character analysis. 

One great Southern wholesale merchant says: "I would select a partner possessing moral, mental, and spiritual force-the gracious character, bearing the fruits of righteousness. We should be compatible in temperament and congenial in tastes. He must be energetic, honest, self-reliant, and with a fighting spirit to grapple with difficulties."

Some emphasize strongly the fact that a partner must be strictly honest and thoroughly dependable. One's entire business, in a measure, is in his partner's hands, since one partner can bind the firm. There may be some question as to whether one can make the firm, but there can be no doubt that one can break it. The partner must be intelligent, interested in the business, enthusiastic about it, have some aptitude for it, and enough education and training to enable him to become efficient in it. He must be loyal and co-operative in spirit. Without these

qualities in all of the partners there can be no team work, and without team work they can pull no load. 

Two partners should, in a way, be complements of each other, one supplying what the other lacks in the matter of personality and mental qualities. If one is strongly introvert and submissive, the other should be more strongly extrovert and ascendant. Both should not be bossy nor both submissive. At least one should possess strong qualities of leadership. One should be practical, one should be courteous, one aggressive, one should like detail work, one should be optimistic, one conservative, and one a good financier.

Two partners of the dominant or ascendant type of personality present an unhappy combination. Both want to boss, and they seldom see alike. One Canadian business man expressed it well when he said: "In most businesses there must be one person at the top whose decision, whether good or bad, is final. Where this decision must be divided between two it is generally a compromise and therefore not one hundred per cent efficient." 

Another business man says partners should be so poised temperamentally that their combined judgment would be better than that of either. This thought is supported by a West Tennessee lawyer who says: "Two men congenial in every way with the same ideals can conduct a business better as partners than as individuals. 

One reason is that their combined thoughts are better than their individual thoughts, and one suggests what the other would not think of. Besides they can work at different phases of the business at the same time and keep it up better."

It should go without discussion that one should give serious consideration to the moral, social, and religious qualities of a partner, but unfortunately they are frequently overlooked or treated lightly. The author knows one big business man who interprets, "Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers" (2 Cor. 6: 14a), to apply to business partnerships, and acts accordingly.

He says he wants his partner to help him pray for the business and to help him to run it so as to serve God, and that an unbeliever cannot do this. Many examples can be cited to show the partner's moral, social, and religious influence upon his associates. A heart-to-heart talk with one's pastor on this point should help to keep one anchored. 

5. Mutual Inspiration

The author would add one characteristic that was overlooked by all of the correspondents and, so far as he has observed, by all writers on the subject of personal associations. It is a quality that, for the lack of a better term, is here called mutual inspiration. The opposite would be mutual depression. This mutual influence is a kind of magnetic field which seems to surround each individual and affects in different ways those who come within its influence. 

Have you observed that with some people your thoughts surprise you and that you can express yourself with unaccustomed ease and clearness? Then have you noticed that with others your mental wells seem to dry up and that all conversation runs out? You probably have thought that this was due to the other's education or culture, or special lines of thought, but if you will observe closely for a while you will find that this will not account for it. It is a personal characteristic and seems to be independent of age, sex, or culture. 

It is also independent of numbers. How often have you seen a company of two or more that seemed to have no life, the conversation was dragging, and all were tense and uncomfortable, until another person entered and everything seemed to change? Or, have you not seen the reverse happen? Everything was. lively until an additional person entered and then all became dull. Then have you not seen similar things happen when some one would leave the company?

A little observation will reveal the fact that this influence is not always due to the popularity of the

individual or his lack of it, or to the personal likes or dislikes of the company. You have no doubt been inspired by one you disliked and dampened by one you loved. 

This quality is mutual in its effect. If one inspires you, he is inspired by you. If he depresses you, you depress him. It is also persistent in its effect. It is a natural characteristic and the author knows of no way of controlling or of overcoming it. 

Again the reader has probably observed that this influence often affects the nature of one's thoughts. In the presence of some, your whole spirit seems lifted up and your thoughts are purer and more elevated, while the presence, not necessarily the expressions, of others have the opposite effect.

The discussion of this quality is included at length for two reasons: First, because it seems to have been generally overlooked, and second, because of its vital importance in choosing a partner, and especially in choosing a mate.

If you find your conversation with one runs out, or tends to be upon the wrong subjects, that your mental and spiritual processes are dulled or dampened in his presence, then you should shun a partnership with that person. This depressing influence will operate downward continually to your mutual hurt. But, on the contrary, if you find that with him you can think and talk better and upon a higher plane, you may be assured that this uplifting influence will continually tend to lift you both and each will rise higher because of it, which would be a strong point in favor of the partnership. 

6. Some General Suggestions

Several writers emphasize the importance of having a frank and perfect understanding. There should be no misrepresentation and no reservations. A partnership built upon prejudiced information, upon dishonesty, or deception cannot stand.

A partnership with an older man or in an established. firm is one of the most satisfactory ways for a young

man to get started in a business or profession. This is especially attractive for young men entering such professions as law and medicine and such business as merchandising. Many aged business and professional men are looking for fine young men to come into business or office with them to carry on the work as the older ones retire. These older men are usually very exacting in their demands as to personality, character, ability, and training.

A young person seeking a partnership must expect to give as well as take. If he seeks capital, experience, opportunity, or influence through a partnership, he must offer something to match them. What does he have to offer?

He has capabilities which he should have carefully analyzed and measured in the studies of the previous chapters. 

He should have careful and thorough training. 

He must have character, good reputation, and high ideals.

He should have a personality, either well developed, or in the process of development. 

He should have some well-defined purpose in life, a definite objective. 

He should have youthful vigor, robust health, enthusiasm, and optimism.

The young person who can offer all of these qualities should have no trouble in getting the backing he needs either in an active partnership or from some one who would remain as a financial supporter only. 

Having entered a partnership, throw yourself into it with enthusiasm. Do not be afraid of doing too much, or more than your share of the work. Rather strive to give more than you receive, to do more than your part. 

Try to be worth more to the firm than you were expected to be. Do not shirk, sulk, or nag. Do not be selfish, hypercritical, or disagreeable. On the contrary, be active, be cheerful, be companionable, be thoughtful, considerate, and optimistic. Be profitable-do your best.

1. Interview a lawyer on the legal aspects of partnership, or better, perhaps, make a study of the subject in books on partnership which may be found in law libraries, and in books on commercial law and in encyclopedias to be found in all public libraries. 

2. Interview some good business men and get their ideas of the qualities and other factors involved in choosing a partner. 

3. Observe carefully and critically all of the partnerships you know. Are the partners well matched? How does each partner help or hinder the firm? Do you see where improvements could be made? 

4. Make a careful personal study of the suggestions in the discussion of "Mutual Inspiration and Mutual Depression."

Do your experiences and observations confirm the author's ideas?

CHAPTER VI CHOOSING A MATE 

"And the Lord God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him" (Gen. 2: 18). The woman thus became a companion for 

Adam. They walked and lived together. She was a partner to help him in his vocation of dressing the garden. 

She was his wife and the mother of his children. It was a life-long partnership. In this there appears a divine connection between vocational service and the marriage relation, hence the propriety of a chapter on Choosing a Life Mate in a book on Planning a Life. In fact, a life can hardly be planned without taking this question into consideration.

Among some peoples the responsibility of the choice of a mate rests upon the parents-in some upon the man only, in a few it rests largely upon the woman, but in our society it rests almost equally upon the male and female concerned. They are usually young and inexperienced, therefore in great need of counsel and guidance. But it is a strange and lamentable fact that after all of this age-long need of guidance there is so little help available that is very dependable for young people. 

I. HELP NEEDED 

If one wants to enter the dairy business, he will find books, schools, and experts ready to tell him just the kind of cattle he needs, how to select them, and how to treat them. Similar information is available for one who would raise hogs, chickens, bees, rabbits, cats, and even rats. Experts can tell him how to select seed-corn and hound-dogs, but when he wants help in choosing a mate he is met everywhere with a grin and joke. Is it any wonder then that so many young people take the matter [82]

as a joke only to find when too late that it is anything else than a joke?

In the first chapter of this book we learned that more than half the vocational choices are failures. No statistics are available to show what proportion of the choices. of life partners are failures. The record shows that twenty per cent end in divorce. We know that many others end in separation, and that a very large additional number are unhappy. So it is quite probable that there is as large a per cent of failures in the choices of mates as there is in the choices of vocations, and for the same reasons lack of information, lack of serious consideration, and lack of proper guidance. 

There are many causes for broken homes, but the most prolific one is incompatibility which in itself is an indictment of choice, and an evidence of the need of help at this point. The need is more urgent because the situation is getting worse instead of better. The number of divorces is increasing alarmingly. But divorce is a symptom, not a cause; therefore, laws on the subject can help but little. The home is broken before the divorce which is simply a remedy that man has devised for relief from the effects of a bad choice. To solve this question, therefore, we must go back of the laws and divorce courts, back of the marriage license, back to courtship to the time of choosing, and make improvements there. 

What are our ministers and churches doing to help young people in choosing and to decrease the rising number of divorces? Most of them are doing very little. 

What are our parents doing? Scolding, ridiculing, and lamenting. What are our schools doing? Nothing. What are our lawmakers doing? Blundering, while our young people go on groping and stumbling in the dark, many of them falling into the morass, while a few find the way to the mountain heights.

II. NEW FACTORS AFFECTING CHOICE 1. The Growing Importance of the Element of Part nership

The element of partnership in the marriage relation is becoming more prominent and more important as woman approaches an equal social and economic status with that of man. This means that the qualities and characteristics affecting business partners are now more vitally affecting the marriage relations than formerly, and so must be taken more seriously into consideration when one is choosing a mate. This complicates the problem and makes wise choice at the same time more difficult and more important. The pair must now have more characteristics in common than formerly—in other words, be more perfectly matched. 

2. Woman's New Freedom 

Woman's rise in social and economic liberty is affecting her freedom of choice, as well as giving her more things to consider in choosing. She is no longer compelled to choose between marriage and dependent, monotonous spinisterhood. She is not confined any longer in her choice of a mate to the small circle of neighbors among whom she is born. She is diversifying her interests, assuming more independence and more responsibility in the selection of the environment for her home, and the conditions under which she shall live. Thus the possibilities for mistakes are multiplied, a successful choice becomes more difficult, and the need for information and wise guidance is greatly increased.

III. SOME CAUSES OF FAILURE IN CHOOSING A MATE 

In the introductory paragraphs of this chapter, attention was called to the fact that a large per cent of all choices of mates ended in failure. It was further stated that most of the causes could be summed up under three heads. These are expanded here for simplicity of discussion into five divisions. 

1. Lack of Information (1) Of self and of the proposed partner-The author has made some studies that are too brief to be conclusive but which seem to show that a large per cent of the unions

which fail were formed hastily, partly through infatuation and without due consideration of the qualities and characteristics necessary for a successful partnership and a happy companionship. Little attempt was made at either self-analysis, or of the analysis of the proposed partner. Oftentimes the ability and training for such analyses, as well as the facts and opportunity for any analysis, were lacking. (2) Of the marriage relationships, their importance, possibilities, and responsibilities-The same studies and observations leading to the conclusions in the above paragraph support the statements in this paragraph. The statements in both paragraphs are further supported by the fact that so-called school matches seldom fail. The writer has been connected with colleges for more than thirty-five years as student, teacher or president, and has seen hundreds of matches grow out of school association, but is not able to recall as many as six failures. 

Surveys have been made in many colleges showing similar results. College students are trained in analyzing problems, so they study this one seriously. They analyze themselves and each other, they study the marriage relationship, choose intelligently, and seldom fail in their choice. Similar happy results often issue from association in the work of young people's training unions. 

2. The Lack of Proper Guidance 

Reference has already been made to the lack of satisfactory printed helps on this subject for young people. 

Parents, teachers, and pastors have shirked or neglected this duty. Often by their jokes and ridicule they have embarrassed those who should and would confide in them for counsel and have driven them to the socially unworthy for the counsel and advice that God has intended parents and pastors to give. 

3. The Lack of Serious Consideration 

Young people frequently do not consider seriously the importance of the step taken and its consequences. This

failure often results from the lack of information and guidance already referred to. It may result from flippancy, sometimes from dissipation of different forms, sometimes from a frivolous desire for thrills, from morbid self-pity, a desire for revenge or spite, or from a multitude of other unworthy causes. False ideals taught by misguided, so-called sociologists, and found in cheap fiction, together with lax divorce laws suggesting the possibility of the equivalent to trial or temporary marriages, account for many misguided marriages which end disastrously. 

4. Failure to Recognize the Relation of Vocation to the Marriage Partnership 

The failure to recognize the relation between the vocation and a wedded partnership often leads to mistakes in choice. A man should choose his vocation before he chooses his mate. Her life as well as his will be bound up in the vocation that he will follow. They will be life partners in it, so both should be interested and happy in it. The vocation will affect her economic life as well as his, her social station, the environment for herself and her children, and she should know these conditions when being asked to share them.

Tragedies are to be seen everywhere resulting from the reversal of this order of choice. Who has not seen a minister's wife who resented her husband's calling, a farmer's wife who loathed the country, the wife of a professional man in the city who longed for the open spaces, or a delicate, pious, aspiring soul forced to live in rude. surroundings among rough companions?

Life partners should be team-mates. If one chooses a vocation first, it enables him to choose a partner who can contribute something toward success in the vocation. 

Many of the principles discussed in the chapter on choosing a business partner are applicable here and one will find it interesting to read that chapter through again with choosing a life partner in mind. 

Life partners, being team-mates, should be inspired by

the same ideals, interested in the same objectives, each one contributing something that the other lacks, and each inspiring the other. 

5. The Failure to Recognize the Divine Elements 

The failure to approach the relationship with a reverent, prayerful attitude, recognizing its divine origin, and its religious relationships is demonstrated by the fact that most of the divorces are among the irreligious. 

Dr. G. W. Fiske, in The Christian Family, gives some very interesting facts bearing upon the religious factor in permanent marriages. He makes the statement that in Christian homes the ratio of divorce to marriage is less than one to fifty, while for all marriages of the United States the ratio is one to seven. Subtract the number of divorces in Christian homes from the total number for the country and it will appear that the ratio of divorces in irreligious homes is much greater than one to seven.

Doctor Fiske refers to a survey made in protestant church parishes. Of a total of 22,001 non-member church-going families there is one divorce to one hundred and thirteen marriages. In 16,940 church-member families, there was only one divorce to every one hundred and forty-five marriages. He offers many other evidences to support the fact that religion is an important factor in making happy homes.

IV. IMPORTANCE OF INHERITED AND OTHER QUALITIES 1. Physical Qualities 

Youthful couples, those in their teens, are usually attracted first by physical qualities, which is the impulse of nature given to perpetuate the race. Older couples are more often first attracted by mental or spiritual qualities. Love based upon physical attraction does not last long unless it leads to mental and spiritual affinity. 

This is one of the chief dangers of hasty marriages resulting from sudden infatuation.

The physical qualities are important chiefly for their effect upon offspring. One wants strong, healthy, wellbalanced children.

Let the reader refer to the profile made in accordance with the physical chart on page 34. Now, make a profile of your sweetheart, or of your dream-mate if you are still looking for him or her. At each point put a plus or minus sign to indicate the family characteristic. For example, if you are checking size on your own profile, and large size is one of your family characteristics, put a plus sign after your check. If small size is the family characteristic, use the minus sign. Do the same for the profile of the other person. The plus and minus signs mean as much for the offspring as the profile lines, often more. If the profiles, upon comparison, are found to be unfavorable for satisfactory offspring, the whole matter calls for serious reconsideration. 

Importance of the Subjects on the Physical Chart (1) Age-Both should be near the same age. There are both physical and mental reasons for this. If the two are to be happy together they must be companionable, which means they should be able to enjoy the same things mentally and physically. If one is much older, the burden of the responsibility is upon him to make special effort to continue interests in harmony with the younger one. It is generally believed that if there is to be much difference in age that the male should be the older. There are biological as well as social reasons for this. (2) Size, strength, nervous and muscular activity—Of these points in the chart little needs to be said. Ordinarily nature cares for its own. Extremes admire the opposite extremes, while averages admire averages. However, natural impulse takes no note of hereditary factors that do not appear prominently so that the plus and minus signs in the profiles are important here. (3) Health, nervous condition, and endurance These factors call for more serious consideration than the factors in section (2), not only for their effect upon off-

spring but also upon the happiness of the individuals themselves. Health is extremely important for the young couple. The nervous condition is important. If one decides to marry another with a weak, nervous, or irritable condition, it should be with the full recognition of the difficulties of living happily with such one and with a fixed determination to overlook the weakness and to live harmoniously with this one in spite of the handicap. (4) Personal appearance-Little need be said of any of the other physical factors except personal appearance, which is important, and may easily contribute much toward marital happiness or subtract much from it. 

While writing this chapter, the author received a letter from a friend announcing her engagement and describing her fiance. She says her chief criticism of him is his lack of personal neatness. She says she hopes to improve him in this, but if she fails she has made up her mind to endure it. This is the proper spirit, but such endurance is a sacrifice that she should not be forced to make. 

A woman wants to be proud of her husband. She wants to admire him and to have others admire him. 

He is a thoughtless, selfish man who wantonly or carelessly robs his wife of this pleasure and instead embarrasses her by his untidy appearance.

We seem to know instinctively that personal appearance means much in winning favor with the opposite sex. When a thirteen-year-old boy suddenly begins to wash behind his ears, to shine his shoes, and to ask for a clean collar, mother knows that her son has seen a vision and is seeking favor. 

Now, if personal appearance contributes much to winning favor, may it not also contribute much toward holding that favor? It is hard to respect one of the opposite sex who is very careless or sloven in appearance, and love cannot live long after respect dies.

2. Mental Qualities 

Each reader will have rated himself mentally before reaching this point in the study. If he has not, he should do so before choosing a mate. After rating himself, he should carefully rate the one he would choose.

One should marry on his own intellectual level or near it. There cannot be much happy, helpful companionship between an intelligent man and a moron woman. There is room for some difference in levels. Culture, training, and personality can compensate for considerable lack of capacity. Physical beauty, social prestige, and money are frequently allowed too much weight against intelligence and personality. This generally ends in disaster, for beauty fades, social life palls, and money either flees or fails to satisfy.

One must carefully weigh the influence of heredity on the mental and personality characteristic of one's offspring. The plus and minus signs on the chart again are important. Any objectionable qualities appearing in both profiles are especially significant. For example, if there is a trace of insanity or the outcropping of imbecility on both sides, it is highly probable that it will appear in the offspring. This is the biological reason for forbidding the marriage of near relatives. Weaknesses have a tendence to breed themselves out when the weak mates with the strong, but they are increased when the weak mates with the same weakness. 

3. Personality Characteristics 

One's own qualities and ideals will affect the requirements that he will make of those in his mate. One's goal in life will be a determining factor for many of them. 

The qualities in the mates should not only supplement each other but should be such as to help rather than to hinder the one who is most prominent in the vocation. 

For example, if one is to be a public man, his wife should possess social qualities in keeping with any position to which her husband may aspire. If he is to be a missionary, she must be a missionary too.

One woman read law and studied politics to help her lawyer husband. Her second marriage was with a ranchman. She immediately donned a cowgirl's suit and learned to ride and shoot. She not only made herself a companion and a helper to her husbands, but she had an immense amount of fun in doing so. 

Now, it is not recommended that every girl try to be a vocational assistant to her husband, but she should know enough of her husband's business and hobbies to be interested in them and to be companionable to him. 

4. Ideals

It is very important that both should have kindred ideals and objectives in life. How can they walk together if they are not going in the same direction? This should be talked out fully and frankly, and if no general agreement can be had, a permanent disagreement should take place while it may be done without harm. 

Some decide to ignore the difference in ideals for the present, hoping to bring about an agreement later. There are several important considerations here. First, the quantity and degree of the differences. Perhaps it is permissible for a woman to hope that she may improve her husband's morals, religion, and social ideals after marriage, but it will depend upon the degree of difference as to whether it is wise for her to undertake it. If his ideals and habits are so bad as to be seriously objectionable, she should demand that he change them before marriage, and stay changed long enough to give assurance of permanency.

Second, if it is a matter of vocational objective, two things may be said: (1) If it is wrong, such as bootlegging or gambling, the girl should demand that it be given up before marriage. (2) If it is merely distasteful, socially or financially unsatisfactory, the two should talk the matter over, and if one, say the man, is convinced that he should follow such vocation, the woman should either accept it and attempt to be helpful and happy in it, or reject the man.

It is not wise, fair, nor right to proceed to marry him secretly planning to change his vocation afterwards. 

Many a bark has wrecked here. She may fail, in which case she will be unhappy. If she succeeds, she will probably wreck the success and happiness of her husband by pulling him into the wrong vocation, so in either case both lose. Better not try it. 

5. Hereditary Factors

No young person should allow modesty, ignorance, or blinding passion to cause him to neglect the possibilities of hereditary influences. One may be ignorant of the general principles of heredity or ignorant of the hereditary background of the proposed mate, but the issues are too important for one to choose without becoming informed on both. One can learn much about heredity in a short time in any library. A few references for study are given in the Appendix.

One will do well to have a good, long confidential talk on this subject with his family physician. Girls especially ought to do this when considering marriage. 

You want to know much about your prospective mate, and you cannot afford to marry until you have this information. The risks are too great. Friends and one's physician can be of assistance here. More than once has the author filled out questionnaires sent to him by lawyers who were employed to get information about young men. Information limited to the one individual is not enough. The laws of heredity operate for three or more generations, and you should not be satisfied until you know about the grandparents. Pedigreed stock are usually traced through five generations. 

Look first at the individual. If his physical, mental, and personal qualities are satisfactory, look at his brothers and sisters. Are they such as you could be proud of? What are their health conditions, their physical, mental, and personality characteristics? Do they have any objectionable inheritances? What about the parents and their brothers and sisters? Then consider

the grandparents. Family trees beyond this are valuable chiefly for sentimental and social reasons. 

What should you look for? Look for health conditions, especially for diseases that may be transmitted, for family tendencies toward constitutional weaknesses. The wise girl will require of her fiance a health certificate from her family physician. If all girls should require this, it would eliminate a large per cent of hospital experience and untold suffering. 

Second, look for family personality characteristics. If there are any of a pronounced type, you may expect them to appear in the next generation. So, if they are objectionable, beware!

Third, get information before it is too late. The facts are too important to be neglected. Love is blind, they say, and often causes its victims to plunge headlong into trouble. So, unless one has unusual self-control, it is wise to make observations and investigations before the eyes are closed.

The following story illustrates a wise procedure in choosing. Paul Anderson made a rather extensive study of the reciprocal physical, mental, and personality requirements for marriage. He made a pretty thorough analysis of himself and his ideals. He chose his vocation and set his goal. He created in mental image the ideal woman to help him achieve his ambition. 

In creating this ideal mate he fixed only the essentials, leaving large liberty for non-essentials. In the physical image, size, feature, and form each had a place, but with considerable latitude. Health, strength, and heredity were given definite consideration. Limits were set for physical habits and characteristics.

Paul had ideals as to moral, mental, and spiritual qualities. The moral standards were definite, but perfection was not expected. The spiritual qualities were pretty well defined; so were the mental and personality attributes. 

Paul said: "I have set for myself a professional career that will compel me to move all of my life in circles of

culture and refinement. My wife must move in the same circles. She must be able to do it by nature, ability, training, and desire. She must be a help and an asset to me there, and must be happy in it."

Paul compared every girl he met with these mental images. At last he made his choice, and so thoroughly had he made his analyses, his images, and his comparisons that he made a happy selection.

Be frank. Many young people are brought up to feel that it is immodest to speak of their possible offspring. 

This is not an indelicate subject when it is discussed seriously and at the proper time. Young people should be frank with each other at the serious stage of their courtship. If one has an aversion for children, or knows himself to be incapacitated to have children, or fears for hereditary reasons to have them, the prospective partner should know it. All should be as frank and fair with their sweethearts as was a girl with a friend of the author's. He was desperately in love with the girl, and she reciprocated his affection. He proposed marriage, and after she assured herself of his sincerity she told him that she could not accept his offer of marriage without first telling him that a surgical operation had made it impossible for her ever to be a mother. 

Did the girl do right? It took courage to do it and it cost her the man she loved, but she retained her selfrespect and his highest admiration, while she would have lost both and his affections, too, if she had withheld this information and thus deceived him.

V. SEEK WISE COUNSEL 

Counsel with parents and pastors. They are God's appointed counselors of youth. Happy is the youth whose parents are wise, keeping open the door for the most intimate confidences of heart and life.

O that all children were so fortunate as to have parents as wise as a father who came into the author's office one morning to tell the following story:

I had a rich and rare experience last night. I have always tried to maintain the most intimate confidential relations with my boy who is now ten years old. I have received sympathetically and treated in the strictest confidence everything he has ever told me. I am his buddy and confidential adviser in his most intimate problems. 

Last night he came in with an air of seriousness and said, "Dad, I want to see you privately in the living room after supper." I sensed that an important conference was coming.

After supper we walked into the living room. He shut the door, locked it, and then floored me with the question, "Dad, what does a man say to a girl when he wants to marry her?" 

My first impulse was to laugh in his face. This ridiculousness of a ten-year-old boy's wanting to propose marriage was almost too much for me. But I knew that if I laughed or ridiculed he would never come to me in confidence again. 

I saw he was serious and was expecting a serious answer. 

Then I suddenly realized that this was my one big opportunity to get over to my boy some things he ought to know and ponder for the next ten years or more before he would need to put them into practice. So I breathed a prayer for help, put on a serious look, and for about an hour I gave him the best I had on the whole subject of courtship, marriage, and married life.

This not only proved to be the father's big opportunity -it was his only one, for he was buried soon afterward, but that boy will never forget his father's counsel on that occasion.

VI. COMPANIONSHIP AND LOVE MUST BE PRESENT 1. As the Dominant Factor in Choosing 

Remember that companionship and love are two big words in marital happiness. Love gives a delicious sweetness to companionship, and companionship will propagate and heighten love. Therefore, analyze your problem from this viewpoint. Are you companionable? Why, and to what degree? What is the basis for this companionship?

Is it intellectual, physical, emotional, or spiritual? Do you believe the basis is permanent?

If the basis is physical or emotional only, it probably is not permanent. If the basis is a mutual interest in some temporary matter, it will probably pass with that temporary interest. But if you are mutually interested in the same vocation, have the same recreations, the same social life, the same religious life, the same objectives in life, and are of kindred mind and heart, similar sympathies if you are especially companionable in any one of these and not antagonistic in any of the others, and love each other, you probably have a safe basis for permanent companionship. This is especially true if with this you find yourselves mutually inspired or quickened in mind and ideals by the other's presence. 

2. To Be Cultivated in Perennial Courtship

Then having made the choice and consummated it, strive to increase the number of things and ways in which you may be mutually interested. Strive to please, continue to win. Feed love that upon which it grows, namely: tokens of love, and manifestations of worthiness. 

Let tokens of affection be a constant source of surprise.

Learn how to give smiles for frowns, and caresses for sharp retorts. Learn how to pour oil on the waters when a storm is brewing, and how to make the sun to shine after the storm.

1. Do you know of cases where the choice of a mate before the choice of vocation resulted unfavorably? 

2. Study the outstandingly successful marriages you know and try to discover the factors that contributed most to success. 

You might interview close personal friends as to their methods of choice. 

3. Study some unhappy cases you know. May the failure be attributed to failure in choice, or to factors developing after choice? 

4. What suggestions can you make to help guide young people in making this great choice? 

5. Make the physical profiles suggested in the chapter and compare them. You will find this an interesting exercise. 

6. Make and compare some mental and spiritual profiles. Improvise your own charts. 

7. Get the divorce records in your county for the past year, for the fifth year back, for the tenth year back. What is the tendency? Study the causes given. Are they similar each year? Can you suggest any remedies? Does there appear to be any difference in the proportion of divorces from 

Christian and non-Christian homes? Suggest reasons why? 

8. Study the reports on the Kollikak, the Jukes, and the Edwards families.

CHAPTER VII GETTING THE JOB 

One's problems are not all solved when he settles on the right vocation. He must find a place to enter it, find the job, and then get it. This is a business in itself and often calls for a fine bit of salesmanship. 

Nearly all vocations have different levels, and usually one must start at one of the lower levels and work up. 

The ambitious youth will consider the possibilities of working up when looking for, and in accepting, a job. 

This brings up afresh the question of one's capacity and training. It is folly for one to look for a job in which he is not prepared to make good.

I. CHOOSING THE LOCATION 

Should one try to find a job away from home? Ordinarily for a permanent location, yes. Homefolks either pamper one or neglect him. One spoils; the other discourages. One will be taken more nearly for his real worth among strangers. However, it is possible for one to make a good beginning and to succeed at home, and he should think seriously before turning down a good home job.

There are four important things to consider in looking abroad for a location. 

1. Consider Health 

The question of health in choosing a location must be given serious consideration, but it need not be discussed here. 

2. Consider Moral and Spiritual Environment 

All too many have made Lot's mistake. He chose his location without regard to the moral and religious in- [ 100 ]

fluence upon himself and his family. Disaster followed as it always does when one goes into unholy surroundings for profit. If Lot had gone into Sodom to help the inhabitants instead of hoping to profit from them, he would have saved himself and the city. The evangel of righteousness purifies himself in lifting others. For this reason the missionary and social worker do not become contaminated when living and working in Sodom. 

3. Consider Opportunity for Advancement

Consider supply and demand, also the fact that merit is more fully recognized and rewarded in some localities than in others. Talents and services of a special kind are more in demand in some places than in others, or, to use a commercial expression, there are different markets for different goods. 

Select the best market for your wares. Study carefully the demands of the market, the quality of goods demanded, the preparation of the goods, the price. Then study your wares. Can you meet the demands? Sell yourself. 

4. Consider Your Own Likes and Dislikes

This includes one's preferences for climate, racial environment, social advantages, cultural opportunities, transportation and other means of communication. 

II. FINDING YOUR JOB 

Up to this point you have been thinking subjectively. 

You have been dealing largely with your own capacities, likes and dislikes. Now, you come to where you are dependent upon others. This situation is often discouraging because you cannot control it. This is particularly true in times of depression when jobs are few and seekers are many. However, there are always some jobs available, and the hustler goes after them. The one who succeeds works hard and is systematic, enthusiastic, optimistic, and persistent. He knows what he wants and uses all of his energies and common sense in finding it.

He does not allow anything to side-track him in his search, nor rebuffs and failures to discourage him. He knows that according to the general law of averages he is bound to get a break some time, so he keeps his courage up and persists with a spirit of ultimate victory. 

1. In the Trades

In hunting a job in the trades or minor professions, the following suggestions should help: (1) Make inquiry through friends and relatives-It is not enough to let them know you want a job. Suggest the kind of job you want and then other jobs you are prepared for and would consider. This definite information will enable them to help you more wisely and will also make a favorable impression upon them so that they will take more interest in helping you. In approaching friends for their help with friends, sell them—do not bore them. Give them facts that they can use. "Refresh their minds" on your record. (2) Search the "Help Wanted" advertisements—

Seach such columns in your local papers if you want a local position, or those of other towns, remembering that you will have to visit those towns in order to get anywhere through the "want ad" route.

If you are looking for a job as a highly skilled worker, you should read the "help wanted" advertisements in the journal of your trade.

To follow up want advertisements calls for diligent and hard work. It also calls for persistence because it may take much time and bring many disappointments.

One must learn to discriminate between certain types of advertisements. Those which promise much always have a catch in them. Don't be deceived. (3) Advertise-Use the "want ad" columns in the papers that are read by the folks for whom you want to work. This may mean the big dailies or the special journals of your trade. Remember that such advertisements in the daily papers are of little value except for local people, so if you advertise, for example, in a Chicago

paper you must give a Chicago address, unless you are an unusual specialist.

Advertisements are valueless unless well written. They must be terse, snappy, say exactly the right things in the right way with no mistakes. Study other advertisements and take note of those that impress you most. 

The leading word of the advertisement must properly classify it. For example, if you are a salesman, let that word lead so that the paper will put it in the salesman column. Say "salesman with experience," and so forth, not "man with experience as salesman."

Have some white space surrounding your advertisement so that it will stand out on the page and thus be sure to be read. Let it run for some time. Men do not look for help among the want advertisements until they need it. Be sure that yours is there when they look. (4) Make personal search-Do a little thinking before you start out. Make some inquiry in advance-know something about your field before you begin. Don't search blindly-use your head. Dress properly, be at your best, put on an air of confidence, be optimistic and of good courage.

It is the expanding business that is looking for help. 

Find the cities and territories that are most prosperous and make a drive on them. You can get this information from trade reports and business summaries, or by writing the United States Department of Commerce, or the United States Chamber of Commerce, Washington,

D. C. They will tell you in which cities business is most active. Then write or visit the Chamber of Commerce in those cities to find out what industries there are most active. Go to the classified telephone directory for the addresses of the businesses you want to investigate, write these on cards and classify them as to routes, and call upon them. (5) Use employment bureaus-Avoid those that make great promises and guarantees, and charge fees in advance. If you enrol with a bureau, do not sit down and

wait. Keep searching and occasionally call upon the bureau lest they forget, and also to let them know that you are still interested. 

2. In the Professions

Some of the methods used in finding a job in the trades are out of place in looking for one among the higher professions. The "want advertisements" offer no help, advertising is out of place, and personal search has to be greatly modified when one looks for a professional position. There must be more dignity attending the search for the position as it ascends in the scale of importance. 

Most of the professions have their own method of approach. The teaching profession is rapidly developing the Teacher's Agency as the chief means of finding the position, although this has not yet displaced the old methods of direct approach, the influence of friends, and the direct search of the superintendent or principals. 

In the ministry, in those denominations that have the congregational form of church government, the preacher's friends usually place his name before a church. 

In such professions as law and medicine, notices of needs in papers, and journals are often found. But since it is possible for a lawyer or doctor to locate independently anywhere, he is not dependent upon finding an employer. In such cases he merely looks for a field that appears promising. This may be found in many ways, as advertising, personal inquiry, writing to friends, to 

Chambers of Commerce, and by studying reports or statistics of localities.

When a lawyer or doctor wants a partnership or subordinate position, he must fall back upon the methods applicable to finding such employers in other businesses. 

Sometimes a well-worded advertisement in a professional journal will find a place. Sometimes the employers advertise, but seldom. Often they write to the schools for applicants.

In many professions, direct applications by mail or in person remain the chief methods of approach. Nurses

write to hospitals and to physicians; engineers write to those who employ engineers; librarians write to libraries and to teacher's agencies, and so forth.

Salesmen advertise and write direct to prospective employers. Pharmacists advertise in trade journals, write prospective employers, and correspond with friends in various localities. Farmers may advertise in farm papers and in county papers. They may write to county farm bureaus or to friends in different localities. They may often get valuable assistance from pastors and country doctors. Those wishing to form business connections may follow a similar procedure, but will write 

Chambers of Commerce instead of farm bureaus.

III. GETTING THE JOB 

Having found an opening, it still remains to get the job. There will be much competition so that your securing the job will depend upon your salesmanship. You must impress your employer with your personal appearance, your personality, your fitness for the job. Keep these three points in mind whether applying in person or by mail: 

1. How to Apply in Person (1) Be careful of personal appearance-More attention to personal appearance is required in making some applications than in others. Let your dress indicate that you know what is required of one in the job sought. In any case, be neat, clean, and careful to the minutest detail, but withal let your clothing be modest and of such nature as to attract no attention. 

A lady called upon President Lincoln in the presence of two cabinet members. When she had gone, Lincoln remarked that she was the best dressed woman he ever saw. Both men expressed surprise saying they did not observe her dress, to which Lincoln replied, "That is just the point. I did not either, so I know everything was just right." (2) Impressing your personality-There are a few

personal traits that should stand out in that first interview: a. Promptness-Be on the dot for your engagement. b. Poise-Be composed and natural. Have an air of self-possession and confidence. There is no occasion for embarrassment or any feeling of inferiority. You compliment your employer when you behave as though the job he offers is important and you want it. c. Sincerity and frankness—If you are short on some requirements or cannot answer all questions satisfactorily, say so frankly. No employer expects perfection, but he does demand honesty and sincerity and these cover a multitude of deficiencies. 

If it appears that your frankness reveals that you are not qualified for the job, ask for counsel and advice. 

Your attitude will have aroused interest and admiration so that you may get a lead to a good job elsewhere. 

I have known men to be so impressed as to call up friends or write them letters in behalf of the applicant. d. Show interest-Pay close attention to everything your prospective employer says. Watch him intently.

Lean a little toward him. Study him, analyze him, deal with him according to his type. If he talks, listen. Don't be impatient or listless. If he eyes you coldly, meet him unflinchingly but warmly. If he asks questions, answer them to the point; don't go afield. Don't say too much. 

Be careful not to arouse prejudices or antagonisms. If he is especially friendly and puts you well at ease, don't be flattered or misled and talk too much. Be on careful guard here. (3) Impressing your fitness for the job-First, be absolutely convinced yourself that you are prepared in every way to make good in the job. Get information about it and your employers' hobbies concerning it in advance if possible. In the conversation, casually express your interest in developing points upon which he is especially interested. Manage in a quiet, unobtrusive way to let your conversation indicate that you know your

business thoroughly. Also show, without presumption, how you could help increase your employer's business.

Present testimonials as to character and fitness. Note it is testimonials, not recommendations. There is a vast difference between a simple, restrained statement recounting the things you have done, your characteristics, your special detailed qualifications, and a mere general expression of appreciation, often flattering. 

Do not ask your friends for recommendations. Ask them for statements of fact about you, such as an enemy would have to concede. 

Facts, not opinions, count; statements of achievements in school, in sports, in church or community work, and in such other work as you may have done. Do not leave a job without getting such statement of your accomplishments in it. Have the writer be specific. It is a good idea to block out the things you would have such employer or teacher to say for you. You are not asking for opinions but for facts only, so there is nothing presumptive or immodest in your suggesting what you want the friend to say. Few people know how to write testimonials; it is a burden to them, so you do them a welcome favor in helping them in this manner. 

Remember that you must state your qualifications and then support them with abundant evidence. Give references if required to do so. But this postpones action. 

Try to present enough evidence to convince your employer on the spot and to get an immediate decision. 

Have your friends call or write the employer in advance, if possible, so there will be no need for him to delay his decision to consult your references after the interview. It is sometimes well in applying for an important position to write a note in advance stating briefly your qualifications, and that you will call in a day or two. Have some friends to call or write in the meantime. Sometimes your friend can render you best service by impressing your prospective employer through one of his friends.

(4) Select a favorable time-In calling upon prospects, select a favorable time. Don't call too early in the morning. Let your prospect have time to answer his mail, to get the day's work started, to get his nerves collected. Just before noon, just after lunch, and later in the afternoon are good times. However, it is wise to find out in advance the best time for talking to this particular man. A pleasant chat with his secretary may give you this information. Monday mornings and

Saturday afternoons are bad times. Likewise the last and the first days of the month are bad days for those businesses that require monthly reports. 

If you find your prospective employer especially nervous, confused, out of temper, indisposed, or desperately busy, impress him favorably by very courteously excusing yourself with a promise to call at a more convenient time. (5) Learn something about your prospective employer -Reach your man through his motives, which may be classified as: First, self-preservation; second, property; third, power; fourth, affection; fifth, reputation; sixth, sentiment; seventh, taste. Study him, his business, his tastes, his hobbies, his family, his peculiarities. Prepare your sales talk to appeal to some of his motives. Show that you can contribute something to his business or happiness.

Avoid giving offense by little foolish habits, antagonistic attitudes, and so forth. One man lost an opportunity for a good job because he had a habit of repeatedly saying, "You don't say." The employer, a nervous man, said, "That man would drive me crazy.' 

Get some friends to criticize you on all points, habits, attitudes, dress, and so forth. 

2. How to Apply by Mail

In securing a position by correspondence, you must do by letter what you would do in person, namely: sell your personal appearance, your personality, and your fitness.

To get your personal appearance over to your prospect, send a photograph-a small, cheap one, and give a brief personal description. Also let the appearance of your letter and stationery speak for you. Address the proper party, not the company, or the wrong subordinate. 

Make your letter brief and pointed. Less than a page is good. Then put all of your other material in supplementary sheets. A man does not object to a dozen supplementary documents if they are neat and in good form, but a long letter disgusts him. Be sure of your spelling, English, and every conventional form. 

The first supplementary sheet, or the first part of it, should help to complete your personal picture by stating age, size, height, whether married or single, and so forth. 

If you happen to know that it will increase your prospects in any particular case, you may add church, politics, lodge, or family connections. Use discretions. and omit any or all of these unless you are sure they will help.

Úse letter-size paper, eight and one-half by eleven inches. Write on one side of the paper only. Use typewriter if possible, and double-space the lines. Otherwise, write so as to be easily read. If you cannot do that, get some one to write or type the letter for you. 

In getting your personality and fitness before your prospective employer, you will be largely dependent upon your letter and the statements of your testimonials and other documents. 

Your letter will be one of perhaps two hundred. Therefore, it must stand out above the ordinary in order to attract favorable attention. Make your employer want you. Then, and not until then, will he write you or your references. To accomplish this, there must be something in your letter and enclosures to make him believe that you of all the two hundred are the one he wants. To do this, you must give him most of the information he needs in your very first letter. It must convince on the three points: appearance, personality,

and fitness. If the vacancy is not filled immediately, keep your application warm by good follow-up letters from yourself or your friends. 

3. How to Get a "Pull" 

It is perfectly permissible to use your friends. If they are not willing, even glad to help you, don't blame them. It is your own fault. They will always speak well of you if they can, will be glad to do it, but they must honor their conscience and their obligations to other friends.

Friends are made, sometimes inherited. Fortunate are you if you have friends for your father's sake. Honor and merit their confidence. Your father can hardly bequeath you a more valuable heritage. One has few sadder disappointments than the loss of confidence in the son or daughter of a friend. 

You envy the man with a pull. How did he get it?

Study him and his history. He either made the pull or inherited it. If the latter, he had to hold it. How did he do it? He at least had to appear to be worthy of it.

You are frequently startled at the rapid rise of some young man. He gets positions far over the heads of others older and more experienced, and nearly always through pull. Again we say, don't envy nor knock him but study him. You will often find that he started with apparently no chance. He was poor, friendless, unknown.

But he had intelligence, character, and ambition. He began to make the most of his little opportunities and diligently to seek others. He made a bold, direct attack. 

He went to successful and worth-while men and, with the air of confidence, secured some little job or connection that made it possible for these men to observe his worth and ambition.

Successful men are always looking for just such characters and are anxious to help them. They want to help educate them and to help start them in life. They are happy, even flattered when they find their confidence in

such person is well placed. They can hardly do too much for him then.

When worth-while boys or girls make the contact with prominent men or women, their success is assured provided they keep doing their best, and do not lose their heads.

Remember that only worth-while people can help you, so seek the best. Do not be afraid of them. They are complimented when one who impresses them as worthy seeks their assistance (not charity). 

If you would have friends, be worthy of them. Make them, hold them, be thoughtful of them, be friends to them. Be worthy of the best, and make a bold, confident effort to have the best and strongest as your friends. They can and will give you real help.

IV. CREATING JOBS 

"Find a Job or Make One" is a good motto. Look for some need and supply it. Show some employer how you can be worth more to him than you will cost him.

One railroad president told the author that he would add any young man to his organization who gave evidence that he would produce enough business to make a profit for the road. 

Joseph made a place for himself by showing Pharaoh that a good man was needed.

One man walked through a factory and saw a certain waste. He had an interview with the manager and walked out with a contract to correct the loss. 

Charles Thompson, a young business man in Chicago, found that he lost much time each day waiting to be served in a restaurant at the busy noon hour. He said, "I will establish a restaurant where a busy man may be served without waiting." The Thompson chain of restaurants is the result. 

Mrs. Clarence Saunders of Memphis sent her husband to the grocery for a few articles. After a long impatient wait for service, he was disgusted to find that he could

not get what he wanted, or that it cost more than at another place. So he said, "I will start a store where a customer can wait on himself, see whether he can get what he wants, and at what price, without waiting."

This thought produced the Piggly-Wiggly self-service stores.

There are always human needs to be satisfied, and a livelihood, perhaps honor, awaits the one who will supply them. Keep the ideal of service and God and humanity constantly before you. Ask God to guide you into the field of service, just as you asked him to help you choose the service to render.

CHAPTER VIII AVOCATIONS 

"I am here in the hospital paying the penalty for the sin of having no avocation." These were the words of a prominent editor who was suffering from a breakdown that would not have come to him if he had had some hobby in which to relax. How many others pay the same penalty!

Everybody knows that play and recreation are necessities for children, but few seem to know that both body and mind must have this relaxation on down to old age. 

I. THE AUTHOR'S SURVEY 

Avocation plays a much larger part in human happiness than is commonly known. With the help of a number of high school teachers, the author interviewed five hundred people in sixty-three vocations to determine the part avocation was playing in their success and happiness. Some of the questions developed the facts as to their happiness in their vocations, and other questions had to do with their avocations. 

Two hundred and seventeen homemakers were studied. 

Of these, twenty-three who had unhappy marriage relations were eliminated. One hundred and fifteen were satisfied with homemaking as a vocation. Seventy-six of these, or sixty-six per cent, had some avocation or hobby. 

Seventy-nine were dissatisfied with their vocation and only nineteen of these, or twenty-three per cent, had a hobby.

Two hundred and eighty-four others, mostly men, in different vocations, were studied. One hundred and eighty-eight were satisfied and ninety-six were dissat- [114]

isfied with their vocations. Of the one hundred and eighty-eight satisfied persons, eighty-two, or forty-four per cent, had some avocation or hobby, while only ten of the ninety-six dissatisfied ones, or barely ten per cent, had an avocation.

From this study it would appear that there must be some relation between avocation and satisfaction with vocation. The survey was too limited to be conclusive. 

But, by comparing the reasons some gave for having an avocation with the reasons others gave for being dissatisfied, one gets conclusive results. For example, many women said they were dissatisfied with homemaking "because the work is monotonous and lacks variety and interest," while a large number of the satisfied women said, "My hobby relieves the monotony of homemaking, adding spice and variety." Some of the women with no hobby said they were dissatisfied because they had little money to spend on their homes, while many of the others said they enjoyed their avocations because of the extra money they made for home improvement. There was hardly a reason given for dissatisfaction that was not met by a hobby in the case of some satisfied one. 

Among the advantages of avocations given by the women are: 

Extra money to spend 

Relief from monotony

Culture 

Religious development Economics 

Utilization of spare time 

Development of personality 

Foundations for a business or professional career 

Variety of interests 

Development of talents 

Social contacts 

Outlet for the emotions 

Satisfaction of desires 

Mental relaxation 

Physical development 

Physical exercise 

How barren must be that woman's life who is dissatisfied with homemaking and has nothing to give her any of the satisfactions that others find supplied by their hobbies!

The men gave reasons similar to those given by the women. One locomotive engineer said his job was drudgery, but that he really lived when he got into his garden, raising flowers for neighbors and sick people. 

II. THE GROWING IMPORTANCE OF AVOCATIONS 

There was a time when only royalty and favorites could enjoy the luxury of hobbies, but our present civilization must consider its importance to all classes in society. Our modern industrial system has accentuated this need in two ways: First, mass production has robbed the worker of the exhilaration of expressing his individuality in his work and has substituted the deadening effect of mechanical repetitions. Second, it has enabled the worker to produce more, to earn more, and has shortened his hours of labor. Modern industrialism has really done three things for society: increased the need for recreation, increased the time for it, and made possible the means for enjoying it. 

The thirty-hour week probably lies just ahead. This will leave fifty hours, more than half of the time, for leisure. What will be done with it? The answer to this question is one of the greatest problems for our social thinkers.

In the past our teachers have been teaching the people how to work. They have succeeded so well that they must now teach them how to play. This must be done to restore to society the development of individuality which the machine tends to take away, and to save society from disaster which must follow if its leisure time be spent in idleness. Morals, religion, health, and public safety are at stake.

III. THE IMPORTANCE OF AN AVOCATION TO THE INDIVID- 

UAL 

1. Relaxation, Stimulation, Tonic 

Every one needs frequent and regular relaxation.

Neither the body nor the mind can bear up long under

constant strain. Even machinery will last longer if given rest periods.

The parts under tension must have opportunity to readjust themselves. We call this rest, but rest in the sense of ceasing from activity is not always enough. It is not enough to unstring the bow. It must be bent backward occasionally. Just so the body and mind must be kept from becoming stiff or set, must be kept pliable by being bent in the opposite direction occasionally. 

Play does this.

Work strains and weakens; play relaxes and stimulates. One's avocation should give the stimulation and relaxation of play. This serves both as a rest and as a tonic to body and mind. In this lies the value of sports, games, and pastimes of all kinds. 

One may relax in sports as a participant, or as an observer-a fan. Our great American sports, ball games, and horse-racing, furnish recreation to many thousands of fans, but there is danger in recreation of this kind. 

The sport itself sometimes fails to furnish the exhilaration desired, so to increase the interest and tension, bets are made. This is fraught with two dangers. One is that it may lead one into the gambling habit and to ruin; the other is the danger that its influence may lead another to gambling.

A pastor's story-A former pastor in the city of 

Louisville, Kentucky, told the author this story: 

One of my leading business men, Mr. Blank, telephoned me to come to his office at once, and to observe the young man at the desk on my right as I entered. 

There was much seriousness in Mr. Blank's voice, so I rushed down to his office somewhat disturbed. I observed the young man. He was a fine-looking chap, clean, intelligent, and hard at work. After the usual greeting, Mr. Blank said: "Did you observe the young man at the front?” 

I answered in the affirmative.

"Well, I have just found him short in his accounts, but he does not know of the discovery. He has been at work for me five years and has been one of the best men I ever had, efficient, faithful, and trustworthy, until now. He is the only support of a widowed mother and plans soon to be married, but he has recently taken to gambling and now is a defaulter. I want you to tell me what to do about it." 

I was stunned, and, stammering, said I didn't see anything to do but to punish him. "But, wait," said Mr. Blank, "I have not told you all of the story. Last spring during the Derby I thought to give 

John a little recreation, so I invited him to go with me to the races. As you know, racing is my hobby. John had never seen a race, so he gladly accepted my invitation. I gave him a ticket and then when in the grounds I bought a chance on my favorite horse. I have done this for years just to add to the excitement. I never bet on anything else and never buy more than one or two tickets at the time. I do it for the exhilaration, not for the profit, so I do not consider it gambling. I bought a ticket for John and gave it to him. This was a new experience for him and it thrilled him greatly, especially when his horse won. That started him toward his ruin, and I feel responsible for it. Now what should I do?" "That changes the whole situation,” I replied. "Call John in here, tell him of his mistake, forgive him, confess yours, and beg his forgiveness. Then together seek God's forgiveness, and pledge each other to live differently from now on." 

The pastor described in detail the touching and beautiful scene that followed, and concluded by saying that both lives were made over and that John today is a very fine and useful business man.

Recreation must be clean, wholesome, and stimulating to the best in one's nature, or it becomes a curse. 

2. Personality Development 

One usually throws himself into a hobby with enthusiasm. Interest is keen, attention alert. This stimulates and quickens many of the other emotional elements.

However, different hobbies stimulate different qualities. 

This tends to develop the qualities so stimulated and produces a type of personality. This fact should be borne in mind in choosing a hobby. It is especially important to consider this when recommending hobbies to children. 

For example, public sports tend to develop social qualities and the extrovert type, while searching for rare flowers, insects, or minerals takes one away from society and tends to develop the introvert type. Certain games may be used to develop the spirit of fairness. Thrift may be encouraged by certain collection hobbies; artistic hobbies will develop the cultural characteristics; and nature studies will develop the scientific spirit. (1) Some personality traits and hobbies which develop them (a) Scientific spirit-Nature studies-collecting insects, butterflies, rare flowers, rocks, minerals, plants, birds' eggs, birds' nests, shells, fossils, reptiles, leaves, and so forth. (b) Social qualities-Ball games, entertainments, functions, picnics, giving outings to friends or shut-ins, entertaining children, hospital visiting, social work, clubs of various kinds, fraternal and other organzations, and also the religious avocations which follow. (c) Religious qualities-Activities in the church organizations as Sunday school, Baptist training union, men and women's organizations, mission work, settlement work, charities, visiting the sick, personal religious instruction, religious music, church entertainments, distributing religious literature, visiting prisons, encouraging unfortunates, taking flowers and fruits to the sick and shut-ins, furnishing flowers for church decoration, inviting people to the services, ushering, collecting notes and illustrative material for the pastor, beautifying church and grounds, greeting people at the church door; vegetables, fruits, and tokens of cheer for the poor; furnishing flowers for neglected graves or for those whose loved ones cannot provide them.

The possibilities in religious hobbies both for personal

development and for the service to be rendered to others have been too generally overlooked. (d) Mechanical abilities-Carving, making toys, furniture and mechanical devices, electrical and radio instruments, building boat models, playhouses, bird boxes, and so forth. (e) Cultural characteristics-Clay-modeling, painting, drawing, music, literary work, flower-gardening, and so forth. (f) Business ability-Truck-gardening, roadside marketing, poultry-raising, agricultural and stock-raising clubs, needle-work for profit, and so forth. (g) Thrift-All hobbies that involve collecting and giving. 

3. Supplementary Satisfactions

Many have special interests and tastes that are not satisfied by their vocations. In fact, most people have interests broader than may be satisfied by any one vocation. The avocation must furnish this additional satisfaction. For example, a teacher satisfies a love for farming by raising a garden, growing an orchard, running a small poultry farm, or by keeping a few cows. preacher may satisfy his mechanical urge by maintaining a shop. A banker satisfies his craving for science by chasing butterflies after business hours. 

4. Supplementing One's Income

Turning spare hours into profit has made many men and even laid the foundations for fortunes. The chickens, eggs, butter, garden vegetables, and flowers produced by wives and children have saved many homes. The small stock farm, orchard, or poultry yard has tided shop men through periods when the factories were closed or men laid off. 

One woman in Middle Tennessee found a market for walnut kernels and, in each of the two winters of depression that followed, made seven or eight hundred dollars and, in addition, furnished profitable employment to several families by collecting and selling walnuts.

Some women teach music or art for part-time and supplement their husbands' income. One pastor in a 

Southern city raises bees. He farms the hives out with friends in different parts of the city, thus increasing the feeding area, and harvests a large and profitable crop of honey.

One woman grows peonies and other flowers for the market. Two brothers are raising long-haired angora cats for fur and have found a profitable market. Ginseng growing has been a profitable hobby for many; however, those who undertake to make a vocation of it generally fail. One pastor makes fine antique pattern furniture and sells it at a nice profit. A bookkeeper makes souvenirs for gift shops. A doctor raises pedigreed dogs and sells them at fancy prices. Some have grown skunks profitably. Some farmers have paid taxes by growing locust posts, others by growing pecans. 

Rabbits have supplied many tables with fresh meat.

One preacher is supplementing a small salary raising tomato, cabbage, onion, sweet potato, and pepper plants for sale.

Other hobbies that have added profit and pleasure are: 

Needlework, painting, cake and bread-making, writing, canvassing, canning, carpentry, conducting tours, photography, inventing, electrical work, lecturing, coaching, radio work, raising guinea pigs, decoy ducks, fish bait, proofreading, criticizing manuscripts, typewriting, mimeographing, multigraphing, printing, painting signs, and so forth. 

5. Laying Foundations for Future Vocations 

Hobbies are a second line of defense. Vocations and conditions change. Most people are forced by age, health, or other reasons, to change vocations later in life. 

Retirement comes to all. Happy is the man who has developed an avocation that will give him enjoyment, profitable employment, and perhaps support when that time comes, but it is a tragedy for one to have nothing to fall back on when his first line of support is broken.

Many find their vocation through an avocation. A lady friend took up flowers as a hobby and is now the leading florist, not only of her city, but of her section of the country. One woman began to make children's garments. They were popular. She employed helpers and soon had a large factory.

Many doctors have begun farming as a hobby and later have given their entire time to it. Teachers engaged in bookkeeping at night and were led by this into banking or business. A lawyer indulged in dairying as a hobby and later became a dairyman and fine stockraiser. One man began raising foxes for fun and started a big fur-growing industry. 

IV. HOBBIES AND MARRIED PEOPLE 

A hobby is the thing one talks about and engages in when off duty. A man who has had a hard day in business does not want to talk shop at the dinner table. He wants to get away from business and for a time forget it. 

How happy is the man who, after a hectic day in the office, can rush home to find the woman he loves waiting to go with him to the golf course, to the hunting lodge, to the little fishing stream, on a motor trip, or to some other recreation!

Husband and wife should develop hobbies in which the other can be interested. If one has developed a hobby to the point where the sacrifice would be great to give it up, granting that it meets the test given above, the mate should develop at least a sympathetic interest in it. 

It is selfish and mean to refuse to do so. 

It is not always desirable that both should have the same hobby; on the contrary, more often they should be different. The important thing is that they both have hobbies, and that each should have a sympathetic interest if not a pride in the other's hobby. 

Our married people need to learn how to play together. 

They need to learn the value of hobbies. Oh, that harsh pragmatic husbands could only realize that those

flowers, or the few chickens, or the crude pieces of needlework are the outpourings of a hungry heart! What a sunburst would come into many a clouded home if husband, upon coming in, should say a few words of appreciation of these little things! 

What a new thrill many a husband would have if his wife should surprise him with a kind word about his golf, his hunting, his hobby!

1. What is your hobby? If you haven't one, why? What would seem to interest you most? 

2. Make a list of the hobbies that appeal to you, or make a list of all you know and add to them from time to time. 

Make a hobby of collecting hobbies. Classify them upon some basis. 

3. Make a note of the hobbies of great men and women. 

4. Ask your friends who have hobbies why they have them and what they seem to get out of them. 

5. In the interviews suggested in Number 2, Exercises at the close of the first chapter, collect information as to hobbies. 

Do most of those who are happy in their work have hobbies, or is the reverse true? Do most contented housekeepers have hobbies? Does it appear that hobbies contribute anything to the success or happiness of those who have them? 

6. May hobbies contribute anything toward the success of a married couple? Would the change of attitude of one toward the hobby of the other contribute or subtract anything? How?

APPENDIX BIBLIOGRAPHY VOCATIONAL INFORMATION 

The following books are among the best that have appeared giving general information on the vocations. In order that the reader may know which books he would be most interested in personally, a brief description and the table of contents of each are given.

Platt, Rutherford H., The Book of Opportunities 

This is one of the most helpful books in giving a brief survey of the general fields of work. It classifies and briefly describes over three thousand occupations. It gives some information about each field and about the more important occupations in each field. The discussions, though brief, are to the point, and generally are sufficient to arouse interest in a vocation, or to eliminate it from further consideration. The book tells where fuller information may be found on many of the vocations discussed.

Bennett, G. Vernon, and Older, Frank E., Occupational Orientation

This book uses fresh material, and is very readable, helpful, and reliable. The authors have divided all occupations into twenty-four occupational families, and have devoted a chapter to each family. Each of these chapters is written by a group of specialists. 

A brief discussion is given of the characteristics of each family, and a more complete discussion of a few of the outstanding occupations in each family. Two hundred and ten typical occupations are discussed. Abundant references are given to fuller information.

Rosengarten, William, Choosing Your Life Work 

In this book there are splendid discussions of forty-two vocations. The discussions are brief, about six pages each, but are to the point. [126]

*Allen, Frederick J., A Guide to the Study of Occupations 

The student will find three hundred and fifty occupations listed with little or no discussion, but with references for each. The value of this book is in its references which enable one to find information needed on any of the occupations listed. 

Fryer, Douglas, Vocational Self-Guidance

In the last half of the text the author gives splendid discussions of twenty-two occupations by a prominent authority in each. 

They are:

Advertising, publicity, journalism, photography, commercial art, industrial purchasing, salesmanship, real estate, insurance, merchandising, personnel, administration, banking, credit, accountancy, business statistics, highway transport, industrial traffic, engineering (in its fundamental branches), chemical engineering, industrial engineering, architecture. 

This book also contains two valuable chapters on Business Professions for Women, contributed by Lorine Pruette. 

*Ernst, Clayton H., What Shall I Be? 

Ernst, Clayton H., Opportunity Ahead

The first contains a discussion of twelve vocations. The subjects discussed in this text are: 

Law, chemistry, banking, accounting, farming, medicine, foreign travel, packing, traffic management, department store, lumber, pharmacy.

The second volume contains "ten occupations that are attractive for young men today." The subjects in this volume are: 

Aviation, radio, teaching, ministry, librarianship, navy, army, insurance, stage, detective.

Each of the discussions in both of the Ernst books is written by a prominent man in his vocation. The discussions are fresh, readable, and up-to-date. 

Fowler, Nathaniel C., Starting in Life

This is one of the earlier books published that attempted to present information on the various vocations.
