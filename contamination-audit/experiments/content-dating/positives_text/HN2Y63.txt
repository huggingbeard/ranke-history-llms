TO THE STUDENT 

This set of drills is designed to help you improve your use of English. 

It aims primarily to establish correct habits of expression. 

In the exercises emphasis is concentrated upon essentials in order that you may overcome the errors most frequently recurring in your speech and writing. To enable you to conduct a varied attack upon these "demons," a variety of material has been introduced, and a cumulative method has been employed in arranging the drills. 

The best results will be attained if you will study and complete the exercises in the order in which they are listed in the Grade Chart. Follow carefully all directions for writing the exercises. After your paper has been prepared, tear it out along the perforated edge and hand it to your teacher. 

After the graded paper has been returned to you, record the grade in the Grade Chart. Do not work ahead; follow the assignments as they are given by your teacher.

Included in the plan are diagnostic and achievement tests to be distributed by the teacher. These will determine your ability and progress at regular intervals. If you do faithful work in the exercises, your grades in achievement tests should be higher than those in the corresponding diagnostic tests.

THE PARAGRAPH: UNITY [Study A MANUAL OF ENGLISH, Section 53.] 

A. In the following exercise improve the paragraphing by enclosing in brackets drawn in the left-hand margin the groups of sentences that logically form paragraphs. Do not change the order, the wording, or any marks of punctuation. 

The paragraph is the unit of writing; it may be a complete composition in itself, or it may be one of a series of paragraphs which together constitute a composition. 

It may consist of one sentence or of a number of sentences that are intimately related in thought and that develop with reasonable fullness a single topic or idea. 

The function of the paragraph, considered as a part of a larger whole, is to make reading and understanding easy.

The paragraph helps to make the structure of a composition readily apparent. 

It marks a distinct break in thought; it invites and holds attention. 

Nothing is so formidable to a reader as an unbroken printed page; the eye at the prospect, and the mind becomes inattentive. wearies

A page that is attractively paragraphed, on the other hand, invites attention rather than repels it—and the attention of the reader must be gained if writing is to accomplish its purpose.

By arranging the material properly in paragraphs, or thought units, the writer has a chance to enforce a single point adequately and to lead the reader, step by step, through a series of related ideas without confusing him or dissipating his interest.

Ordinarily, the mind cannot retain many ideas at one time, and for that reason it needs relaxation frequently.

The breaks between paragraphs are necessary resting places for the mind of the reader.

The thoughtful writer, therefore, should make skilful use of paragraphs, keeping in mind always that what he is writing is to be read by someone else. 

His first and last duty is to make that writing instantly clear and effective with no loss of the reader's time and energy.

To this end, the paragraph is an indispensable aid.

A commended stranger is expected and announced, and an uneasiness between pleasure and pain invades all the hearts of a household. His arrival almost brings fear to the good hearts that would welcome him. The house is dusted, all things fly into their places, the old coat is exchanged for the new, and they must get up a dinner if they can. Of a commended stranger, only the good report is told by others, only the good and new is heard by us. He stands to us for humanity. He is what we wish. Having imagined and invested him, we ask how we should stand related in conversation and action with such a man, and are uneasy with fear. The same idea exalts conversation with him. We talk better than we are wont. We have the nimblest fancy, a richer memory, and our dumb devil has taken leave for the time. For long hours we can continue a series of sincere, graceful, rich communications, drawn from the oldest, secretest experience, so that they who sit by, of our own kinsfolk and acquaintance, shall feel a lively surprise at our unusual powers. But as soon as the stranger begins to intrude his partialities, his definitions, his defects, into the conversation, it is all over. He has heard the first, the last and best, he will ever hear from us. 

He is no stranger now. Vulgarity, ignorance, misapprehension are old acquaintances. 

Now, when he comes, he may get the order, the dress, and the dinner, but the throbbing of the heart, and the communications of the soul, no more.-EMERSON.

B Poetry is, as was said more than two thousand years ago, imitation. It is an art analogous in many respects to the art of painting, sculpture, and acting. The imitations of the painter, the sculptor, and the actor are indeed, within certain limits, more perfect than those of the poet. The machinery which the poet employs consists merely of words; and words cannot, even when employed by such an artist as Homer or Dante, present to the mind images of visible objects quite so lively and exact as those which we carry away from looking on the works of the brush and the chisel. But, on the other hand, the range of poetry is infinitely wider than that of any other imitative art, or than that of all the other imitative arts together. 

The sculptor can imitate only form; the painter only form and color; the actoruntil the poet supplies him with words-only form, color, and motion. Poetry holds the outer world in common with the other arts; the heart of man is the province of poetry and of poetry alone. The painter, the sculptor, and the actor can exhibit no more of human passion and character than that small portion which overflows into the gesture and the face, always an imperfect, often a deceitful, sign of that which is within. The deeper and more complex parts of human nature can be exhibited by means of words alone. Thus the objects of the imitation of poetry are the whole external and the whole internal universe, the face of nature, the vicissitudes of fortune, man as he is in himself, man as he appears in society, all things which really exist, all things of which we can form an image in our minds by combining together parts of things which really exist. The domain of this imperial art is commensurate with the imaginative faculty.-MACAULAY. [93]

THE VALUE OF MISTAKES 

(1) This subject is important for several reasons. In the first place, all persons make mistakes, or nearly all persons. In the second place, most people profit by past mistakes. A third reason why mistakes are valuable is that they develop character. (2) Mistakes are an essential part of modern life; they have been so for countless ages. Without mistakes the world would be revolutionized. Thousands of persons would be thrown out of employment, and we should waste millions of dollars. In place of everyday hazards, life would present a dreary monotony of humdrum existence. With mistakes, progress; without them, stagnation. (3) Think how many things we now do would go undone if nobody ever made a mistake. With no words ever misspelled and no sentences ever wrongly punctuated or constructed, with no paragraphs ever violating the principles of unity, coherence, and emphasis, we should never know the beauty of red ink or the pleasure of rewriting for clearness. With algebra and physics problems always solved by the one correct method, with totals of added figures always coming out right, there would be no chance to do better on the next test. With speeches always delivered with the precise inflection and emphasis, with solos always sung with the perfect tone and volume and interpretation, there would be no need of effort for improvement. (4) If nobody ever made a mistake, trains would always be on time, and so would all travelers; nobody would ever need a physician or a dentist, and lawyers would have to earn an honest living. With everything always done exactly right, no work would be the least imperfect or would have to be done over. There would be no need to read proof on any printing job; all lessons would be perfectly learned and recited, and all pupils would get the same perfect grades. Who knows? Teachers, principals, study hours, and even schools might be abolished. (5) Why do people make mistakes? Because they are careless, or indifferent, or hasty; or because they lack training and judgment. Nobody, however, makes them deliberately. A mistake might even be defined as something wrongly done. 

We see them all about us, and everybody recognizes them at sight. (6) But life cannot go on so perfectly. Mistakes do occur, and we need them. 

First of all, a mistake offers a chance for improvement; it invites a more vigorous effort in each succeeding trial. Everybody, from childhood to old age, learns by trial and error. Deprived of money for careless spending, a boy soon finds out its real value. Having fallen short of perfect attainment in conduct or in tasks of whatever sort, a boy still has a goal to strive for. Secondly, a mistake now and then keeps a person from being conceited, and hence is useful in the development of character. 

Anybody who thinks that he is always right and others wrong is intolerable. Finally, 1

a person who makes mistakes himself is charitable toward others who also make mistakes. Thus the whole round of human understanding and good will depends upon mistakes. We can't get along without them; we wouldn't even if we could.

A TRIP TO YELLOWSTONE PARK 

Last summer my family and I took an automobile trip to Yellowstone Park. 

It was one of the most delightful experiences that I have ever enjoyed. Although the journey from our home to the Park was very pleasant, the trip through the Park itself far exceeded our expectations. We had heard others tell about the marvels of the Park, but we soon discovered that nobody had done them full justice. 

First of all I was impressed with the variety of natural wonders. From the day of our entrance to the Park to our departure five days later, one glorious spectacle after another presented itself to us. In addition to wonderful scenery, we had delightfully cool weather and enjoyed meeting people from all over the country. It was an experience I shall long remember. 

The first point of peculiar interest was Thumb Station, on the shore of Yellowstone Lake. Here we ate lunch and then went to see the countless springs of boiling water and steaming, bubbling pools of hot lime along the lake shore. The lime pools resemble thick paint, and from their varied colors are called paint pots. 

We saw also the famous geysers, some of which threw out steam and boiling water to a height of several hundred feet. Most of them erupt at irregular intervals, but one is so regular in its action that it is called Old Faithful. 

The Yellowstone River flows through a deep and wide canyon of gorgeously colored limestone. The sides of the canyon are so steep that it is possible to descend to the river only at places where steps have been built for that purpose. 

Before it strikes the canyon, the river flows over two beautiful waterfalls, called 

Upper and Lower. 

There were other places of interest, but these we liked best. The mountain scenery was grand, and we liked the bears and other animals that we saw. We could have enjoyed a longer stay, and we were sorry to leave. The bears are so friendly that they will crawl into your car if you are not careful.

WHY I AM GOING TO COLLEGE 

Every high-school senior should have a reason for the faith that is in him. He ought to know, and he ought to be emphatic about his knowing, exactly why he would get a working position after he is graduated or why he would go to college. 

Here are my reasons for going to college.

My strongest reason for going to college is that it will give me the best possible opportunity for studying myself and for finding the place in society that I am best fitted to fill. Few high-school graduates have given the problem of their life work more than passing thought, and without several years in college they really do not know how or where they can best serve their generation. By contact with broadminded and experienced teachers, by acquaintance with a wide variety of subjects, and by understanding the needs and the advantages of various professions, I shall be better able to choose that field of endeavor in which I may be best qualified to labor.

It has been pointed out many times that a man with college training quickly overtakes and passes by the man without that training. Larger and wider opportunities open before the college man, and he soon earns the larger salary. Most of the men in high positions of trust and responsibility today are college men. In mere dollars and cents, then, it pays to go to college. 

My third reason for going to college is that it will introduce me to the world's storehouse of culture. Through the study of the several arts and sciences in college, one may secure the basis of an intelligent appreciation of life, which is the only proper foundation of true happiness.

My parents never went to college; they had no opportunity to go. But they have long known the value of college training, and they have religiously planned that their children should enjoy some of the advantages that they themselves were denied. 

I cannot be false to those parental yearnings. Besides, nearly all my classmates are going to college, and naturally I want to go too.
