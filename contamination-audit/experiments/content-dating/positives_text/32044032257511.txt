(Article by Homer G. Powell, Chief Justice, at the Request of the Editor) THE 'HE Court of Common Pleas of Cleve land is a county court, created by the state constitution. We now have fifteen judges and four additional non-resident judges are assigned for nine months of each year. The average number of cases filed in the common pleas court each year is over 21,000, of which approximately 1500 are criminal, 5,000 divorce, and 3,000 non-jury cases, of which number 2,400 are foreclosures, leaving 11,500 in which the parties are entitled to trial by jury. The administration of the court is divided into three divisions, Criminal, Civil and Domestic Relations. 

Since the organization of the court in 1810 until a few years ago the business of the court was conducted in the usual manner that courts were being operated in the various jurisdictions of the United States. There was a rotation of judges in the various divisions with no centralization of authority or responsibility. As the business grew additional judges were added who followed in the footsteps of their predecessors. 

The judges overlooked the fact that conductprinciple than conducting any other business. They further overlooked the fact that a court composed of more than two judges cannot give its best service without an administrative head in whom should be vested directing authority, as well as the responsibility, for the enforcement of rules. 

Dockets Congested Under Old System 

Due to the failure of the judges to apply business principles in court management, and to adopt an efficient administrative system the docket of the court became so congested that in the civil branch four years would elapse between the date of the filing and the trial of a case. The criminal docket was generally two years behind. Every person who has to do with law enforcement knows that the judicial system is antiquated, technical and productive of vexatious delays. The procedural law obstructs rather than aids the efficient administration of justice.

Part of the cause of congested dockets and delay may be attributed to this. Yet in the final analysis, a large share of the responsibility must rest on the judges who are in charge of the judicial branch of our government for their failure to make use of all the means at their command.

Improvements In Criminal Procedure 

In relating how the judges of Cleveland have solved their problems, we make no claim to originality, neither have we made any startling discoveries. All we have done is to apply well-known principles in conducting the work of the court. The greatest improvement has been made in the criminal branch. 

Through the absence of system in that branch the judges of our court had no knowledge of the number of indictments pending nor of the offenders on bond. Under a provision of state law the setting of criminal cases was entirely in the hands of the prosecuting attorney, who usually followed the line of least resistance, placing on trial only those offenders who were confined in the county jail. As a result the giving of a bond in a criminal case was very often equivalent to an acquittal. This situation brought about justifiable criticism in the newspapers. 

Took Power of Assignment From Prosecutor 

Acting upon the principle that a court has an inherent right to make reasonable rules and regulations to govern the conduct of its AUG 8 

LAND 

vn business, with which no legislative body as a right to interfere, we took from the prosecutor the assignment of criminal cases for trial, and assumed complete control of this function, by placing a court constable, designated as "Criminal Assignment Commissioner" in charge of such assignments. It is his duty to make a report of all criminal cases pending in our court, to assign those cases for trial, preference being. given to the trial of cases in which the accused is a known criminal.

It is also his duty to see that the witnesses for the State are present, to keep in constant touch with the trial room, and so regulate the call of cases that, as the case on trial before the Judge is completed and the jury has left the court room, the jury in the next case is coming in, and counsel are taking their seats at the trial table. By that means the Court is kept busy, and there is no delay or wasted time between cases.

Criminal Docket Up-to-Date 

The criminal assignment commissioner makes a weekly report to the Chief Justice, with detailed information of the number of prisoners under indictment, the number in jail, the number on bond, and the various classifications of the offenses. A daily report is made of the cases disposed of by the trial judges. There is, therefore, always at hand information whereby the Chief Justice knows at any time how many cases are pending. Sufficient judges are assigned to the criminal division to do the work. The average time between indictment and trial is five days. 

Today, instead of being two years behind, our docket is right up to the minute. 

Probation Dept. and Psychiatric Clinic 

The laws of the state authorize a judge to place certain prisoners on probation. The state had failed to make adequate provision for the proper supervision of probationers. 

There was no method by which the trial judge might obtain information in order to properly determine whether or not a person should be given the benefit of the probation law. Since it is more important to prevent than punish crime, and since it is realized that if the first offender can be saved from becoming a criminal a great deal has been accomplished in the solution of the criminal problem, we sought some way to meet the deficiencies of the law. 

In the absence of any statute directly authorizing the court to do so, we made use of the law that permitted us to appoint court constables, and established an efficient "Probation Department" in the county.

We adopted a rule that before a judge could place any applicant upon probation, the case should first be submitted to the probation department and the psychiatric clinic for a full and complete investigation. Established a Record Department

There was no system whereby the presiding judge or trial judge could obtain information as to the prior record of the accused. We again made use of our court constable law to establish a "Record Department," whose duty it is as soon as an indictment is returned, to assemble all knowledge as to the record of the prisoners. This is given to the judge at the time the prisoner is arraigned in order that he may fix a proper bond, and is also available at the time of sentence. It is then forwarded to the institution to which the offender is committed.

The problem of forfeited bonds gave us considerable difficulty until we adopted a rule that before the Clerk could accept any bond, the person desiring to qualify as surety should furnish a certificate of title or abstract of the property offered as security, showing the record owner, all mortgages, mechanics' liens, judgments, court bonds and encumbrances of any kind which appeared, uncancelled of record; also any pending action in which the premises pledged are specifically described; the valuation of the land and buildings as shown by the county auditor's duplicate; and the bond in question. This rule was adopted and became effective December 15th, 1929. Since the adoption of this rule there has been only one forfeiture of a bond given in common pleas court. 

Speeded the Trial of Cases 

Experience teaches us that CELERITY and CERTAINTY, and not SEVERITY, tends to diminish crime. It is a well-known fact that professional criminals prey most upon the community where there is the least chance of his being caught; and, when apprehended, where he can indefinitely postpone trial.

The failure of juries to return proper verdicts in first degree murder cases is a contributing cause of criticism directed against law enforcement. Very often trial judges lose sight of the fact that many jurors have never been

They overlook their need of receiving instructions before entering upon their duties as jurors. Before jurors begin service in the common pleas courts we give them oral instructions as well as printed information, regarding their duties, in both the civil and criminal branches, which aids them materially in performing their duties intelligently. Since we began the giving of these instructions there has been a marked improvement in the quality of the verdicts by juries. 

Same Principles Applied In Civil Branch 

By the application of the same principles, used in our criminal division, to our problems in the civil division, we have not only saved the taxpayers and litigants a large amount of money, but have also expedited the trial of cases. The usual time from the filing to a final entry in injunction cases is from ten days to two weeks. In non-jury cases from four and one-half to five months. We have so systematized the work of the domestic relations division that one judge is able to dispose of 5,000 divorce cases and 4,000 motions in one year.

Let me illustrate how we met some of the difficulties in foreclosure and marshalling of lien cases. In many instances the person who had a junior lien would bring suit for the sale of the property without making prior lienholders parties to the suit. The case would be heard and the property ordered sold. After the advertisement had been running in the newspapers, some one with a prior lien would discover this fact and would ask for a vacation of the prior order and leave to become a party so that he might set up his lien. This procedure not only took up a great deal of the time of the court but very materially increased the cost of litigation. Under these conditions people would hesitate to purchase property at sheriff's sale, because the doctrine of "Caveat emptor" (let the purchaser beware) applied.

The ordinary person who did purchase property from a sheriff and thus obtained a deed from the court, believed that he had the best possible deed or title obtainable; and the judges of our court felt that we should do everything we could to justify that confidence in the court. We therefore adopted a rule that the person, bringing this class of litigation before answer day, should file a certificate from an abstract company, showing that every person who had an interest in that real estate which he has purchased has been made a party thereto. Cost of Administering Justice 

The cost of law enforcement has grown so stupendous that the entire country is aroused over the rapid increase. We have realized it is highly important that judges give some thought to lessening this burden of the taxpayer. One of the large items of the cost of a trial are the jury fees. Jurors in the common pleas court perform two weeks' service. Each week we impanel enough jurors to give each trial judge a jury of twelve, with one additional jury for reserve. The names of all jurors are placed on small slips of paper and put in a wheel in charge of the Assignment Commissioner, who, when a jury is called for, draws the names by lot. By this method we have no idle jurors.

Business Principles Applied 

Prior to the adoption of the Chief Justice system in September of 1923, we had the same judges, the same assignment system, and substantially the same rules as we did after we commenced operation under the chief justice system. The judges on the bench at that time were industrious. They were doing their best to give full service to the citizens of our community. But the trial of cases were not keeping pace with the filing of cases. The results were badly crowded dockets. 

Immediately after the adoption of the procedure and rules above outlined, and after we began the operation of conducting our court as a business man would manage his business, the results were amazing. The average amount of work accomplished by each of the judges increased 66 2/3%. The judges put in no longer time than they did under the old system; but each trial judge was free to devote his attention to the business of trying cases. In 1922, the average number of cases tried by each judge was 697, which was the highest average up to that time. Last year (1930) the average number of cases per judge was 1200. In 1922 the average cost per case to the taxpayers was $38.25. In 1927, the average cost per case was $21.30, a saving of $16.95 upon each case disposed of in the court. This same high record has been maintained ever since.

In one year the common pleas court now disposes of more than 20,000 cases. On the basis of the average saving per case this means a total saving to the taxpayers of more than one-third of a million dollars each year. Each year since 1926 we have disposed of almost double the number of cases we did in 1922, at a saving each year to taxpayers of approxi-

Still Larger Savings In View Beginning with the September term of this year two plans have been placed in operation, which, if successful, will result in additional large savings to the taxpayers as well as to the litigants. One of these plans might be designated a "Settlement Division." Here the parties with their lawyers are called in and the good offices of the judge are tendered to these litigants to aid them in arriving at a settlement before the case is reached for trial. This plan is now in operation and within a period of three weeks it has resulted in an average of three cases thus being finally disposed of each day. This plan should result in a saving of at least $25,000 annually on jury fees, as well as much of the time of two judges, which can be devoted to other matters. 

The second plan is "Waiver of Trial by Jury." Experience has shown that a case can be tried to a judge in about one-half the time that it takes to try the case before a jury. 

Moreover there are many cases where a judge is better qualified, than a jury to determine the facts. The judges have no intention, and it is not the purpose of the plan, to deprive any litigant of his right to trial by jury. But in the interest of economic operation of the court, we have adopted a rule that unless a jury is demanded by a specified time, it will be considered as waived and the case assigned for trial before a judge.

The System Intended to Insure Justice 

In developing this administrative system our judges have carefully kept in mind and endeavored to carry out the constitutional injunction that justice shall be had without denial or delay; for in many cases justice delayed is justice denied. We believe the system has tended to promote justice. 

The secret of what success we have had, is due to three things: (1) centralization of authority and responsibility, (2) team work by the judges, and (3) cooperation by the members of the Bar. The judges endeavored to install a system that would keep the trial judge busy, and permit him to give his entire attention and energy to the case on trial. We also sought in so far as possible to give such service to the lawyers of the court as would conserve their time and energy. The system has reduced the cost to litigants; and at the same time has reduced the cost to the taxpayers of maintaining the court.
