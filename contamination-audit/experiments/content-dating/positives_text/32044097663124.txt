PREFACE 

The consternation and chaos that the trying ordeals of this Second World War have brought upon us, and its effect upon human mentality has at last given credence to these crucial facts: That a lasting peace can be maintained only through the use of force, rather than through the offices of diplomacy. That peace and justice may reign only when force is put to its proper use. And that the present emotionalistic sentiment of the World, which has been so involved in greed, lust, hate and aggression must be sobered and so tempered that all peoples upon this Earth may more effectively assert their rational faculties and thereby enjoy the blessings of unity, equality and fraternity. 

The general belief is that a great change is imminent. The present trend of thought is away from isolation and selfish interest toward the universal concept and collective accord. The interests of the World and its peoples are greater than those of any one nation or empire. Even the idea of a World State is in no way unnatural for the reason that the general society of mankind is gradually coming to accept more and more the proposition of World Unity and Free Intercourse with and between all nations and peoples.

So long as nations remained separate and independent sovereignties, isolation was the safest and wisest policy; and such was our policy in times past. But we have now come to that impasse when the peace-loving nations, in order to protect themselves against the powers of destruction and future wars, must come together in concord and co-ordination. And if there is to be a lasting peace, then the Global Goal must be:

THE WORLD STATE A Federation of The Nations 

Since time began there has floated in the minds of men that all-engaging vision, that omnipotent idea of the creation of a World State, to be constituted in perpetuity and vested with powers adequate to promote and preserve peace among all peoples and between all nations; to proscribe and prevent aggression, imperialistic or capitalistic monopoly, control or subjection; and to protect and defend all mankind in the exercise of their rights and the enjoyment of their freedoms. 

History records several World Conquests, resulting in great Empires, such as those of the Greeks and the Romans. 

Such were established and maintained mostly through military force and authority. But however great military force may be, yet the power of peace is still mightier and more enduring. For as it is written, "Peace is of the nature of a conquest." And the sustaining factors of which are Unity, Equality and Fraternity, resulting in the presence of equity and concord among all peoples.

It was said that Alexander the Great wept in disappointment when he was told that none were left for his armies to conquer. Yet soon after his death his Empire dissolved into lesser States. So it is the fate of all Empires in time to decay and disintegrate. Self determination is the first right of all free people. Imperialism has divided the World, and wars have ensued. Each nation must stand free as a sovereign unit, each as an integral in and of the World State, if unity and peace are to prevail.

And so do I predict that the present era of imperialistic aggrandizement is nearing its end, and that the British, the French, the Japanese, the Dutch, the Portugese, and other Empires will in the near future be dissolved. Therefore the first action that must be taken, if and when there is established a World State, in which all nations shall be federated and allied, is that all Dominions, Provinces, Colonies, or other territories not contiguous to the Mother Country must be constituted separate and independent political units. And, if the

Peace of the World is to be established in perpetuity, then all peoples and nations must also be constituted equal in rights, in authority, and in the enjoyment of Freedom.

Those who study the History of the World soon perceive that it is but a chronicle of wars, spaced with short interims of peace. And so whoever would draft a code of Justice for 

Humanity that will insure "Peace on Earth and Good Will among men," must be mindful of the fact that two vital alternatives have ever beset the path of man-Peace and War. 

And the paramount problem before the World has ever been, how to preserve the one, prevent the other. 

In our time, that which we now designate as the First World War was fought with the vain idea that it was to end war. But twenty-five years later we are today immersed in a 

Second World War, the purpose of which, as declaimed by Messrs. Churchill and Roosevelt in their Atlantic Charter, is to overpower the Aggressor Nations and establish the Four Freedoms, and further "encourage all other practicable measures which will lighten for peace-loving peoples the crushing burden of armaments."

The Atlantic Charter is a most profound and magnanimous document, an innovation in prospectus. But it does not provide means or process whereby the ends it would obtain shall be made permanent and enduring and the peace of the 

World assured for time to come.

Therefore the Author has undertaken the task of drafting a Constitution for a World State by which the Nations of the Earth shall be federated and allied in civic unity. To state what should be, as does the Atlantic Charter, is one thing, but to provide the instrument by which such shall be put into force and effect is quite another. However great the magnitude of this proposition may be, yet as I have found, the greatest documents are the simplest and most direct, and that the genius of their instrumentation lies in the provisions whereby amendments may be made to meet all exigencies that may arise in the future.

The Constitution of the World State as formulated by the Author is in design much like that of the Constitution of the United States, and in many ways but a replica of it. It provides for the federation of the Nations whereby they can in unity act to protect themselves, and preserve the peace of the World. "The force of unity conquers all."-Homer.

CAUSE OF WARS. 

In order to promote the ways of peace we must first discover the true cause of war and provide means to prevent its presence in, or effect upon, the true course of humanity and human life. What is the cause of war?-is a question that no one answers better than the great Greek law-giver Solon when he said that "Equality causes no wars." Remove the cause of wars and there will be none.

If we are to realize the true scope and concept of equality, we must have cognizance of the factors through which such condition may be brought into being and its stabilizing effects made permanent within the general society of mankind. Peace we may premise as that condition which ensues when equality prevails, and War the condition that results when inequality is prevalent. Yet in each condition there are inherent forces that may sustain them. The power of Peace we may construe as that equation of rights, opportunity and enjoyment which all people are pleased to accept and exercise. Whereas the forces which impel men to war are aroused and sustained through the implications of inequality that work in and between men and nations. 

Rousseau's discourse upon the question proposed by the Academy of Dijon nearly two centuries ago:-"What is the 

Origin of the Inequality among Mankind, and whether such Inequality is authorized by the Law of Nature?"-is most enlightening. In his "endeavor to exhibit the Origin and Progress of Inequality" his deductions are lucid and profound; but upon the effects, which breed lust for power and dominion, subjugation, war and strife, he merely relegates these to "the institution and abuse of political societies"; and thereby contends that society is in a natural state only when peace and freedom reign, and that inequality in organized society is abnormal, nor authorized by the Law of Nature. 

Next, consideration should be given the work of Thomas Paine, "The Rights of Man," wherein he gives to rights priority over force. Nor does he overlook the initial fact that freedom and civilization among men advance only, and to such extent, as they are allowed to enjoy equality of rights; and that the greater freedom enjoyed by the people in the exercise of their rights, the less will arise contention among them. And so he concludes that the first and greatest office of political authority is to ameliorate the effects and prevent the growth of inequality in the society of men and among nations.

Therefore, we may affirm that the fundamental tenet which actuates peace in human society is to be found in the equation of human rights, which gives to each man and to each nation, plenary power to exercise all of their rights; and that such should be made as uniform and universal as possible. While no two men in the World are exactly equal physically, yet all have equal right to exercise their rights as far as their abilities may allow. And, though no two nations are found equal in territory, numbers, wealth or resources, yet each has equal right as a sovereign unit. The inequality of endowment among men, does not overrule their equality of right in the general society of mankind or as segregated into nations.

The Author cannot agree with those who hold that man, as an individual, or in society, is to be considered as only an animal, a beast of prey, or to be preyed upon; and that his destiny is manifested by the inexorable law of nature, "the survival of the fittest," and that the strong shall inevitably triumph over the weak. To him man is more than an animal, for he has understanding and has progressed in nature to become a human being and advanced in civilization. Granted that man has an animal and a physical nature, nor can we deny that he also has a psychical and a spiritual nature which common animals have not. And though he can and often has and does act the part of a bestial brute, yet he is also capable of emulating the gods. Every man possesses some god-like attributes. Each has an individuality, a conscience and between all men there is an interdependence. For no man suffices entirely unto himself alone. If man has the right to life and to enjoy it, then it becomes his duty to help others in peaceful pursuit. Thereby peace we may term the first right of man, and his freedom to peacefully pursue happiness his second right. And to nations must also be accorded the same rights to peace and freedom.

Thus in equality of right is found the origin of both individual freedom and the peaceful operation of democracy. And when equality is denied, man loses his identity as a free being and becomes a servile creature, subject to the overlords and dictators. But here is the rub that faces us,-In the World today there is not a single nation wherein freedom of right and equality in society is found fully established. However the curse of human slavery has nearly disappeared from the 

Earth.

NATIONAL ANTIPATHIES 

Ill-will between nations is not engendered through inequality of size, population, power or wealth, but rather through inequality of right. Where the people in one country enjoy the same rights as those in another, there will be little reason for hatred between them. Nations may act the part of good neighbors, but too passionate attachment with one breeds antipathies against others. It is well for nations to extend a magnanimous and candid felicity to those in distress, but they should not thereby become entangled in fiscal alliances. 

Friendly nations are those that "observe good faith and justice towards all nations, cultivate peace and harmony with all; and are pleased to amicably adjust all their disputes." 

Those that allow themselves to become involved by intriguing diplomacy or the duplicity it supports, are destined to regret it. Treaties made between nations affording protection, too often result in an entreaty by the weak for mercy under the subjection of the strong. Those nations that are least bound by treaty are least subject to distrust and treachery. 

According to Tacitus, “The hatred of those who are the most nearly connected is the most inveterate." Therefore, the first wisdom of contiguous nations is to make the bonds of peace and friendship between them firm and enduring. And if World Peace is to be preserved, each nation must extend an equal friendship to all other nations. Man's inhumanity to man must cease.

There is one established institution in the Western World that has made good, The Pan-American Union. But its success has been attained not only through union of aim and purpose -the Peace and Welfare of the Americas-but largely through that unity of co-ordination and corroboration of effort and good will which has sustained and confirmed each of the Republics in the free and equal exercise of their rights, granting uniformity of privilege, practice, and promotion to all, and special powers and monopolies to none. And in such successful manner may it continue to function. 

TO PREVENT WARS AND PRESERVE PEACE 

We are now engaged in a Second World War which in devastation and destruction far exceeds anything that has

happened in the past. Through the implements of science military force has become so monstrous as to stagger the imagination. Yet as always, there still stands that eternal issue as between men and between nations. Shall we live as brothers in peace, or as beasts in ruination? You cannot overcome force entirely with force. All-conquering force, so-called, cannot conquer all, for still it has yet itself to conquer. "Force cannot annul right.” Because "Right reason is stronger than force." The opinion of the World is properly minded only when guided by justice and right reason. Might makes right when force is used in the cause of humanity, and to further peaceful pursuit. Such is the purpose of the World State, by federation of the nations, to insure the protection of all in the exercise of their rights and prevent the encroachments of the strong against the weak. The goal of peace is but to establish and maintain a humane society throughout the world. 

To prevent future wars is the great purpose of the World State. Which would thereby void all treaties between nations, and debar all diplomats from manipulation of the balance of power. For all diplomacy, eventually ends in a compromise with the devil.

Nations that seek for, and would enjoy peace, must act peaceably. All peaceful intercourse between nations is predicated upon the doctrine that the exactions made, privileges taken, or monopolies held by any one or more nations, shall not work injustice or injury to any other nation or nations. 

Nations may be divided into two classes, the aggressors and the non-aggressors, or the offensive and the defensive. 

It is the nature of some nations to act like wolves, others like sheep. Those nations that give the most study to the arts of war are usually the first to make war. For example take Germany, which since time immemorial has been an aggressor nation.

But there can be no peace among nations of the Earth unless and until they each shall respect the equal right of sovereignty and independence of all other nations. Peace is the veneration of equal rights, war is the annihilation of them. 

Probably the most effective means that may be used for preserving World Peace, and dissipate the inordinate spirit of aggression, would be to disarm, segregate, and castigate the aggressors, and then provide that they shall be gradually infiltered through the populace of the World.

CAUSE OF THE SECOND WORLD WAR. 

After a review of the Affairs of the World, past and present, you can only conclude that despotism is bred of inequality and results in the subjugation of mankind. And, if peace is to endure then men must live as brothers, not as beasts of prey. And then properly comes the question:-What is the underlying cause of the present titanic struggle between the Totalitarian Powers and the Allied Non-Agressors? And further ask why Germany, Italy, and Japan have been so angered and enraged against Great Britain especially? 

And the answer is that Great Britain has held the imperious monopoly of World Trade for Centuries.— 

And because Great Britain along with the United States and other Capitalistic Countries has manipulated their affairs for the benefit and profit of the Lords of Finance and allowed them to exploit the resources of their own dependencies and the substance of other nations through the arts of economic subjection. When the treasures of the 

World become impounded in the coffers of the Great and Wealthy Nations, it is only natural that the Aggressor Nations, along with the weak and poor ones, should be moved by lust and envy. And so such condition may be said to carry the underlying cause of the Second World War, and which in effect was but a revolt of the Totalitarian Nations against Im perialism, Trade Monopoly and Financial Subjection. 

However, there is this to be said in Great Britain's favor: -She is only one of several Imperialistic Countries with dependencies, but to her dependencies Great Britain has extended a greater degree of self-determination than any of the other Empires. And so I believe it is but plausible to state that if and when the other Empires, among which is the United States, shall be pleased to free their dependencies, so likewise will Great Britain acquiesce. And by such action would be made possible the creation of a World State. 

Further, it may be with all candor stated that the idea of a World State is in no wise repugnant to the British viewpoint; but rather may be considered as culminating the long established policy of the Empire of affording to its dependencies as much self-determination as practicality will allow. There is no doubt but that Great Britain would reject the proposition that she give any one or more of her dependencies their freedom and right to become independent states. But for Britain to give her dependencies the right to join the World State, and

along with the Mother Country, become separate sovereign units therein, is quite another matter. 

From the above I believe we can draw but one conclusion, and which lies in this crucial fact:-That there never can be a lasting peace established in or among the Nations of the Earth, until all Empires shall be dissolved, all International Trade Monopolies abolished, and all Diplomatic Manipulation and Entangling Alliances proscribed; and that therein lies the key to World Peace.

Much has been written and prognostication made upon the subject of the Post War World and the maintenance of peace. Messrs. Willkie, Wallace, Wells, Hoover and Gibson have written their "Prefaces", which are most edifying and praiseworthy. But, for those who vision a Lasting Peace and 

World Unity, with Universal Freedom and Equality for all, the Author submits his proposition in the form of a text of law. 

Herewith follows the Author's draft of a Constitution for

The World State. While he realizes only too well that it is neither perfect nor complete, yet he believes that in form it is feasible, and in effect applicable and enforceable; and that it provides a most powerful instrument for the preservation of World Peace. No dissertation is made upon the efficacy of its provisions, because they speak for themselves, and no better argument can be made.

CONSTITUTION OF THE WORLD STATE 

We the peoples of the Several Nations, in order to form a Federation of them, promote unity, equality and fraternity in the general society, and secure to all mankind their rights, the enjoyment of freedom and the blessings of peace, do ordain and establish this Constitution for the World State.

ARTICLE ONE. 

SECTION 1. 

The Several Nations, each constituted as a separate sovereign unit, do hereby covenant unto each other equality of right and the exercise thereof, the enjoyment of their freedoms, protection for the peaceful pursuit of happiness; and in Federation do accept and agree to obey and conform with the powers herein granted by them to the World State. 

SECTION 2. 

All Dominions, Provinces, Colonies, Territories, Possessions, or other Dependencies, not contiguous to the Mother Country, nor a constituent part of it, are hereby freed from all allegiance to, or political connection with any Nation, Power or Empire; and as separate political units, are free to join the Federation of the Nations in the World State. 

SECTION 3. 

Those aggressor nations, Germany, Italy and Japan, components of the Axis League, their dependencies, and other nations and peoples that have been conquered and subjected by them, shall be made subject to the World State and held under its direction and control, until disposition shall be made, or they be qualified to join the Federation of the Nations, and their sovereignty and rights be restored to them. 

ARTICLE TWO. 

SECTION 1. 

All Legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the Nations, consisting of Envoys appointed by the Executive Authority of each nation, by and with the advice and consent of its Legislative Body, (or Senate, if such holds the power of confirmation). 

SECTION 2. 

Envoys shall be appointed for the term of three years; and they shall be divided into three classes whereby as nearly as may be, one-third of the Envoys shall be appointed each year. When vacancies happen in the representation from any nation, the Executive Authority thereof shall appoint for the unexpired term, or until the next regular session of the legislative body of such nation. 12

SECTION 3. 

The number of Envoys appointed by any one nation shall not exceed twenty, but each nation shall be entitled to appoint at least one. 

Nations whose population exceeds 100,000,000 may appoint twenty Envoys. The allotment of Envoys shall be based according to the number of inhabitants in each nation, their literacy and advance in culture, in peaceful pursuit and self-government. Isolated Islands or Dependencies of less than one hundred thousand inhabitants, may enjoy self-government under the World State, or be annexed to some near nation, as the Congress shall determine. A re-allotment of Envoys shall be made at least once every ten years. (By the above provision for the appointment of Envoys, the Membership of the Congress of the World State would be somewhat over 350.)

SECTION 4. 

The Congress shall choose its Speaker and other Officers, and have sole power of impeachment. And it shall provide for the appointment of a panel to try and determine the conviction. It shall determine the rules for, and make record of its proceedings, punish its members for disorderly behavior, and with the concurrence of two-thirds expel a member. Each Envoy shall have one vote; and the votes of a majority of those appointed shall be required for the passage of any measure, question, or Bill. Envoys shall be paid a salary of $5,000, per year or compensation equal thereto, and the costs of transportation to and from the place of meeting of the Congress.

SECTION 5. 

The Congress of the World State shall assemble each year on the first Wednesday in January, and such meeting shall be held on a different 

Continent each year, and in order as follows: North America, Europe,

Asia, South America, Africa, Australasia. And the Congress may, if it sees fit, establish an International Capital on each Continent, for such meetings and the administration of the business of the World State. 

SECTION 6. 

Every Bill which shall have been passed by the Congress, before it becomes a law, shall be presented to the President of the World State; if he approve it he shall sign it, if not he shall return it to the Congress with his objections as in toto, or of certain specific items thereof, and the Congress shall proceed to reconsider, and if re-passed it shall become a law. The President shall be allowed ten days in which to sign or make objection to Bills. If he fail to sign or return a Bill it shall likewise become a law.

SECTION 7. 

The Congress shall have power to lay and collect taxes to provide for the general expenses and support of the World State; but such taxes shall be uniform and universally imposed in and among the several Nations. 13

To borrow money on the credit of the World State.

To regulate all commerce, communications or other intercourse between the Nations, and upon the High Seas. 

To establish uniform rules of immigration of the citizens of one nation to and from another, their passage through, or sojourning therein. 

Provided that each Nation shall have the right to prohibit or restrict the migration or importation of such persons as it may think proper or fit to admit, and to prescribe the qualifications of those persons who may make residence or settle therein.

To charter an International Bank with branches in the several Nations, issue an International Currency, regulate the value thereof, coin money, and fix the standard of Weights and Measures so they may be as uniform as possible throughout the World. 

To provide for the punishment of counterfeiting the securities, currency and coin of the World State, and of the Several Nations, and of abuses in the exchange thereof.

To facilitate the Postal Service in and between the Nations. 

To promote the progress of science, of useful arts; and to afford and encourage the establishment of free schools for secular education and the advancement of learning throughout the World, and especially in those nations where illiteracy is prevalent. 

To define and punish piracies and felonies committed upon the High Seas, and offenses against the laws of the World State and of the Several Nations.

To raise, support and maintain an Army, Navy and an Air Force adequate to protect all Nations and their peoples against encroachment of other nations, to suppress rebellions within the nations, and to patrol and police the High Seas.

To guarantee to each nation the right of the people thereof to peaceably assemble and choose their governors and prescribe the form and determine the offices of the government under which they will live, its polity and maintenance. 

To render help and assistance, and provide protection to any nation or its people, wherein emergency shall arise or catastrophe threaten. 

To make rules for the government and regulation of the land, air and naval forces, and for the use of them to execute the laws of the World State and those of the several Nations, and to repel invasions, and suppress insurrections.

To exercise exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever over such districts (not exceeding ten miles square) which on each Continent shall be designated the Capital of the World State, and to exercise like authority over all places purchased and needed for use of the Military Forces. 

To make all laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the foregoing powers, and all other powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the World State, or in any Department, or Officer thereof. 14

SECTION 8. 

The privilege of the writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it.

No Bill of Attainder, or Ex Post Facto law shall be passed by the Congress.

No capitation or other direct tax shall be laid. 

No tax or duty shall be laid on articles exported from any nation. 

No preference shall be given by any regulation of commerce or revenue to the ports of one nation over those of another: Nor shall vessels bound to or from one nation be obliged to enter, clear, or pay duties in another.

No money shall be drawn from the Treasury of the World State, but in consequence of the appropriations made by law; and a regular statement and account of the receipts and expenditures of all public money shall be published from time to time. 

No title of nobility shall be granted by the World State: And no person holding any office of profit or trust under it, shall without the consent of the Congress, accept of any present, emolument, office, or title of any kind whatever, from any king, prince, or nation. 

SECTION 9. 

No nation shall enter into any treaty or alliance, league or confederation with any other nation without the consent of the Congress, nor pass any bill of attainder, or expost facto law, or law impairing the obligation of contracts.

No nation shall, without the consent of the Congress, keep troops or ships of war in times of peace, enter into any agreement or compact with another nation, or engage in war, unless actually invaded, or in such imminent danger as will not admit of delay.

No nation shall without the consent of the Congress, lay any imposts or duties on imports or exports, except what may be absolutely necessary for executing its inspection laws, and the net return therefrom shall revert to the Treasury of the World State:-Provided, that each nation shall have the right to restrict, in whole or in part, of imports of those commodities of which such nation produces more or less than it consumes, whereby total production and import shall balance the consumption of such commodities therein.

No impediment to Free Trade between the Nations shall be imposed either by the Congress of the World State, or by any Nation, except as above provided; and they each and severally shall endeavor to further the enjoyment by all nations, great and small, of access, on equal terms, to the trade and to the raw materials of the World which are needed for their economic prosperity and domestic comforts. 15

ARTICLE THREE. 

SECTION 1. 

The Executive Authority of the World State shall be vested in a President who shall be elected by the Envoys from the several Nations, The President of the World State shall hold his office for the term of three years, and together with the Vice-President chosen for the same term, which shall begin on the first Monday in February. 

SECTION 2. 

The election of the President and the Vice-President shall occur on the second Wednesday of January, and if on the first ballot no one has a majority, then from the two highest the choice shall be made. And if in case of a tie vote, the choice shall be made by the nations, the Envoys of each shall cast one vote.

SECTION 3. 

In case of the removal of the President from office, or his death, resignation or inability to discharge the powers and duties of said office, the same shall devolve upon the Vice-President, and the Congress may by law provide for the case of removal, death, resignation or inability of both the President and the Vice-President, declaring what officer shall then act as President, until the next regular assemblage of the Congress, when a President and Vice-President shall be elected for the regular term.

SECTION 4. 

Before the President shall enter on the execution of his office, he shall take the following oath, or affirmation:-"I do solemnly swear, or affirm, that I will faithfully execute the office of President of the World State, and to the best of my ability, preserve, protect and defend the 

Constitution of the World State."

SECTION 5. 

The compensation or salary paid to the President and other officials of the World State for their services, shall be determined by the Congress. 

ARTICLE SECTION 1. 

FOUR. 

The President shall be Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the World State, and of the Armed Forces of the Several Nations. 

He shall make report each year to the Congress upon their convening, of the state of the World Affairs, and of the several nations. 

He may require the opinion in writing of the principal officer in each Department, upon any subject relating to the duties of the respective offices, and he shall have power to grant reprieves and pardons, for offenses against the World State, except in case of impeachment. 16

He shall have power, by and with the advice and consent of the Congress to appoint all other officers of the World State whose appointments are not herein otherwise provided for: But the Congress may by law vest the appointment of such inferior officers, as they think proper, in the President alone, in the Courts of Law, in the Administrative Boards, or in the Heads of the Departments.

He shall have power to fill up all vacancies that may happen when the Congress is not in session, which appointments shall expire on the first day of April next following.

SECTION 2. 

The President, Vice-President and all other officials of the World State shall be removed from office on impeachment for, and conviction of treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors.

The President shall have the authority to ask for the resignation, or to demand the discharge of any appointed officer of the World State ad interim, submit his reasons for such action to the Congress, such to wait their concurrence to be made permanent. 

ARTICLE FI V E. 

SECTION 1. 

The Judicial power of the World State shall be vested in one Supreme Court, six Continental appelate Courts, and such Inferior Courts as the Congress may ordain and establish in the Several Nations. 

SECTION 2. 

The judicial power shall extend to all cases in law and equity arising under this Constitution, the Laws of the World State, and treaties made, or which have been made between the Nations and validated by the Congress.

In all cases between one or more of the Nations or the World State. 

To all cases of admiralty and maritime jurisdiction of the High Seas. 

To controversies to which the World State shall be a party, to controversies between two or more nations, between a nation and citizens of the same nation holding proprietary claims under a different nation. 

SECTION 3. 

In all cases between the nations, or between a nation or nations, and the World State, the Supreme Court shall have original Jurisdiction. In all the other cases before mentioned the Supreme Court shall have appelate jurisdiction, both as to law and fact, with such exceptions, and under such regulation as the Congress shall make. 

SECTION 4. 

The Code of Justice to be used by the Supreme and Appelate Courts shall be determined by the Congress; but the Code of Justice used in the 

Inferior Courts shall be that of the nation wherein such Court shall be seated 17

SECTION 5. 

Treason against the World State or of the Several Nations shall consist only of levying war against them, in adhering to their enemies, giving them aid and comfort, or by agitation, perversion or other means they shall undertake to overthrow the present regime, or by coup, conspiracy, subversion, or alienation of allegiance to destroy or prevent the peaceful or orderly pursuits of the people.

SECTION 6. 

The Congress shall have power to declare the punishment for treason, and of all other crimes and misdemeanors which shall come under its jurisdiction.

ARTICLE SI X. 

SECTION 1. 

Full faith and credit shall be given in each nation to the public acts, records, and judicial proceedings of every other nation, and to those of the World State. And the Congress may by general laws prescribe the manner in which such acts, records, and proceedings shall be proved, and the effect thereof.

SECTION 2. 

The Congress may by law provide for the calling of a Legislative Convention composed of representatives from the Several Nations; the business of such convention shall be to put in form and effect measures for enactment whereby the General Laws may be made as uniform as possible throughout the World; and submit them to the Legislative Bodies of the Several Nations.

SECTION 3. 

A person charged in any nation with treason, felony, or other crime, who shall flee from justice and be found in another nation shall, on demand of the executive authority of the nation from which he fled, be delivered up to be removed to the nation having jurisdiction of the crime. 

ARTICLE SEVEN. 

SECTION 1. 

An Executive Council, composed of not over thirty members, shall be appointed by the President, not more than one from any one nation, whose duty it shall be, under the supervision of the President, to formulate the policies, and lay out the course of action whereby the affairs of the World State may be efficiently and economically administered, the Welfare of the Several Nations promoted, and the Peace of the World preserved.

It shall also be the office of the Executive Council to furnish the Congress with information, and recommend to their consideration such measures as they may judge necessary and expedient. 18

SECTION 2. 

In and for each Continent there shall be appointed by the President a Board of Administrators whose office shall be to administer the business affairs, and supervise, direct and control all promotions, projects, works or institutions which the Congress shall sanction and support whereby the employment, livelihood and welfare of the peoples of the Continents may be advanced and assured.

ARTICLE EIG H T. 

SECTION 1. 

Nations may be admitted by the Congress into this Federation, but no new nation shall be formed or erected within the jurisdiction of any nation; nor any nation be formed of two or more nations without the consent of the Legislatures of the nations concerned. 

SECTION 2. 

The Congress shall have power to dispose of and make all needful rules and regulations respecting territories, possessions, dependencies, or isolated lands of small population which shall be made subject to the 

World State. 

SECTION 3. 

The Congress shall establish an International Court of Claims to equitably adjust and make settlement of all claims beholding as against and between the Several Nations before they became federated in the World State. And nothing in this Constitution shall be so construed as to prejudice any such claim.

SECTION 4. 

The World State shall guarantee to the peoples of each and every nation in this Federation the full right to choose the form of government under which they will live; and it shall protect them in the exercise of their sovereign rights, the execution of their civic authorities, and, on application of the Legislature of any nation, or of the Executive (when the legislature cannot be convened) against domestic violence. 

SECTION 5. 

The Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof, or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press, or the right of the people or the representatives of any nation to peaceably assemble and petition for a redress of grievances.

SECTION 6. 

The powers not delegated to the World State by this Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the Nations, are reserved to the Nations respectively, or to the peoples thereof. 19

SECTION 7. 

The judicial power of the World State shall not be construed to extend to any suit in law or equity commenced or prosecuted against one of the Several Nations by citizens or subjects of another nation. 

SECTION 8. 

Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment of crime, whereof the party shall have been duly convicted shall exist within any nation, or in territory under the jurisdiction of the World State.

ARTICLE NINE. 

SECTION 1. 

Once each year at such time and place as the Congress may by law provide, the President, Vice-President, Executive Council, the Administrative Boards, the members of the Supreme and Continental Appelate Courts, and the Envoys of the Nations shall meet in concurrent session, for the purpose of considering Amendments to this Constitution, which by two-thirds vote they shall be passed. Amendments may be proposed by the Executive Council, by the Administrative Boards, by the Congress, or by petition of one hundred Envoys. The President shall be the presiding officer of the Constitutional Assembly. 

SECTION 2. 

This Constitution, and the laws of the World State, and all treaties validated by the Congress, shall be the supreme law of the World, and the judges in every nation shall be bound thereby, anything in the Constitution or laws of any nation to the contrary notwithstanding. 

SECTION 3. 

The Envoys, Executive, Administrative, and Judicial Officers of the 

World State, and all public officials of the Several Nations shall be bound by oath or affirmation to support this Constitution. But no religious test shall be required as a qualification to any office or public trust under the 

World State. 

SECTION 4. 

All treaties, alliances, agreements, leagues or contracts between the several nations, which were entered into before the establishment of the World State, and which do not contravene. the Articles of this Constitution, shall remain in force and effect until validated or invalidated by the Congress.

ARTICLE TE N. 

SECTION 1. 

The ratification and acceptance of thirty nations shall be sufficient for the establishment of this Constitution for the World State, as between the nations so ratifying the same.
