EMPLOYMENT TRENDS ST LOUIS AREA CURRENT SITUATION AND OUTLOOK 

The high production and employment records of 1947 were maintained in 1948. Total non-agricultural employment in the St. Louis Labor Market 

Area, (includes the City of St. Louis, St. Louis 

County, St. Charles and Portage des Sioux Townships in St. Charles County, Missouri & Madison & St. Clair Counties in Illinois) excluding selfemployed, unpaid family workers, and domestic service workers, was estimated to be approximately 672,000 in October. (Estimate revised since previous statement). Total employment in the manufacturing industries climbed to a peak of 

268,400. Unemployment showed a corresponding decline.

During the last two months of the year the labor market situation assumed a slightly different aspect. Department store sales lagged behind 

1947 in November, but picked up during the four weeks preceding Christmas. The number of claims filed for unemployment benefits increased and have been running considerably higher than for the corresponding period in 1947. In addition to reductions in seasonal industries, a number of layoffs occurred in manufacturing firms. TRENDS IN MAJOR INDUSTRIES 

Prospects for the St. Louis Area, however, are not too discouraging. Federal Reserve forecasts for retail sales in the first half of 1949 are optimistic. Construction, which reached a total of 

38,000 workers in September, is expected to resume a high level of activity next summer. Peak employment on the new bridge across the Mississippi River will be reached late in 1949. It is hoped that the new veterans' hospital in St. Louis will also get under way. Despite the layoffs in the latter part of November and in December, and excluding a brief shutdown of two General Motors plants for model changes at the end of the year, it is believed that employment in the manufacturing industries held up fairly well during the quarter. This was because the shoe and apparel factories, which had been slack, began to pick up in December; because employment was seasonally high in most of the packing houses; and because production on government contracts in the aircraft industry resulted in a substantial gain. LABOR SURPLUS Total unemployment in the St. Louis Labor 

Market Area declined to the low level of 22,200 in 

November. Female unemployment dropped to 

9,700. Due to the post-holiday slump in retail trade and other employment reductions, unemployment increased sharply by the end of the year and may exceed 30,000 by the middle of January, as high school graduates are added to the number of available workers. The surplus includes men and women in the older age groups; construction workers; truck drivers; female salespersons; and salesmen without cars. Young men subject to the draft also find difficulty in getting jobs.

LABOR SHORTAGES 

Layoffs of qualified workers plus a tapering off of demand have greatly relieved any critical labor shortages which have existed in the area during the past year, including office workers and power sewing machine operators. However, there is still a need for experienced power sewing machine operators, as well as for skilled shoe workers, auto mechanics, auto body repairmen, tool and die makers, printers, skilled machinists, and ornamental iron workers.

GENERAL EMPLOYMENT TRENDS 

Total non-agricultural employment in the area was approximately 0.9 percent higher in November, 1948 than in 1947. Manufacturing industries showed a net rise of 0.8 percent during the year. Construction is slightly lower than in 1947. The November, 1948 level of trade and service employment is about 3.9 percent above the 1947 figures.

NON-AGRICULTURAL EMPLOYMENT NOVEMBER 

1948 Estimates, prepared in cooperation with the U. 

S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, show that employment in manufacturing industries declined 2600 from October to a total of 347,200 in November.

While employment in durable goods showed noticeable fluctuations, the end result revealed only a slight decrease, since losses in fabricated metals, nonelectrical machinery, lumber and timber products, and scientific, photographic and optical goods were largely offset by gains in electrical machinery, transportation equipment, and stone, clay and glass. 

Manufacturing employment in nondurable goods suffered a loss of 2400 between October 15th and 

November 15th. The leather industry dropped 1800, and textile mills and apparel 600. A slight loss in chemicals was compensated by a slight gain in foods. 

A comparison of November 1948 with November 1947 shows a decline of 5500 in manufacturing employment, 700 in durable and 4800 in nondurable goods. A glance at the percentage changes in the right hand column of the accompanying table shows a very wide range of fluctuations in employment durable goods during the year. Nonmanufacturing employment (excluding con tract construction and domestic service) in Novem ber totaled 746,000, a decrease of 4500 from Oct ober. The loss was shared by all major group except trade. Railroads dropped 2200 and govern ment 2100.

GIVE THEM A BREAK The Missouri State Employment Service is greatly handicapped in placing older workers by the age limits on practically all employers' orders. 

In many cases there are no apparent reasons for these limitations but the reasons most frequently heard are employers' insurance and pension plans. 

Since the great majority of employers offer neither one nor the other, we may assume they have been infected with a "bug-a-boo" germ. The age limit may appear logical where strong backs and quick hands are requisites but there are thousands of jobs where brains are more desirable than brawn.

Most of our opportunities for placing older workers are the result of downgrading in which the worker loses the use of part or all of his skill. A review of recent placements of older workers shows railroad engineers, skilled mechanics and executives placed as watchmen. Job Counseling has been of little use for displaced older workers because there are so few fields that will accept them. 

One case, however, which transpired in the Kansas City Area can be recited as an exception to this general rule. That is the case of a machinist who was separated from his employment because of his age (64). There was an order for an Orthopedic Technician which required some machinist background to shape and fit metal artificial appliances. This older mechanic was referred and hired and proved so successful that he is now used by the employer as an instructor for other workers. It is hoped he will not be displaced by those he trains. 

Employer relaxation of hiring requirements insofar as age is concerned will do much to aid in the recruitment and selection of qualified employees. 

19 PERCENT OF EMPLOYEES WITH AGENCY OVER TEN YEARS 

Of the eight hundred and sixty five employes of the Division of Employment Security, one hundred and sixty two or nineteen percent have served the Agency ten or more years. Every major operation in the Headquarters office is supervised by a ten year man. Of the thirty one local offices, sixteen are supervised by men with ten or more years of experience.

Within the long term group, forty two percent are male and fifty eight percent are female. One hundred and five are found in local offices and fifty seven in the Headquarters office.

EMPLOYMENT TRENDS 

KANSAS 

CITY AREA 

Unemployment in the Kansas City area increased at the rate of about a thousand per month during the last quarter of 1948 despite the fact that seasonal employment in wholesale and retail establishments increased 2500 or slightly over 2-1/2 percent. Meat packing was the only major manufacturing industry to show any gain in employment and most non-manufacturing industries had decreases in the total number of workers employed. 

Construction had the largest percentage as well as numerical loss which, of course, is the usual seasonal trend. Weather conditions at the end of the year and early in January increased construction layoffs. Railroads have practically discontinued hiring extra gangs because weather conditions are extremely bad for outside work and the turnover in this line is so great that total employment is declining rapidly.

More than 10,000 new applications for jobs were taken by the Kansas City, Missouri offices during the quarter and this was double the number taken last year. Unemployment compensation claims filed during the first week of the first quarter of 1949 were 93% greater than those filed in the first week of the last quarter of 1948. 

As the new year (1949) starts, the employment outlook is indefinite. It appears weather conditions will cause more layoffs in construction at the same time trade establishments are making seasonal layoffs, thus combining two important factors to increase unemployment. Few new labor demands appear in the manufacturing industries but on the contrary some layoffs are anticipated because supplies of goods are catching up with demands in some lines.

BENEFIT APPEALS IN REVIEW As of January 1, 1948, the Appeals Tribunal of the Division, had pending before it 788 benefit appeals matters. During the year 1948 it received 

7,034 new matters, and disposed of a total of 7,181 matters. Of the total dispositions, 5,067 concerned benefit appeal matters under the provisions of the Missouri Unemployment Compensation Law and 2,114 related to the Servicemen's Readjustment Allowance Act. As of January 1, 1949, there were 641 benefit appeal cases pending.

The workload represented by the 7,181 benefit appeal matters was processed by seven appeals referees headquartered in the Jefferson City Central Office who traveled the entire State. 

During 1948, 513 benefit appeal cases were appealed from the Appeals Tribunal to the Industrial Commission. The Commission, during the year, disposed of 544 such cases. Of this total number, 4 cases were appealed from the Industrial Commission to the Circuit Court.

The Appeals Tribunal also heard and made findings of fact for the Director of the Division in 107 contribution matters during 1948. Of these matters, 24 were appealed to the Industrial Commission and were all disposed of. During the year, 9 employer contribution cases were appealed to the Circuit Court from the Industrial Commission decisions.

Both the Appeals Tribunal and the Industrial Commission make every effort to dispose of all pending matters as promptly as possible. Because of the very large number of cases it must handle, (about 600 a month), the Appeals Tribunal has adopted a policy of granting continuances only for the most essential reasons.

DISQUALIFICATIONS UNDER THE LAW 

During 1948, 18,285 individuals were disqualified from receiving Unemployment Compensation

Benefits under the Statutatory Provisions of the Missouri Unemployment Compensation Law. This is 8.5 percent of all initial claims filed. During 

1947, 13,835 disqualifications were applied out of 242,055 initial claims and in 1946, 16,698 disqualifications were levied against 307,673 initial claims. 

Measured as a percent of disqualifications to initial claims for 1947 and 1946 reflects 5.7 and 5.4 respectively. Of considerable significance in comparing 1948 to 1947 and 1946 is the fact that disqualifications in 1947 were only 75.7 percent as large as 

1948 and in 1946 were only 91.3 percent as large as 

1948; whereas, initial claims taken in 1948 were 

69.9 percent of 1946 and in 1947 were 78.7 percent of 1946. It thus can be conclusively stated that the increase in disqualifications is not the result of increased claims activity. In fact the exact reverse is true insofar as 1948 is concerned. A substantial portion of the increase in disqualification in 1948 results from the application of the provisions of House Bill No. 466 enacted by the Mis souri General Assembly and which became effective on July 18, 1948. A study of the accompanying table reflecting disqualification experience during the last three years will indicate the immediate effects of the Law change beginning in the third quarter. 

Section 9431-1; Paragraphs (a), (b), and (c) were so re-written to substantially strengthen the penalties for voluntary quitting, discharge for misconduct, and failure to be available for suitable work and to accept suitable work.

Section 9431-1 before amendment read as follows: 

"An individual shall be disqualified for benefits under the following conditions and in each case the weeks of such disqualification shall be deducted from the benefit period and his wages credits charged correspondingly as if benefits had been paid, whether or not such individual obtains other employment during such weeks of disqualification. (a) for the week in which he has left work voluntarily without good cause, if so found by the commission, and for not more than the four weeks which immediately follow such week, as determined by the commission according to the circumstances in each

NON-AGRICULTURAL EMPLOY- MENT DECEMBER 

1948

Estimates, prepared in cooperation with the U. 

S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, indicate that Missouri employment in manufacturing industries dropped slightly from November to a total of 

345,500 in December.

While employment in most durable goods industries tended to decline or remain steady, trans-

portation equipment picked up 700 workers during the month.

Nondurable goods as a whole lost 700 employees between November and December, but contracting trends were evident among the various industries of the group. Manufacturers and processors of food employed 700 fewer workers in December; paper, printing, chemicals, and miscellaneous manufacturing were all down slightly, but textiles and apparel picked up 200 workers, and 500 were added in leather.

The annual picture shows a drop of 6200 manufacturing employees since December 1947. In durable goods, fabricated metals lost 600 during the year, electrical machinery lost 2200, machinery other than electrical 700, and lumber and timber products 1300. These losses were offset to some extent by a gain of 3400 in transportation equipment and slight gains in other groups, resulting in a net loss of only 1100 for the durable goods group as a whole. ily. Only foods, and the paper and printing group remained steady.

Trends in nonmanufacturing employment were upward, both for the month and for the year. December 1948 nonmanufacturing employment was 

17,200 above that of November. The increase is accounted for by seasonal pickup in railroad traffic, retail sales, and government employment in the post office department. A comparison of December 1948 nonmanufacturing employment with December 1947 shows an overall increase of 8200 workers. Mining dropped 1100; railroads and bus transportation were each down slightly, but an increase of 1000 in public utilities more than offset this.

In trade, a loss of 5700 in retail was partially offset by a gain in wholesale. Finance, insurance and real estate was down 2400, but service picked up 900. The most significant gain was in government employees, both state and federal. This increase of more than 10,000 since December 1947 more than offsets all losses in other groups of nonmanufacturing as well as manufacturing, resulting in a gain of 2000 in total employment during the year.

NON-AGRICULTURAL EMPLOYMENT JANUARY 1949 

January employment estimates, prepared in cooperation with the U. S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, show a decrease, both in manufacturing and non-manufacturing industries. 

Manufacturing employment was down 3500 from December to 342,000 in January. The decline was evident in both durable and non-durable goods but was heavier percentage-wise in the manufacture of durable goods, where transportation equipment was the only group to show an employment gain. 

The lumbering industry lost 800 workers, fabricated metals, and stone, clay and glass, each lost 600, and the two groups of machinery each lost 400. In nondurable goods, the leather industry picked up 800 employees and textiles and apparel 400, while all other groups suffered declines, ranging from 200 in chemicals to 1500 in foods. The loss of employment in food manufacturing was sharpest in beverages, but was reflected also in candy, bakery goods, and meat packing. A comparison of manufacturing employment in January 1949 with January 1948 reveals a drop of 7800, of whom 6400 were workers in nondurable goods. Nearly 4000 employees were lost in the leather industry during the year, while food lost 700, and the miscellaneous group 1400. 

The monthly trend in non-manufacturing was sharply downward, January employment dropping 35,600 from December. The loss affected all major and minor groups except finance, insurance, and real estate which remained steady. While the decrease is largely seasonal, reflecting the return to normal from the seasonal December high in retail trade, transportation, and government postal workers, a glance at the comparable change from December 1947 to January 1948, shows that the employment decrease is more than seasonal. The after 

Christmas drop was nearly 10,000 greater this year than last. Moreover, percentage changes from January 1948 to January 1949 shows losses of employment in most lines. Exceptions are public utilities, wholesale trade, finance, insurance, and real estate, and government.

APRIL 1949 

DIPLOMA TIME IS HIRING TIME 

This year an estimated 30,000 students will be graduated from Missouri high schools. Of this number about 1/4 will enter college and a smaller portion of the remainder will not be in the labor market because of the girls getting married, personal choice, and varied other reasons. All of this means that after graduation day this spring approximately 20,000 young men and women will be available for employment in Missouri's industries, businesses, and on Missouri's farms on a full-time basis. 

The Missouri State Employment Service has a plan for facilitating the entrance of these young people into full-time, summer, and part-time productive status as follows: (1) Employers will be visited in an effort to determine what their needs will be for both summer and full-time workers. (2) Many high school seniors will hear an Employment Service representative explain how the Employment Service can help them find the job for which they are best suited. (3) Those students who are interested in jobs will be interviewed by trained Employment Service personnel and classified according to temporary work experience, training, vocational interest, type and primary interests. (4) 

Employer orders and applicant qualifications and interests will be compared so that the most suitable applicants can be referred for the most desirable jobs.

Many advantages accrue to the employer who hires these new entrants into the labor force. Most high school graduates are co-operative, academic, and eager to learn. Many have manual skills and training in the operation of business machines. All have had academic training to help them become adjusted in their communities with an understanding of their responsibilities as citizens. 

Yes, Mr. Employer, you can help your business, a young man or woman, and your community by placing an order for one or more of these young workers with the State Employment office in your local area.

For the school year ending June 30, 1948 there were 7,711 men and women graduated from Missouri colleges and universities. Of this total 6,574 received bachelors degrees, 639 masters degrees, and 404 doctors degrees. There were 94 non-degree graduates. 

Yes, Mr. Employer, you had quite an investment in those graduates of last year and you have the same investment in this years graduates. Last year and every year many of these Missouri people trained in Missouri colleges take their priceless human resources out of our state.

EMPLOYMENT TRENDS ST LOUIS AREA Current Situation and Outlook 

Unemployment in the area continued to be high during the first quarter of 1949, although it is believed to have reached a crest sometime in February. (Area definition includes the City of St. Louis, St. Louis County, St. Charles and Portage des Sioux 

Townships in St. Charles County, Missouri & Madison & St. Clair Counties in Illinois.) The rise in unemployment began about the middle of November, 1948, and was attributed to seasonal changes plus an unexpected wave of cutbacks in manufacturing industries. A number of reasons were given for the layoffs, but the principal reason was probably the increasing availability of raw materials and manufactured goods and a drop in consumer demand. 

Although total non-agricultural employment in the area dropped about 2,000 between January 15 and March 15, 1949, the present level of employment is high, as compared with this time last year. Employment trends in the next six months should be generally upward. 

Trends in Major Industries

Sharp reductions in some of the manufacturing industries continued during the quarter. Employment losses were heavy in primary metals; fabricated metal products (particularly stove factories); non-electrical machinery. 

The net drop of 1,600 in manufacturing employment between January 15 and March 15 occurred largely in the Illinois Section of the area. In the 

Missouri Section, where layoffs were equally heavy, there were the following counterbalancing factors: staff expansion in automobiles and aircraft; staffing of the new Simplicity Dress Pattern plant; seasonal increases in shoe factories and breweries (many of these being recalls). On the East Side, the only comparable rise occurred in the fertilizer plants, which operate on a seasonal basis.

Construction employment did not show any appreciable net change during the quarter, due to weather conditions, and was estimated to be about 32,000 in March. During the last two weeks of the quarter, construction in St. Louis and St. Louis County was virtually tied up by a jurisdictional strike of construction laborers, which has been settled. Prospects are fairly good except for the high cost of residential building. Low rental living quarters continue to be very scarce.

Construction employment showed a slight rise in the Illinois section, where there are several large projects in progress, including the canal (to by-pass the Chain of Rocks) and the new bridge.

Wholesale and retail trade was slow during the period, showing some pick-up in March. Weather conditions, expectation of lower prices, and a late Easter were probably responsible. 

Several hundred miners are reported to be unemployed in the neighborhood of Belleville and 

Edwardsville, Illinois. Mines have closed due to lack of demand for the quality of coal found in this area. Not reflected in the figures of total unemployment were the large number of temporary layoffs, such as those due to the strike of operating Wabash Railroad employees, which lasted about ten days.

A number of firms in the area have tried to avoid excessive layoffs by working less than the scheduled 40 hours per week. 

Labor Surplus and Shortages 

Non-agricultural unemployment in the area was estimated at approximately 39,200 on March 15. This represents roughly about 5 percent of the non-agricultural civilian labor force. The labor force was augmented during the quarter by a number of graduates, and veterans dropping out of school, but there were also a significant number of withdrawals to rural areas and a few military enlistments.

Recent layoffs have alleviated most labor shortages with the exception of stenographers, typists, and office machine operators. Salaries for the group range from $150 per month to $250 per month. 

The need for power sewing machine operators and skilled shoe stitchers is not urgent. At the present time there is a demand for landscape gardeners and strawberry pickers. Farm hands are also needed in the Illinois portion of the area. Domestic service workers are still scarce. Occasional openings exist for men possessing high and unusual skills. 

Pharmacists are still in demand.

Employers are becoming more rigid in their specifications as to age, place of residence, education, and work history, generally demanding a "higher type applicant". Surpluses include men and women in the slightly older age groups; young people without experience; construction workers (temporary); semi-skilled machine operators (male and female); truck drivers; bartenders; "hash house" waitresses; stove mounters and foundry laborers (Belleville); sales and general office clerks.

NON-AGRICULTURAL EMPLOYMENT FEBRUARY 1949 

February employment estimates, prepared in cooperation with the U. S. Bureau of Labor Statis tics, show slight declines from January, in manufacturing as well as nonmanufacturing industries. 

Manufacturing employment was down 3200 to 338,800 in February. The decline in durable goods was slight compared with employment losses in nondurable goods industries, but only one group in either section made any employment gains. Transportation equipment picked up 600 workers, chiefly in aircraft and railroad equipment, to partially offset losses in all other durable goods industries. In nondurable goods, foods and leather each lost over a thousand employees, but a gain of 800 textile workers acted as a compensating factor. The decrease of employment in food manufacturing was chiefly in the meat packing industry but was shared by confectionery and beverages also.

The annual picture of manufacturing employment shows an overall decline of 3 percent since February 1948. Losses in nondurable goods were about twice as heavy as in durable goods, where a pick up of 4500 employees in the manufacture of transportation equipment offset more than half the losses in all other durable goods industries. Sharpest downward trends in the manufacturing group during the year were found in electrical machinery, and in leather products. The monthly trend in nonmanufacturing employment was also downward. Interstate railroads lost 1100 employees during the month, retail trade 3300 and government 1900. The annual picture in nonmanufacturing employment is more encouraging. 

While employment in mining, transportation and retail trade, is below 1948 levels, public utilities, the finance, insurance and real estate group, and government have each made substantial gains during the year. Wholesale trade and service have made only slight gains, but the resulting over all trend in nonmanufacturing employment is almost at the same level as a year ago.

Missouri Manufacturing Employment

National average for March.

Electrical machinery took the sharpest cut in this group. In nondurable goods only foods and apparel show employment gains within the year. Leather sustained the heaviest employment loss.

EMPLOYMENT HIGHER FOR THIS SUMMER BUT NO SHORTAGES FORECAST 

Total employment will be higher than the present level this summer as construction, railroad maintenance and agricultural employment come into their own. Two new manufacturers have begun hiring and the two firms shut down due to a strike in Detroit will swing back into production with the end of that strike. Total employment is not expected to reach the high point this summer that was reached last summer.

Although demand will be some greater in the next few months, it is not expected that there will be any shortage. Surpluses of truck drivers, unskilled laborers for light work, office workers with too little experience and older salespersons are particularly large. 

In general, employment will be high by any prewar standard but with the immediate prospect of a lower level than that of the post-war high reached in 1948.

FRAUD PREVENTION AND DETECTION 

Operations of the fraud prevention and detection unit of the Division of Employment Security during the first five months of 1949 has resulted in uncovering 7181 cases of fraudulent and erroneous payment of benefits. The amount of money represented by these cases is $417,193 of which $177,093 is in readjustment allowances to ex-service men. 

Direct fraud constitutes but a small portion of the total amount erroneously paid out, and when processed, these cases are remanded to the courts for prosecution. Of the 94 unemployment compensation fraud cases referred to the courts to date, the Division has obtained 70 convictions. Eleven cases await disposition. 

During the first five months of 1949, the Division has effected restitution of fraud and overpayments in the amount of $280,946, of which $142,177 is in cash, the balance in restitution agreements by off-setting against subsequent benefits.

To date, there is an uncollected balance of $1,029,810 charged to the trust fund account and which represents 13,912 processed cases. The major portion of this amount will eventually be credited to the trust fund by direct restitution. 

Employer accounts and merit rating status are protected and adjusted to employer advantage in those cases of processed fraud and erroneous overpayment. The burden of proof of overpayment and fraud rests with the Division and in those processed cases whereby overpayment for any reason is established, the employer account is credited with the total amount of overpayment.

Although the Division assumes the primary responsibility for the prevention and detection of fraud in unemployment compensation benefit payments, it cannot hope to succeed unless it has the cooperation of employers and the general public. Those interested in the protection of individual employer accounts and in the trust fund are urged to notify the Division of suspected fraudulent claimants. Information to the Division will, of course, be held in strict confidence and in no case will the source of information be revealed. A simple statement giving the name of the suspected claimant is all that is necessary, investigators of the Division will process and develop all information required to determine the legitimacy of the claim.

NON-AGRICULTURAL EMPLOYMENT APRIL 1949 

Downward Trend in Manufacturing

Employment is Accelerated 

Manufacturing employment in Missouri was down 

2.1 percent in April to a total of 330,600, marking the sharpest monthly decline of the downward trend which began last November.

All durable goods industries sustained cuts, and most nondurable groups as well. Chemicals and leather made only slight gains, while the miscellaneous nondurable groups remained steady. The metal industries took rather heavy cuts, while an important segment of the transportation equipment industry had layoffs due to material shortages resulting from a strike at the parent company in Detroit. 

Textile mills and apparel lost 2,000 workers during the month. 

Hours and Earnings Edge Downward 

Average weekly hours and average weekly earnings for April were slightly below March in total manufacturing, although weekly earnings in durable goods were 17 cents higher. Hours worked dropped at a faster rate than weekly earnings, resulting in a slight increase in average hourly earnings.

Non-Manufacturing Employment Swings Upward 

In April

An increase of 8,800 workers in non-manufacturing was shared by all major groups except mining. 

A decided gain in interstate railroads more than offset slight losses in other transportation, and in public utilities. Although wholesale trade fell off a few hundred, retail trade added more than 7,000 2

workers between March 15 and April 15. Retail apparel and general merchandise accounted for more than half this increase, but drug stores and retail goods and liquor stores also reflected substantial gains in employment. Trends in finance, insurance and real estate are also upward.

Employment Levels Are Still Below 1948 

Although 1949 trends are still below those of was 1948, the gap between the two annual trends in nonmanufacturing is narrowing. Trade in April close to the 1948 level, while government employment, public utilities and the finance, insurance, and real estate groups had all climbed above last year's level. Although employment in mining shows a high percentage decrease, this field does not constitute a very large component of the total non-manufacturing employment picture.

NON-AGRICULTURAL EMPLOYMENT 

Downward Trend in Manufacturing Employment is Retarded. 

While manufacturing employment was down 

2100 from April to a total of 328,500 in May, the rate of decline was only 0.6 per cent compared with a 2.1 per cent decrease between March and April.

Employment in durable goods was up 1500, due almost entirely to resumption of operations in a motor company after a strike in April. Except for transportation equipment, all other durable goods groups except fabricated metals lost employment in May.

In nondurable goods, only foods gained employment. A pickup of 2000 workers here was more than offset by layoffs in textiles, leather, and other major industry groups. Paper, printing and publishing showed no significant change. MAY 1949 

Hours and Earnings in Manufacturing are Up in May. 

Average weekly earnings in Missouri factories in May were 32 cents higher than in April, due largely to increased earnings in the automobile industry. Lumber, furniture, machinery, textiles, printing and several other major industry groups also showed increases in their weekly pay checks.

Apparel, leather and primary metals showed reductions in weekly earnings.

Weekly hours were up in most durable goods industries, particularly in transportation equipment, but hours in nondurable goods suffered a slight drop.

The trend in average hourly earnings was upward in both durable and nondurable goods. Average hourly earnings in Missouri for all manufacturing in May was $1.330 compared with a national 2

average of $1.375. Weekly earnings of $51.50 was approximately $1.50 below the national average.

Average hours worked in the state were slightly above the national average. 

Non-Manufacturing Employment Turns Downward. 

Following a definite gain in April, nonmanufacturing employment showed a reverse trend in May, due to sharp losses in trade, and a lesser décline in interstate railroads. While retail trade showed the normal drop following the Easter boom of April,

May employment was still considerably above the 

March level.

The drop in railroad employment was more than offset by increases in other transportation, and in public utilities. Gains in service, government, and the finance, insurance, and real estate group were not enough to offset the heavy loss in trade. 

Trends are Still Below 1948 Levels.

Employment as a whole is still definitely below last year's level, although in spots, 1949 trends are higher. This is particularly true in transportation equipment where 1949 employment is more than 30 per cent higher than in 1948. Foods also have climbed above the 1948 level, as well as government employment, and the finance, insurance and real estate group. Heaviest percentage losses are found in metals, machinery, and lumbering.

Voluntary Contributions 

Calculations Simplified

By Regulation Revision 

A recent revision of Regulation No. 26, reproduced herewith, details information which will allow employers to make voluntary payments during the fifteen (15) day period, December 22, 1949 to

January 5, 1950, inclusive. Such payments will be used in calculating contribution rates for the calendar year 1950.

As a result of this revision, the Division will now be in a position to actually calculate contribution rates for 1950 and mail "Experience Rating 

Statements", Form MODES 527, to all employers prior to the period during which voluntary payments may be accepted (About December 1, 1949). Employers will then have a complete statement of their account, including their reserve balance and experience ratio. This information will greatly facilitate employer determinations on the advisability of making voluntary payments and should tend to eliminate errors as to the adequacy of such payments.

Voluntary payments made during the period provided by Regulation No. 26 will be credited to the employer's account as of the calculation date and the employer's rate will be recalculated. Amended 

"Experience Rating Statements", Form MODES 527, reflecting the recalculation, will be prepared and mailed to such employers.

In view of the fact that employers will be furnished with a complete statement of their account prior to the period for making voluntary payments, the Division will not forward quarterly notification of benefit charges to those employers whose accounts have not been charged.

NON-AGRICULTURAL Manufacturing Employment Edges Upward 

Manufacturing employment in Missouri rose slightly in June to a total of 330,900. 

Although fabricated metals and electrical machinery each added a few hundred workers, the durable goods group as a whole lost employment during the month. Primary metals dropped 500 workers machinery other than electrical dropped 400, and stone, clay and glass 300. Transportation equipment which has been moving upward all year except for the April strike recession is now showing a tendency to level off.

The non-durable goods industries gained 3500 workers in spite of slight losses in several groups. 

The most significant June increase was in leather goods which reported 3000 more workers than in May. A part of this apparent increase, however, was due to reopening of plants closed down temporarily in May for mass vacations, inventories, and repairs. Foods picked up 600 workers in dairying, canning, and beverages.

Non-Manufacturing Also Registers A Slight Rise 

Non-manufacturing employment picked up 1100 workers in June to return to the April level.

All non-manufacturing groups shared in this gain except mining and government. Railroads and other transportation accounted for most of the employment increase. Wholesale trade added 400 workers, but retail trade remained steady. 

A comparison of June 1949 employment with that of June 1948 shows that the gap between the two annual trends has distended slightly since May. In manufacturing employment the percentage decrease from June 1948 to June 1949 was 3.8, while from May

Non-manufacturing employment showed a decline of 1.9 per cent from June to June, compared to 1.4 per cent from May to May.

Other groups showing slight annual increases are foods, government, and the finance, insurance and real estate group.

HIRE THE HANDICAPPED IT'S GOOD BUSINESS 

Public Law 176, 79th Congress, provides for the annual observance of National Employ the Handicapped Week during the first full week in October of each year. This year the "Week" will be October 2 to October 8. There will be a presidential proclamation to this effect prior to October 2. 

The handicapped are not a group set apart from their fellows in a community, they are our neighbors and our friends. Actually, everyone, in some way, is vocationally limited or handicapped, either by lack of training or talent, by preference, or by potentialities. It is obvious that while a worker may rightly be considered handicapped in one job, he may not be considered handicapped in a job where such worker is properly placed. The average physically handicapped person when properly placed will make a better showing than the average unimpaired worker---statistics prove this. Actual work records of physically handicapped workers having at least fifty percent disability in one or more of the nine types of impairment studied were matched with work records of unimpaired workers. This survey proved that the handicapped workers when properly placed are more efficient, are as stable as unimpaired workers, are reliable, are careful and are versatile.

It is estimated that about 5-1/2 million persons with significant physical handicaps are gainfully employed. From the records of these workers already employed, one fact stands out, it's the ABILITY, not the disability, that counts.

As a nation, we have been slow in understanding the employment problems of the handicapped and in realizing the benefits that come to the whole community where their capabilities are fully utilized, Much remains to be accomplished on behalf of the physically handicapped. During the week of October 

2 to October 8 a concerted attempt will be made to place before employers and the public the fact of the problem and to seek their cooperation in finding suitable jobs for the handicapped.

A further objective of these activities is the dissemination of information to make known available rehabilitation services provided for the physically handicapped and to encourage these men and women to take advantage of their rights under the law to job counseling and placement services. A suitable job and financial independence is of primary importance to the handicapped and this can be accomplished through earnest cooperation of every employer and citizen of the United States.

Two members, leaders in union organizations, represent labor. They are James A. Davis, Secretary of the Missouri State Industrial Council of the

The sixth and seventh members, with Chairman

The Advisory Council has been meeting regularly and working hard to help the Division of Employment Security solve some of the state's unemployment compensation problems. Among other things, the Council considered at length House Bill No. 262 and found objectionable features in it. Again it deliberated over the House Committee's Substitute for House Bill No. 262 and recommended additional amendments for the consideration of the House of Representatives when it resumes its legislative work the second week in November.

The Council voted unanimously to support House Committee Substitute for H.B. No. 262, if amended according to its recommendations. The decision was reached at a meeting in the Division's offices 

September 15, and the recommendations reported officially to the Division. 

The Division's Director, through personal letters, first reported the Council's action to some business and industrial associations in Missouri as early as September 20.

Following this, the Division in mid-October advised employers of the Council's unanimous action. 

This was in accordance with the laws of Missouri: 

Section 9426(d). This law directs "the division with the advice and aid of the advisory council *** to promote the re-employment of unemployed workers throughout the state in every way *** that may be feasible; and to these ends to carry on and publish the results of investigations and research studies."

The Substitute for H.B. No. 262, if amended in keeping with the Advisory Council's recommendations, would, among other things, calculate the weekly benefit amount at the existing 4 per cent rate instead of 5 per cent. It also would eliminate all charges against employers whose workers have left them voluntarily and without good cause, or who have been discharged by the employer for misconduct, or have refused reemployment.

A worker found by the Division to be in one of the foregoing three categories is now temporarily ineligible to receive weekly benefits. But under existing law an insured worker can cure his ineligibility simply by earning subsequently a sum equal to ten times his weekly benefit amount. With benefits amounting to about half of his usual weekly earnings, the worker, by obtaining employment for about a five-week period, again can be eligible for benefits. Assuming that such a worker again becomes eligible for benefits, his claims are charged against employers in his base period. The Advisory Council doesn't believe the affected employer should be charged for the benefits paid to a worker who voluntarily quit, was fired for misconduct, or who refused reemployment.

What is the "pool fund?" The "pool fund," as it is termed here, refers to monies within the Compensation Trust Fund, in excess of total contributions credited to existing employer accounts. This so-called "pool fund" in reality is merely an arbitrary administrative designation. In those cases involving benefit payments which may not rightly be placed against an employer account, the charges are, in effect, assessed against the "pool fund.' The growth of the "pool fund" is attributable mainly to interest earned on Missouri's account in the federal unemployment trust fund and by cancelled reserves of employers covered by the law, but who have gone out of business, etc. Thus these funds, to all intents and purposes, are to a degree "frozen" within the total reserve fund. The Advisory Council believes these funds should be used to the benefit of the employer as well as the worker. 

How much is in the Fund? The Missouri account in the federal unemployment trust fund now exceeds 186 million dollars. Within this total is the so-called "pool fund" which now totals about 25 million dollars. 

The same system of individual accounting of claims will be maintained under the proposed law and the employers participating in the reserve fund will continue to have available to them all information now furnished through the Division. There definitely would be no secrecy about the amount of "pool" charges.

If active employers were exempted from benefit charges as provided in the pending revised bill, their reserve fund accounts no doubt would be spared charges running into hundreds of thousands of dollars annually.

ARE THEY TOO OLD? 

"Too old to fight at 40 and too old to work" -- a situation prevailing extensively at the start of World War II, but widespread publicity did much to break down such opposition. It is surprising that such an attitude has arisen again so soon during the post-war period, in view of the satisfactory employment record made by older workers during the critical labor shortage of the war years. Many workers in the age range of 40 and up are finding it difficult to obtain jobs because of age limitations maintained by some employers.

Some see irony and an economic boomerang in the fact that longer life, made possible by modern medicine and improved working and living conditions, could gravely disturb the nation's future welfare if millions from our aging work force are relegated to the scrap heap of idleness. 

All World War I veterans are well over 40 (average age 53), and a surprisingly large number of World War II veterans have passed the two score age. More than 3,000,000 U.S. workers are over 65 and the number will grow rapidly in future years. 

Are they too old? Prominent authorities in the fields of medicine, industry and labor recog- 

AGENCY ACTIVITIES 

nize the extended usefulness of the American worker's life and urge that the skills, abilities and experience of the older worker be fully utilized if our high standard of living, based on production, is to be maintained and improved.

EMPLOYMENT TRENDS KANSAS CITY AREA Total employment in the Kansas City Area increased about 500 during the third quarter although there was a loss of approximately 2000 workers in manufacturing industries. Decreased activities in the food and apparel industries along with a layoff of 600 workers in one plant in the machinery manufacturing business were responsible for most of this loss. 

By mid-September, wholesale and retail trade had started a seasonal increase reaching a total employment 2500 above the June figure.

Construction employment reached a peak in 

August and declined to the June level of 24,000 in September.

About 1500 workers were separated from the labor force when school started in September, thus bringing unemployment down to 15,000.

Employment offices in the Missouri section of the Kansas City Area received 10,749 new applications for work during the third quarter which was 14 percent less than the total received in the second quarter. The second quarter, of course, was high because of the new entries into the labor force in June. The third quarter total, however, was 58 percent above last year's figure. Placements declined 18 percent but were 16 percent above last year. There was a 2 percent increase in openings received but the total was 8 percent lower than in 

1947. There were 11,600 job applications in the active file on September 30, compared to 13,600 three months ago and 13,100 a year ago. 

As in most election years, forecasting of business and, consequently, employment is not entirely dependable. Employment reports representing 50 percent of all employment in manufacturing industries indicate total new worker requirements in these industries during the following sixty days will be about 600 and employers generally hesitate to anticipate beyond that period. Trade establishments are expected to add about 3500 workers by 

November which will be near the seasonal peak.

Construction is expected to follow the usual seasonal pattern by laying off 1500 in two months with more to follow until early Spring. These layoffs with the after Christmas layoffs in trade establishments will increase unemployment considerably at a time when it appears probable that the new lessee of the Pratt and Whitney war plant may start recruiting workers for the manufacture of jet turbines for military planes. These plans are not flnal but may require the employment of 5000 workers before the end of the next year. There will be temporary jobs during late November and December for 3000 to 5000 workers (veterans preferred) in the Post Office and Railway mail service. The regular labor demand lists have not varied greatly during the past several months. There are continuous demands for stenographers, typists, bookkeepers and experienced clerks of several types, all classes of restaurant workers, welders, good machinists, tool and die makers and a few other highly skilled shop workers, street car and bus operators, taxicab drivers, automobile mechanics with tools, factory trainees and unskilled workers under 35 or 40 years of age.

EMPLOYER CO-OPERATION WITH THE DIVISION'S EMPLOYMENT AND CLAIMS SECTIONS RESULTS IN GREAT SAVINGS TO UNEMPLOYMENT COMPENSATION RESERVE ACCOUNTS 

Much has been written recently about "chiselers" who are defrauding state unemployment compensation funds through illegal benefit claims. It is unfortunate these articles did not disclose how "chiselers' are being brought to justice and made to pay for their fraudulent activity.

Many employers in Missouri are among those who have taken the lead in co-operating with the Division of Employment Security in its effort to combat the "job insurance racket.”

Abuses Decline With Employer Co-Operation 

This co-operation extends along three lines of activity. Employers are greatly increasing use of the state's free employment services and are placing job orders where workers file benefit claims. 

Another factor aiding materially in the Division's anti-fraud campaign is that the employer is becoming more and more prompt in reporting wage payments to employees, in filing benefit payment protests, and advising the agency of voluntary quits, lay-offs and dismissals for misconduct. Likewise, the employer is co-operative in helping the division in encouraging other employers through a personal letter-writing campaign to make use of the Division's placement services. This is being done effectively in St. Louis.

General use of the Division's free employment services by all employers would have the effect of reducing benefit payment charges to employers, and eventually, should lower their experience ratings. The example is that if “Employer X" uses the service, it conceivably would help cut down benefit payments to "Employer Y's" workers, and vice versa.

Employer Co-operation Pays Off 

How this co-operation is paying off is forcefully demonstrated in figures from the Division's fraud detection unit. From January 1, 1939, to November 30, 1949, $993,268 has been uncovered in over-payments in workers' benefits. This amount has been restored to the individual employer's unemployment compensation reserve fund. Over the same period, the Division has paid out in benefits discovered fraud or over-payments is nine-tenths of one per cent for the almost 11-year period. $745,229 Restored in 23 Months 

What happened when the employer threw his full cooperative weight into the campaign against fraud?

The figures show that from January, 1948, through 

November 30, 1949---23 months---that $745,229 of the total $993,268 was uncovered and restored to the employers' unemployment compensation reserve fund. During this 23-month period the Division paid out $33,789,991 in benefit payments.

Thus the percentage of fraud found, because of greater employer co-operation, and intensified 

Division activity in the fraud detection unit, rose to 2.2 per cent.

Wilful Fraud Brings Convictions 

This year approximately 50 fraud cases were filed with prosecuting attorneys over the state, with convictions in about 87 per cert of them. There also were uncovered about 1,000 G.I. fraud cases, which were filed with the United States District Attorneys. The veterans cases are those in which the G.I. filed claims and received veterans readjustment allowances illegally.

G.I.'s Also Prosecuted 

Veterans claims paid totaled $121,751,250 on

October 1, and over-payments at that time totaled $1,157,588. The Division's fraud detection unit has recovered $447,304. Charges of the full amount now are on record against any future federal veterans payments until the over-payment is satisfied. 

There were 367 veteran fraud convictions in Missouri in 1949. 

Missouri cancelled the accounts of 413 workers in 1948 because it was found they had made illegal benefit claims. In 1949 the Division cancelled 961 employee accounts. This action resulted in confiscation of the past wage credits of the worker, on which his possible future benefits might have been determined. October Employment Holds Steady 

October estimates of non-agricultural employment in Missouri indicate no appreciable change from September. A drop of 4,300 employees in manufacturing was balanced by a rise of 4,000 in non-manufacturing, resulting in an overall change of less than one-tenth of one per cent in total nonagricultural employment between September 15 and October 15.

Steel Strike Takes A Toll in Durable Goods 

As a result of the nation-wide steel strike, employment dropped nearly 3,000 in primary metals. 

October employment in blast furnaces, steel works and rolling mills was less than 30 per cent of the figure for September. Fabricated metals added 

600 workers during the month. Electrical machinery picked up 500 workers but these gains were insufficient to offset the heavy loss in primary metals. 

Drop in Leather Products is More Than Seasonal 

Loss of employment in leather goods was industry-wide, and much greater than is customary at this season. Nearly 3,000 workers were dropped during the month. 

Foods Also Decline

In food manufacturing and processing, definite gains were reported in meat products, grain mill products, and the confectionery line, but these gains were offset by a heavy seasonal drop in beverage industries.
