PREFACE 

THIS little work does not pretend to have any literary merit, and was only undertaken to interest my grandchildren in the knowledge that their grandfather, early in life, had lived among scenes which are almost forgotten by the present generation. 

I have left to abler writers the story of the hardships as well as the poetry of the sea. 

There are many close calls and sometimes the ships come through without accident, but more often they emerge in a maze of tangled rigging and splintered spars as they limp into port. Such was not my experience but I have had my share of reefing in winter gales when the foot ropes were icy and the frozen sails, slatting in the wind, were a menace to the men aloft battling them.

By contrast, imagine a moonlit sea in the tropics with the sparkling waves slipping by [3]

the bows, a tang of salt in the air, the easy roll of the deck under foot as the ship with all sails set leaves a foaming wake. This is the picture which dwells more often in the minds of those who love the sea.

HIS is a narrative of three voyages in THIS the years 1877 to 1881 made by a Boston. boy in the ship Southern Cross, which was built in 1868 at East Boston by Curtis, Smith & 

Co. for Baker & Morrill of Boston.

This Southern Cross was built after the days of the famous clippers and was known as a half clipper, being the second ship of the same name. The first one, built by Briggs Brothers in 1851, had been captured and burned by the Confederate cruiser Florida during the Civil War. The second Southern Cross was a fullrigged main skysail yard ship of about 1130 tons, 178 feet long. The name was taken from the constellation in the southern skies somewhat resembling an old-fashioned kite, and a representation of the four stars was [و]

, ༄« { } { *,་ 

engraved on the capstans of the ship. The house flag was a red diamond on a blue field. 

As a boy I had longings to go to sea, probably inherited from my grandfather, Captain 

Thomas Stinson, who died on the island of

St. Thomas while on a voyage to the West 

Indies. Very early I began visiting the Boston wharves, it being a favorite custom of those days for boys to go on board ships to ask for foreign postage stamps, and in this way I became somewhat acquainted with ships and sailors.

Afterward, for a time, I dropped all thought of going to sea and paid more attention to my school duties. Before becoming a sailor fate seemed to destine me to be a soldier, as about this time I left the Boston High School, where I was a member of the class of 1875, and entered the Highland Military Academy at Worcester. I stayed there, however, only one year and then went to work in a wholesale dry goods store in Boston. One day my [10]

EARLY DAYS 

father said, "I just met a friend who is a sea captain, sailing for Australia in a day or two, and he asked if you would like to go to sea." 

My father told him he did not wish me to go, so, of course, the captain sailed without me.

The news of this must have reawakened my early ambition and I immediately announced that I would find a way myself to go to sea.

Within a short time, learning that the ship Southern Cross was loading a cargo of ice at Charlestown to be taken to Java, I interviewed Mr. Baker, one of the owners, who was well acquainted with our family, and asked for a chance to sail on his ship. This was granted if I could get my father's consent, which was finally given, so it was arranged that I was to be taken on the ship as a green hand to learn the profession. Another home boy, making his first voyage, was Fred Hamlen of Taunton, a minister's son.

This was the usual school for American boys who wished to become masters of ships. Our [II]

captain was Atkins Hughes of North Truro,

Massachusetts, and the chief mate was David Annable of Barnstable; both of them being sterling men of Cape Cod ancestry. Mr. 

Annable I shall always remember as one of the finest seamen I ever met, and he proved to be a capable teacher of navigation and seamanship. [12]

When the process of loading was completed and the ship about ready to sail, Fred Hamlen and I went aboard and almost before we knew it Mr. Pope, the second mate, had us piling up cord wood in the forepeak. This did not appeal to either of us as being a part of the duties of sailors, for instead of being out on the broad ocean we were in a close and stuffy between-decks working harder than ever before. We soon found, however, that second mate Pope was a friend and not a taskmaster.

After the day's work we began to look over [13]

the ship and try our skill in climbing the rigging, but it soon became dark and we retired to our room in the after-part of the deckhouse and fell asleep. The next morning we were up bright and early being awakened by Mr. Pope's call to turn out. Our first duty was to take buckets of cold water and wash the paint work before breakfast. 

May 3, 1877, was a fine day with a strong westerly breeze, and everything was being done in readiness for our departure. Stores were being brought aboard. The shore gang which had been rigging the ship left, and there was a bustle, showing that some great event was expected. This turned out to be the arrival of the owners of the ship and their families. Before this I had been pleasantly surprised to see my father appear at the ship's side laden with sundry bundles, which proved to be fruit and sweetmeats, also ten pounds of smoking tobacco. This, however, did not seem to make any impression on the officers, who kept me at various tasks until it was time for the watch to go below. Then I again changed my clothing and fell into my bunk, feeling that I had made a mistake in trying to be a sailor.

When I came on deck for the next watch things had not changed much as the wind was blowing fresh and the ship was rolling heavily.

All this time I had eaten very little, which perhaps explains that dejected feeling I had, but after the night was over, as I came on deck in the morning watch, I found the gale had blown itself out, and we were under full sail. Also I began to realize I was hungry.

The trades left us in 10° north latitude and we floated around in the doldrums about ten days waiting for the southeast trades to spring up. This was really the most tedious part of our passage, as at every light puff of wind we were obliged to trim the yards and sails in order to take advantage of it. We finally drifted across the line and one dark night we caught the trade winds in a sudden squall of wind and lightning and were off, bound for 

Tristan da Cunha and the Indian Ocean.

This island belonging to England, 1500 miles west of Cape Town, in latitude 35° south, [23]

was sighted by us one morning. In appearance it resembled a loaf of sugar standing up out of the water about 7500 feet high. We did not touch at the island, which had only a few inhabitants and is rather barren and unproductive.

It soon became cooler with stronger winds, and immense flocks of birds were to be seen flying after the ship to pick up any refuse food which might be thrown overboard. There were albatross, boobies and gulls in large numbers. Some of the sailors had a cruel kind of sport. By tying two pieces of salt pork to some twine, they would throw the line over the side. Almost immediately the birds, often two at a time, would swallow the bait and would not know enough to let go, so would be drawn on to the deck where we would have some fun with them and finally throw them back into the water. Some of the albatross were quite savage and would make [24]

frequent rushes at us. A great many had a wing spread of six feet or more.

In preparation for running down our easting the main skysail and mizzen royal yards were sent down on deck, as we were we were expecting heavy weather, which soon developed. We had a steady, strong half gale directly astern, which sent us along about thirteen or fourteen knots per hour. At the same time the seas following developed into tremendous waves and as our yards were square we rolled considerably, taking water over either rail, so that the main deck was generally awash. One had to take a chance in going aft to escape being knocked down by the seas, which was taken as a matter of course by all concerned. Sometimes the water would come into the rooms, which Fred and I occupied in the after-part of the house, by the main hatch, and we frequently had three or four inches of water running over the floor. 

One morning Captain Hughes was driving [25]

the ship which was under topsails and main topgallant sail. There had been frequent squalls, but the Captain hung on to the main topgallant sail until about the middle of the morning watch. Suddenly there was a sound like an explosion which was caused by the main lower topsail sheet parting, and immediately the sail was blown off the yard but hung by one earing and was thrashing about snapping like a whip. The iron clew of the sail struck the long-boat, which was on top of the house directly over our room, and smashed in the bottom. The main topgallant sail was stripped into shreds and all hands were called and ordered aloft to capture the topsail. I got out on the lower topsail yard and drew out my knife planning to cut the sail adrift, but the officers shouted not to do this. While the men stood in the top and on the yard, a rope was used as a lasso with which we finally captured the sail and got it [26]

down on deck. This was the only time I ever saw the Captain caught by a squall. 

After sighting St. Paul's Island about 2200 miles east of Good Hope, we changed to a more northerly course and soon found ourselves in warmer waters. During all this time we had sighted no ships since leaving New York, but soon found ourselves in company with other vessels all making for the 

Straits of Sunda. 

One day being aloft on the fore royal yard 

I discovered a young whale disporting himself by swimming around the ship and diving under the keel as nimble as a trout. This could not be seen from deck, as the whale must have been twenty feet below the surface and seemed to be having a good time. 

As it was approaching the monsoon season in the Indian Ocean we had heavy rains accompanied by tremendous thunder and lightning at which time we often saw balls of fire rolling along the yards and exploding. We [27]

also saw several water spouts which were formed by inky black clouds above, beginning to twist and whirl until they formed a cone, reaching down toward the sea where the water would become agitated and start whirling and rising toward the clouds. When the sea and cloud met, this mass of water would go traveling over the sea at tremendous speed and would be likely to swamp a ship if in its path. 

We managed to keep to windward of these moving columns of water although I have seen half a dozen at a time careering over the water. 

One morning we sighted Christmas Island, which is about 250 miles from Java Head. 

At once we began overhauling the anchor chains, getting them out of the lockers and attaching them to the anchors, as we were approaching the Straits of Sunda through which we had to pass to reach the port of Batavia where we were bound. The next day we sighted Java Head where there was a [ 28 ]

white lighthouse and signal station. We immediately set our flags, giving the name of the ship, and word was telegraphed to the Boston owners that we had about reached our destination ninety days from Boston. This is a fair average passage by a half clipper ship like the 

Southern Cross.

As we passed Java Head and entered the Straits, the breeze grew more gentle and we found that we were drifting with the tide. 

We could hardly maintain headway, so the port anchor was dropped into what we thought was about ten fathoms of water. However, we were evidently on a bank which shelved off into deeper water, as after all the sails were furled it was noticed that the ship was drifting. Immediately the starboard anchor was dropped, as it happened, right on top of the port anchor, which we did not discover until next day. We paid out more chain and our drifting was checked.

The scenery which was all new to us boys [29]

was extremely tropical and very pleasing after ninety days at sea. We were within sight of the island of Krakatoa, which two or three years later blew up with terrible destruction of life, although at this time it was a very peaceful spot.

When we dropped our first anchor we started a number of inhabitants of the deep, including a large hammer-head shark which scurried away. We had not been at anchor long before native boats put out from the shore and, coming alongside, we found they had various fruits and food for sale, which we were only too glad to get hold of as a change from the salt beef and hard-tack. 

At this time I learned to eat a green cocoanut by sawing it in half and eating the meat, which was soft and creamy, with a spoon.

We also refreshed ourselves with pomelos, which were a new kind of fruit to us, but later were known at home as grapefruit.

The next morning it was back-breaking work [30]

heaving those two anchors, as the chains were tangled and it took about six hours to clear them. We had no patent windlass as the ship was equipped with the old-fashioned style, the handles of which moved up and down like a pump, requiring half a dozen men on each side to work them. Finally the anchors were brought up to the catheads, the sails were spread and we tried to gain a few miles, but by this time the current was running westerly and we did not make much headway so the anchors were dropped again until there should be a turn in the tide. 

It was a trying time getting through the Straits and reaching Anjer Point, as the breezes were very light and we were in danger of touching reefs and shoals, in fact did touch the bottom at one time. Finally we reached 

Anjer, which is a point of call halfway between 

Java Head and Batavia. 

Here we dropped anchor and waited a day or two to hear from our consignee at Batavia. [ 31 ]

At last we got a favorable slant of wind and reached our destination, where we were to remain four weeks. [32]

The mother calmly laid her oar down and jumped after the child, who was floating, as the smaller members of the boat's family have a bladder attached to them for just such contingencies.

At our entrance he stopped hearing some Chinese litigants and asked if he could be of service, whereupon we explained what had happened.

These wealthy natives wore richly embroidered white linen shirts, somewhat resembling coats, which hung outside of their trousers.

CHAPTER IV AT HOME 

HAD no thought of giving up going to sea when I left Liverpool, but visiting Mr. 

Baker soon after my return he said, "I believe you ought not to continue this, as the shipping business is on the decline. We are about to sell our ships and I think there is no great future for American boys in the profession." 

This, together with the wishes of my father, decided me to remain on land and was a wise move on my part, as American shipping declined steadily for the next thirty years. 

I think the time was not wasted that I spent on the water and I consider it was a liberal education fitting me for work in later life. It certainly was an experience I enjoyed and has been of much benefit to me. 

The Southern Cross went the way of most [83]

of the square-rigged American ships of that day, being sold to foreign ownership. Steam has taken the place of sails and the stately ship with lofty spars and white canvas is a thing of the past.
