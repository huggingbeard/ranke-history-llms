Preface 

THIS BOOK is published in commemoration of the onehundred-and-fiftieth anniversary of St. Paul's Lodge, celebrated on June 13, 1931. No complete history of the lodge has ever been published, and the chief aim of this volume is to present in concise form an account of Freemasonry in Litchfield as a permanent record. Such a book is of necessity somewhat statistical in nature, but the Mason will discover many facts and traditions of the - early days of the lodge of which he was not aware, and all Litchfield residents should find many interesting sidelights on local history.

The records of the Masonic organizations in Litchfield are fortunately very nearly complete, and the text of this book has been based largely on these original sources. Every effort has been made to retain the spelling, punctuation and phraseology of the sources quoted. 

The information obtained from the records has been supplemented by correspondence and by reference to numerous historical books, pamphlets and encyclopedic words.

In the Beginning 

FREEMASONRY is very old, but that it is "ancient as having subsisted from time immemorial" is seriously to be questioned.

Masonic scholars have attempted to trace the order to various ancient civilizations, but not a few have allowed their enthusiasm to distort their historical judgment. One man goes so far as to state that Adam was the first Mason, but this, like the report of Mark Twain's death, is probably exaggerated. More credence may be given to the more generally accepted theory that the order had its origin in the masons' guilds, which flourished during the building of the great cathedrals in the middle ages. It cannot be stated with certainty, however, that the present, speculative, order has a direct connection with the former, operative, order.

Freemasonry, in substantially its present form, arose in Great Britain during the seventeenth century. It may or may not have been an offshoot of the guilds of the late cathedral era. At first, lodges were entirely independent, though many recognized the priority of certain mother lodges from which they sprang. It was not until 

1717 that a grand lodge was formed in England. Other grand lodges, frequently overlapping in jurisdiction, were later formed. The order soon spread to the continent, and, partly because of the rapid expansion of the British Empire, was soon established in the far corners of the earth. Today there is scarcely a country in which Masonry is not active.

Masonry was introduced into the British colonies in America in 1733, when Viscount Montague, Grand 

Master of Masons in England, appointed Henry Price,

Provincial Grand Master of New England. A grand lodge was formed at Boston the same year, and a charter was

issued to St. John's Lodge, in Boston. The grand lodge became known as St. John's Grand Lodge. The following year, Grand Master Price received orders from the Grand Lodge in England to establish Masonry in all North America, and charters were issued to lodges throughout the colonies.

A previous deputation had been issued, in 1730, to 

Daniel Coxe as Provincial Grand Master of New York,

New Jersey and Pennsylvania, but little is known of his doings. Benjamin Franklin's Pennsylvania Gazette refers, in 1730, to several lodges existing in Pennsylvania at that time. Henry Price, however, has generally been regarded as the father of Freemasonry in America.

The first Connecticut lodge to be chartered was 

Hiram Lodge, at New Haven, in the year 1750. General 

Wooster, who was killed in the British raid on Danbury in 1777, was appointed the first Master. Several other 

Connecticut lodges were instituted by the St. John's 

Grand Lodge. 

In 1752, a group of Masons began to meet as a lodge at the Green Dragon Tavern, in Boston. They met without a warrant until 1754, when they applied to the Grand Lodge of Scotland for a charter. A charter was granted in 1756, under the name of "St. Andrew's, No. 82," and was signed by Lord Aberdour, Grand Master of Scotland. The charter was not actually received until 1760. 

Naturally, St. John's Grand Lodge was very indignant at what they considered to be an invasion of their jurisdiction by a foreign body. For several years, members of St. Andrew's Lodge were refused admission to the other lodges.

The antagonism of the other lodges in no way affected the prosperity of St. Andrew's Lodge, and plans were made for the establishment of an ancient grand lodge 2

in the colony. This was accomplished with the aid of several of the traveling lodges in the British army. 

A commission was received, in 1769, from the Earl of Dalhousie, then Grand Master of Masons in Scotland, appointing Joseph Warren as Grand Master of Masons in Boston, New England, and within one hundred miles of the same. He was later appointed Grand Master for the Continent of America. Grand Master Warren was killed in 1775 in the battle of Bunker Hill. The new grand lodge became known as the Massachusetts Grand Lodge, and charters were issued from time to time to lodges throughout New England. Thus we find the peculiar situation of two grand lodges, both located at Boston, and both claiming jurisdiction over the same territory. 

The two grand lodges were finally merged into the present 

Grand Lodge of Massachusetts in 1792.

St. Paul's Lodge received its charter in 1781 from the Massachusetts Grand Lodge, and hence traces its ancestry to Scotland, and not to England. 

Before entering upon a detailed account of the beginnings of the lodge, let us examine the background out of which it sprang.

EARLY LITCHFIELD 

Litchfield was settled in 1720 by citizens of the towns of Hartford and Windsor. The original grant gave a huge area to the new town, but subsequent adjustments reduced this somewhat. In 1781, the town had substantially its present boundaries, with the exception that the present town of Morris, then known as South Farms, belonged to Litchfield.

After sixty years, the town was still essentially agricultural. It had been fortunate enough to be made the county seat, and the courts with their retinues brought new interests. Judge Tapping Reeve had taken some law 3

students, among them his brother-in-law, Aaron Burr, but he had not yet definitely launched his famous Litchfield Law School. Litchfield was somewhat off the main lines of travel, and its social life was of necessity in and of itself. This was before the era of stagecoaches, and the only communication with the outside world was by means of an occasional rider to Hartford and neighboring towns.

There was no newspaper and no postoffice. (An interesting item of lodge history in connection with the latter is to be found elsewhere).

What community life there was may be said to have centered in the church on the green. It was not then called the Congregational Church. Why should it be?

It had no need of a denominational tag. It was the church, supported by taxation, to which every one was supposed to belong. True, a group of misguided souls had forsaken the faith and, to the horror of all, had established an Episcopal church. But the symbol of the community was still the austere, puritan church on the green. The avalanche of fraternal orders had not yet descended upon an unsuspecting world, and the focal point of society was not the lodge-room, but the Sunday services of the state church. It will be seen that conditions were ripe for the introduction of other means of social intercourse, and Masonry was admirably suited to this need. 

LITCHFIELD IN THE REVOLUTION 

The outbreak of the Revolution, in 1775, probably had as much to do with the spread of Masonry in America as any other single event. The colonies were stirred to the depths, and every community was forced by the exigencies of the hour to break the bonds of its provincial isolation and join in the common cause. The army brought together companies of men from every section of the country.

Traveling lodges were formed, and the benefits of the fraternal tie were made manifest to all. 

The war turned Litchfield from a quiet, easy-going, country town to a center for military activities. A company of thirty-six men had been sent to Fort Washington to help defend New York. These troops were imprisoned upon the surrender of New York to the British, and only six returned. While the loss of New York was a blow to the colonial cause, it was a decided boost for Litchfield. 

The most direct line of communication between New England and the armies west of the Hudson was thus cut, and a more northern route from Boston, through 

Litchfield, was used.

A training camp for recruits was maintained, and more than five hundred men were sent to the army from Litchfield at various times. Practically every able-bodied man participated in the war at some time. (Perhaps this accounts for the fact that the rolls of membership in the various orders of "Sons" and "Daughters" total more than the roster of the Continental Army). When the 

British marched on Danbury there were only fourteen men capable of bearing arms in the town, and they left for the scene of the battle.

Three buildings for military supplies were erected. 

One stood on the site of the Spring Hill School, another on East Street, just west of the East Cemetery, and another stood where the Court House now is. The Jail, then on the brow of East Hill, was frequently used for military prisoners. Probably the most noted prisoner confined here was William Franklin, son of Benjamin Franklin and royal Governor of New Jersey. All of these buildings were patrolled by sentries, both day and night.

In 1776, Ashbel Baldwin, named in the charter as the first Master, graduated from Yale and returned to his native Litchfield. He was soon commissioned, and was

placed in charge of the quarter-master stores, with the rank of Captain. After several years, he was honorably discharged and was succeeded by Oliver Wolcott, Jr., also a recent graduate of Yale, and later Governor of 

Connecticut and Grand Master of Masons in Connecticut.

ST. PAUL'S LODGE CHARTERED 

The next notice we find of Ashbel Baldwin is in the following note taken from the minutes of the meeting of the Massachusetts Grand Lodge on May 22, 1781: "Brother Baldwin presented a petition to this Grand Lodge, signed by himself and others (from Litchfield in the State of Connecticutt) requesting a grant of a Constitution, under this jurisdiction." "The same was taken into consideration, and inasmuch as no Grand Lodge nor Grand Master ever has nor does at present exist in said State; the 

Grand Lodge are of unanimous opinion there is a propriety both in the supplicants and in them. In granting the prayer, therefore voted the Secretary be directed to furnish the suppliants with a Charter of Erection by the name of St. Paul. The expenses of this evening defrayed by Brother Baldwin." 

The petition which Brother Baldwin presented to the brethren at Boston was signed by fourteen men, under the leadership of the Rev. James Nichols, then Rector of

St. Michael's Episcopal Church. Those whose names appeared on the petition, and hence on the charter, are as follows: Ashbel Baldwin, James Nichols, John Watkins (sometimes Wadkins), Thomas Phillips, Eaton Jones, Benjamin Hanks, John Collins, Noah Blakesley, William 

Durkee, Daniel Starr, John Colvill, John (Jonathan) 

Kettle, Josiah (Eber) Norton and Adino (Timothy) Hale.

The frequent travel between Litchfield and Woodbury is reflected in the fact that six of the petitioners were members of King Solomon's Lodge, of that place, and four were only fellowcraft Masons, also of King Solomon's. 

The home lodges of three petitioners, Brothers Phillips,

Durkee and Colvill, are unknown. Benjamin Hanks was from Albany (lodge unknown).

The charter (see Appendix) was signed by Grand 

Master Joseph Webb and other grand officers June 1, 1781. One signature, of which the lodge is extremely proud, is that of Paul Revere, then Senior Grand Warden of the Massachusetts Grand Lodge. The patriotic ride of Paul Revere has been made immortal by Longfellow's poem. In this connection, it is interesting to note that many of the men of the Boston Tea Party were members of St. Andrew's Lodge, and it has been said that the plans for dumping the tea into the harbor were formulated in its lodge-room.

Detailed instructions for instituting the lodge were drawn up, and a list of the sixteen lodges under the jurisdiction of the Massachusetts Grand Lodge was made. 

Wooster Lodge, at Colchester, was the only other Connecticut lodge under this jurisdiction at the time, other Connecticut lodges being subservient to the St. John's 

Grand Lodge or the New York Grand Lodge. 

As we have said, there was no postoffice, and the Charter, together with the documents noted above, was sent to Litchfield by special messenger as a military dispatch. The messenger arrived in Litchfield about the 

10th of June, 1781, and no time was lost in establishing the lodge.

Some of these by-laws seem odd today, but each section had a reason behind it in 1781, and, believe it or not, it was these quaint sections which were most strictly enforced. The brethren quite freely ignored sections such as the 21st, dealing with the conferring of more than one degree on a candidate in the same evening, but they hewed strictly to the line in all sections having to do with moral conduct and lodge discipline. Many men were fined for not attending lodge, and there were numerous instances of trials for intoxication and other irregularities. 

Though there were some backsliders, they were not simply winked at; they were promptly brought to account, and suitable penalties were meted out. The Puritan strain still ran strong in New England veins. Another Yankee characteristic may be noted in section 18. Perhaps the 13

following from the minutes of the meeting of July 25th explains this: "Two visiting Brethren from Camp."

Julius Deming was the first man raised to the Sublime 

Degree of Master Mason in St. Paul's Lodge. This was on 

September 27, 1781. On December 25th, a special meeting was held at the home of Alexander Catling (Catlin). "Mr. Alexander Catling balloted for Admitted & Initiated. 

Lodge Room established at Br. Catling's." Brother Catlin lived at the end of North Street in the house now occupied by Mrs. Blake. This was the meeting-place of the lodge for about five years.

FIRST ANNUAL COMMUNICATION 

Two days later, on December 27th, the feast of St.

John the Evangelist, the first annual communication was held. The principal officers were re-elected. New officers were, "Br. J. Demming, Treasurer; Br. E. Kirby, Secretary; Br. J. Kettle, Post Master."

These were all excellent choices. Brother Deming was unquestionably the most outstanding business man and merchant in Litchfield County. Brother Kirby was also prominent in business, and his records as Secretary are a joy to behold; they are like a steel engraving. The office of Postmaster is most unusual. Brother Kilbourn writes as follows on the reason for this office: "The Post 

Office department of the Colonial government was unorganized, and except to the more important points no way was provided to send or receive letters. Julius Deming was a prominent merchant; his messengers were constantly going to and from the cities of New York, New 

Haven, Boston, Hartford and Albany. And he agreed to transmit all the letters of Masons with his own dispatches." A Postmaster was appointed to distribute the mail for several years, until the organization of the govern- 14

ment system. Unfortunately, the box of pigeon holes used for the purpose was destroyed about fifty years ago.

13th The glorious 27th of December 1781' "The expences of the Celebration. £13-16-2." Quite a sizeable sum even today. But what a "glorious 27th of 

December" it must have been, particularly when two meetings later, a brother (who shall remain nameless) was charged and brought to trial for, among other things, drunkeness, disorderly conduct, and "publishing & exposing many of the secrets of masonry." The culprit admitted that he had been boisterous, but offered as a defence that all present were in more or less the same condition. 

This defence availed him nothing, for he was found guilty on all charges but the last, but the officers said, “he was guilty of tattling & babling what he ought not." He was ordered to make public apologies, which he did. 

In reading of this trial, it must be remembered that 15

this was before the beginning of the temperance movement. Contemporary literature is full of accounts of prodigious consumption of alcohol. The church had not yet declared liquor to be anathema, and the clergy was not the least of the offenders. Wines and liquors were served at lodge meetings, and we find in the records entries like the following: Voted that the Treasurer provide "a suitable number of Wine Glasses." "Voted that the Master draw an order on the Treasurer for 20/ to pay brother Ephm. Starr for a Case of Ginn." It was soon voted that no ardent spirits be paid for by the lodge, and they were later excluded from the lodge-room altogether. 

There is another side of this picture. The first temperance pledge in America was signed in Litchfield in 1789 by thirty-six citizens, eighteen of whom were members of 

St. Paul's Lodge.

MASONIC TRIALS 

The Masonic trials of which this was the first in a long series, make very interesting reading. The brothers would not condone offences against Masonic laws, and, especially in the early years, a brother had no hesitancy in openly charging anyone whom he suspected of violating his obligations. In one instance, the lodge intervened to settle a dispute between two brothers which, it thought, was reflecting on the craft in general. As was usually the case, the brothers were summoned to appear before the lodge, present their cases, and submit to a vote of the members present. The testimony in this case is recorded in the minutes with stenographic exactness, and the lodge found "both parties atrociously to blame." The brothers were ordered to apologize to each other and to the lodge. 

One "readily comply'd," but the other "absolutely refused and was therefore expelled." There have been few trials in recent years the last was in 1913. The verdicts 

16

have ranged from acquittals to reprimands, suspensions and expulsions.

GRAND LODGE OF CONNECTICUT 

At a special meeting on March 14, 1783, action was taken which eventually led to the formation of the Grand Lodge of Connecticut. It was "Voted that a commity be chosen to represent St. Paul's Lodge at the grand convention of Masons at N. Haven Tuesday next [March 

18th]." Benjamin Hanks, Master; Ezekiel Woodruff, Senior Warden; and S. Woodruff Calvin were chosen.

Thirteen lodges chartered by the two Massachusetts 

Grand Lodges and the Grand Lodge of New York met and recommended that another convention be held at 

New Haven on April 29th. St. Paul's was represented at this gathering by Julius Deming. These meetings were not held to consider forming a grand lodge but were simply to regulate and standardize Masonry in Connecticut. On 

August 7th of the same year, it was "Voted that Br. 

Hanks and Br. Woodruff attend the Grand Convention of Masons at Middletown the last tuesday of Sept. next.'

The Secretary was ordered to make out thirteen lists of members for the thirteen lodges of the State. 

On May 5, 1785, the following appears: "Recev'd and read a Letter and resolves from a Convention at Boston & ordered that the Secty. write a letter in answer to the one recev'd." This letter threatened the lodge with erasure from the rolls of the Grand Lodge for non-payment of dues. On September 4, 1788, we find the following vote: "This Lodge having recev'd a Letter from the Grand 

Lodge at Boston, Voted that Brothers Julius Deming, Isaac Baldwin Jr. & Ashbel Baldwin be a Committee to write to Grand Lodge in answer to sd. Letter, & forward it as speedily as possible." The present Grand Secretary of Massachusetts informs us that St. Paul's was on a list 17

of lodges to be erased for non-payment of dues in 1785, and presumably was dropped. The receipt of the 1788 letter would seem to dispute this. On October 2, 1788, a committee was appointed to meet with other delegates from lodges under the jurisdiction of the Grand Lodge of 

Boston, at Farmington, on the last Monday of October, to make "a united representation to sd. Grand Lodge.'

The demands of the Massachusetts Grand Lodge had their effect, and on May 7, 1789, a circular letter to all lodges in Connecticut, prepared by a previous vote of the lodge, was presented. Sir "The following Letter was received and read viz 

Litchfield, March 31, 1789

Impressed with the Importance of adding Stability & reputation to the Masonic Art as well Uniformity in the Communications & working of the different Lodges in this State who profess to be founded upon & governed by the antient principles of the fraternity, as well as to correct such abuses in the Workings of the Craft (if any such there are) that have inadvertently crept into our Lodges by blending the antient Manner with the Modern, as well as to stimulate the brethren in this State to embrace the present favourable Opportunity of placing the Society in that laudable point of view with the world that the noble principles of our order really Demand; & thereby restore that Gem of Antiquity to its primitive lustre. 

We profess to embrace all mankind in the Arms of Benevolence and to do good as we have opportunity from an impulse that becomes the proficient in the Royal Art.

NEW MEETING-PLACES 

Several changes were made in the meeting-place of the lodge at about the turn of the century. In 1797, the lodge was moved to the home of Brother Asa Morgan, which was on the north side of West Street, opposite the present schoolhouse. This house has been torn down. 

Two years later, they again moved, this time to the home of Elijah Wadsworth on South Street, now occupied by Mrs. Pennoyer. Previous to this, in 1798, a committee was appointed "to procure a plan of the School-House that is to be built" to see if an upper room could not be used for a lodge-room, but the lodge did not meet there.

THE GOLDEN AGE 

The last years of the eighteenth century and the early years of the nineteenth have been rightly called the "Golden Age" of Litchfield. Small industries flourished and both the town and the lodge showed increased activity and prosperity. Litchfield was also becoming a cultural center. The Litchfield Law School, founded by Judge 

Tapping Reeve, the first independent school of law in

America, attracted over a thousand students to its doors before the foundation of law schools at Harvard and Yale forced it to close. Men who later became famous acquired 25

their knowledge of law here. Among the most noted graduates were John C. Calhoun and Noah Webster. 

Miss Pierce's school for young ladies, later known as the

Litchfield Female Academy, also developed during this period. It is estimated that about three thousand girls attended this school during its approximately sixty years of existence. MORGAN AND ANTI-MASONRY 

The year 1826 was full of portent for Masonry; for it marked the beginning of a widespread persecution of 

Masons, known as the Morgan Excitement. This affair is one of the most curious in American political history. 

There have been many good accounts published, and we will outline the case very briefly. The facts are in dispute, but we base our account on a statement by John Whitney, one of the participants in the affair, which, by his direction, was not published until after his death. 

A few years prior to 1826, William Morgan arrived in Batavia, New York, and succeeded in gaining entrance into the local lodge. There is no record of his raising, and it is not believed that he ever received the three 29

degrees of Craft Masonry. Morgan was a dissolute fellow, and had contracted numerous debts. In order to pay his debts, he conceived the idea of publishing an expose of the secrets of Masonry. In 1826, he, in conjunction with a local printer, David Miller, announced the forthcoming publication. The Masons were, of course, very much wrought up, and they attempted to persuade Morgan to stop the project. An agreement was finally reached with Morgan, whereby he was to give up the manuscript, leave the country and never return all this in return for $500.00 and the assurance that his family would be sent to him when he had found a home. Unfortunately, he was on jail limits because of debt, and could not leave Batavia. He was therefore arrested on a criminal charge, which necessitated his removal to Canandaigua for trial. 

As no one appeared against him, he was released, and was at once re-arrested for debt. The debt was paid by 

Masons, and, on his release, he was met by a carriage and, of his own free will, taken to Fort Niagara to await the escort of Canadian Masons. Morgan, in the meantime, signed the articles of agreement. Three days later, two Canadian Masons took Morgan into Canada, took his receipt for the $500.00, and left him at a point near 

Hamilton, Ontario. Meanwhile, the populace was accusing the Masons of having murdered Morgan. This was a turn of events which the group had not forseen, and, becoming alarmed, they sent a messenger to bring Morgan back, but he had disappeared.

No one knows what became of Morgan. He is reputed to have been seen in various foreign countries, including 

Smyrna. The following letter to Brother Kilbourn from a member of St. Paul's is a contribution to this theory: "Bro. D. C. Kilbourn. As I hear you are picking up Masonic matters,

I have one to tell. About 1870, an old purser of thirty years service, in our Navy, by the name of Bloodgood, told me the following: 30

Politicians at once saw the possibility of beclouding other issues by fanning the flames of public indignation against the supposed abduction and murder of Morgan. 

Fully thirteen months after the disappearance of Morgan, a body was found in Lake Ontario, forty miles from Niagara. It was at once claimed to be the body of Morgan (though his wife could see no resemblance) and it was buried with much show. The body later proved to be that of a Canadian, Timothy Monro, and was re-interred in Canada. However, the politician, Thurlow Weed, is reported to have said, "It's a good enough Morgan till after election." 

An Anti-Masonic political party was formed, and was particularly strong in New York, Pennsylvania and

New England. In 1832, the height of the excitement, this party was able to win all the electoral votes of Vermont, but Jackson, a Mason, was re-elected. About the only good that can be said of the Anti-Masonic Party is that it inaugurated the practice of nominating candidates by national conventions. In addition to the political movement, Anti-Masonic, or Mock-Masonic, lodges were formed, one of which, St. Jacob's Lodge, was formed at 

Harwinton, by Abijah Catlin. 

Membership in the Masonic fraternity was considered by the public at large as incompatible with good citizen- 31

ship. Masons were supposed to be bound together by diabolical oaths which made them an extra-legal body which ought to be abolished. Churches dismissed ministers who were known to be Masons. Many were threatened with physical violence. Colonel Heman W. Childs, Master of St. Paul's Lodge, received a letter threatening to burn his mansion. 

With such widespread persecution, men fell away from the order like leaves before the autumn wind. 

Hundreds of lodges were forced to surrender their charters. 

Even two Grand Lodges, Maine and Vermont, were forced to suspend. The lodges in all the towns surrounding 

Litchfield dissolved, but St. Paul's managed to ride out the storm.

The Morgan excitement does not appear to have affected Litchfield until 1828. The minutes are missing for six years from February 28, 1828 to December 31, 1829, and again from January 13, 1831 to January 19, 1837. We are, however, able to show through other records that Masonry was not dead in Litchfield, but only somnolent. We were either represented or reported at the 

Grand Lodge every year except 1834 and 1835, and our excuses for absence were accepted for these years. The 

Treasurer's accounts of Darius Chapter show entries for every year except 1836. This activity in the Chapter indicates that there was probably similar activity in the "blue" lodge. A resolution of the Grand Lodge setting forth the principles and tenets of the order was published in 1832 and sent broatcast through the State. Hundreds of Masons signed this resolution, and twenty-three members of St. Paul's indicated their loyalty by affixing their names. We are thus able to fill in the blank years. 

It was necessary to meet in secret during the Morgan excitement, and much of the information about this period is tradition only. An entry in the record dated January 32

13, 1831, is most unusual in Masonry and shows the irregularity of the meetings: "Adjourned without day.”

Tradition tells us that the brothers would meet once a year in some brother's home to elect officers. They would leave their horses outside the village, and walk separately and by different routes to the appointed meeting-place. 

Certain meeting-places are known. At the beginning of the excitement, in 1826, the lodge was meeting in the County House (then a hotel), where they stayed until at least 1829. Most of the meetings up to 1840 were at 

Roger Cook's.

The records and paraphernalia were hidden in various places. George Dewey is said to have had some in his garret, and General Frederick Buel some in his farmhouse. Brother Kilbourn describes the old furniture, which was missing upon the revival of Masonry. The lodgeroom must have resembled the throne-room of an oriental potentate. We quote Brother Kilbourn: "The Master and Senior and Junior Wardens sat in small pavilions with showy canopies overhead, and the backs were bright red velvet with heavy drapery and immense tassels, giving the hall a very gorgeous appearance. 

From 1840 to 1845, the lodge met at Brother William Odell's Hotel (This is the house on the Southwest corner of Lake and West Streets), part of the time in the Jail, and part at the United States Hotel, now Phelps' Tavern. 

The Mansion House, which stood on the site of Crutch's drugstore, was the meeting-place in 1845. In 1846 they were back again at Bulkley's Hall.

LODGE LIBRARY 

On December 28, 1846, the members of St. Paul's

Lodge Library presented the same to the lodge for the use of all members. Various votes appear in reference to this library. In 1794, Ephraim Kirby was appointed to draw up a plan for the library, and he gave many of the books when he left for the South in 1804. It appears that there was a separate organization within the lodge which owned the library. There were originally about three hundred volumes, but only about two hundred volumes exist today. They are stored at the Historical Society building. The books are full-leather bound, mostly sheepskin, and each book contains the book-plate of the lodge with the catalogue number. There are no books on 

Masonry; they consist chiefly of the classics, books on theology, sermons, and travelogues.

In the next ten or fifteen years, Masonry began to regain its foothold. It had withstood the persecution of the Anti-Masons, and managed to survive the economic depression of 1837 which closely followed it. Many of the entries in the minutes of this reconstruction period deal with the purchasing of furniture, the renting of halls, and similar entries concerning rehabilitation of the order.

THE CIVIL WAR 

If the most trying period in the history of the lodge was the Anti-Masonic persecution, certainly the most 35

hectic period was that of the Civil War. The call had gone out for more troops, and Litchfield County decided to raise a regiment of its own. The Nineteenth Connecticut 

Volunteer Regiment, afterwards the Second Connecticut 

Volunteer Heavy Artillery, was enlisted throughout the county, and in August, 1862, encamped at Camp Dutton, on Chestnut Hill. Leverett W. Wessells, a member of

St. Paul's Lodge, was appointed the Colonel in command of the regiment.

Many of the soldiers wished to be made Masons before leaving for the South. David E. Bostwick was 

Master of St. Paul's Lodge and Deputy Grand Master of the Grand Lodge, and on September 3, 1862, he issued the following order to the Tyler: "Litchfield, Sept. 3d., D. C. Connecticut Volunteers & you will please to notify the

The candidates were many, and, by special dispensation, the lodge met very day to confer degrees. For twelve days, from September 3d to September 14th (inclusive), the lodge met once, twice and sometimes three times a day. The usual procedure was to meet at one or two o'clock in the afternoon, adjourn for supper, and reconvene at seven o'clock, conferring degrees until about midnight, or as the minutes of one meeting read: "1 hour past low twelve, adjourned." The candidates were apparently not initiated en masse, as the minutes state that they were "severally received." The great accession of members during the Civil War did not permanently augment the roll of members, and about ten years later there was a general purging. In 

1870, the membership was reported as 145. In 1874, thirty- 38

eight members were dropped from the rolls at one meeting for non-payment of dues, and more members were dropped at a later meeting in the same year. The membership in January, 1879, was 113, but nineteen men were dropped in May of the same year.

The curious article of the present by-laws, whereby the stated communications of the lodge in each month from October to April are held on the Wednesday preceding the full moon, was passed February 20, 1878. A somewhat similar by-law had previously been in force, and a still earlier one set the day at Thursday instead of 

Wednesday. The traditional reason for the full-moon provision was to enable the members to drive their horses home by moonlight after the meetings.

CENTENNIAL CELEBRATION 

June 1, 1881 marked the one-hundredth anniversary of the granting of the charter by the Massachusetts Grand Lodge, and elaborate preparations were made to properly celebrate the event. 

On April 20, 1881, a committee reported against a public celebration of the centennial, but a new committee on arrangements presented a program to the lodge on 

May 11th, which provided for public participation.

Armory Hall (the former Congregational Church, since restored to its former use as a place of worship) was secured for the exercises and for the supper and ball which followed. The hall was elaborately decorated with flags and with Masonic emblems. 

At 6:30 on June 1st, eighty-two Masons (Brother 

Kilbourn says two hundred) assembled at the Masonic

Hall, opened the lodge in due form, and received Grand 

Master James McCormick and Senior Grand Warden

Dwight Waugh. A procession was then formed, with 39

NEW MASONIC HALL 

A special meeting was held April 1, 1885, at which 

Brother D. C. Kilbourn offered the following resolution: "Whereas it is shown to their lodge that the present Methodist Church can be obtained for use of this lodge upon the following terms: That the purchase price, repairs and alterations shall not exceed $2000.00, that the interest shall not exceed 5 per cent per annum and taxes and insurance, that possession be given Aug. 1, 1885, that the principal sum may be payable at rate of $100.00 per year - Voted that we accept such proposition and that a committee of five be appointed to negotiate and complete such purchase." The building was accordingly acquired, and payments on the loan were made from time to time, but it was twenty-seven years before the debt was cleared. 

The hall was dedicated on November 12, 1885. All the lodges in Litchfield County had been invited and the brothers met at two o'clock in the afternoon. Grand

Master Waugh, assisted by other grand officers, dedicated the hall. The following excerpts from the minutes describe the events which followed the formal dedication: "After the lodge closed, the rooms were open to visitors who 42

were received by - a reception committee. The rooms. of the lodge were thronged with visitors, both ladies and gentlemen. A bountiful table was spread at the town hall, about one hundred were at the tables, and many fragments were left after the feast. At 7 P. M. a public reception was held in the hall. Dwight C. Kilbourn delivered an historical address and interesting remarks were made by officers of the Grand Lodge and others. At the close all repaired to the town hall for refreshments. After the interchange of much kindly feeling and sentiment, the party broke up, and thus ended a red-letter day in the history of St. 

Paul's Lodge No. II." 

We are hearing much at present of "the economic cycle." Masonic activity also seems to move in pendulumlike swings. There is a period of intense interest, then a gradual slowing up of lodge activities, which in turn is followed by a revival period. The years following the 

Centennial and the acquisition of the hall are typical. 

An act of the Legislature incorporating St. Paul's Lodge was approved March 4, 1886, and accepted by the lodge 

March 31, 1886. On November 12, 1886, the lodge chartered a special train and visited Rising Sun Lodge, No. 27, in Washington, Connecticut. Brother Granniss reported "$8.50 profit on Washington Excursion." 1888, 1889 and 

1890 were inactive years, and attendances were not large. 

LODGE CHARITIES 

Little has been written in the preceding pages of the charities of the lodge. Scarcely a year passed without donations being made to brothers and their dependents. 

The earliest recorded charity is in the following vote on 

February 3, 1803: Voted to pay ten dollars to the widow of Brother W- "as an object of charity." There were undoubtedly earlier charities than this, but they were not 43

always recorded in the early years of the lodge. August 4, 1814: "Voted that the Treasr. pay B. Cook one Dollar advanced to an Indigent Brother." September, 1818: "Master directed to draw from the Treasury the necessary sum to defray the expences of Schooling Mrs. A-'s child the ensuing Winter." March 23, 1864: Ten dollars voted for the relief of a family (not Masons) “who lost their all by fire." The charitable record of the lodge has been maintained in recent years, and many of these private charities have amounted to more than one hundred dollars each. The lodge has not been unmindful of distress in other localities, and contributions have been made in times of disaster as in various Mississippi floods, the 

Chicago fire and the recent Florida hurricane. All this is in addition to the several hundred dollars annually which the lodge gives by assessment toward the support of the 

Masonic Home at Wallingford. 

The 1890's brought new vigor to the lodge. New members were taken in, and the indebtedness on the hall was reduced considerably. Perhaps the most frequent action taken in this period was the appointment of W. T.

SESQUI-CENTENNIAL CELEBRATION 

June 13, 1931, marked the one-hundred-and-fiftieth anniversary of the first meeting of St. Paul's Lodge, and it was decided to celebrate the sesqui-centennial at this time rather than on June 1st, the anniversary of the issuance of the charter, as was done at the centennial celebration in 1881.

A well planned program had been arranged, and at seven o'clock on the evening of June 13th, over one hundred brethren sat down to a chicken-pie supper in the banquet-room of the lodge. Beautiful, symbolical folders giving the menu and the program of the evening were found at each plate. After the supper, all present repaired to the lodge-room and were grouped in the East for a photograph. 

Worshipful Master Albert E. Childs then opened the lodge in due and ancient form, and Past Grand Master 

W. G. Granniss presented Grand Master Howard E. 

Middleton, Past Grand Masters Andrew J. Hallock and 

Leonard J. Nickerson, and District Deputy C. W. Tyler to the East, and in a short address welcomed them to 

St. Paul's Lodge. 48

Grand Master Middleton expressed his pleasure in being present to celebrate with the lodge and commented on the notable history which it had. Rev. Brother Hugh 

Shields gave a humorous address and regaled the lodge with one joke after another, closing his talk on a more serious note and calling upon the brothers to exemplify 

Masonry in their daily lives.

The principal feature of the evening was the historical address presented by A. William Pruner, the author of this book. This address was in the main a much abbreviated version of the contents of this book. Brother Pruner was followed by Past Grand Master Hallock, a native of Litchfield and a frequent visitor to St. Paul's Lodge. 

Brother Hallock gave reminiscences of his boyhood in Litchfield, and recalled attending church in the lodgeroom before the hall had been acquired by the lodge. 

Past Grand Master Leonard J. Nickerson was then introduced, and spoke on the part that the Masons have played in the development of the nation. He called attention to the fact that St. Paul's Lodge was older than the nation itself.

Worshipful Master Childs then closed the lodge in the ancient manner, and thus brought to a close one hundred and fifty years of Litchfield Masonry. What a glorious past we have had! What sterling manhood has been molded in St. Paul's Lodge by the precepts of 

Freemasonry! Who shall say that the spirit of the fathers does not still live and encourage us to strive for greater heights of manly courage and character? A recent Grand 

Master of the Grand Council of Massachusetts states it thus: "All the past is shut up within us, and is a sort of perpetual present. All the future is before us, and, though duty is a present thing, it is constructed out of the past, and runs endlessly into the future. We thus have the past with its memories, the present with its duties, and 49

the future with its anticipations. One for wisdom. One for action. One for hope."

We can perhaps do no better than to close with words taken from a sermon delivered to the craft by our first 

Worshipful Master, Rev. Ashbel Baldwin: "Let every one, then, who has the honor to be a member thereof, cause his light so to shine before men, that glory redound to God in the highest, peace be promoted on earth and good will stream forth from every heart to its brother."

Darius Chapter, No. 16, Royal Arch Masons A CHARTER for the institution of a chapter of Royal Arch 

Masons in Litchfield was issued by the Grand Chapter of Connecticut at New Haven, October 19, 1815, and was signed by John R. Watrous, Grand High Priest, and by other Grand Chapter officers. The charter designates the chapter "by the name and stile of 'Darius Chapter, No. 

16, held in Litchfield," " and names Roger Searle as High 

Priest, Timothy Clark as King, and Joel Bradley as Scribe.

The chapter was instituted on December 27, 1815, by John H. Lynde, Grand High Priest pro tempore, and by other Grand Chapter officers, also recorded as "pro tempore." Companion Lynde was presented with thirty dollars for his services, and assistants received ten dollars each. The Rev. Burham was presented with fifteen dollars "for his well adapted sermon.' 

Several meetings had been held prior to the official installation, but, though the charter had been issued, they were apparently not considered "regular," as they are headed: "At a Meeting of Royal Arch Masons," and 

Companion Searle is recorded as "Chairman." The first recorded meeting was held November 4, 1815, at the home of Joel Bradley, in Harwinton. A letter from Roger 

Searle, in Plymouth, to Roger Cook, in Litchfield, has been preserved, in which he complains that not a single Litchfield man was present at this first meeting. He also suggests to the committee on arrangements for the installation that "System, decision, and energy, will all be necessary.

Buel Council, No. 20,

Royal and Select Masters THE ORGANIZATION of Buel Council did not mark the introduction of cryptic Masonry to Litchfield. Adams

Council, No. 12, was established in 1825. The records of this council have disappeared, but we are able to obtain certain facts concerning it from the records of the Grand Council of Connecticut and from the records of St. Paul's Lodge. It is quite likely that the first Thrice- 

Illustrious Master was Joseph Adams, who was then active in both the "blue" lodge and chapter, and that he gave the new council its name. Adams Council was granted a charter at an assembly of the Grand Council on May 13, 1825. The council was born at an inopportune moment, as the Morgan excitement soon engulfed it and made work almost impossible. At the Grand Council assembly on May 14, 1840, Adams Council was reported on a list of councils having made no returns for several years, and its charter was revoked. 

On June 18, 1852, a dispensation to form and open a council of Royal and Select Masters in Litchfield was granted by Cyrus Goodell, "Grand Puissant." An organization meeting was held at Bulkley's Masonic Hall, September 2, 1852, with Augustus C. Chapin as Chair man, and General Frederick Buel as Secretary. Companion Buel was elected the first "Grand Master," and a "Deputy Grand Master," Treasurer, and Recorder were also elected. On February 16, 1853, Grand Puissant 

Goodell and Deputy Grand Master George Giddings were present and conferred degrees on six candidates. The remaining officers were chosen at this meeting and all officers were installed by Companion Goodell. The council received its name at an assembly on May 4, 1853, when 55

the following vote was passed: Voted "that the name of this Council of Royal and Select Masters be Buel, in honor of our present Grand Master, and those of the name who have been and still are members of the Masonic Fraternity in this town." A petition for a charter was presented to the Grand Council, May 12, 1853, and a charter was granted by the name of "Buel Council, No. 

20, of which Illustrious Companion Frederick Buel was appointed the first Grand Master, Chester G. Bridge, the first D. G. Master, and Samuel G. Braman, the first 

P. Conductor." The charter is dated May 25, 1853, and is signed by Francois Turner, Grand Puissant, and E. G. 

Storer, Grand Recorder.

The early years of the council are not very inspiring. 

From 1855 to 1869 there are minutes of only six assemblies. 

David E. Bostwick had succeeded Companion Buel as 

Thrice-Illustrious Master in 1863. He reported to the 

Grand Council of 1868 that the council had not been meeting for some years, and the Grand Master commented that "either its officers are not very efficient, or that it is located in a barren field." In 1870, Alexander B. 

Shumway was elected Master, a position which he held for twenty-five years (1870-1877, 1881-1899), and the council became more active. Companion Shumway was 

Most Puissant Grand Master of the Grand Council of Connecticut from 1884 to 1885. The minutes of the assemblies from June, 1885, to December, 1888, were destroyed in the fire of August 9, 1888, which swept the business section of Litchfield.

Much of the strength of Buel Council in recent years has come from the fact that it has jurisdiction over the city of Torrington. In 1892, Tyrian Council, No. 31, of 

Winsted, sought concurrent jurisdiction over Torrington from the Grand Council. Resolutions were passed by 

Buel Council protesting the action as "unjust and op- 56

pressive," and the Grand Council refused to divide the jurisdiction. The Council has met in Torrington about a dozen times to confer degrees on large classes of candidates. The first recorded Torrington assembly was on 

March 21, 1902. A reminder of the pre-Fordian era is found in this quotation from the minutes of the March 

21st assembly: "The weather being fine, and with a nearly full moon; the drive to Litchfield was accomplished without accident, arriving at about 3 A. M.”

Few institutions can claim the loyalty of their members as can the Masonic fraternity. Some of the records for long service of officers of Buel Council are simply amazing. We have already mentioned the service of the first Master, Frederick Buel, and of the only Grand 

Master, Alexander B. Shumway. A few of the more outstanding records follow some of these same members have held other offices for shorter terms. W. J. Bissell was Recorder for thirteen years (1872-1881, 1903-1907), Treasurer for forty-three years (1884-1899, 1903-1931), Master for three years (1899-1902). E. W. Meafoy was

The chapter was quite active, initiating candidates, holding whists, visiting other chapters — particularly

The chapter was forced to close in the fall of 1918 because of the influenza epidemic, and did not meet until January 20, 1919. The annual election, which should have been held in December, was not held until February 17, 1919. Following close on the heels of this trying period came the agitation for the formation of a chapter in Bantam. A special meeting was held on 

December 8, 1919, with the Grand Patron and Grand

This event acted as a spur to flagging interest, and in the next three months over twenty petitions for membership were received, many of which were from Bantam. The chapter has continued to be one of the most active organizations in Litchfield. Material assistance has been given to St.
