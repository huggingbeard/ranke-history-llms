INTRODUCTION 

Prior to the enactment of specific legislation aimed at simplifying the giving of securities to minors, what began as a simple gift transaction often ended in a tangle of legal complications and expense to the detriment of the minor and the confusion of the donor. 

While giving securities to a minor had always been possible by having the securities registered in the minor's name, serious legal difficulties arose when the minor later attempted to sell the securities or reinvest the dividends. A broker, bank, transfer agent, or any other person dealing with a minor who sought to buy or sell securities, did so at "his own peril," as the minor generally had the power to repudiate his contract or disaffirm a sale upon reaching majority. The only cure for this so-called "minor's disability" was to have a guardian of his property appointed by the court or, in the alternative, by establishing a living trust for the minor's benefit. In both cases the cure proved to be disproportionately complex, expensive and cumbersome. 

In contrast to the pitfalls and "red tape" often involved in connection with gifts of securities to minors, very simple procedures for gifts of United States Savings Bonds or bank savings accounts to minors have long been available to parents and other donors. G. Keith Funston, President of the New York Stock Exchange, long advocated the creation of a simple but effective method for giving and handling gifts of securities to children to enable them to understand the workings of our economic system. The need for a new but sound approach to this problem resulted in the development of a model "Gifts of Securities to Minors Law" by the 

New York Stock Exchange. 

MODEL LAW 

Beginning in 1955 with the sponsorship and promotion of the Associa tion of Stock Exchange Firms, the New York Stock Exchange's "Model 

Law" was enacted in 14 states and the District of Columbia. The Model 

Law cut the legal "red tape" and made it possible to make an outright gift of securities to a minor child by using prescribed words of registration and, at the same time, reserved the power of management to a "custodian” until the child reached 21 years of age. Early experience with these new laws led to the drafting of a shorter, improved version called the "Uniform Gifts to Minors Act". Of the 14 states which originally enacted the 

Model Law, 11 subsequently replaced their statutes with the Uniform Act.

UNIFORM ACT 

In 1956 the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws adopted the Uniform Gifts to Minors Act, which broadened the old Model Law to permit gifts of money as well as securities, and enlarged the choice of custodians. As in the case of the Model Law, the Association of Stock Exchange Firms, with the cooperation of the New York Stock Exchange, spearheaded the legislative effort. Never has any new law had such nationwide and prompt acceptance. 

The Uniform Act, originally enacted in 36 states, has now replaced the earlier Model Law in 11 other states-a total of 47 states. The remaining three states (Alaska, Georgia and New Jersey) and the District of Columbia are still using the Model Law. The basic Uniform Act will be found beginning on page 25 of this booklet.

Procedure for Making Gifts of Bearer Securities 

To make a gift of bearer securities a donor must deliver the securities to the designated custodian accompanied by a Deed of Gift. The donor is not permitted to designate himself as custodian for this type of gift. 

Normally, in the Model Law jurisdictions, he may designate an adult member of the minor's family or a guardian of the minor. In Uniform Act states, with the exceptions listed on page 8, he may also designate another adult person or a trust company as custodian. See Table of Custodians on page 8.

The accompanying Deed of Gift recites the name of the donor and custodian, the fact of delivery, a description of the securities delivered and is signed by the donor. the donor. At the bottom of the Deed the custodian acknowledges receipt of the securities by signing his name. By these actions and the delivery of the securities, the gift to the minor is completed and the custodian commences the duties prescribed under the statute. 

Examples of gift forms which may be used in jurisdictions operating under the Model Law or Uniform Act are illustrated on page 7 of this booklet. In general, these forms are in compliance with the laws of the various Model and Uniform jurisdictions, however, a donor should check the local statute in order to be sure of the exact wording.

Questions & Answers on Gifts to Minors Laws 

1. Who may use the new laws?

Any adult person may make a gift under these laws. Preferably a donor should use either the law of his own state or the law of the state where the minor resides. If they reside in different states, the donor may select the state with the most favorable law. In order to avoid possible transfer problems (or conflict of laws problems upon the death or resignation of a custodian where the minor resides in a different state) it is much better to have as many factors of the gift as possible (residence of donor, minor or custodian) present within a given state. 

2. What securities may be given?

Any securities may be given. Normally under the Uniform Act the term "security" does not include a security of which the donor is the issuer. Section 1(1) of Uniform Act on page 26 indicates the types of securities which may be given under its provisions. In some states the definition of "security" has been broadened to include savings and loan association shares. U. S. Savings Bonds can not be made the subject of a gift to a minor under these laws. 

3. May life insurance be given under these laws?

It is possible to make a gift of life insurance in six states: Illinois, Kentucky, New Hampshire, North Carolina, Ohio and Wisconsin.

In Illinois, New Hampshire and Ohio endowment policies and annuity contracts may also be given. In general, the policies or annuity contracts may only be on the life of a minor or a member of a minor's family as defined in the statute; all the incidents of ownership in the policies or annuity contracts, as the case may be, shall be in the custodian as if he were the owner; the beneficiary of such policy or contract shall always be the minor, or in the event of his death, the minor's estate.

In Illinois, New Hampshire and Ohio a life or endowment insurance policy or an annuity contract may be given to a minor by assigning the policy or contract to the custodian (donor, another adult, bank, trust company) in his own name, followed by the wording indicated in Form U on pages 4 and 5 for those states, and delivering same to the designated custodian.

To make a gift of insurance in Kentucky, North Carolina and Wisconsin the donor must (i) register the ownership of the policy in his own name, the name of an adult member of the minor's family or

the name of any guardian of the minor, using the wording indicated below:

Kentucky: "(name of custodian) as custodian for (name of minor) under section 385.020 of the Kentucky 

Revised Statutes." 

North Carolina: See Form U on page 4.

Wisconsin: “(name of custodian) as custodian for (name of minor), a minor under section 319.62 of the 

Wisconsin Statutes" and (ii) deliver the policy to the named custodian. No delivery is necessary if the donor names himself as custodian and the policy is registered in his name. 

4. Is delivery to the custodian necessary to complete the gift?

The Model Law requires delivery of the securities to the custodian as a prerequisite to the completion of the gift, except in the case of registered securities where the donor registers securities in his own name as custodian. Delivery can be made by the donor, his broker as agent or by the transfer agent. Although the Uniform Act does not require the delivery of the securities or money to the custodian to consummate the gift, it is the duty of the donor to do everything in his power to put the subject of the gift in the possession and control of the custodian. Exceptions: Louisiana, Oregon and Washington seem to require delivery of the gift in all cases. 

5. What is the legal effect of a gift under these statutes?

The gift vests indefeasible legal title in the minor and may not be revoked by the donor. In Virginia, although a gift made under the statute is irrevocable, the reference to "indefeasible legal title" has been omitted. 

6. May property already owned by a minor become the subject of a gift so as to bring it under the statutes?

Only new gifts are permitted. Property owned by a minor prior to the enactment of the laws, i.e. U. S. Savings Bonds, cash, securities, may not be made the subject of a new gift under these statutes and normally can not be brought under the custodian's authority pursuant to these statutes although exceptions to this rule have been made in some states by court order. 10

7. Is there any limitation on the size of the gift?

No; however before making a substantial gift of securities to a minor a donor should consider the advantages of creating a trust. Exception: In Washington, the donor may not make gifts of custodial property to any one minor exceeding $3,000 in aggregate value in any one year, or exceeding $30,000 in aggregate value to any one minor. 

Value is computed on basis of actual value of each unit of property on the date the gift became effective. 

8. May a single gift be made to two or more minors jointly or severally? 

No; a gift may only have one beneficiary. 

9. May a donor make a gift to a minor in his last will and testament which will be effective under the statutes?

No; the donor may only make an effective gift during his lifetime. 

However in 1959, Nebraska enacted a separate statute to become effective September 27, 1959 which permits a testator to bequeath securities or money to a minor in his will under the same terms and conditions as those now provided for gifts under the Nebraska Uniform Gifts to Minors Act. The testator merely provides in his will that the bequest be paid or delivered to a custodian subject to the Minor's Act. He should designate the custodian in the will, within the limits of those eligible under the Nebraska Uniform Act, but if he fails to do so, or the designated person is unwilling or unable to serve, the executor is authorized to designate the custodian with the approval of the court. The executor makes distribution of the bequest by transferring the securities or paying the cash, as the case may be, as provided by Section 38-1002 of the Nebraska Uniform Act (See

Sec. 2 of the Uniform Act on page 26) and obtaining the receipt of the custodian which acts as a release and discharge of the bequest. 

10. May a minor make a gift effective under these statutes? 

No; only an adult can make an effective gift. 

11. What becomes of the minor's property if he dies before attaining the age of twenty-one years?

The death of the minor terminates the custodianship and the custodial property must be paid over or delivered to the minor's estate. 

12. May gifts of money be made under the statutes?

Yes; under the Uniform Act money may be given to a minor by paying it into an account with a broker or bank (definition of "bank" varies from state to state) with instructions to open an account in 11

the name of the custodian for the benefit of the minor. The table beginning on page 4 indicates the wording to be used for such gifts. 

Money gifts are not permitted in Alaska, District of Columbia and 

New Jersey. 

13. What advantages are gained by the donor making a gift of money rather than securities?

A gift of money instead of securities by the donor enables the custodian to take advantage of the annual Federal gift tax exclusion while awaiting favorable market conditions before investing. The provision for cash gifts permits the donor to add funds to the account which will enable the custodian to exercise stock rights or to add to the accumulated interest, dividends or account proceeds for the purchase of additional securities. In addition, the donor does not have to pay a transfer tax on a money gift. 

14. May the New York Stock Exchange Monthly Investment Plan be used with these statutes?

Under the Model Law it may be used indirectly. The donor may open a plan to accumulate securities in his own name and periodically give the accumulated shares to the minor by having them registered in the name of the custodian pursuant to state law. Under the Uniform Act, the Monthly Investment Plan may be used directly by the donor opening up the MIP account in the name of the custodian for the benefit of the minor and making periodic cash gifts into the account to be used for the purchase of securities. 

15. How should a gift of securities or money be handled by a broker? 

MODEL: Since money gifts are not permitted in Alaska, District of 

Columbia and New Jersey, the purchase of securities should be made in the donor's account with the donor's funds, accompanied by the necessary registration instructions as to the names of the custodian and minor. (See pages 4, 5 and 8.) Where the securities are already owned by the donor, all he need do is instruct the broker to have the securities registered in proper statutory form. (See 

Note 6 on page 5.)

UNIFORM: For securities, the procedure is the same as above. Where cash is the subject of the gift, it should be deposited with the broker or bank (as defined in the statute) for credit to an account in the name of the custodian for the benefit of the minor, using the statutory language. Such money deposits may be used for the outright purchase of securities or for a purchase under the New York Stock

Exchange Monthly Investment Plan. 

127

S 3: 16. What are the broker's duties with respect to a custodian's account? 

Any account opened in the custodian's name should be carried on the books in accordance with the registration language of the statutes (See Note 6 on page 5), as should securities purchased in such an account. 

17. May securities kept in the custodian's account be registered in “Street name"?

No; the gift is not effective until the securities are fully registered as required by the applicable statute. The statutes require such registration to be maintained thereafter. 

18. May securities be purchased on margin?

No; not by the donor for gift purposes because the securities must be registered pursuant to the statute (no street names) before the gift becomes effective, nor by the custodian because he is required to invest as would a prudent man seeking a reasonable income and preservation of capital. 

19. What responsibility does a broker, bank or transfer agent have under these statutes?

Under the provisions of these statutes, issuers, transfer agents, banks, brokers and others who deal with a "donor" or "custodian" are not responsible for determining the validity of a custodian's appointment, whether his purchases, sales or other acts are authorized or whether instructions or any instruments executed by them are valid and proper. 

Likewise, a broker is not bound to inquire of the custodian as to the disposition of custodial property delivered to him. 

Where, however, a broker handles a transaction under these statutes with actual knowledge that the donor has not complied with the statute or that the custodian is exceeding his powers or that securities or cash already owned by the minor are the subject of the gift, he may lose the protection of the statute. (See Sec. 6 of the Uniform Act on page 30.) 

20. Who may be original custodian of a gift under these statutes?

In general in Model Law states, the donor, an adult member of a minor's family or guardian, may be custodian. The Uniform Act broadened this classification to include other adult persons and trust companies. (See page 8 for table indicating eligible custodians in each state.) 13

21. Are joint custodians authorized?

No; only one person may be custodian at any one time. 

22. Who is eligible to be successor custodian?

Generally, an adult member of the minor's family as defined in the statute, or a guardian of the minor is eligible to be successor custodian with the same powers and duties as the original custodian. In Uniform Act states, a trust company is also eligible to be successor custodian except in Missouri and Oregon. 

23. Should the donor obtain the custodian's consent before appointing him?

Yes; it would avoid difficulties later as the custodian may renounce the appointment or resign if he so chooses. 

24. What effect, if any, will be caused by the appointment of an ineligible custodian?

There is the possibility that a transfer to the ineligible custodian will be rejected by the transfer agent. Also, under the Model Law, such an appointment might result in the gift not taking effect. 

25. How long does a custodian retain control over the minor's property?

If he is not removed and does not resign, die or become incapacitated, he retains custody of the property until either the minor reaches the age of twenty-one years or dies, whichever is sooner. 

26. In general, what are the powers and duties of the custodian? 

He is empowered to collect, hold, manage, invest and reinvest the custodial property. He may pay over to the minor or expend for his benefit any or all of the custodial property as he deems advisable, for the support, maintenance and education of the minor. He must turn the property over to the minor when he becomes 21, or to the minor's estate should the minor die before becoming 21. He may sell, exchange or dispose of the property in the manner and at the times, prices and terms he deems advisable. He must properly register and segregate the property and keep records with respect thereto. 

His guiding rule is to invest the custodial property as would a prudent man of discretion and intelligence who is seeking a reasonable income and the preservation of his capital. (See Sec. 4 of the Uniform Act on page 28.) 14

27. May the custodian maintain a bank account in his name as custodian? 

Yes; if the minor has acquired money under the Model Law, i. e., from dividend income or the proceeds of a sale of securities, the custodian is required to hold such money separate from his own funds and readily identifiable as the minor's. The Mississippi and New Mexico statutes contain this same wording. Under the Uniform Act, such money, or cash gifts, must be held in an account with a broker or bank in the custodian's name using the applicable statutory language. 

28. May proceeds of security sales or cash dividends be held in the custodian account with the broker?

Yes; if they are to be reinvested. However, in Model Law states, any proceeds from the sale of minor's securities or dividends which are not promptly reinvested should be remitted to the custodian for deposit in a bank savings account. In most cases dividend checks are received directly by the custodian and therefore never pass through the custodian account. Under the Uniform Act, cash proceeds or dividends may be held in the custodian account with the broker for a reasonable period. It might, however, be good policy, where funds become substantial, to remit them to the custodian or ask for instructions. 

29. How may stock subscription rights or warrants be handled in a custodian account?

Under the Uniform Act, the donor may supply the necessary money to the account, assuming little or no cash in the account, and thus permit the custodian to exercise the rights. In Alaska, District of 

Columbia and New Jersey, this simple procedure is not possible because of the prohibition against money gifts. Unless there is enough money in the custodial account to exercise the rights, the custodian may have to sell the rights and retain the proceeds. 

30. As transactions occur in the custodial account and dividends, cash, and securities are received, how should the property be handled by the custodian?

All registered securities held by the custodian should be registered as provided in the applicable statute. (See Note 6 on page 5.)

Money should be kept in a bank account (or with a broker in Uniform Act states) using the same statutory wording. Other property (bearer securities, etc.) must be kept separate and distinct from his own property, clearly identifiable as belonging to the minor. (See 

Sec. 4(g) of the Uniform Act on page 29.) 15

31. What principle should guide the custodian in dealing with the minor's property?

Under both statutes it is the custodian's responsibility to invest and reinvest the securities or other custodial property as would a prudent man of discretion and intelligence who is seeking a reasonable income and the preservation of his capital. The Model Law states (and 

Louisiana and Oregon) restrict a custodian's reinvestment of the minor's property to investments in securities. Generally the custodian is not required to observe the provisions of statutes which deal with investments by fiduciaries. Exceptions: In Idaho, Illinois and New

Mexico a custodian would appear to be required to observe the provisions of local laws which restrict investments by fiduciaries. 

32. Does a custodian receive compensation for his services?

In the Model Law states he is only entitled to reimbursement for reasonable expenses incurred in the performance of his duties. However, if the court-appointed guardian of the property of the minor is acting as custodian, he is entitled to be compensated as if he were holding the custodial property in his capacity as guardian. In the 

Uniform Act states, provision is usually made to permit compensation for a custodian's reasonable services (except if he is also the donor), as well as reimbursement for necessary expenses. However, the amount of compensation is usually controlled by the donor, applicable custodian or guardian statutes, or by order of the court. Exception: No compensation is permitted in Louisiana, Missouri or Oregon unless the guardian is custodian. Iowa prohibits a donor from receiving reimbursement or compensation. In New York only a trust company or guardian of the property of a minor is entitled to compensation for service as custodian. 

33. Is the custodian required to furnish a bond?

Under the Model Law, only if he is being compensated for his services-i. e., he is also guardian of the minor's property. No bond is necessary under the Uniform Act unless the local court orders a bond to be furnished. 

34. Is a custodian liable for losses to custodial property?

If a custodian is not compensated he is not liable for any losses except those which are the result of his bad faith, intentional wrongdoing or of investing the minor's property in a manner other than as a prudent man of discretion and intelligence seeking reasonable income and 16

preservation of his capital. In addition, under the Uniform Act, he is responsible for losses due to his gross negligence. In New York a compensated custodian is subject to the same liabilities as a guardian of the estate of the minor except as his powers differ. 

35. May a custodian resign and designate his successor? 

MODEL: Yes; he may resign by executing an instrument of resignation designating a successor custodian (an adult member of the minor's family or a guardian of the minor) and delivering same with the minor's property to his successor. It would seem that a formal letter from the resigning custodian addressed to the successor custodian, reciting the history of his custodianship, listing the custodial property and indicating his resignation might suffice as an instrument of resignation. The custodian may also petition the court for permission to resign and to have a successor appointed. 

UNIFORM: A custodian, other than the donor, may resign and designate his successor in the same manner as under the Model Law. 

An adult member of the minor's family, a guardian of the minor and a trust company (except in Missouri and Oregon) are eligible to become successor custodians. A donor who is also custodian generally has to petition the court for permission to resign and for the designation of a successor. Exceptions: Florida, Louisiana and Vermont in all cases require the custodian to petition the court for permission to resign and for the appointment of a successor. 

Connecticut, New Mexico and New York also permit a donor-custodian to resign and designate his successor, but other custodians in New Mexico must petition the court. Virginia permits a donorcustodian to petition for his replacement by the minor's guardian who will thereafter act as guardian and not successor custodian. 

36. If the custodian is ineligible, dies, renounces or becomes incapacitated before the minor becomes twenty-one, who is appointed in his place? 

MODEL: If there is a duly appointed and acting general guardian of the minor's property, he becomes the successor custodian. If there is no guardian, and the minor is over fourteen, the minor may designate either an adult member of the minor's family or a guardian of the minor as successor custodian except in the District of Columbia. If the minor is under fourteen, such successor may be designated by the last acting custodian or his legal representative. Generally the laws also provide a procedure for court-appointed successors if the position 17

can be filled in no other way. Some provide for the replacement of a custodian if it is in the best interests of the minor. 

UNIFORM: Under Sec. 7(d) of the Uniform Act, the guardian of the minor becomes the successor custodian. If the minor has no guardian, the court will designate one upon petition by the persons mentioned in the Section. Some states provide for removal of a custodian for cause, or in the minor's best interest, by petition to a court. Exceptions: Upon the death of a custodian in Connecticut, the court will not grant the petition for appointment of a successor until a "consent to transfer" has been obtained from the tax department and a complete inventory of custodial property has been made. Hawaii also permits a minor over twenty to designate the successor custodian. 

Illinois and Louisiana in all cases require a petition to the court for the appointment of a successor custodian. Massachusetts permits a minor over fourteen, or other person if the minor is under fourteen, to designate an adult member of the family or a trust company as successor custodian. Missouri provides a detailed procedure for succession in Section 8 of the law to cover all eventualities. Missouri and Oregon do not permit a trust company to be successor custodian. 

37. May the custodial property be used for the support, maintenance, education and general use and benefit of the minor? Who determines such use?

Yes; generally any part or all of the custodial property may be used for these purposes as the custodian in his discretion deems suitable and proper, without court order and without considering other property belonging to the minor or who has the responsibility for his support. In addition, in Uniform Act states, the parent or guardian of the minor (or the minor if he has reached the age of fourteen years) may petition the proper court for an order calling upon the custodian to pay over a part or all of the custodial property necessary for these purposes. 

38. What records are required to be kept by the custodian? 

No provision is made for record-keeping under the Model Law states (or in Oregon) but since a custodian may be required to account he would be well advised to keep detailed records. Under Sec. 4(h) of the Uniform Act the custodian is required to keep records of all transactions and to make them available for inspection at reasonable intervals. In Massachusetts the custodial accounts must be exhibited once a year. 18

39. Must a custodian account for his term of custodianship? 

MODEL: Not unless the minor (or legal representative), his parents or the successor custodian, petitions the court for an accounting within one year after the minor reaches the age of twenty-one years or dies, whichever is sooner.

UNIFORM: Besides keeping records available for inspection, the custodian may be required to give an accounting at any time. (See Sec. 8 of the Uniform Act on page 31.) Exceptions: California requires the custodian to account upon delivering or paying over the custodial property to the minor when he reaches 21, or to his estate should he die before 21. The right to petition for an accounting exists for one year after the filing of a final accounting and delivery of the custodial property. In Massachusetts, the custodian must deliver a final account within six months from the date the custodianship terminates and the right to petition for an accounting exists for two years from the delivery of such final account. In Missouri the custodian may also petition for permission to account voluntarily. Louisiana law permits the minor, parents or guardian to petition for an accounting if the custodian fails to render one within a reasonable time after written request. The right to an accounting in Louisiana exists for a four year period after the minor reaches 21 or dies. In Oregon, no accounting is required unless the minor, parent, minor's legal representative or successor custodian petitions for one within 2 years after the minor reaches 

21 or dies. 

40. Does a minor's previously appointed guardian have any power over the custodial property?

No; the statutes provide that no guardian of the person or property of the minor has any control over the custodial property unless he also happens to be custodian under the provisions of the statute. In 

New York, a special guardian may be appointed by the court during a custodial proceeding for the protection of the minor. 

41. If a state replaces its present Model Law with the Uniform Act do any complications arise?

Usually not because the repeal provision in the Uniform Act does not affect gifts made under the Model Law. This provision is customarily worded so that it will apply to gifts made under the old Act except insofar as such application impairs constitutionally vested rights. In addition, the repeal provision ordinarily stresses that the 19

Uniform Act shall be construed as a continuation of the Model Law and not as a new enactment. Therefore for most purposes gifts made under the old law will be governed by the provisions of the new law. 

However, in California and South Carolina, gifts made under the old law apparently will continue to be governed by that law. 

42. Do any complications arise where the parties to a gift move from one state to another?

The validity of gifts made in a state prior to the move is not affected. 

However, there may be some difficulty bringing such gifts under the laws of the new state of residence. Transfer agents may be reluctant to approve this change since there may be an impairment of constitutionally vested rights. Of course the laws of the new state may be used for any new gifts which are made. In the case of families who are sent out of the country in the armed forces or foreign service it would seem that the laws of their state of domicile might be applicable. 20

Tax Consequences of Gifts Made Under These Laws. 

INCOME TAX FEDERAL: 

The Internal Revenue Service has held that regardless of the relationship of the donor or of the custodian to the minor, income from a gift which is used to discharge or satisfy the legal obligation of any person to support or maintain the minor is, to the extent so used, taxable to such person under section 61 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. To the extent that the income from such gift is not charged to the person obligated to support or maintain the minor, such income is taxable to the minor. (Rev. Rul. 56-484, restated in Rev. Rul. 59-357)

Therefore, to the extent that income from the gift is accumulated (or reinvested), it is taxable to the child. With the usual exemptions and deductions to which he is entitled, the minor may have over $700 of taxfree income each year. Furthermore, the parents of the minor may still claim the $600 exemption regardless of the minor's income if the parents actively support him and he is under 19, or a student. 

STATE: 

Inquiries to the Tax Commissioners of states which have adopted the Minors Law reveal a tendency, where possible, to follow the Federal rule as stated in Revenue Ruling 56-484 etc. above.

FEDERAL GIFT TAX MODEL:

Three years later, Rev. Rul. 59-357 restated the previous ruling and added that no taxable gift occurs by reason of a subsequent resignation of the custodian or termination of the custodianship. 

A gift is valued for gift tax purposes at the time of the gift at the full fair market value of the property transferred.

ESTATE TAX 

In 1957, the Internal Revenue Service ruled that the value of property transferred by a donor to himself as custodian for a minor child under these statutes is includible in the donor's gross estate for Federal estate tax purposes in the event of his death while acting as custodian before the minor reaches 21. (Rev. Rul. 57-366) (Rev. Rul. 57-366) In 1959 Rev. Rul. 

59-357 restated this ruling and also said that in all other circumstances custodial property would be included in the estate of the donee-minor. 

Since this ruling only applies where the donor appoints himself as custodian, the estate tax consequences can be avoided by appointing some person other than the donor as custodian-preferably someone who is under no legal duty to support the minor. This ruling is relatively unimportant to a donor of moderate means since no federal estate tax is payable unless his net estate is more than $60,000. Rev. Rul. 59-357 also includes the value of such transferred property in the estate of the donor if the property is given in contemplation of death within 3 years of the donor's death.

STOCK TRANSFER TAXES 

STATE: Stock transfer taxes are levied by Florida, New York, South Carolina and Texas. Consult the appropriate statute for application to gifts of securities or custodian transactions.

FEDERAL 

Gifts of Money-No stock transfer tax is payable on a gift of money to a minor.

Gifts of Securities-Since a gift of securities from a donor to a minor results in a transfer of legal ownership, it is subject to a federal stock transfer tax. In Revenue Ruling 58-73 it was held that the transfer tax attaches at the time the donor registers the securities in the name of the custodian or delivers them by deed of gift.

Buy or Sell Transactions by the Custodian--Where the custodian uses cash gifts or account proceeds to purchase securities or where he sells the minor's securities, he is in the same position as any other customer with respect to the imposition of stock transfer taxes. 

Transfer Tax Rates-Gifts of securities are taxed at the rate of 4¢ on each $100 or major fraction thereof of the actual value of the stock transferred with a maximum of 8¢ on any one share for shares valued at more than $200 each. In no event will the tax on a gift be less than 4¢, the minimum rate for any transfer involving $50 or less of actual value. 22

The actual value of a gift of securities for transfer tax purposes is the mean between the highest and lowest selling prices on the day of the gift. Pending further clarification by the Treasury Department, if the mean cannot be determined because there are no sales on the day of the gift, the New York Stock Exchange suggests using the mean between the bid and asked prices on such day, or the bid price where there is no asked price and the asked price where no bid price, to determine actual value. 

The transfer tax will not apply to any sale of an odd-lot of stock to a broker, pursuant to his customer's order, by a registered odd-lot dealer or specialist. Bond transfers are taxable on their face value at the rate of 5¢ per $100 or fraction thereof.

BASIC UNIFORM GIFTS TO MINORS ACT 

(The following provisions are generally representative of the Uniform Act which has been adopted in 43 states. Although the wording deviates in some states from that indicated below, in most cases it is identical. To be sure of the exact wording in your state you would be well advised to have your attorney consult your local statute or the Commerce Clearing House 

Stock Transfer Guide, Volume 1. Beginning on page 5241 of that Guide, the Uniform Act is reproduced and has been annotated by sections to show variations in each state.)

SECTION 1. Definitions. In this act, unless the context otherwise requires: (a) An “adult” is a person who has attained the age of twenty-one years. (b) A "bank" is a bank, trust company, national banking association, savings bank, industrial bank, . . ., etc. (c) A “broker” is a person lawfully engaged in the business of effecting transactions in securities for the account of others. The term includes a bank which effects such transactions. The term also includes a person lawfully engaged in buying and selling securities for his own account, through a broker or otherwise, as a part of a regular business. (d) "Court" means the . . . (e) "The custodial property" includes: (1) all securities and money under the supervision of the same custodian for the same minor as a consequence of a gift or gifts made to the minor in a manner prescribed in this act; (2) the income from the custodial property; and (3) the proceeds, immediate and remote, from the sale, exchange, conversion, investment, reinvestment or other disposition of such securities, money and income. . . . court. (f) A "custodian" is a person so designated in a manner prescribed in this act. (g) A "guardian" of a minor includes the general guardian, guardian, tutor or curator of his property, estate or person. (h) An "issuer" is a person who places or authorizes the placing of his name on a security (other than as a transfer agent) to evidence that it represents a share, participation or other interest in his property or in an 25

enterprise or to evidence his duty or undertaking to perform an obligation evidenced by the security, or who becomes responsible for or in place of any such person. (i) A “legal representative" of a person is his executor or the administrator, general guardian, guardian, committee, conservator, tutor or curator of his property or estate. (j) A "member" of a "minor's family" means any of the minor's parents, grandparents, brothers, sisters, uncles and aunts, whether of the whole blood or the half blood, or by or through legal adoption. (k) A "minor" is a person who has not attained the age of twenty-one years. (1) A "security" includes any note, stock, treasury stock, bond, debenture, evidence of indebtedness, certificate of interest or participation in an oil, gas or mining title or lease or in payments out of production under such a title or lease, collateral trust certificate, transferable share, voting trust certificate or, in general, any interest or instrument commonly known as a security, or any certificate of interest or participation in, any temporary or interim certificate, receipt or certificate of deposit for, or any warrant or right to subscribe to or purchase, any of the foregoing. The term does not include a security of which the donor is the issuer. A security is in "registered form" when it specifies a person entitled to it or to the rights it evidences and its transfer may be registered upon books maintained for that purpose by or on behalf of the issuer. (m) A "transfer agent" is a person who acts as authenticating trustee, transfer agent, registrar or other agent for an issuer in the registration of transfers of its securities or in the issue of new securities or in the cancellation of surrendered securities. (n) A "trust company" is a bank authorized to exercise trust powers. 

SECTION 2. Manner of Making Gift. (a) An adult person may, during his lifetime, make a gift of a security or money to a person who is a minor on the date of the gift: (1) if the subject of the gift is a security in registered form, by registering it in the name of the donor, another adult person or a trust company, followed, in substance, by the words: "as custodian for (name of minor) under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act"; 26

(2) if the subject of the gift is a security not in registered form, by delivering it to an adult person other than the donor or a trust company, accompanied by a statement of gift in the following form, in substance, signed by the donor and the person designated as custodian: 

“GIFT UNDER THE (Name of State) Uniform Gifts TO MINORS ACT 

I, (name of donor), hereby deliver to (name of custodian) as custodian for (name of minor) under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act, the following security (ies): (insert an appropriate description of the security or securities delivered sufficient to identify it or them) (signature of donor) (name of custodian) hereby acknowledges receipt of the above described security(ies) as custodian for the above minor under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act.

Dated: 

(signature of custodian)" (3) If the subject of the gift is money, by paying or delivering it to a broker or a bank for credit to an account in the name of the donor, another adult person or a bank with trust powers, followed, in substance, by the words: "as custodian for (name of minor) under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act". (b) Any gift made in a manner prescribed in Subsection (a) may be made to only one minor and only one person may be the custodian. (c) A donor who makes a gift to a minor in a manner prescribed in Subsection (a) shall promptly do all things within his power to put the subject of the gift in the possession and control of the custodian, but neither the donor's failure to comply with this Subsection, nor his designation of an ineligible person as custodian, nor renunciation by the person designated as custodian affects the consummation of the gift. 

SECTION 3. Effect of Gift. (a) A gift made in a manner prescribed in this act is irrevocable and conveys to the minor indefeasibly vested legal title to the security or money given, but no guardian of the minor has any right, power, duty or authority with respect to the custodial property except as provided in this act. 27

(b) By making a gift in a manner prescribed in this act, the donor incorporates in his gift all the provisions of this act and grants to the custodian, and to any issuer, transfer agent, bank, broker or third person dealing with a person designated as custodian, the respective powers, rights and immunities provided in this act.

SECTION 4. Duties and Powers of Custodian. (a) The custodian shall collect, hold, manage, invest and reinvest the custodial property. (b) The custodian shall pay over to the minor for expenditure by him, or expend for the minor's benefit, so much of or all the custodial property as the custodian deems advisable for the support, maintenance, education and benefit of the minor in the manner, at the time or times, and to the extent that the custodian in his discretion deems suitable and proper, with or without court order, with or without regard to the duty of himself or of any other person to support the minor or his ability to do so, and with or without regard to any other income or property of the minor which may be applicable or available for any such purpose. (c) The court, on the petition of a parent or guardian of the minor or of the minor, if he has attained the age of fourteen years, may order the custodian to pay over to the minor for expenditure by him or to expend so much of or all the custodial property as is necessary for the minor's support, maintenance or education. 

(d) To the extent that the custodial property is not so expended, the custodian shall deliver or pay it over to the minor on his attaining the age of twenty-one years or, if the minor dies before attaining the age of twentyone years, he shall thereupon deliver or pay it over to the estate of the minor. (e) The custodian, notwithstanding statutes restricting investments by fiduciaries, shall invest and reinvest the custodial property as would a prudent man of discretion and intelligence who is seeking a reasonable income and the preservation of his capital, except that he may, in his discretion and without liability to the minor or his estate, retain a security given to the minor in a manner prescribed in this act. (f) The custodian may sell, exchange, convert or otherwise dispose of custodial property in the manner, at the time or times, for the price or prices and upon the terms he deems advisable. He may vote in person or by general or limited proxy a security which is custodial property. He may consent, directly or through a committee or other agent, to the reorganization, consolidation, merger, dissolution or liquidation of an issuer, security which is custodial property, and to the sale, lease, pledge or mortgage of any property by or to such an issuer, and to any other action by 28

such an issuer. He may execute and deliver any and all instruments in writing which he deems advisable to carry out any of his powers as custodian. (g) The custodian shall register each security which is custodial property and in registered form in the name of the custodian, followed, in substance, by the words: "as custodian for (name of minor) under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act". The custodian shall hold all money which is custodial property in an account with a broker or in a bank in the name of the custodian, followed, in substance, by the words: "as custodian for (name of minor) under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act". The custodian shall keep all other custodial property separate and distinct from his own property in a manner to identify it clearly as custodial property. (h) The custodian shall keep records of all transactions with respect to the custodial property and make them available for inspection at reasonable intervals by a parent or legal representative of the minor or by the minor, if he has attained the age of fourteen years. (i) A custodian has and holds as powers in trust, with respect to the custodial property, in addition to the rights and powers provided in this act, all the rights and powers which a guardian has with respect to property not held as custodial property.

SECTION 5. Custodian's Expenses, Compensation, Bond and Lia bilities. (a) A custodian is entitled to reimbursement from the custodial property for his reasonable expenses incurred in the performance of his duties. (b) A custodian may act without compensation for his services. (c) Unless he is a donor, a custodian may receive from the custodial property reasonable compensation for his services determined by one of the following standards in the order stated: (1) A direction by the donor when the gift is made; (2) A statute of this state applicable to custodians; (3) The statute of this state applicable to guardians; (4) An order of the court. (d) Except as otherwise provided in this act, a custodian shall not be required to give a bond for the performance of his duties. 29

EM (e) A custodian not compensated for his services is not liable for losses to the custodial property unless they result from his bad faith, intentional wrongdoing or gross negligence or from his failure to maintain the standard of prudence in investing the custodial property provided in this act.

SECTION 6. Exemption of Third Persons From Liability. No issuer, transfer agent, bank, broker or other person acting on the instructions of or otherwise dealing with any person purporting to act as a donor or in the capacity of a custodian is responsible for determining whether the person designated by the purported donor or purporting to act as a custodian has been duly designated or whether any purchase, sale or transfer to or by or any other act of any person purporting to act in the capacity of custodian is in accordance with or authorized by this act, or is obliged to inquire into the validity or propriety under this act of any instrument or instructions executed or given by a person purporting to act as a donor or in the capacity of a custodian, or is bound to see to the application by any person purporting to act in the capacity of a custodian of any money or other property paid or delivered to him. 

SECTION 7. Resignation, Death or Removal of Custodian; Bond; 

Appointment of Successor Custodian. (a) Only an adult member of the minor's family, a guardian of the minor or a trust company is eligible to become successor custodian. A successor custodian has all the rights, powers, duties and immunities of a custodian designated in a manner prescribed by this act. (b) A custodian, other than the donor, may resign and designate his successor by: (1) executing an instrument of resignation designating the successor custodian; and (2) causing each security which is custodial property and in registered form to be registered in the name of the successor custodian followed, in substance, by the words: "as custodian for (name of minor) under the (name of state) Uniform Gifts to Minors Act"; and (3) delivering to the successor custodian the instrument of resignation, each security registered in the name of the successor custodian and all other custodial property, together with any additional instruments required for the transfer thereof. (c) A custodian, whether or not a donor, may petition the court for permission to resign and for the designation of a successor custodian. 30

(d) If the person designated as custodian is not eligible, renounces or dies before the minor attains the age of twenty-one years, the guardian of the minor shall be successor custodian. If the minor has no guardian, a donor, his legal representative, the legal representative of the custodian, an adult member of the minor's family, or the minor, if he has attained the age of fourteen years, may petition the court for the designation of a successor custodian. (e) A donor, the legal representative of a donor, an adult member of the minor's family, a guardian of the minor or the minor, if he has attained the age of fourteen years, may petition the court that, for cause shown in the petition, the custodian be removed and a successor custodian be designated or, in the alternative, that the custodian be required to give bond for the performance of his duties. (f) Upon the filing of a petition as provided in this Section, the court shall grant an order, directed to the persons and returnable on such notice as the court may require, to show cause why the relief prayed for in the petition should not be granted and, in due course, grant such relief as the court finds to be in the best interests of the minor. 

SECTION 8. Accounting by Custodian. (a) The minor, if he has attained the age of fourteen years, or the legal representative of the minor, an adult member of the minor's family, or a donor or his legal representative may petition the court for an accounting by the custodian or his legal representative. (b) The court, in a proceeding under this act or otherwise, may require or permit the custodian or his legal representative to account and, if the custodian is removed, shall so require and order delivery of all custodial property to the successor custodian and the execution of all instruments required for the transfer thereof.

SECTION 9. Construction. (a) This act shall be so construed as to effectuate its general purpose to make uniform the law of those states which enact it. (b) This act shall not be construed as providing an exclusive method for making gifts to minors.

SECTION 10. Short Title. This act may be cited as the "[name of enact ing state] Uniform Gifts to Minors Act".

SECTION 12. Repeal. (Section 12 has been used when a Model Law 

State replaces its law with the Uniform Act)... is hereby repealed, but the repeal does not affect gifts made in a manner prescribed therein nor the powers, duties and immunities conferred by gifts in such manner upon custodians and persons dealing with custodians. The provisions of this act henceforth apply, however, to all gifts made in a manner and form prescribed in the act hereby repealed except insofar as such application impairs constitutionally vested rights. The sections of this act shall be construed as a continuation of the provisions of the act hereby repealed, modified or amended according to the language employed, and not as a new enactment.
