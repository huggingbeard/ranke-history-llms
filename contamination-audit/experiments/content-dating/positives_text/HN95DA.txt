OURS IS A UNIVERSE OF UNIVERSAL LAW 

(2) This is a universe of law. So far as man's investigations go, it is orderly in all its parts. And one of its wonders is the simplicity of the means by which Nature achieves her purpose.

The reader is, of course, familiar with the saying that self-preservation is the first law of nature; but he is asked to note that it applies to animate nature only. It does not apply to sticks or stones, nor to anything else in the inanimate realm.

He is asked to note also that, although it is true that men and the wild beasts are both gregarious, the two groups differ in the degree to which their team-work is perfected. 

Wolves hunt food in packs, men organize to produce wealth; but one [2]

notes a great difference in the way they share the results. 

MEN'S PECULIAR PROBLEMS CALL FOR SPECIAL LAWS 

(3) (See The Answer, Nos. 9, 14, 36, 68, 92.)

When a wolf pack brings down its quarry in the hunt, each wolf in the pack helps himself by direct action to a share; there is in the wolf world no problem of distribution.

However, in the case of man, the processes of production are so complex that the problem of distributing the product is (apparently) a complicated and difficult problem. 

This problem has baffled mankind for ages and grown more baffling as our communities grew in population and our social life became complex. 

This problem is distinctively a human problem. Although they live in communities, bees and beavers [3]

are confronted by no such problem. 

In our search for the key to this problem of wealth distribution, we must look for a provision of Nature which applies exclusively to human relations; for, in all the universe, there is no other need like this. 

Here is a field for which it is necessary, if this portion of her universe is to have the stability which characterizes its other portions, that Nature provide for the apportionment of our product by means of a separate science as exact and dependable as any of our sciences.

Here lies the need for a science of distribution of wealth. 

Physics, chemistry, botany, zoology, biology, physiology, botany and the other recognized sciences are the sciences of production.

We study production as a science and in that field we have succeeded. [4]

Men need, now, to discover the science of wealth distribution and to study that as we have studied the sciences of production. 

As Nature has never been known to overlook anything, the presumption is that Nature has ordained the laws necessary to provide a just distribution of our product.

It is our task to find these laws and learn to use them as we had to find and learn to use the laws in the field of production.

In these pages the author seeks to introduce the reader to that science, to inspire confidence in it and to outline its processes; for, as yet our schools offer nothing scientific in this field. The "economics" and "economists" of our schools are better called alconomics and alconomists, because of their likeness to the alchemy and alchemists of oid. [5]

PRIMARY PRODUCERS AND THEIR CO-PRODUCERS 

(See The Answer, Nos. 7, 20, 68, 92.) (4) 

Unlike the products of bees and beavers, our product is always a joint product; there is always a primary producer who is aided by innumerable co-producers. 

Farmers are primary producers of wheat; makers of plows are among the co-producers of wheat. 

Thanks to these co-producers, the farmers need not make their plows. 

The doctors, dentists, attorneys and others who help us to keep fit and help us in other ways are our co-producers. 

So are the firemen, policemen and the traffic officers.

Co-producers save primary producers time, labor and money. 

The more co-producers, the more this saving is. [6]

The greater this saving, the more a people can produce.

Each producer, whether primary or secondary, is entitled to his share of the product.

But men have so far failed miserably in the matter of distribution. 

PLANNING IS POSSIBLE ONLY UNDER NATURAL LAW 

(5) (See The Answer, Dialogues 5, 6 and 8.)

To solve this problem, men must first meet two conditions:

They must agree on a plan and must work together to carry out their plan. 

Their plan must be in harmony with the laws of Nature operating in that field. 

These conditions cannot be met by men unless they have, in nature outside of themselves, a dependable standard by which economic opin. ions may be checked and corrected. 

Except men have such a standard, it is impossible for them to know [7]

which of their opinions are correct. 

And, until this is known, it is impossible for men to agree; and, for this reason, successful planning in this field is impossible. 

It is because we have such standards in physics and chemistry that we can plan successfully in those fields. (See p 4.) 

But, as economics has not been accepted as a science as exact as any science, men have no such standards in this field and are unable to check and correct economic opinions and so cannot agree on economic issues. 

SUCH STANDARDS EITHER DO, OR DO NOT, EXIST 

(6) 

(See The Answer, Dialogue No. 9.) 

Natural law controls the relations of cause and effect.

But for it, like causes would not always produce like effects. [8]

But for natural law, fire might boil water one time, and freeze it the next.

But for natural law, our planning and labor would be in vain; because we could not be sure of anything. 

If we are to solve the problems which hinge upon the distribution of our product, we must have the assurance that this, too, is a field of law; and we must have here the guidance of dependable natural law as we have in the shop sciences. 

Either economics is a science as exact as any, and like economic effects always follow like economic causes, or this is not true. 

Either Nature has provided laws designed to serve the body politic as the laws of physiology serve an animal body, or she has not.

The fate of civilization depends on the answer to this question. [9]

NATURE'S DUAL SYSTEM OF CONTROLLING MATTERS 

(7) (See The Answer, Dialogues 96, 104.) 

Nature is a very clever Old Lady! 

And she has her own plan for the control of her creatures. 

She gives the individual freedom of the will. 

And gives him that freedom in the field where freedom means most to him—that is, in the field of his individual or personal interests. 

Then, to protect others from having their interests disturbed by the misuse, by individuals of their freedom, she limits that freedom to the field of their individual interests. 

She does this by placing all general, or public, interests under the control of unchanging and unalterable natural law.

From this it follows that only the [10]

problems of private life are problems in morality; and that the problems which are vital to all mankind are problems in natural law. 

By this clever device of dual control, Nature recognizes the sovereignty of the individual and yet protects her species from injury by the conduct of individuals.

HUMAN INTERESTS ARE OF THREE DISTINCT TYPES 

(8) 

(See The Answer, Dialogue No. 23.) 

Upon analysis, it is found that men's interests are of three distinct types.

First, there are the personal and individual interests, such as one's choice of religion, etc. 

There is only one right answer to problems in this field, and that is the answer of the person involved. 

The second class of interests con- [11]

sists of the interests controlled by natural law, such as the interests hinging upon the tides, the seasons, or the processes of nature.

There is only one right solution for a problem in this field, and that is the solution which is indicated by the decree of Nature herself. 

Finally, there are problems which lie outside these two fields. Among these are such matters as the location of roads, bridges, parks, etc. 

To these problems, there are always more than one acceptable answer; but, as each person interested has a right to be consulted, the only way such matters can be fairly decided is by taking a vote.

This is the only field in which the democratic process is properly applicable; for we cannot overrule the laws of Nature; and, in the field of our individual and personal inter- [12]

ests, it is undemocratic to resort to the democratic method. 

The failure of human government is not due to any lack in men of fitness for self-government; it is due, instead, to our failure to take advantage of Nature's provisions for the welfare of human society, and to our attempts to substitute control by human statute for that of the instincts and of natural law. By endowing man with the gregarious instinct Nature provided for all of the contingencies of his community life.

Not knowing this, men have undertaken to control such matters as wages, hours of labor, city zoning, prices, etc. Failure was inevitable. 

Not only that, but any attempt by men to control such matters necessarily involves an invasion of the rights of the individual. [13]

THE MOST REVOLUTIONARY OF ALL REVOLUTIONS 

(9) 

(See The Answer, Dialogue, 26(1-4) 

If there are no natural laws of economics, it is impossible for men to know the either the cause or the cure of our economic ills.

In that case it is a waste of time to even discuss economic problems. 

Even if we agreed upon a plan, that plan would fail, if there are no dependable laws in nature by which like economic causes are always followed by like economic effects. 

Even if some plan seemed to be a success, we could not be sure (if there is no dependable sequence of cause and effect in the field of economics) that the same plan would ever again work out that same way. 

If there are in nature no dependable laws for the control of the relations of economic cause and effect, [14]

planned economics is as futile and senseless as human planning in the field of astronomy would be. 

But, if these laws do exist, men can by resort to them solve problems in civics and in statecraft with the same certainty that they solve shop problems, and can solve them without regard to men's morality. 

Once the view that economics is a science as exact as any science is accepted, men will see that here as in other scientific fields the moral law is limited to the attitude men take toward their problems and that the problems themselves are problems in natural law. (See pp 9, 10.)

When men realize that their economic ills are the result not of the selfishness of men, but of our ignorance of economics, men will find it possible to be as impersonal in attacking their problems in civics and [15]

statecraft as they are in attacking a problem in mathematics. (pp 4, 13) 

The suspicion, resentment, bitterness and hostility which now mar our relations would then disappear; and men would cooperate in civics and statecraft as naturally and as wholeheartedly as they do in the solution of shop problems.

Man is gregarious—that is, he is by instinct a social creature. 

There is general agreement upon that point; and it is, of course admitted that gregarious creatures are instinctively cooperative. 

But if man is gregarious and if, as we are taught, gregarious creatures are instinctively cooperative, the theory that our economic ills result from the selfish conduct of men must be abandoned as false.

Instead of complaining of human nature and trying to reform men, [16]

we should investigate the possibility of using the forces in this field as we use fire, steam and electricity in the field of physics. (See p 13.)

To say that a gregarious creature has non-gregarious instincts which need to be curbed for the good of the species is a self-contradiction. 

It is true, of course, that we have revolutionized society by mastering the shop sciences and learning to use fire, steam and electricity; but the results of this accomplishment are trivial in comparison with the revolution which would follow the mastery of the science of economics and the successful use of men's selfinterest as our motive power in the social field. (See p 13.)

Some of the conclusions to which we are led by the study of economics as a science are set forth in the following section. [17]

THE CLIMAX OF THE LONG WAR FOR THE RIGHTS OF MEN 

Virtue is individual, not joint. 

There is no such thing as joint virtue. 

A man and his wife may each be virtuous; but there is no such thing as family virtue, or corporation virtue, or community virtue, or national or race virtue. 

Morality concerns only men's attitude toward their problems; the problems themselves are problems not in moral law, but in natural law. (See p 15.)

Just as the study of the science of sanitation led men to abandon the superstition of the evil eye and the witch-hunting inspired by it; so the study of economics as a science as exact as any science will lead to the abandonment of the superstiof the evil heart and the witch-hunting inspired by the belief that our ills are the result of other men's selfishness.

The student should note that, in commerce, there are only three recognized ways for men to obtain wealth:

They can get it as wages; 

They can get it as interest; 

They can get it as rent-meaning the part of the rental which is over and above the charge for housing. (The public has the permission of the copyright owner to reprint this section, No. 10.) [18]

Wages and interest can be got only by making a corresponding investment of labor, or of capital—that is, by being useful. 

Limited to wages and intrest, men prosper only as they are useful and agreeable. 

Economics is not a substitute for virtue!

Men need economics and virtue; just as as they need mathematics and virtue. 

Limiting men to wages and interest will not make them honest; but it does compel them to act like honest men and good neighbors.

From this one learns that many current beliefs must be abandoned as fallacies. 

Among these fallacies are the beliefs- 

That high "land values" are an asset.

That Labor and Capital have conflicting interests.

That a gregarious creature can have instincts which would harm society. 

That law and order among men is a matter of compromise. 

That a government supported by taxation can be democratic. 

That the people profit from economies in government expenditures.

That people who pay taxes can truthfully say they do not pay tribute. 

That economic ills are the result of men's selfishness and indifference. 

That ability to pay should be consid- [19]

ered in raising government revenue. 

That money, a symbol of the value of a product, has power over the producer. 

That man (the maker of the machine) has anything to fear from it, or from the Machine Age.

That there can be conflict between the sovereignty of society and the soverty of the gregarious individual. 

That the success of public ownership, or of "self-liquidating" government projects, benefits ultimate consumers.

That human government is necessary to maintain order among, and guarantee justice to, the gregarious members of human society. 

That crooked politics can be prevented by a strong government at home, and that a strong international government could protect us from war. 

That our own government here in the United States is the better because we limit the power of each branch (the legislative, executive and judicial) by giving some of its powers to other branches-as though the road to good government lay through the jungles and swamps of compromise and poltical log-rolling. 

Crooked politics and war are economic "short circuits," due not to men's selfish- [20]

ness but to our ignorance of economics. 

We can solve all our economic problems by resort to the natural laws of economics regardless of men's morals. (See p 15.)

Nature reserves to herself the exclusive control, by natural law and by instincts, all matters vital to the welfare of the race. 

She has trusted to men the control of those things only which concern the private, or personal, interests of individuals. 

Nothing vital to the welfare of mankind is subject either to the control of, or to interference by, any man or government. 

As the democratic method is futile in matters conrolled by natural law and is undemocratic in those matters properly subject to the individual will, there is left only a narrow field in which the democratic method is properly applicable. 

This means— 

That cities can be safely trusted to zone themselves, automatically.

That we can depend upon Greed to protect us from war. (See p 13.)

That we can depend on Ambition and Avarice to cause all business men and politicians to act honestly.

That we can enlist Race Prejudice as a guardian of race harmony and enlist all these as champions of democracy. [21]

DIRECT AND INDIRECT COMPENSATION 

(11) (See The Answer, 7, 13, 20, 33, 68, 92.)

The primary producer gets aid of two distinct kinds. (See p 6.)

He gets direct aid from his employees and from dealers and from service men. 

He gets, also, the indirect aid of our co-producers. 

He knows what help he has had directly from others, and what is due them. 

But he does not know what is due his co-producers. (See p 6.)

He does not know how many they numbe, or where to look for them. 

The co-producers, themselves, do not know what is due them.

OUR CO-PRODUCERS 

SHOULD BE PAID (12) (See The Answer, Nos. 7, 13, 20, 80, 92.)

The co-producer service described in Section 4 should be compensated. [22]

A business or professional man is a primary producer and is paid directly for service rendered by him to his customers, to his clients, or to his patients. (See p 6.) 

What is paid him for this direct service is wages and interest. It is distinct from the compensation due him for his co-producer service. 

The firemen serve, even though they only sit in the fire house and wait for a call; and they are paid for rendering that service. (See p 6.)

Physicians, dentists, lawyers and other professional men als serve when they sit and wait; they, too, should be paid for this service.

Indeed, society owes the business and professional men more than it owes the firemen; for the firemen do not furnish the fire house nor the fire-fighting equipment.

Business and professional men do [23]

furnish the offices and shops, stores, factories and necessary equipment; and they lay in stocks of merchandise and maintain pay rolls in order to be ready to serve us. (See p 6.)

Our security and convenience are served by the professional man's equipment and by the merchant's stock of goods, even when these are gathering dust in slack periods.

GOVERNMENTS ARE ALSO OUR CO-PRODUCERS 

(13) (See The Answer, Nos. 33, 46, 47, 49.)

Governments render co-producer service by providing coin and currency to facilitate the exchange of our products in the market place; and by building and maintaining roads and bridges and maintaining the police force and the courts.

The Postoffice is a co-producer of all producers who use the mails. [24]

OUR CO-PRODUCERS AND THE ORIGIN OF RENT 

(14) (See The Answer, 7, 13, 20, 33, 68, 92.)

On the frontier there are few coproducers. Out there men usually serve themselves or go unserved.

Because of the privations of the frontier men will not pay much for the privilege of living out there. 

But this condition is gradually remedied by the development of the country; because, as the need arise, roads and bridges are constructed, the postoffice and school are opened, the police and fire departments are organized and the sewers built. All this is paid for with public funds. 

Physicians, surgeons and dentists open offices; churches and theaters are built; telephone, telegraph and express service are provided; a railroad is built; a company provides gas, light and power; and a daily [25]

paper is established. All this is done with private capital. (See p 23.)

In doing all this, these men are of course serving their own selfish interests; yet, by their readiness to serve, they contribute to the convenience, comfort and security of the residents of the community. 

As more and more of this community service becomes available, the privilege of living in the community is more keenly appreciated. 

People gladly pay for this privilege a price over and above and distinct from the charge for housing. 

What is paid for this is rent. 

Clearly rent is the wages and interest due on the investments of labor and capital by which the community has been developed. 

RENT DEFINED 

Rent, as this term is used in this book, is applied only to the part of a rental payment which is over and above the [26]

charge for housing. It is that part of the payment which is paid for location advantages. 

In cities one finds all about him men who have made heavy investments for the purpose of preparing themselves to serve the public. 

It is because these co-producers are so numerous and are so much better prepared for service than are the co-producers who serve in rural sections and in the small towns that rent is so much higher in the cities. 

SOME COMMON ERRORS REGARDING RENT 

(15) (See The Answer, Nos. 4, 16, 26(10-14), 27, 29, 33, 34, and 37-38.) 

It is mistakenly customary to call the whole of a rental payment rent; but only that part of the payment which is over and above the charge for housing is really rent.

A charge for housing is interest. [27]

Rent is not for land, or anything in or on the land. (See pp 26, 27,)

If it were, it would vary only as changes occur in the land and not, as it does, with changes in the locality. (See pp 26, 27.)

Rent is not interest on money invested in the purchase of the title. 

If it were, the rent would be permanently fixed by the amount invested in the purchase of the title. 

Rent is the public's wages and interest; it is a factor in fixing the prices in the market. (See p 26.)

PEOPLE ALWAYS PAY WHAT GOVERNMENT IS WORTH (16) 

(See The Answer, 26 (10-14), 29, 34, 55-7)

We pay in full for the community service available to us, regardless of what is done with the rent; for rent is always all the traffic will bear. 

Those who live under any govern- [28]

ment will always pay in rent what it is worth to them to live there.

It is a law of economics that government service is self-financing.

In addition, the rent includes the compensation due for a co-producer service rendered at private expense by innumerable individuals, firms, and corporations. (See pp 23, 25.)

The rent is the market value of the community service; hence it is sufficient to cover cost of production, interest, insurance, depreciation and all other cost items. 

The rent is always more than sufficient for the reasonable and necessary needs of the government. 

If we had all the rent in the public treasury, the part of it paid for privately-financed service would be left as an unexpended balance after all the costs of maintaining the government service were met. (p 25) [29]

This money would then be available for new capital investments; it is the money with which we would finance new schools, streets, etc.

While it is impossible to divide the rent among the co-producers directly, it is possible to compensate our co-producers indirectly by collecting the rent instead of taxes and using it to pay public bills. (p 22) 

Each co-producer would then get a just return in community service for his contribution to the life of the community. (See pp 23-26.)

The more useful the co-producer, the more rent would be collected as a result of his usefulness and the more public service there would be to compensate him for his service to the community. (See Section 4.)

In this way every producer would get all he produced-and get exactly the compensation due him. [30]

HENCE, TAXES ARE REALLY STOCK ASSESSMENTS 

(17) (See The Answer, Nos. 26(23-37, 63, 78.) 

But, because of the mistaken belief that rent is paid for land, we allow title-holders to claim the rent. 

They act as middlemen, getting our community service of the government at the tax rate and selling it back to us at the market rate, and profiting by the net rent-that is, by the difference between the rent and the taxes.

We must supply service equal in value to the rent; and (all items included) it costs as much as the rent to produce the service. (See p 28.)

When we sell community service for less than cost (i. e. less than the rent), we must levy a stock assessment (a tax) to balance our books. 

This stock assessment (called a tax) must equal the net rent, plus [31]

the enormous cost of assessing and collecting these taxes.

The more useful we are, the more we are taxed!

IT IS THE TITLE, NOT THE LAND, WHICH HAS VALUE 

(See Nos. 26 (15-22, 28-37), 61, 69, 78.)

A deed to land is a license to collect rent and is valuable in propor- [34]

tion to its yield-that is, in proportion to the net rent. (See p 31.) 

The market value of a deed to land is the capitalized value of the real, or potential, net rent. 

If the rent is $1,000 and taxes are $280, the net rent is $720, equal to 

6% on $12,000.

The title is, then, worth $12,000. 

If the taxes were reduced to $100, the net rent would be $900 and the title would be worth $15,000.

If, on the other hand, we claimed our rent, there would be no net rent for the title-holder, and the title to the land would not sell at any price.

WHY TITLE-HOLDERS SEEK TO CONTROL GOVERNMENTS 

(19) (See Nos. 26 (29, 30, 46-58), 27, 30.)

The title-holder must pay the title tax himself, if it takes all the rent. 

He cannot pass his title tax on by [35]

raising the rent; because the rent is fixed by the rental market and the market rate is all that he can get.

If he could get more, he would do so regardless of the taxes he pays. 

There are two ways title-holders can increase their net rent. 

One is to get the public to make inprovements where the result will be to raise rent where they do the collecting. 

The other is to get taxes shifted from land titles to Industry and Thrift. 

A favorite plan is to get the State or the Federal Government to make the improvements or to assume the expense; for neither California nor the United States taxes land titles. 

The California sales tax raises a million dollars a week for schools, enabling title-holders to keep that much more of the rent they collect. 

The rent paid in Berkeley, California, the site of the University of California, and in other educational [36]

institutions runs into the millions. 

The California gas tax raises another million dollars a week, adding another million dollars a week to the net rent of our title-holders. 

The most of the relief bill is paid with state or federal money; so that title-holders are further enriched by this shift of taxes. (See p 36.)

As shown in Section 17, even local government expense is shifted from the rent account to the wages and interest account. 

Public ownership of utilities and of toll-bridges is another trick by which title-holders are enriched. │All self-liquidating projects are of this type. We pay the bill in our cost of living; they take the rent. 

These tricks succeed because the 

Public doesn't know what rent is and so doesn't understand what is happening, or how to protect itself. [37]

A DOLLAR OF "LAND VALUE" IS A DOLLAR OF PUBLIC DEBT (20) 

(See The Answer 26 (23-27), and 78.)

In Sec. 13, the rent is assumed to be $1,000—that is, that the community service available there is worth $1,000 per year, which means that (all items counted) it costs us $1,000 to maintain that service.

We sell that service for $280, and lose $720 on the transaction. 

That costs us $720 in taxes. 

That is exactly what we would be taxed to pay the interest on an issue of $12,000 in 6% bonds. 

Every dollar of "land value" is, therefore, a dollar of public debt on which we are taxed to pay interest. 

Yet we are the people who boast that we have millions for defense; but will not pay tribute money to any one! What is this but tribute? [38]

TITLE-HOLDERS LAY WAGER THAT WE ARE DUMB 

(21) 

(See The Answer, Dialogue No. 67.) 

A man who "invests in land" lays a wager that we are dumb and will sell him community service for less than it costs us, and then tax ourselves to make up what we lose!

Numerous deals have established the rental value of the city-owned acreage at the port of Stockton at $300 per acre per year; yet speculators hold 1,500 acres of that waterfront for (tax) payments of $3 (or less) per acre per year.

Titles to those acres are not taxed to raise any of the State or Federal money going into the port; and over a million dollars of city tax money had to be paid title-holders to get them to move out of the way of the port construction! 

What is that but paying tribute? [39]

WHY THE MOST OF US GET ONLY A BARE LIVING 

(22) (See The Answer, Dialogues 68, 87.) 

Believe it or not! The greater our advance in the arts, sciences and in culture, and the richer the country, the more cruelly are we exploited! 

The greater our progress is, the higher our rent is. (See Section 14.)

The higher the rent, the more do title-holders profit by our ignorance of economics. (See p 25.)

On the frontier, there is but little community service and people will pay but little for the privilege of living there. (See p 25.)

If the frontier rent were all lost, not much would be lost; but, when he community grows and the rent rises, the loss of even a part of that rent may be a serious loss.

This higher loss is an inevitable result of progress, if the rent is pri- [40]

vately appropriated; for the rent is always all the traffic will bear that is, it absorbs the financial benefits of progress, leaving us (as before) just enough to keep us "on the job."

This is why there is so much poverty and misery in our cities.

HOW THE FARMERS ARE CRUELLY EXPLOITED 

(23) 

(See Dialogue No. 71 in The Answer.) 

The farmers have been cruelly deceived by being led to think that they belong to the "landed class" and profit by policies favorable to title-holders; the truth is that land (in the sense of "land value") has been "moving to the cities."

Even on the face of the assessors' record, farmers have but little "land value"; the shadow of an automobile at noon will cover more "land value" in some cities than is in the [41]

farms in some of our rich counties. 

More than half the "land value" of California is in Los Angeles and 

San Francisco. Included in the remainder of the State's “land value” is the "land value" of more than two hundred other California cities. 

But, even though the assessors' figures show that more than half of the "land value" of the state is in these two cities, these figures still misrepresent the farmers' interest in this matter.

Much of the farmers' so-called "land value" consists of labor values mistakenly assessed as "land value." 

Fencing, drainage, levelling and ditching values are not separately assessed, but are included in the socalled value of his land and are assessed as "land value."

In some cases, the farmer's "land value" is less than 20% of the as- [42]

sessment value put upon his farm. 

The farmers have the most acres; but it is "land value" not acreage, that counts in this matter and the "land value" is in the cities.

Rent in rural sections is low; the holders of title to farm land collect very little rent. (See p 25.)

The rent is the Public's wages and interest; it it is charged into all prices and expense bills. Because the rent is so high in the cities, this is a big item there. (See p 29.) 

But, like other co-producers, the farmers (as shown in Sec. 11) are not compensated for the part they play in the production of wealth.

Farmers have to sell their products and buy their supplies in the cities, where rent is high; and, in doing so, they pay the high city rent which is charged into the bills for these transactions. (See p 29.) [43]

The farmer is the victim of an unfavorable balance of rent. Although he collects little or no rent, he pays high rent both in buying and selling.

IS THIS A CONSPIRACY OR MERELY A COINCIDENCE 

(24) (See The Answer, Dialogues 30, 31, 32.)

Why is it that those who are so fond of discussing wages and interest do not see that wages and interest are necessarily low where "land values" are high? (See p 38.) 

Wages and interest are freely discussed, and we sometimes hear men discuss the amount of rent they pay; but discussion of the nature and significance of rent is suppressed. 

Have you heard this matter discussed in any school or college? 

Or over the air? Why not?

Or seen it discussed in any editorial column of the regular press? [44]

Or have you heard the nature and function of rent discussed at any meeting of a chamber of commerce? 

Have you ever heard this subject discussed, or heard of its being discussed by Congress, or by any legislature? Or by any educational association, or by any church group? 

Have you ever known a civic organization to discuss this matter? 

Or a columnist, or a clergyman?

Isn't it time for men who are interested in knowing the cause and cure of crooked politics and of war to break this conspiracy of silence!

MOST TITLE-HOLDERS ARE VICTIMS OF THE SYSTEM 

(25) (See The Answer Dialogues 34 and 52.) 

Because this discussion has been suppressed, few realize that most of our title-holders lose more in taxes than they gain in rent; still fewer [45]

realize that the private appropriation of rent is (morally) an embezzlement of public funds. 

The only ones who profit as collectors of rent are those who have more in "land value" than in improvements and personal property.

When the problem is understood, Public Opinion will back the movement to recover our rent and abolish all taxes, direct and indirect. 

Until then, we are helpless.

Our public schools cannot save us; for, being financed with public money, they cannot safely get too far ahead of public opinion. 

The newspapers cannot lead.

Asking a newspaper to take a position in advance of public opinion is like asking an army to maintain itself by barter with the enemy. 

The churches cannot save us; for their financial support, like that of [46]

the newspapers, is dependent upon public opinion. They cannot lead.

The radio is even less free than the press and the pulpit; for it is subject to drastic licensing rules, which are frightning even when not actually hurtful.

A man's judgment is no more dependable than the information upon which it is based; those who control publicity control our elections. 

Title-holders are tempted to control, if possible, conditions affecting rent where they do the collecting; and, in a democracy the best way to maintain this control is to control public opinion; and the surest way to do that is to let the people know only as much as the title-holders believe it to be good for them to have the people know. (See p 44.)

Under such conditions, Ignorance and Prejudice rule unchallenged. [47]

WHY WE HAVE WARS AND WHO PROFITS BY THEM 

(26) (See The Answer, 26(46-58), 27, 80, 98)

Because we have in ignorance allowed title-holders to claim the rent as their own, the business and politics of the world have become a brutal scramble. 

Unsuspected and even unintentional as it is in most cases, this meddling in public affairs by titleholders is world wide. 

The quarrel between the countyseat and the rest of the county is born of the rivalry of title-holders over rent; and the sectionalism so costly in cities is also born of this competition for rent. (See p 28.)

This rivalry over rent is what has hindered the development of our inland waterways; for the cities dependant upon rail transportation are more numerous and have more [48]

political influence than the "waterfront cities.”

Ignorance of economics and the misappropriation of rent are worldwide. 

Regardless of the form of government, or of religion, or the kind of money, or of the people's culture, all peoples of earth suffer the same ills.

Everywhere the producer is exploited by the collectors of rent. 

And when business slows up at home each country seeks to save its rent by building up a foreign trade. 

Naturally, they seek to protect themselves by controlling the conditions which affect that trade. 

Under these conditions it is inevitable that a nation's meddling abroad should bring it into conflict with other international meddlers. 

Not knowing that these are problems in natural law, and mistakenly [49]

believing them to be problems in human conduct, men blame their difficulties upon the misconduct of other men and demand that these suspects be restrained, or punished. 

This false belief breeds suspicion, resentment and hostility; and each side prepares to defend itself. 

Thus is the stage set for war. 

No matter how the matter may be camouflaged, war is always more or less directly traceable to meddling by rent collectors who seek to control conditions which affect rent where they do the collecting. 

The wars between Rome and Carthage were fought over the question whether the business of the Mediterranean World should center in 

Carthage where the Carthiginians collected rent, or in Rome where the rent was collected by Romans. 

The Civil War between the North 

[50]

and the South in this country was fought over the question whether the cotton of the South should be worked up in mills in New England towns where New Englanders collect the rent. (See p 36.)

The North had demanded a high tariff to develop American industry. 

So far as the business in cotton textiles of that day is concerned, this meant that New England was given a monopoly of the cotton textile business of this country. 

It was this interference with the freedom of trade that caused the 

South to secede. (See p 49.)

Italy's conquest of Ethiopia was not to get room for Italian colonies, but to make sure the products of Ethiopia would be worked up where the rent is collected by Italians. 

The World War (I and II) was brought on by the struggle over the [51]

question whether the business of

Europe should center in England where the English collect rent, or in Germany where Germans collect it. 

The war in the Pacific is over the question whether the commerce of the Pacific shall center in Japan, where the Japanese collect rent, or on our West Coast where we collect rent.

TITLE-DEEDS ARE EXEMPT FROM ALL WAR TAXES 

(27) (See The Answer, 26(39, 40), 27, 41, 45.)

Although war is always due to the meddling of rent-collectors, the 

United States Government exempts land titles from the levies by which it raises the billions needed for war. 

Yet, even in the midst of the war, we are hindered by the rivalries of men who want the war funds spent where they collect rent. [52]

Then, when we win the war and this is a better place to live, titleholders will raise rents because of advantages won by the war. 

The rent should be higher then! 

It will be worth more to live here when the objectives for which we are fighting have been won.

But why should title-holders be allowed to keep as their own rent which is paid them because of advantages won at the cost of war?

THERE IS BUT ONE WAY TO SAVE CIVILIZATION 

(28) (The Answer, 2, 12, 26(1-4), 27, 91, 93.)

There is but one way for us to save ourselves! That is to accept economics as a science as exact as any science. (See pp 2, 10 11.) 

Men do not argue with Nature!

Once economics is accepted as a science as exact as physics, men will [53]

accept without debate the truths set forth in these pages.

Then no conspiracy of silence will be able to halt the march of economic truth; and the most stubborn prejudices will give way before it. 

When we study economics as we study physics and chemistry, we will learn to harness and to use instinct and human passion as we do fire and steam and electricity. 

Then, instead of wasting time and energy trying to make men over, we shall use the forces of human nature as our social motive power as we use natural forces in the shop.

NATURE REFUSES TO TRUST SUCH MATTERS TO MEN 

(30) (See The Answer, Dialogues 13, 96.)

Nature refused to trust such a vital matter as our social security to either our intelligence or our virtue. [54]

She entrusts men with management of those things only which are of importance to them as individuals or as small groups. 

Within the limits of these individual interests, men are endowed with freedom of the will. (See p 10)

But outside those limits, in the matters which are vital to all men, man has no choice; there he must comply with Nature's conditions.

THIS DEADLY DRIFT CAN BE COMPLETELY REVERSED 

(29) 

See The Answer, Dialogues 20, 35.)

We have our choice of two methods of financing the government: 

One is to require people to contribute according to their ability. This is taxation, the method now in use. 

The other is to collect the rent instead of taxes. By this method, the government would get what each of us pays for the service society renders him. 

Taxation is very inefficient as a [55]

method of raising public revenue. 

Taxation penalizes industry and thrift by transferring wealth from the pockets of producers to those of non-producers; it penalizes truthfulness and makes cheats and liars of men who are otherwise honest.

However, just as the gear-shift in an automobile reverses the direction of the automobile without reversing the engine; so can we by a shift in our revenuesystem reverse our present course without reforming men. 

By collecting the rent instead of taxes, we would insulate men's selfishness-that is, we would prevent it from "short-circuiting"; and we would confine it to those channels in which it would serve society. (See p 20.)

The impulse of the heart would be unchanged; but the machinery of society would be reversed. [56]

Selfishness for gain would then be selfishness for wages and interestneither of which can be got without making a corresponding investment of labor and capital. (See p 18.)

As men could get wages and interest only by being useful, they would become selfishly eager to be both useful and agreeable.

When men are selfishly eager for the cooperation of others who are just as selfishly seeking their cooperation, harmony and neighborliness become a matter of self-defense and as natural as breathing. 

Nature does not depend upon our culture! She has ways of her own of keeping order in the family! 

Believe it or not, our talk about backward people not being qualified to govern themselves convicts us of ignorance, for Nature has based our social stability not upon our intelli- [57]

gence, nor upon our virtue. Instead, she has provided for it by means of absolutely dependable instinct and and natural law.

This is Nature's method of providing for the preservation of the human species. In our case, as in all others, she grants the individual freedom in those matters only which freedom or action in those matters only which concern individuals; she controls matters vital to the species.

NATURE'S PLAN FOR RACES, AND OUR INTERFERENCE (28) 

(See The Answer, Dialogue 26 (59-61.)

Not till the United States became possessed of Oregon and Washington and so had ports on the Pacific, did our government become interested in the trade of the Orient. 

It was then that a United States admiral took vigorous steps to open [58]

Japan to the trade of the world.

This same influence is shown in the fact that it was the demand of the Eastern States for a share in the Oriental trade that led to the building of the Panama Canal.

In California, the immigration of 

Mexicans, Chinese, Japanese and of

Filipinos was encouraged by operators of large tracts who wanted to obtain this cheap foreign field labor. 

Each race instinctively prefers its own companionship. Miscegenation is instinctively offensive to all races. 

Nature put the Negroes in Africa; they were brought to this country for buyers interested in the rent of 

Southern plantations. 

Kipling's statement that East is

East and West is West and never the twain shall meet seems to be justified by the fact that Nature put the Oriental and the Occidental on [59]

opposite sides of the globe-as far apart as it was possible to put them. 

But race prejudice is not a barrier to trade; for the reason that, strong as their race prejudice may be, men's desire to live is stronger. 

And, with those who make their living from trade, the desire to live is transformed into a passion for maintaining pleasant relations with those with whom one deals.

In commerce, men get only wages and interest, neither of which can be got, except by making one's self useful and agreeable. (See pp 9, 10)

It is only when the rent is privately claimed and men clash in attempts to control conditions affecting their rent collections, that friction arises between the races. 

Collecting rent instead of taxes would solve our Oriental problem. [60]

· THE REAL CLASS WAR vs. THE FALSE MARXIST ISSUE 

(30) (See The Answer, Dialogues 28, 29.)

Anything which helps of hurts either Labor or Capital, helps or hurts them both.

They are fellow victims of the misappropriation of our rent.

The real class war is between Labor and Capital on the producers' side and the appropriators of rent on the exploiters' side.

The true relations of Labor, Capital and the Public are shown by the illustration of John Labor and Richard Capital, partners in the business of Labor & Capital Inc., in which the corporation represents the Public. (See The Answer, Dialogue 29.)

John puts in the labor and gets the wages, Richard furnishes the capital and gets the interest. The rent belongs to the corporation. [61]

THE WAR WITH ORGANIZED VICE A CLASS WAR, ALSO 

(33) Organized vice has its origin in the temptation which besets men when they are permitted to claim as their own any part of the rent they collect.

Here is another class war. This, too, is a war between producers and exploiters.

To get more net rent, the holders of titles try to control conditions affecting rent where they do the collecting. To do this, they need help. 

Operators of harlotries are glad to pay extremely high rents, if assured protection from police interference.

And title-holders (some of them) are glad to use their political influence with the city government in return for these fancy rentals. 

Let the reader note that forgers [62]

and and pick-pockets and the others who maintain no headquarters and carry no payrolls have no friends; they practice these crimes at their peril. 

If the reader will contrast the promptness with which the police run them down and the fate they suffer with that of law-breakers who carry large payrolls and pay high rents, he will see that crooked politics is born of the lure of rent. 

In cities where vice is well organized, there is little if any graft; for i grafters maintain no headquarters and carry no payrolls.

Graft does not stimulate rents! 

Indeed, the money that leaks out from a treasury in graft is lost to the collectors of rent. Title-holders who play the game of crooked politics want all the "pickings" themselves; they demand that the police [63]

and public officials be content with their jobs. 

Westbrook Pegler said of Kansas

City that one of its policemen could as easily shake down the Santa Fe 

Railroad Company as a Kansas City bawdy house; and that were a policeman to try it, he would be severely disciplined by the Vice ring. 

Were these title-holders denied their rent, they would lose interest in politics and the operators of vice would soon be voted out of business.

THE NEW SCIENCE IS NOW MAKING RAPID PROGRESS 

(35) (See The Answer, pp. xi-xvi and No. 97.)

Constructive progress in the Science of Economics began in 1776, with the statement by Adam Smith of the law of rent. 

In 1879, Henry George stated the law of wages and the law of interest. 

That completed what may be called the framework of the Science of Economics. 

Unfortunately, neither Adam Smith nor 

Henry George correctly comprehended the nature and significance of rent. 

For years, beginning almost with first issue in 1921, The Forum of Stockton, California, had been teaching that rent is for service and is the Public's wages and interest; but it had, very inconsistenly, continued to speak of it as paid for land. 

Not till 1932 was the nature and significance of rent correctly set forth. 

In that year, W. R. B. Willcox, head of the Department of Architecture of the University of Oregon, set this matter forth correctly in a report made to the national convention of the American Institute of

Architects by a committee of which he was chairman.

In 1934, Otto Cullman of Chicago published "$20,000,000 Every Day," in which he set forth correctly the basic truth that [70]

rent is for service; however he continued even in the text of this book to refer to rent by the contradictory and confusing term, ground rent.

Land is not a factor in economics. 

The use of this unscientific term diverts attention from the fact that rent is for service; and turns it, instead, to land, to "land investments" and to land owners.

In 1935, The Road to Better Business and Plentiful Employment by Emil O. Jorgensen of Chicago appeared. While Jorgensen employed the contradictory and confusing terminology of Single Taxers, his book settled affirmatively the question whether rent, like wages and interest, is a factor in determining price. 

1938, the Institute Report of 1932 by W. R. B. Willcox was elaborated by him into a book of 140 pages under the title, Taxation Turmoil re-published two years later as The Curse of Modern Taxation.

240 pages; and it is designed to serve as a complete textbook in Economics. 

In 1942, Otto Cullman published Behold the Future. This book supplements and strengthens "$20,000,000 Every Day." [71]

From this later book, Cullman has omitted the troublesome term, ground rent. 

This year, 1942, the little book, The Secret (now in your hands), was added to this growing list of dependable textbooks in Economics.
