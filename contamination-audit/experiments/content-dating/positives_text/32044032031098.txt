SUMMARY 

During the period May 9 to June 26, 1973, teams from the Office of Revenue Sharing made compliance visits to 103 states, cities and counties in the United States. Those jurisdictions selected for visits had received 52% of all revenue sharing funds disbursed through the third payment (April 9, 1973). The visits were intended to ascertain how the units of government were complying with the provisions of the State and Local Fiscal Assistance Act; to make a preliminary survey of financial operations; to begin the development of a compliance system with the assistance of state and local officials; and to discuss revenue sharing generally with the state and local officers having responsibility for administering the program. 

The visits yielded much detailed data on personnel administering the program, state and local budgets, planned and actual use of funds to date, and the elements of a prospective compliance system already in place. The visits also identified certain compliance problems which are either in process of resolution or which will be dealt with in the immediate future by ORS. This report is confined to the body of data assembled during the course of these trips.

Responsibility and Information Flow 

In both state and local government, governmental finance officers are generally assigned responsibility for administering revenue sharing and for internally disseminating information about the program. In planning their compliance programs, most state governments rely solely or primarily on information provided by the Office of Revenue Sharing with only secondary reliance on indirect sources of information. On the other hand, the majority of the city and county governments visited place much greater reliance on intermediate sources of information such as publications by Public Interest Groups (e.g. 

National League of Cities). Citizen participation in the allocation process, while limited at the state level, is widespread in cities and counties visited. 

Use of Revenue Sharing 

Legal constraints of the State and Local Fiscal Assistance Act encourage local governments to allocate revenue sharing funds to a restricted number of program areas so that compliance problems will tend to be minimal.

-vi- Some states allocated all their general revenue sharing for aid to local schools. Many state governments are hesitant to use the funds for recurring expenditures out of concern that general revenue sharing may not be continued. Another group of states, responding to actual or anticipated cutbacks in specific federal categorical aid, have allocated funds to operating and maintenance expenses of the ongoing programs affected. 

Most local governments visited (i.e. large cities and large urban counties) are using the funds in part to forestall tax increases. Equal numbers of jurisdictions allocated funds entirely to capital expenditures, entirely to operating and maintenance expenses, or allocated some funds to both. Revenue sharing generally enabled local governments to prevent cuts in services. Impact A thorough analysis of the impact of revenue sharing was not attempted. However, officials of jurisdictions surveyed were queried regarding the major effects of the program. While it is difficult to determine precisely the net impact of a new revenue source, the principal

-vii- effect of revenue sharing was reported as tax relief. 

A majority of states provided some form of tax relief or debt service with the funds. Of the remaining states, some reported that the program would have no substantial impact, and others were unable to determine the impact.

Use of the funds for tax relief at the local level was nearly as widespread as at the state level. Generally revenue sharing enabled local jurisdictions to maintain their level of services in their F/Y '74 budgets, without tax increases.

Civil Rights 

Most state and local governments have staff officers responsible for protection of government employees' civil rights. Most governments use merit system procedures for hiring, and approximately forty percent of state and local jurisdictions reported that their entrance tests had either been validated for nondiscrimination or were undergoing validation studies.

-viii- It is clear, however, that a number of officials responsible for the revenue sharing program are not fully aware of civil rights enforcement organizations able to assist in ensuring nondiscriminatory use of revenue sharing funds. Specific confirmation was received, including copies of standard legal language, that nondiscrimination clauses will be included in all contracts funded through revenue sharing. 

Beginning of Audit System Design 

Since ORS proposes to construct a compliance system relying as much as possible on in-place controls, possibly modified to meet the requirements of revenue sharing, governments visited were asked to identify audit systems presently in operation. All states and all local governments visited, with the exception of one, reported requirements for independent audits. Forty-six of the forty-eight states require audits to be made of some, or all of their local governments. Quality and type of audits vary considerably, and more information, technical assistance and perhaps negotiation will be required before the Secretary of the Treasury can formally include many of these audits

-ix- in the ORS compliance system.

Nevertheless, it seems clear that a substantial base exists within state and local government for constructing an effective financial compliance system.

Financial Management 

All states visited had invested their revenue sharing funds either in whole or in part. Of the twenty-six states which had appropriated funds, ten had made appropriations based on anticipated revenue sharing receipts. Five states had expended nine percent of total receipts. 

At the local level, one jurisdiction had spent its funds as soon as it had received them, while the other governments had invested them. Forty-five jurisdictions had appropriated funds, including nine which appropriated funds based on anticipated receipts. Forty jurisdictions had expended 24% of the total receipts received. 

Compliance Issues 

Various compliance issues were identified during the course of these visits, and ORS is taking appropriate action to deal with these. It is anticipated that the majority can be resolved by letter, although some return visits may be required. 521-897 - 73 - 2

Because states and local units of government operate under different constraints and influences in their allocation of revenue sharing receipts, their fund utilization will be discussed separately.

At the time of the ORS visits, only twenty-six states had appropriated any revenue sharing funds. Thus, data on planned use and impact of revenue sharing funds was taken,

State government allocations and executive budget recommendations fall into three categories: allocation of funds largely to capital outlays and nonrecurring expenditures, allocation largely as aid to local schools, and allocation principally to operations and maintenance expenditures. Each of these patterns of budgeting is partially a response either to legal constraints upon allocation of revenue sharing funds, or to changes in the level of total federal funding. The Act prohibits use of revenue sharing funds for matching to obtain federal funds. Moreover, it requires that expenditure of the funds leave a clear "audit trail." Such legal requirements encourage allocation of revenue sharing funds by jurisdictions to a limited number of expenditure categories.

2. Impact on Local Governments 

Caution is necessary in assessing the impact of revenue sharing on local jurisdictions. instances it is certain that the net impact of revenue sharing was felt in areas other than those to which funds were allocated. For example, one jurisdiction allocated its entire funds to public safety, but reported that the real impact was an increase in social services funding. 

This shifting of funds appears to be fairly widespread on the local level, but is difficult to examine without study of complete past and prospective local budgets. 

In several

Application of revenue sharing funds to tax relief or debt service was nearly as widespread on the local level as it was on the state level. of the fifty-four local jurisdictions surveyed, five reported that taxes would be reduced, and seventeen stated that tax increases had been deferred as a result of the program. Four jurisdictions used the monies for debt service, or to prevent increases in the public indebtedness. 

Of the twenty-eight jurisdictions not reporting change in anticipated tax and debt levels, most cited impact on their level of services. Seven jurisdictions reported that revenue sharing had enabled them to avoid forced cutbacks in services, while nine stated that the funds allowed increases in services. Four local governments reported that revenue sharing would have no impact, due to the magnitude of their budgets, and four jurisdictions were not yet able to determine the impact. Two local units cited acceleration of their capital outlay programs as the principal impact, and another noted increased citizen involvement in government. One jurisdiction reported the progress in federal/local relations to be the chief impact of revenue sharing. In general,

revenue sharing enabled most jurisdictions to continue their existing level of services without tax increases in their fiscal year 1974 budgets, despite anticipated loss of federal categorical aid.

D. Civil Rights 

In visiting state and local officials, ORS staff members sought to assess those officials' understanding of the jurisdictions' capability of enforcing the civil rights requirements of the general revenue sharing law. 

Nine states reported that no agency existed which was responsible for civil rights enforcement throughout the state. On the other hand, local governments in only six states reported the absence of an agency responsible for civil rights enforcement throughout the state. Obviously there is some misunderstanding among some officials as to what civil rights enforcement machinery now exists. 

Forty-one states have an office within the government responsible for the enforcement of civil rights of state employees. Seventy-one percent of

the local governments visited report the existence of such an office. In a few cases, the local government was unionized and felt that any grievances involving discrimination would be handled through the grievance procedure.

Five states reported that a nondiscrimination clause was not normally required in contracts with their subcontractors, although almost all states, and all local governments stated that nondiscrimination clauses were included in contracts involving federal money and would be included in contracts involving revenue sharing funds. 

Reliance upon the 1970 census to determine minority population was found to be the rule rather than the exception. 

Approximately two-thirds of the states maintain an analysis of their employees by minority class and grade. Forty-three percent of the local governments have such a grouping. It should be noted, however, that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission

issued final regulations requiring that minority statistics be maintained by all state and local governments having 15 or more employees after our survey was begun. Most jurisdictions indicated a willingness to comply with these regulations although some local jurisdictions felt that such requirements were in violation of state law. 

The overwhelming majority of state and local governments use civil service or merit system procedures in their hiring for all but noncompetitive positions. Seven states, however, indicated that patronage was the principal recruitment method used to select employees for non-federally funded programs. 

Nineteen of the states and twenty-two of the local jurisdictions visited (41%) reported that entrance tests had been validated or that validation studies were underway. Many jurisdictions were using tests patterned after those used by the United States Civil Service Commission. Some tests have been validated by HEW. In a few jurisdictions, private consultants or psychometricians had been employed to determine whether or not tests reflect bias.

E. Beginning of Audit System Design Title I of the State and Local Fiscal Assistance 

Act provides that, in carrying out his responsibility for auditing revenue sharing funds, the Secretary of the Treasury is authorized to accept an audit by a state of a state government or unit of local government. This authority is amplified by the Regulations, which provide that it is the intention of the Secretary to rely to the maximum extent possible on audits of recipient governments by state and local government auditors and independent public accountants.

Accordingly, one objective of the review was to determine the extent to which the audits being made of the recipients, with an extension of procedures principally with regard to compliance, would satisfy the audit requirement of ORS. 

ORS teams found that all 48 states visited required audits of state funds. Of those, only two were performed by independent public accountants (IPA's) while the remainder are the responsibility of the designated state auditor. Of the 48 states, 46 required audits of local

units of government, although in some cases, only certain designated units such as counties were required by law to be audited. Of the 46 states whose laws require units of local government to be audited, 20 required audits by either the state audit agency or 

IPA's; 11 by the state audit agency; 14 by IPA's only and one by the local units' audit agency.

Of the 54 units of local government that were visited, only one did not require post audit. This anomaly was Allegheny County, Pennsylvania, which has continuous pre-audit for approval of payment. The remainder were audited as follows: 12 by the state audit agency; 34 by IPA's; and 7 by the local unit's own audit agency.

From the foregoing, it is obvious that practically all recipients visited have some existing audit. It seems reasonable to conclude that this pattern exists in all the larger units of government. 

The next step, therefore, is to determine in how many instances the recipient and/or auditors will extend their audit

processes to the extent necessary to meet the revenue sharing audit requirements. 

Additionally, a procedure must be developed to review the professional practice of the auditors on a sample basis to provide the maximum assurance practicable that the quality of the work done meets acceptable standards.

As an aid to the auditors who will be making audits of revenue sharing recipients, as well as for ORS's use, there is being developed an audit guide which will contain the auditing standards and minimum audit procedures for use in auditing revenue sharing funds.

2. Local Governments 

Of the fifty-four counties and cities visited, twenty-three have fiscal years ending June 30; twenty are on a calendar year basis, and the balance vary. 

Of the $1,222,840,911 received by the local governments, $1,058,445,514 was invested, primarily in certificates of deposit and U. S. Treasury Notes. 

The average estimated rate of return on investments was 6.1% (the range was from 3.7% to 8%). The difference between the amount received and the amount invested is accounted for by expenditures. All but one jurisdiction invested the funds; that jurisdiction spent its funds as soon as it had received them. 

At the local level, $698,926,120 or 57% of the total available for appropriation, had been appropriated.

Only nine entities had not appropriated any funds as of the date of our visits. Of the forty-five jurisdictions that had appropriated funds, nine appropriated funds based on anticipated receipts. 

The amount reported as expended totalled $296,968,424, or approximately 24% of the amount received.

This per- centage is higher than that reported for the states (9%), due to the legislative and budgetary procedures for fund allocation and in expenditure. Some expenditures have been made by forty of the fifty-four local governments compared to five of the forty-nine states visited.

III. SUGGESTIONS BY JURISDICTIONS VISITED, AND ITEMS OF SPECIAL CONCERN 

1. 

ORS teams made clear that the purpose of the compliance visits was threefold: to verify compliance with the provisions of the Act, to obtain information from the units of government visited, and to secure their input to the revenue sharing program, especially suggestions for assuring compliance. Much of the available time during the visits was used to answer questions about the law and the regulations, and to clear up problem areas. However, a number of jurisdictions offered additional comments and suggestions, among which the following were typical: 

Jurisdictions expressed some concern at the number of reports being required not only by ORS, but by GAO and what appear to be numerous private groups. 

This concern related not only to the workload generated by these reports, but to the fact that requests for such reports were being generated both by ORS and others on what appeared to be a random basis. Units of government were particularly concerned that there was

no consolidated list of ORS reports due and suggested that ORS list in one single document for easy reference the type and dates of reports required.* 

2. Cities and counties were concerned that possible ORS over-reliance on state government for ensuring compliance by sub-state units might alter the sensitive balance between state and local government. This same type of concern was expressed by state governments where the tradition of local autonomy is strong.

Essentially, these various units of government were saying that revenue sharing should be neutral in its effect on state/local relations. 3.

Units of government frequently requested that more basic data be furnished to them. There was a general desire to be aware of the specific numbers being used to calculate the respective jurisdiction's allocation of revenue sharing funds. 

4. The request was made that ORS not depend on other federal agencies to assist in enforcing compliance provisions of revenue sharing. The thought here was that ORS had performed in quite a satisfactory manner *The two reports required by ORS annually of local governments are the Planned Use Report and the Actual Use Report, both required by law. ORS also requires these two reports annually from state governments and will require an annual report on maintenance of transfer payments to local governments.

to date and its specific compliance provisions were sufficiently different from ongoing federal categorical programs that auditors from other agencies could not properly assess compliance with the State and Local 

Fiscal Assistance Act. 

5. A number of the large cities and counties requested assurance that ORS would not force smaller units of jurisdictions to use private auditing firms. 

While the jurisdictions making this recommendation were themselves audited by private firms, they felt that the cost of private auditing would be prohibitive for smaller jurisdictions. The majority of jurisdictions were reasonably content and informed but displayed some uneasiness about general revenue sharing simply because it is so new. There was a general expression of intent to understand and comply with the program's few requirements, as shown in the almost universal expression of concern about proper handling of the reports required by ORS. 

6. Some jurisdictions wanted to know when they could expect return of the funds that had been withheld from the first and second entitlement period. (The

Office of Revenue Sharing set aside one percent of first period funds and five percent of second period funds to use as a reserve from which adjustments in entitlement amounts could be made subsequently. This was thought to be necessary, for in the early stages of the program a great deal of the data used in calculating entitlement amounts had to be estimated. Since that time, new and verified figures have been introduced and the withheld funds are being returned to eligible units of government as fourth entitlement period funds are paid.) 

7. Another concern was related to the longevity of the program. Many jurisdictions' officials felt that they should treat general revenue sharing entitlements as "windfall" money because the program may terminate at the end of its current five-year authorization. 

Officials seem reluctant to use revenue sharing money to fund operating programs which must be financed after 1977. 

8. The overwhelming majority of those who commented were concerned about the total amounts of federal aid for all purposes that their jurisdictions would be receiving in fiscal year 1974 and in the years

to come.

It seems clear from these comments that general revenue sharing will not, in the final analysis, be judged solely on its own merits. Despite its success in meeting local needs and encouraging a renewed interest in the democratic process on the part of citizens at large, if the changes now occurring in federal aid policies cause most jurisdictions to suffer a net loss, their enthusiasm for general revenue sharing likely will diminish. 

9. Finally, the ORS teams were most pleased to find a decidely positive attitude toward the Office of Revenue Sharing and the manner in which the program has been administered. Reception was most cordial, and ORS was generally commended for responsiveness, and for effectively communicating the operational details of a relatively complicated program to the units of government.
