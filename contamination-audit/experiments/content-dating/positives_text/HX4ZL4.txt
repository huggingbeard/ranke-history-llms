INTRODUCTION 

Emanuel B. Hoff was my most intimate friend-outside my own family. We loved each other, trusted each other, and worked together for many years. 

I first met Brother Hoff in 1893 or 1894 at McPherson 

College, where I was a student and where he and his little motherless boy were resident with the boy's grandparents. He was then a missionary and evangelist. On occasions when he preached I enjoyed his sermons greatly because of their profound prophetic insight and spiritual understanding. That more than anything else bound me to him. Others criticized his delivery; I forgot about that and was moved by the spiritual power and authority with which he spoke. 

In 1899 I spent the summer quarter as a guest in his home in Chicago, where he and his wife and I were students at the university. Those were the days when I was preparing for Bethany and I studied him as a possible partner.

Next we planned a tour of Bible lands together, in 1901 and 1902, after he had been at Manchester College two years as Bible teacher. It was on this journey in 

Palestine, riding our horses, that he definitely agreed to stake his life with mine on the venture of building a seminary. Then later, on the Mount of Olives overlooking Bethany, kneeling under an olive tree we named the unborn child Bethany Bible School. 

From then on every major decision was taken together. 

We consulted endlessly and prayed over our problems together times without number. I made perhaps hundreds of trips to his home to counsel him. I seldom left

without his suggesting prayer over our problems before 

I would go.

Often we disagreed; then we waited and searched and prayed for further light on our problem. Once there was a serious rift through complete misunderstanding of the facts in the case. But even that cleared away in time by the grace and forbearance of God. 

It was a great personal loss when, in 1928, he died. I have been lonesome ever since.

It was a great experience to have known so great and so true and so loving and so utterly unselfish a man of God. He always reminded me of a typical prophet. As a prophet he waited upon God; as a prophet he got his message from God; as a prophet he spoke God's message; and as a prophet, too, he was often misunderstood by some but greatly loved, revered, and followed by many. 

A. C. Wieand

PREFACE 

My father was a man of the Book if there ever was one. His life passion was to know what the Bible means, follow it, and teach it. He lived in the day when the educational awakening in his church was turning toward the Bible, and he had a determinative influence in the process.

Fifteen years have passed since his death. From this distance the significance of his contribution stands out the more clearly. At the present time and in the days that appear to be just ahead we face a great Bible awakening. The principles of Bible study which he used and the truths which he found should be useful to many if a faithful and clear interpretation of them can be given. 

Fortunately there are still with us his partner in his lifework, the companion of his home, two sisters, a roommate from Mount Morris College days, fellow learners from university days, and many Bethany students and teachers with whom he shared his soul. These have been generous with their help in this project. Let no one who is unable to identify his contribution in the pages that follow feel that his effort was in vain. Perhaps he has contributed inspiration at vital points. 

It is inevitable that some idolization should enter into the picture. Time and affection have a beneficent way of letting faults fade and virtues glow. But an earnest effort is here made to present a faithful account of father's life and work. For the author the writing has been a labor of love and gratitude.

Ernest G. Hoff

CHAPTER I OF NATIVE HARDWOOD 

Our pioneering fathers selected the good land by the stand of native hardwood which it supported-oaks, walnut, and beech. Only now when it is late are we coming to see the real treasure in these sturdy trees. For the most part, they fell before the march of progress, giving way to corn, wheat, and cities. But in the process, something of the quality of the hardwood got into the men and women who cleared the forests. They became strong in fiber and character. Their strength is the heritage of later generations. 

Emanuel B. Hoff, the subject of this sketch, came of such stock. His forefathers had pioneered in Pennsylvania. His paternal grandparents, Peter and Sarah

Blocher Hoff, found western Pennsylvania already too much occupied to suit their purpose for the building of a home. Though their first child was born in Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania, their second first saw the light in a cabin in the woods in Wayne County, Ohio. 

Peter's brothers, George, Henry, Jacob and Philip, came to Ohio also and hewed them out homes adjoining. This became a Hoff community, centering four miles north of the village of Smithville, Ohio.

Peter Hoff was both a farmer and a blacksmith. The writer has in his possession three account books containing records of Peter's business transactions from 1817 to 1860. This is a first-rate historical source for the way of life of those days. In it appear the industry, simplicity, frugality, thrift, and struggle of that generation and time. The eighth of the nine children in Peter and

Sarah's family was John B., father of Emanuel. He was born March 27, 1833.

Emanuel's maternal grandparents were Emanuel and Sarah Berkley Buechly. Emanuel Buechly was a preacher and a doctor, living until middle life in Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania, and then moving to the vicinity of Congress, Ohio. The fifth child of Emanuel and Sarah, born June 1, 1836, was named Mary. She lived until she was fourteen within sight of tree-covered Laurel Hill which remained a standard of beauty to her even in the later years of her life when she resided within sight of the rugged western mountains. 

John B. Hoff grew up in the Hoff community. His kinsfolk were mostly Lutherans, but he and his father's family were Brethren, worshiping in the Beech Grove house of the Chippewa congregation. What little formal education he got was in a school where it was necessary to use only first names; last names were with few exceptions Hoff.

It was an event when visitors came in those days. The visit of one family in the Hoff home was of special interest to young John. The Emanuel Buechly family from 

Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania, was moving to Congress, Ohio, and stopped with the Hoffs en route. 

Their fourteen-year-old daughter attracted John. Several years later when both had had time to mature, he was riding horseback across the Killbuck Valley and hills to see Mary Buechly. They married on Christmas

Day, 1855-five years after that first meeting-and settled on the western edge of the Hoff community about eight miles north of what became the city of Wooster, Ohio. Their first son did not live; their second, Ephraim 

J., was born January 29, 1859; their third, Emanuel B.,

13 came December 21, 1860. This third son was named 

Emanuel Buechly for his mother's father. In this there must have been something either prophetic or formative, for Grandfather Emanuel was a minister and Grandson Emanuel early received the call to the ministry. The mother who stood between might well have been a minister had she been of the preaching sex. She was godly, spiritual and had a forceful personality. The sound of the mother's voice praying in her private chamber became familiar to the growing children. Like Hannah of old she gave her son to the Lord but never ceased to bring him the protecting robe of spiritual interest and motherly concern.

Pioneer blood still ran in the veins of John and Mary 

Hoff. Taking their two small sons, aged five and three, they moved in 1864 to the Iowa prairie. They were not the first of their kin to do this. In the early fifties Martin Buechly had visited the village of Prairie Rapids, now the city of Waterloo. He brought his family in 1855. 

Other Brethren families joined him the next year and they organized a Brethren church. Buechly, Myers and 

Miller were familiar names in this group. A steady stream of settlers followed and located in Orange Township. Many were from Somerset and Westmoreland counties in Pennsylvania; some, like the Hoffs, were second generation from the Pennsylvania group, coming from way stations in Ohio. At first the journey was made by train to Chicago and by wagon the rest of the way. Then the railroad was extended to Dubuque. After the arrival of the Hoffs, it was built on through Waterloo and westward.

John and Mary Hoff bought a farm on the Black Hawk road three and one-half miles southwest of Waterloo and

continued to reside there for twenty-seven years. Among other things they grew corn, wheat, apples and boys and girls. Most important of all were the boys and girls. 

Four girls were born to them in Iowa, only the oldest, Sarah Della, and the youngest, Mary Myrtle, living to maturity.

The Hoff home was about four miles from the South Waterloo church house built in 1868. In the snows and mud of the young Iowa community four miles were some distance. It was not always possible for the family to attend church. They went when they could and kept the fires of the spirit always burning at home. Services were held at times in the near-by schoolhouse. When there was baptism, the Black Hawk creek on the Hoff farm was the place, and the Hoff home provided the dressing rooms. The Hoffs built a roomy house and developed a substantial farm. Life was simple and wholesome in those days.

John Hoff should have been a blacksmith like his father; he was mechanically inclined and was always happier tinkering than farming. This did not keep him from being a practical farmer, but it was an interest to pass on to his son Emanuel. 

The boy grew. He made something of the fourfold development—mentally, physically, spiritually, socially -that Holy Writ connects with the Master. There was the fiber of native hardwood.

CHAPTER II  

The Hoff family was much like other farm families of the time. Making a living and building up the farm consumed the best efforts of the entire family. But they were not too busy for neighborly sociability and for the things of the spirit. Often on Sunday one or more families of friends would drop in for a visit, or the Hoffs would be visiting in some neighbor's home. Families were sizable in those days. But the well-filled cellar and smokehouse or garden in season furnished adequate resources for any emergency. Visitors were always heartily welcomed. A number of cousins had been attracted to the South Waterloo community. There was visiting back and forth and a strong community fellowship developed. This provided a social fabric wholesome for the growth of young life. The Hoff home was seldom without more permanent guests-an aunt, some other relative or friend. Among these was the habitual wayfarer who had the habit of showing up occasionally as he passed that way in his arduous life of doing nothing. 

Color was added to life by the occasional encampment of a band of Indians, who still frequented the valleys of the Cedar and the Black Hawk.

The home and the church were the centers of education and culture in those days. These settlers at Waterloo were Pennsylvania Dutch. In the Hoff home the 

Dutch was much spoken in the early days. Children learned it more readily than the less-used English. Emanuel was five when baby Della came. In order to get him and his brother to using English, the mother told

them: "The baby is going to talk English. If you want her to understand you, you'll have to talk English to her."

With this coaching at home, the baby and the public school did the job. The boys got the habit of speaking and thinking in English.

The church of the community made a great contribution to the mind as well as to the spirit. The South Waterloo group was more ready than most Brethren congregations to accept new ways. Brethren ministers traveled and preached in those days. Revival meetings were getting under way. There was no trouble to get a crowd from night to night for periods as long as four weeks. Some day-meetings were also held. It was in one of these revival meetings (in 1877) that Emanuel joined the church at the age of sixteen. This was not a radical step for him to take, for he was a serious-minded youth. A friend of his youth says that he was always religious, "must have been born that way." He recalls that one winter day when the road was impassable, Emanuel walked to Waterloo to hear a sermon in the Presbyterian church. Upon returning he remarked that one statement made by the minister had repaid him for his seven-mile walk. South Waterloo welcomed the

Sunday school at an early date. This, too, had a large place in the lives of her growing young people. 

One dark cloud came over the scene. The division from the main body of the church of Old Orders and 

Progressives in 1881-82 brought strife and bitterness.

The Old Orders did not enter into the Waterloo situation much, though they did affect the Hoff relationship. 

One of the mother's brothers in Ohio became Old Order. 

But many of the Waterloo Brethren went with the Progressives. This made tense feelings. Three of the moth-

er's brothers became Progressive, as well as other relatives and friends of the family. All of the father's immediate relatives remained with the church. Church strife is bad for adults, but it is worse for children. They get the emotional results untempered by even the modicum of reason that helps to conserve spiritual values for older folks. And so it was with the South Waterloo young people. 

Emanuel, however, was old enough to escape the worst effects of the division. He was grown and capable of reason. He was already thinking of the ministry. Later on he told one of his Bethany classes that by the time he was twenty he had decided that if he was to be a minister he must master grammar. And master it he did. 

He grew up in the time when the church was getting its rebirth in higher education. His father had only a few short terms of country schooling. But Emanuel got all that the country school had to give and thirsted for more. He and his brother attended the Prairie Home 

Seminary in Waterloo the winter of 1880-81. In this private school he had opportunity for mastery of grammar, mathematics and other subjects such as were needed for schoolteaching. He then taught in the district school at Spring Creek, a community in the southern part of Black Hawk County some twenty miles from his home. This was the winter of 1881-82. Then he taught a summer term near his home in the Orange Grove school. He attended teachers' institutes and there got further inspiration for education. A friend of his of those days says that much of his own inspiration for literature came out of reading Dante and Milton with Emanuel

Hoff. But all was not work and study. The same friend recalls the games they used to play. Chess was one of

them but it had to be played under cover. "Sticks" was another, and the rather shaky nerves of both of them brought on many disputes. 

About that time Elder J. G. Royer began coming to the 

South Waterloo church to find students for the Brethren school at Mount Morris, of which he was president. 

Emanuel Hoff was among the first to go. Three or four may have preceded him. He went in the fall of 1883 and enrolled in the classical course. He went to college to learn. His ready mind devoured the learning opportunities he found there. His parents aided him in his undertaking but he helped himself financially all he could. He pared potatoes in the kitchen and did general repair work on the college buildings, fixing door latches, windows and what not about the place. He was mechanically minded, having made wire puzzles as a boy, designed a grain sack holder that might well have been patented, rigged up a telephone from the house to his shop and undertaken to build one to a neighbor's home. He also had carpentered as need required at home, building an up-to-date chicken house with windows for sun and air, which was quite a novelty in those days. These skills served him well in college as well as in his later life. But in school he chose a classical training.

He found his place in the group of "steadies" at college with folks of the type of James M. Neff. His roommate, Prof. F. G. Muir of Pasadena, California, says he was affectionate, human and always ready to help anyone. 

He was a good student and exemplary in his habits. 

He graduated in 1886. The commencement program of 

June 11, 1886, carries as its first item The Civilizing 

Power of Faith, by E. B. Hoff. Other names of class mem-

bers on the program are Abba Fager, George L. Shoemaker, James M. Neff, M. Eliza Canode, and M. Elva Newcomer.

It was in 1885, the summer after his middle school year at Mount Morris, that Emanuel Hoff was called to the ministry. The exact date is not at hand. The Mount 

Morris College paper, The School and Home, under date of July 1885, carries the following item: "E. B. Hoff arrived home safe and well the day after he left the Mount, and found the folks well. We learned since from other parties that he has been called to that great and noble work, preaching the Gospel. May his labors be blessed with much good is the prayer of your writer and all interested in the School and Home." 

Emanuel Hoff was not a ready speaker; we must look for other reasons if we are to learn why he was called to the ministry. He was somewhat tedious in speaking and a little awkward in delivery, having a tendency to gaze at the ceiling instead of facing his audience. But people liked him. Those who knew him respected and loved him. He was genuine. He had taught school and that gave a young man a rating in those days. Then he had gone to college. The people at Waterloo, as in other churches that valued education, had the feeling that educated young men should be put to use in the ministry. 

Especially was this true of those who took part in church work when they returned to their home congregations. 

The South Waterloo church did much to provide a growth program for her young people and entrust them with leadership opportunities. There were Sunday evening meetings resembling the Christian Workers' meetings of more recent times. There were Sunday-school meetings, some of them all-day meetings, and these were

planned with young people especially in mind. The 

Brethren colleges in those days conducted training courses for Sunday-school work. The church used the young people so trained when they returned. Young men who took part in such activities were likely to be elected to the ministry. Some who did not want the ministry refused to take part in the church meetings lest they be called. But Emanuel was always ready to do what he could. It was natural then that he should be called to the ministry. The church intended to elect two ministers on that occasion, but the voting indicated that three should be set apart. These were E. B. Hoff, W. H. Lichty, and Hershel Maust. Elder J. G. Royer was there at the time and most probably conducted the proceedings. Elder Royer persuaded S. H. Miller to remain with the church instead of going with the Progressives, and he was made elder on this occasion. Elder 

S. H. Miller was a spiritual father and adviser to Emanuel. He

Emanuel was ready personally for this call to the ministry. His grandfather had been a minister. was wont to say, however, that had it not been for the influence of his mother he might well have been a locomotive engineer. His home and church gave him spiritual interests. He was personally committed to the 

Lord. He had an unquenchable thirst for learning and a deep desire to know the Bible. He was ready to enter any door of opportunity which the Lord opened. He might lack oratorical ability, but he had consecration, faith and a willingness to work. He also had a keen mind and although he was bashful he had a real interest in people. He was thoughtful of children. His youngest sister recalls that he sometimes took her with him to fill

appointments in his early ministry. He was thoughtful of people younger than himself. Here one and there another has mentioned the interest that he took in them as new students or as young Christians. And whenever opportunity afforded he was likely to be found calling upon some elderly people or shut-ins in the neighborhood. These human interests coupled with his devotion and his keen interest in the Bible helped people to forget his mannerisms and led them to seek his preaching. His first text was the passage, "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel," from Isaiah 61, the text that Jesus took as the theme for his ministry (Isaiah 61:1-3; Luke 4:16-21).

This became the text not only for his first sermon but for his life.

After his final year at Mount Morris he returned to his home and there set himself to a discipline that was prophetic of the achievement he was to make in learning and Bible study. He had already accumulated a considerable number of books. He fixed up a room away from the road, covering the lower sash with heavy curtains so that he could work with a minimum of distraction. Here he put his books, got a student's chair and table, and set about to prepare himself further for the ministry. He helped with chores and farm work as occasion required, occasionally did a little carpentry, raised chickens one year to pay for his keep, and helped the neighbors at times, but devoted the major portion of his time religiously to study. He carried this on for three years, from 1886 to 1889. Something of the thoroughness of this study is evident from the fact that he went completely through Webster's unabridged dictionary marking significant words and making extensive lists

22 

of words derived from the same roots. Pinned into his sermon notebook is a list of Christian, non-Christian, and common traits of character, but the interesting thing to observe here is not this list but the fact that it is scribbled on the back of a letter from Dr. William Rainey 

Harper, then of Yale University, in answer to an inquiry about how to proceed in getting started on the study of Hebrew. The letter is dated April 17, 1888. This was the Dr. Harper who a few years later was to organize the University of Chicago and become one of the most influential teachers of our inquiring student. 

Emanuel's sisters and their playmate, Eliza Miller, have definite memories of this study room and the proceedings there. The latter recalls tiptoeing into this room awed by its array of books and papers. The close application to study made a heavy drain upon his strength. He was thin and pale and never was rugged. 

Some say that in those days when he would stand up to preach they wondered whether he would have strength to go through with his sermon, but he always preached with fervor. When tired in his study, he would doze off for a few minutes and awake refreshed; sometimes he would go out and work around the place for a while, but these pauses were not ordinarily for any great length of time. He needed no teacher or approaching examination to whip up his power of concentration; he stuck to his task under his own enthusiasm. 

His thoughts during this period were not all of books. 

He was thinking of a wife and home. His last year at 

Mount Morris was the first for an attractive but unassuming girl from the vicinity of Manhattan, Illinois, named Anna Gockley. Anna took great interest in Sunday-school work, earning one certificate after another

for normal study and delighting to teach primary children. She could sing, too, leading music as opportunity afforded. She was about seven years younger than he, and any special interest which he may have had in her then was not apparent even to her. But a year or so later while she was teaching, she got a letter from Emanuel B. Hoff. She asked her landlady's counsel as to whether she was worthy to answer it. With encouragement she did answer. Her autograph book carries an entry under his hand dated June 30, 1887: "Life-we speak of its stupendous work; but oh, eternity-... we are hushed into a silent, reverent awe. A humble sharer in friendship's ties. E. B. Hoff." There was the awesomeness of love in the developing friendship. He visited her home, and in the holiday season of 1888 she visited his home. That was her senior year at Mount

That night the bride and groom took the train for his parental home at Waterloo. A primary source of information for the period following is Anna's diary begun

on that day and running until the next June 16, two days before the birth of their son-but that is getting ahead of the story. When the newly married couple came into church the first Sunday evening at Waterloo, they sat down together on the women's side, which was an innovation for the South Waterloo Brethren church.

CHAPTER III  

A series of meetings was under way at South Waterloo, conducted by T. T. Myers. The second evening after the bride and groom's arrival, when they returned from church they found a party of neighborhood boys approaching to give them a "belling." The diary charitably call it a "serenading." Naturally, the boys would not stop until they had seen the bride. 

The work for the first summer and fall of married life was to consist of preaching in the scattered points of northern Iowa and encouraging Sunday schools. The

Brethren in those days had great evangelistic enthusiasm and seemed not to be contented until there were religious services in as many schoolhouses as possible. The South 

Waterloo church itself had numerous outposts, such as 

Hudson, Spring Creek, Eagle Center, Laporte, Crane 

Creek, Maple Grove, Sandhill and Dunkerton. Other churches of the district had outposts also. Wherever a few members could be gathered, meetings were held in schoolhouses. 

On June 21 the bride started "helping 'Manuel arrange parts of a few sermons." On June 26 they got one pony for their team and a set of harness. On July 6 the first trip was taken-to Dunkerton. "A warm and dusty ride, having had to travel through a great deal of sand. Found a sick mother with seven children. Here I had one of my first experiences with children, such as we shall expect to meet quite frequently in our travels," comments Anna in the diary. ""Manuel preached on Love the Fulfilling of the Law, Romans 13:10." In the eve-

ning he preached at the Mount Hope service from John 

4:8, God's Love for Man. Monday's entry says: "We stopped by the way both Saturday and today to gather bouquets of wild lilies."

The next two trips did not turn out very well. On 

August 3 they drove to Grundy, only to find the schoolhouse locked and no meeting appointed because the arrangements card had not been received. The following week end they drove twenty-seven miles to Spring

Creek and found a Sunday school of twelve but preaching had been recalled. They got their new buggy on 

August 14 and started two days later on a trip that was to continue until cold weather drove them home. On this trip they covered northern Iowa from Greene to 

Sheldon. They held meetings at Greene, at Reed's schoolhouse and Union Ridge schoolhouse in Franklin County, in schoolhouses at Clarion and Goldfield, in Hancock County near Corwith, in Clay County, at Sheldon, in Cherokee County and in Ida County. Everywhere they stayed with members or friends of the church. 

They had to go to a hotel only once when night found them among strangers on the return trip. There was not much expense; the members fed the ponies as well as the preacher and his wife. Sometimes in traveling they picked up corn on the road for the team. The only references to income for the three months are as follows: "After service they collected $5.10 for us" (August 

23). "A kind lady slipped a quarter in 'Manuel's hand" (August 28). "Mrs. E. gave me a silk handkerchief and 

Bro. E. gave 'Manuel $1" (September 8). "The members made up $4.25 for us" (October 16). When they started for home in November they carried gifts: eight slips of cactus and three pairs of pigeons.

Few tangible results of the trip could be seen, but seed was sown and encouragement was given to many isolated folks who were trying to maintain and propagate their faith. 

Favorite sermon themes and texts used on this trip were: God's Goodness to Man in Creation, Genesis 1;

The Fall of Man, Genesis 3; Bondage in Sin and Freedom in Christ, Exodus 1:17 and John 8:31-36; The Bruised

Head and the Bruised Heel, Genesis 3:15; The Prodigal 

Son, Luke 15; The Fiery Serpents, Numbers 21; Jesus 

Knocks, But in Vain, Song of Solomon 5:12; Love the Fulfilling of the Law (sometimes extended to two sermons, including the commandments and ordinances as we observe them), Romans 13:10; Faith and Repentance, Acts 

2:38, John 14, John 15, Hebrews 3:7-10, James 1: 21-25, 1 Corinthians 11; The Mustard Seed and the Leaven,

Matthew 13:31-33; God's Love to Man in Redemption, John 3:16-17; Baptism, from selected New Testament texts. 

Returning to the Hoff home at Waterloo, they moved his library downstairs and gradually got a room into shape for housekeeping. They spent much time in reading and Bible study together, working on sermons and on a systematic study of the Gospel of Matthew. They helped with the work of the household and farm. Emanuel spent many days and half days in the timber cutting cordwood. Nearly every Sunday he was preaching somewhere at the home church, the Waterloo city church, Sandhill, the Strayer schoolhouse across the creek or at 

Spring Creek. They read Butler's Bible Work, Parker's sermons, and numerous other books. 

The last half of March Anna took a trip to McPherson,

Kansas, to visit her mother, sister and brothers, who had moved there. While she was gone, Emanuel moved their belongings twenty-seven miles to Spring Creek and started putting in shape an old house at the edge of the timber. She came on the twenty-eighth and supervised the completion of the home. One step in the process was the killing of sixty rats "which gives us more rest." They located at Spring Creek in order to build up a church. 

Sunday school was held in two schoolhouses, and preaching also. They planted a garden with the opening of spring, also hatched out turkeys and chickens. The members brought them a sack of flour and some groceries, and once a load of hay. It was a labor of love, faith and struggle.

The diary stops on June 16, 1890. On the eighteenth, the anniversary of their marriage, their baby came, a son. They called him Ernest G. The G is for Gockley,

mother's maiden name. On May 26 they had set six hens with one hundred and four eggs, so it is likely that the sermon for June 22 did not get as much preparation as most sermons of Preacher Hoff. With chicks and a baby there was plenty of activity that week in the Hoff household.

There is little information about that summer and the following autumn. Their living was very scanty, too much so for mother's health, especially when the rigors of winter came on. Tuberculosis developed and the home had to be broken up the following February. A trip was made to St. Louis for medical aid, and then they went to McPherson, Kansas, to her mother's home. 

Death came on April 23, 1891. Grandmother Gockley accompanied the bereaved husband and ten-month-old babe on the sad journey to Waterloo for the burial in the 

Orange cemetery near the South Waterloo church.

The companionship between father and mother was thus broken after only a year and ten months. 

It was precious while it lasted. Not only were they congenial and much in love, but she fitted beautifully into his work and life interests. The notebook of sermon notes and jottings partly in his handwriting and partly in hers gives tangible evidence of this. She was neat and precise. Her diary, essays and letters look like the pages of a well-kept copybook. She had the sweetness and the cheeriness of disposition that he needed to keep up his spirits and inspire him to be his best. Her death was a great blow to him. He returned to his parents with his motherless child, and made his home with them for more than three years.

In the fall of 1891, Grandfather Hoffs left the old homestead at Waterloo and moved to McPherson, Kan-

sas. Father went with them and helped them to move a house and put it in shape on a plot of ground near the young Brethren college. Grandmother Gockley already lived at McPherson; also Auntie Flack, the oldest sister of Grandmother Hoff. Auntie Flack's adopted son, Freeman Muir, was a teacher in the college. McPherson offered the associations of a college faculty and environment, and father valued these. His younger sister became a student in the college. 

It was at McPherson a couple of years later that he first met Albert C. Wieand. The meeting took place on the college campus west of the old dormitory. It introduced a fellowship out of which was to come the lifework of both of them. A. C. Wieand was a young man from the Ohio community where father was born. He knew father's uncles there and friendliness with the Hoff family developed immediately. Father cordially invited him to come and see his library. He showed him 

Young's Analytical Concordance, Godet's Commentary on John, and other precious books. And what was better, he invited him to come and use the books whenever he wished. A. C. Wieand was not yet in the ministry, and father's interest and preaching made a deep impression upon him. He says he liked to hear father preach better than others because father's sermons showed deep insight into the Scriptures. Mannerisms didn't bother him.

Father was gone from home for about a year, so long in fact that his little son scarcely remembered him when he returned. He preached on the northern frontier in Dakota. And he attended the Hygienic College in St. Louis for a time. It was while there that he helped to nurse through a siege of fever a good friend of Anna's,

Ida Wagner. This association opened the way for another intimate companionship.

Ida Wagner was a member of the Mount Morris graduating class of 1890. She also graduated from Dixon 

Normal College but her interest developed along lines of health and medicine. Besides the Hygienic College at St. Louis she attended Hering Medical College in Chicago and received the M. D. degree in 1894. Her first meeting with E. B. Hoff was at his wedding with Anna 

Gockley. She says she wondered then what Anna saw in him. But time and opportunity changed things. The 

Gospel Messenger for October 16, 1894, carries the following correspondence item from the Franklin Grove church, Illinois: "Last Sunday evening, September 23, at our usual service, Bro. Emanuel B. Hoff of McPherson,

Kansas, preached in an earnest manner a very acceptable discourse from Psalm 119: 11. At the close of the services he stepped down into the audience and led Sister Ida M. Wagner, M.D., to the altar, and they were there united by Bro. Daniel Dierdorff in the holy bonds of wedlock.

They left the same night for the West, with many kind wishes for their prosperity. Sister Ida was reared in this community, and with her education and talents will make Brother Hoff a most excellent helpmeet in the work of the Master."

They had rooms in Grandfather Hoff's home at Mc- Pherson for a few months. It was of this period that 

Dr. G. H. Van Dyke says: "I knew him first at McPherson. He was kind, tenderhearted, unselfish, a fine and safe counselor, sacrificing his own interests to help anyone in need. In this spirit he and his wife came to our home the evening we were elected to the ministry in McPherson. His kind counsel, encouragement, and

prayer can never be forgotten. He presented to us some helpful books which I have used much and still prize highly." Others recall the great earnestness and enthusiasm of his preaching at McPherson. He was ordained to the eldership there.

After a wagon trip over the western plains for health's sake, E. B. and Ida Hoff took charge of the recently organized mission in the city of Des Moines, Iowa. The son, Ernest, now five years old, was so well at home with his grandparents and aunties that he was left behind, and their home was his home until he had one of his own. However, he spent periods of a month or so on various occasions in the home of his father and stepmother. Little information is at hand regarding the work at Des Moines. E. B. conducted the mission and 

Ida practiced medicine there for about two years. He spent some time one summer working in the country on the Goughnour farm. There he thought out sermons on the sulky plough and fortified his health with country food. The Gospel Messenger indicates that the mission had been organized in 1894 with thirty members. 

A correspondence item in 1896 reports that by that time there was a lively Sunday school of 130 and a preaching service with still larger attendance. 

At the Annual Conference at Ottawa, Kansas, in 1896, the Mission Board extended a call to E. B. and Ida Hoff to go to India to help to stabilize the newly established mission there, but the medical examiner at Des Moines said father was not in physical condition to endure the India climate. They continued on in Des Moines. 

In October of the next year, 1897, they pulled up stakes and he entered the Divinity School of the University of Chicago. Here his passion for Bible study had

its day. Dr. William Rainey Harper, the president of the university, was a master of Hebrew and famous as a teacher of Old Testament. Father had been in correspondence with him nine years before regarding the study of Hebrew. Dr. Ernest D. Burton, an outstanding man, was teacher of New Testament. And there were others, Shailer Mathews, for instance. Father would not line up for a degree. He had no taste for systematic theology or church history; he wanted only Bible and 

Biblical languages. He studied intensely, repeating some of his courses in order to master them. He had struggled along for so many years by himself and with such limited resources that his university opportunity was almost too good to be true. Here as in many later projects that enriched his life and usefulness, Ida's financial resources proved a blessing. They brought him books and the best of conditions for study.

A. C. Wieand and Edward Frantz lived in the Hoff home at the university during the summer term of 1899, and both say room and board that summer were without cost to them. A. C. Wieand has a vivid picture in his mind of father down on the floor studying Hebrew. Dr. 

Burton's exegetical method proved of special interest to father. I recall being in father's home, near the university, in the summers of 1898 and 1899, and attending the 

Naperville Annual Conference with him in 1898. But what an eight-year-old boy remembers of an Annual Conference or a university does not belong in this kind of a story.

John Luke, the first-born of Emanuel and Ida, was born in Chicago, June 6, 1898. He was named for his grandfathers and the "beloved physician."

Father remained in the university through August

1899, and then went to Manchester College as dean of the Bible school and teacher of Interpretative Study of the Bible. Here he found his stride in Bible teaching. 

He remained in the college for two years. 

On March 30, 1900, the second son of Emanuel and Ida was born. He was named Amos Harper, after the prophet of God's righteousness and father's favorite university teacher.

This leads to the threshhold of a new experience that brought father into his real lifework.

CHAPTER IV  

Father left his Manchester home August 26, 1901, and was joined in Ohio a couple of days later by A. C. Wieand for an eight months' trip to Bible lands. The trip had been planned in the summer of 1899. Father's diary of the trip is at hand and invites a more detailed account than we have space for. Careful preparation had been made for the trip so that it might yield the highest values. 

Lists were prepared of places to be visited, with all Bible references recorded so that they could be read on the ground. The guidebooks were supplemented by a notebook full of facts and things to look for. Thanks again to Ida's financial resources, this trip was possible for father. In accordance with A. C. Wieand's financial limitations, however, they traveled third class and hunted their own way instead of taking guided tours. 

The ship they took was bound for Italy. They found themselves in steerage with about two hundred Italians, mainly of the working class. Living conditions were so bad that a special arrangement was made for a bunk. 

This was better than open steerage but the bunk got full benefit of the odors from the kitchen and the pitching of the waves, being located in the prow of the ship. 

After a day in Naples, they went on to Athens, Corinth, Ephesus and Smyrna, passed Rhodes and the coast line as Paul did on his journeys, stopped at Mersina for 

Tarsus, and left ship at Beirut. From there they journeyed by train through the Lebanon mountains to Baalbec and Damascus, making a horseback side trip to the cedars of Lebanon. Leaving Damascus for Tiberias

on horse with two Arabs, they came to Banias (Caesarea Philippi), the upper Jordan valley, and the Sea of Galilee. On the forenoon of October 20 they read the Matthew and Mark accounts of Christ's Galilean ministry beside the lake which featured so prominently in his ministry. The next day they spent boating on the lake and swimming in it. Then they journeyed on to Nazareth, Esdraelon, Sharon, Joppa, and Carmel, arriving at Jerusalem on October 30. 

On the first afternoon in Jerusalem, father said, "Let's go to the Mount of Olives." He had lived with Jerusalem in his mind to such an extent that he readily found his way around. His traveling companion says that father saw everything first and made quick conclusions as to sites and meanings. Most of these conclusions proved valid, but A. C. Wieand put them to the test of analysis and scrutiny and occasionally found them erroneous. Together they saw and learned more than either could have done alone. Father had little patience with ecclesiasticism and the commercialization of sacred sites, but was greatly appreciative of real Biblical settings and places. He left to his traveling companion the matter of bargaining and making of arrangements. 

Father carried a heavy camera and glass plates in order to get pictures on the trip. He lacked experience in photography but brought home some very good pictures. He wanted to get pictures of olive trees, fig trees, oaks, and other things out of the common life depicted in the Bible. He felt his limitations in photography to such an extent that he said, "I am going to get Ernest a camera as soon as I get home so that he will know how to take pictures when he travels in Bible lands." Father got me the camera and started me on an

interesting and useful hobby, but so far the trip to Bible lands on which to use it has not materialized. 

Father had a month of serious illness in Jerusalem of which he makes no mention at all in his diary. Polluted water was probably the cause of this illness. On 

December 6 they started a fifteen-day horseback trip through Samaria, Galilee, and Perea. Then in January they went to Egypt. Cook's asked three hundred dollars for their tour up the Nile. Father and A. C. Wieand went for the most part on the same boat and saw the same things at comparatively little expense but with more inconvenience and risk. Leaving Egypt February 

13 they returned to Italy and visited Rome. Coming up through Switzerland, they parted at Geneva, A. C. Wieand staying there and in Germany for a year's study but father returning home via France and England.

There was more to this trip than the increase of Bible knowledge which it brought. A. C. Wieand had long pondered in his heart the founding of a Bible school for the Brethren. He had talked about it to almost everyone who would listen. In the summer of 1899 at the university he and father talked it over by the hour. 

Father was under contract with Manchester then and nothing was said about his participating in it. All the time A. C. Wieand was prospecting for the right man to help him in founding the school. It was while they were riding horseback in Palestine that he "proposed" to father. Father said he too had been thinking much about the matter. Near the place where Jesus loved to be the school was christened. The diary account reads: "Stayed an hour or more under an olive tree on the side hill overlooking Bethany. Here we read the Scriptures

Many museum specimens were collected on this trip and shipped home ultimately to become school equipment. There were an Oriental plough, a mill, costumes, and a great many other things. The summer after his return he used these things in lectures and Bible institutes. In the fall he went to Chicago, and was a student in the university for the autumn and winter quarters. 

He then took charge of the work of the Chicago church on Hastings Street. There he labored for two years and planned for the opening of Bethany.

Perhaps we had better let father tell his own story of the Bethany project as he told it at the tenth anniversary exercises on October 3, 1915. The account is from the Bethany Bulletin of October 1915, taken by stenographic report from his address. "If we were to talk about the development of our school and the remembrances of things gone by, a good many things took place even before we had our services ten years ago, even before we got together as men, Brother Wieand and I. A good many things took place while we were riding donkeys in Palestine. We got the name of our school decided on the eastern edge of Olivet, under an old olive tree overlooking Bethany. We got a good many things besides that while we traveled together. We could not talk to anybody else. We separated a while and he went back to Germany and I settled in Chicago, here, helping in the church work at Hastings. 

He told me I should find a location for Bethany somewhere, so I tramped all over the west part of town, I do not know how many times; put a bid on a house west of Garfield Park, and did not get it; went out to Elgin

and looked up a location there and got the consent of the church and we planned to start out there, but that was upset for us. "By and by the problem seemed to point towards starting in a little old frame house over at Hastings. We dug out the basement for a dining room. We fixed up the upstairs for our schoolroom, and some of us lived in the story below, and then we crowded ourselves into the basement and left the other half for storage and for the rats-more rats than storage, however. "We stayed there until the thing was crowded. We crowded out one partition after the other to make room. 

Sometimes when we sang real hard the house would shake, until someone threatened to report us to the city officials for endangering our students. We hammered away and found the posts fairly solid yet, and we stayed still longer. We crowded those walls until the thing almost burst. That simply gives me an illustration for what I wanted to say along the line of our spreading. 

I do not think those walls, those low ceilings and shaky posts are very much more out of harmony with what they ought to have been than was the narrowness of our own work. I guess most of you know what it means to start work and build up courses and classes of this kind. 

I know sometimes, in the courses I taught, things were rather shaky and I do not know but that the ceilings were low. The faculty was about like that building, too, when we started. There were just three of us-Brother 

Wieand, Sister Van Dyke and I. Brother Wieand taught pedagogy and Bible elocution; Sister Van Dyke taught music, and I taught what Bible work there was. We went along as best we could. I am sure there was much room for improvement in study, in student body as well

as in faculty. I think it was in exquisite harmony all the way through. And I am sure, very sure, that if the 

Lord had not put a whole lot of grace into us it would have been all failure; but the Lord in his mercy graced the thing through in every sense of the word. He graced it through in the matter of financial development, with much earnest prayer and hard work. For actually, my dear brethren and sisters, we believe in prayer and hard work going together; not working alone and not praying alone, but we have found out long ago to our satisfaction that much prayer and much work go together to make success along any line that is worth while. Through much prayer and much hard work we have grown to this place here. "We have worked for a faculty until we have a number of devoted workers who are being well prepared. 

None of us are prepared as we should be for the work. 

I can assure my dear people that our courses have developed very much. Our curriculum shows it some, but our courses, if you could see the inner workings of them, would show it very much more. Teaching Romans is not the same as it was ten years ago. Teaching John or 

Matthew is not the same. Teaching the prophets is not the same, I dare say, and probably the pedagogy and psychology are not the same as they were ten years ago. "By the grace of God we went along. In the university I used to say that if I got my schooling in ten years 

I would be satisfied. I would like now to say, 'ten years more.' I do not know whether He will allow it or not, but I would like to prepare myself very, very much better now, and then work ten years more. I would like, if Bethany could spare me, for five years right now and then work ten years more. I see so many, many things

I have touched very little. I must thank God this afternoon for his rich grace, and I want to say that what cheers us is the earnest prayers that have been offered for us throughout the whole brotherhood. How many scores of good strong men and women have come to me and said, 'We are praying for you.' "While those brethren have been praying, they have been helping too, financially. Some of them have gone to their reward. Many hard-earned dollars have come in here. We have not gotten in large Rockefeller endowments and millions, but we have gotten little driblets and medium-sized driblets of money, of hard-earned labor. And I would not be doing right not to say that much that has helped me, very much that has helped me to do what I have been able to do by the grace of God, is that the hard-working parents of my good wife years ago, before we were building Bethany Bible School, laid up money for us that we might build and be better endowed for this work. God has rewarded them and rewarded us in this work. Those out in the field farming are teaching within these walls. God bless them, every one. Unconsciously they are doing these things, not enjoying the blessings as we are here-these songs and prayers, these encouraging words, but God will mark them out every one and bless them. Let us remember them wherever they are, toiling hard and giving of their earnings to the work of the Lord."

The facts contained in this statement might have been summarized in much less space, but the quotation has been given in full to show father's spirit-which is more important even than the facts. Not a great deal more need be said here about father's part in starting and building up Bethany. A. C. Wieand should write the

story of Bethany; it is a big story in itself. A few things, however, may be made definite here. 

Father and stepmother purchased the two-story house across the street from the Hastings Street church, and fitted it up for use in the opening of the school. They financed the whole project. Father's library became the school library and classroom. Other classes were held in the church as the school grew. Bethany opened October 3, 1905, with thirteen students. The total enrollment the first year reached about thirty-five. The attendance the next year was larger. The house was too small. The third year in order to make room for the school the family moved out. They got a plot of ground at Maywood where they had room for boys, a garden, chickens, horse and a cow. This was rather difficult of access to the school, especially while it was on Hastings Street, but it proved a great blessing to the health of the family. Father enjoyed the relaxation of his little farm through the years. It also provided facilities for corn huskings, wiener roasts, and many other social events with groups of students. On one occasion, there were one hundred fifty-three in such a gathering. There were other occasions that were more intimate in which a single family or one person alone enjoyed the hospitality of the home with an opportunity to know the Hoffs and get light on vexing problems from a sympathetic counselor.

After the Des Moines Conference in 1908 the Van Buren Street block where the school now stands was purchased. The first building was built in 1909 and the school moved into it in the fall. The phenomenal growth which made this move necessary was partly due to an advertising booklet which the Hoffs financed to the ex-

tent of $1,500. This booklet was carried far and wide by the students. The enrollment doubled and enough money came in to purchase the block. The sacrifice in those years made a strong appeal. Both father and President

Wieand supported themselves for the first thirteen years of the school's history. This was more difficult for A. C. 

Wieand than it was for father, but father filled full his measure of sacrifice by giving liberally in addition. He and stepmother gave a hundred dollars a month to the scholarship fund for a time, contributed $2,000 to one of the buildings, and helped at other points too numerous to mention. This seriously depleted their resources but was the source of great joy to father. His heart, head and purse were in the work of Bethany. 

His twenty-three years of teaching showed very few absences from the classroom, except when he was away for an institute or on some other special mission. He did not have rugged health, but kept going whether he felt like it or not. He was happier teaching than doing anything else. He seldom came to his classes without thoughtful preparation, regardless of how frequently he had taught the course or how great was the pressure of events. He took another trip to Bible lands in the middle twenties, taking his wife, and John Luke and Amos and their wives. He kept pursuing research and studying.

CHAPTER V  

Some say that E. B. Hoff buried himself in teaching, and that teaching is too undramatic a profession to write much about. His career may not have been as dramatic as some but it was not without color. The wholeheartedness with which he taught and the enthusiasm for the Bible that he was able to inspire in his students were striking indeed.

He was a church man through and through, but he took little part in national or district church affairs. He served on the Tract Examining Committee and the Hymnal committee, but he was not often on Conference committees. He was seldom on the Annual Meeting program except when a Bible institute was held in connection with it. The local congregation did not use him much in its organizational setup. His interests were not much in organizational and program matters. His heart was in the heart of things. He preached and taught with prophetic insight and fervor. 

Perhaps he was a little short on patience with those who see programs through and work much on administrative matters. In the difficult process of starting a school by private initiative for general church service, it was inevitable that there should be many misunderstandings and opposition. In the early days of the school J. H. Moore, editor of the Gospel Messenger, was always a staunch supporter and dependable friend of the Bible school project, but not many such as he could be counted. Father was slow to trust those who opposed, and sometimes needed the diplomacy of his

teammate in the school to smooth the relationships. But he was thoroughly conscientious, and everyone knew that he was genuine. He was unassuming and unassertive, but it was not hard to know where he stood. 

Father was associate president of Bethany from its founding until his death. Although such work was not much to his liking, he carried a heavy share of administrative responsibility, especially in the period before Conference assumed ownership and direction of the school for the brotherhood. President Wieand was out of residence occasionally for health, travel or service, and father carried things through without serious break. 

He always participated in the enrollment of students and was much sought as a counselor. He would have no secretary between him and students who were seeking his counsel. When the offices were moved from one building to another, he insisted that his office remain where he was most accessible to students. President 

Wieand provided initiative and great visions for the school. Father was a stabilizer and helped to keep a practical balance in the building program, finances, and other phases of development. 

In the early days the entire scope of Bible teaching in the school was his responsibility. When the faculty grew and positions were classified, he was designated professor of Biblical and historical theology. He was a great 

Bible teacher. It has been my privilege to study under a number of the outstanding Bible teachers of the universities and seminaries of the country, but I have found none greater than he. No other gave me anywhere near the measure of help that he did in knowing the Bible; none helped me as much in getting hold of sound methods of independent study; none contributed enthusiasm

and inspiration in such measure as he. Though I am his son, I have no hesitancy in saying this, for it has been a common experience of his students. If I refrained from saying it, I would be withholding what they would want me to say.

Somehow he managed to challenge both the trained graduate student and the unlettered reader of the Bible. 

Not many Bible teachers can do that. I have met numbers of unschooled brethren and sisters, many of them older people, at Conference and elsewhere who said with enthusiasm, "I knew your father. I attended such-andsuch a Bible institute. He said thus-and-so that I shall never forget. He was a great teacher." And it is common knowledge that the seminary required some time to recover the power to hold advanced students after his death. Much of the drawing power of the school with such students centered in him. One of his students expressed it in this way: "Some will say, 'I've been thinking about this question for twenty-five years, and more and more I am convinced that I reached the right conclusion as many years ago,' but Brother Hoff would say, 'I have been thinking about this question for twentyfive years and every time I am about ready to formulate my conclusion some new evidence appears and I am forced to consider it and postpone my conclusion.'" He maintained a constantly growing point of view which kept his thinking fresh and pertinent. 

He did not do the thinking for his students, though he knew well where he wanted to take them. He counted the ability to think correctly for themselves to be much more important to his students than the holding of any particular views. He wanted them to learn how to study the Bible for themselves so that they could carry on a

lifelong quest. He led them to think with him, not after him. His students often tried to get him to commit himself on perplexing questions. He used his wit to turn such occasions so that the responsibility for conclusions would be thrown back upon the students. He was not ashamed to say, "I don't know." He pigeonholed many things for more light. Common expressions of his were: "Let's hang that one on a hook." "Put it to soak until we can agree on a Biblical answer."

This does not mean that he lacked convictions. His basic convictions were so deep that he had no fear of investigation. Truth to him was a unity. Some of it he knew, and he was not afraid to search for what he did not know. He was not disturbed by the expression of honest doubt. In his quiet manner he would begin the search for the solution of the problem. Oftentimes he could show that others had similar doubts without disturbing their foundations. Such occasions brought glimpses of eternal verities. He himself had faced critical positions, extracted the values from them, and found a satisfying Christian philosophy that utilized and carried forward the heritage of his church. 

There were certain principles that he stood for and worked for. Many church leaders and folks who did not know him distrusted him and questioned the soundness of some of his interpretations and theological positions. 

Time has for the most part justified his wisdom. It is my conviction that to father perhaps more than to any other is due the credit for keeping the church from the theological detours that some of our sister groups have taken. He helped the church, directly and through his students, to keep faith with the truth in the growing outlook of his generation. He demonstrated that it is

possible to think deeply and believe. Only such sound and open faith can become an adequate basis for the action required of us in a day like this. 

Observations made by Edward Frantz at the funeral service are to the point here: "Brother Hoff had a manifest degree of sensitiveness to the presence of God. He had a rather extraordinary degree of sensitiveness to the comfort and well-being of others-he was unselfish. He loved truth supremely, and wasn't afraid that anything would turn out to be truth which was not truth. He has served the church by putting his spirit into the lives of others."

Writing was not easy for him, but he did write. Many articles in the publications of the church and school came from his pen. His books are the Biblical sections in Training the Sunday School Teacher, Books I and II, and The Message of the Book of Revelation. The responsive readings in the Hymnal, Church of the Brethren, are largely his compilation. 

The thoroughness of his scholarship and the significance of his work were recognized by the bestowal of the following honorary degrees: Master of Arts, by Manchester

College (1914); Doctor of Sacred Literature, by Bethany 

Bible School (1917); Doctor of Divinity, by McPherson

College (1927). 

One of father's greatest struggles was with his health. 

He had liver trouble and other physical problems that tended toward discouragement. It was with great effort and personal discipline that he kept up courage. He learned not to make decisions when he felt below par. 

His relaxation in gardening and similar pursuits at his Maywood home and his native wit and humor were fac-

tors in keeping up his efficiency. Greater than these, however, were the joy he found in his work and his close walk with God. 

A college president says of him: "I remember him for his genial, lovable way. He often appeared at school with some flower, shrub or twig which he had picked. 

His sympathetic helpfulness was memorable." 

His son Amos observes: "As I see it now, one of his strongest characteristics was his optimistic outlook on life, his ability to appreciate the worth-while features of a situation and minimize those features on the negative side of the ledger. This manifested itself often in enthusiasm and faith in a new proposition. However, his was not an unwarranted optimism but rather one founded on an appraisal of all arguments. His outlook was forward rather than backward."

He was always common and informal. Timid indeed was the person who could not approach him. He usually put on his house slippers as soon as he got to his office and went to class and chapel so shod. In class he was wont to perch himself on the corner of his table and sometimes wind his legs around the table leg in a precarious fashion. All this provided atmosphere for his native wit and his pointed, proverbial sayings. His wit relieved countless tensions and was an important factor in the success of his teaching. 

One who worked for several years in his home observes: "To my mind the biggest thing about him was his home life. The life he lived in the home was the life he lived outside. He met varied situations with the calm, steady faith that his Father's hand held the throttle which guided the affairs of men."

Those in his home were impressed with his devotion.

When his children were small and his wife cared for them by day, he insisted on looking after them at night. 

When Amos had scarlet fever he secured release from teaching so as to be able to care for him. He encouraged his men students to relieve their wives of household cares as much as possible so that they in turn could take a Mary's part in helping in the ministry. He practiced what he preached. He expected no reward except that which comes "where moth and rust do not corrupt."

His home was a part of his life and he wanted it, like the rest of his life, to be a blessing to others. He was eager to have the fellowship of guests. He often said to his wife, "Don't cook so much extra for company so we can entertain our friends oftener. We can become better acquainted with our friends by eating with them." "You could not be a guest in his home," writes one, "and leave it without feeling that you have carried away with you a benediction."

To many of his students my frequent use of the term father in this story will not seem incongruous. Says one: "Students feel that he was a father to them." Another: "His unassuming attitude begat confidence and responsiveness in others who came to think of their teacher rather as a friend, brother, or spiritual father. And this was a real relationship, for Brother and Sister Hoff shared everything with those who had need, giving away even the necessities that their own comfort required." 

Let more of his students speak their mind about him. 

One says: "It was through him that I became interested in the Bible. I attended the Bible institute that followed the Los Angeles Annual Conference of 1907. 

Before that time I read the Bible out of duty, thinking that it would do me good. Before the two weeks were

over I was in love with the Book and was planning to attend Bethany. In the years that followed at Bethany my appreciation for both the Book and its devoted teacher grew."

A pastor writes: "He was a puzzle to me for the first two weeks I sat in his classes; his original mannerisms together with his quiet humor kept one bubbling over with expectancy. His personality grew on one like the view of a mountain—at first just discernible, then clearer and larger and more beautiful as you approached it."

Many have been impressed by his humility, sincerity and utter self-forgetfulness. "If there had been any question as to his sincerity Bethany would have failed and fallen in the critical years when I knew the school," says one. "But the way he studied, taught and lived the truth which he found in the Scriptures with his whole soul carried us over." "He was gentle, humble, and approachable, yet possessed a stern sense of the deadliness of sin." "With all of his independent effort, he was humble. I have heard him say that he had more confidence in conclusions reached when in a class of spiritual men and women than in conclusions reached in his study alone. He would listen to the humblest and weakest students and consider their opinions. He never knew whom God might use as an avenue of information for him." "He 'walked humbly with his God.' We could sense his dependence upon God." "I think of E. B. Hoff as one who seemed to be entirely content to be only himself and willing to let all others know what he really was. He seemed freer from pretensions and pride in himself than anyone I ever knew. It seemed that he sought only for simple relationships with all persons in terms of pure good will." "I lived in his house in May-

wood for two years and spent many evenings with him. 

He was emphatic in insisting that at any time in the evening I needed help in my studies he could pull out of his own studies, help me and get right back again into deep concentration." 

He respected personality whether strong or weak. "Brother Hoff had the gift of making each one feel that there was something he could do. He always made me feel that it was worth while for me to try." His ability to read character was noteworthy. "He had keen insight into the lives and motives of other people." 

He was open to new light. "I think that no matter how often he had taught a passage, he went as a learner each time as he prepared to teach it."

He was whole-souled. His body did not express his thought too readily. "His thoughts and convictions seemed to struggle for expression, and it seemed to me, as was said of Wendell Phillips, 'His very body thought.'' "He was charitable with pocketbook, time, strength and wisdom, as well as towards the opinions of others." 

All of his students remember his graphic illustrations. "His illustrations were taken from everyday life and the listeners couldn't fail to get their significance. He laughingly told about the time he started to read the encyclopedia through to get illustrations." "His illustrations were pointed and plainly understood. Peering over his spectacles, and with eyes sparkling like diamonds, he would try to express something with emphasis. Then, arising from his chair and bending forward for an instant, he would suddenly whirl around on one foot and with an upward fling of the right arm and a still quicker movement downward make crude marks with the chalk

333 and land his emphasis. His students always got the point and remembered it."

He enjoyed good humor and knew how to use it with telling effect. "His quick wit was shorn of all sting by his kindly humor-which cannot be set down on paper. 

He always had an answer and a quick one." "Another teacher said, 'I can't understand how Brother Hoff can say anything he wants to say and the students never get hurt about it.' Maybe it was that habitual twinkle in his eyes or the smile on his face that made the student know he didn't mean anything by it. Or maybe it was just because his life radiated so much love and kindness that his students knew he never meant anything to hurt."

His greatest contribution was his own personality and spirit. This functioned best in his personal relationships with students, as some of the incidents in the next chapter will show.

As his sixty-eighth birthday came and went, he seemed to realize that his time was short. He hardly realized, however, that it was to be only a week. In addition to his usual teaching he was working intensively in preparation for some writing. He had a secretary employed to gather and organize material for him for these projects. 

On December 28, 1928, he spent the day in his library and outlined enough work for his secretary for some days to come. In the evening he expressed satisfaction at having accomplished an especially good day's work. 

He retired early, and about nine-thirty as his wife was retiring she heard him breathe his last without a word.

His heart, which had carried him through strenuous years, at last ceased functioning, and his spirit was re-

leased to enter into the fullness of the realities which had so long been fundamental in his life. 

Funeral services were conducted on December 31, at the Maywood home by Pastor H. L. Hartsough and at the Bethany chapel by Elder J. W. Lear, assisted by W. 

W. Slabaugh, Edward Frantz, F. N. Sargent, J. E. Miller, and A. C. Wieand. The last named was prevented by illness from being present in person but contributed a written message. Burial was in the Oak Ridge cemetery southwest of Maywood in the plot belonging to Bethany 

Bible School. 

Edward Frantz truly observed at the funeral service, "He built his monument in the hearts of his pupils."

CHAPTER VI  

In this chapter it is proposed first to relate a number of incidents that folks recall about father, incidents which reveal his character and interests, and then to record some of the pointed sayings that were so characteristic of him. A few of his rhymes and poems will be added for good measure.

INCIDENTS Dr. G. H. Van Dyke relates the following: "In the corner of my office stood a barrel of sugar. His boys and ours were playing around it and the lid strangely (?) became dislodged. I censoriously interfered. His mild words, 'Now, John Luke and Amos, you should not do this,' were a deep rebuke to me which I never forgot." 

In a homiletics class he said one day: "Never depend much on your notes. The window may be open and the wind blow them away; then you'd be lost." The next day he forgot his glasses and couldn't see to use his notes. He smiled and said: "I'll add to what I said yesterday: You might forget your glasses and you couldn't read the notes you have. So you'd better have the outline pretty well in your head."

He was always ready to laugh at a joke on himself as well as on others. One day he was telling how folks who go to the zoo always stop longest to look at the monkeys. He said he didn't know why unless it was because they are so much like them. All laughed and the class session proceeded. At the close of the period he

again referred to the monkeys and their pranks, saying, "Somehow I have to watch them more than any other animals in the zoo." Peals of laughter followed.

He often told his students: "When you get all nervous and worried about your studies, then do something else entirely different until you calm down." Once when one of the students was in his home he said, "I've so much studying to do I think I'll go hoe the garden."

A student who was often in his home asked his advice about the summer. He strongly advised a vacation and closed the conversation by saying, "Now mind your old father and do as I tell you." She did, although she did not want at all to do so. 

A prospective missionary reports counseling with him about going to China. She said: "I feel very definitely called to China; what if the Board should ask me to go to India?" He answered, "Stay by your call and go to 

China if God calls you there." After her appointment to China, he said, "I'm depending on you to be one of the ablest missionaries in China and my prayers go with you." She thought of this many times when up against difficulties in China and it helped her to carry through in the spirit in which she thought he would do it. 

This missionary was in an interior village when she learned of his passing. She says: "Somehow the truth of his passing dawned on me only gradually as I realized that I no longer had my spiritual father to advise me.

Tears filled my eyes every time I thought of him, yet with a smile I remembered his joy in the reunions that must be his. He has seen his Savior by now and is perhaps hunting for Saint Paul and talking to him about his writings. Next he may be looking up Saint Peter."

A student borrowed his notebook thinking to get an accurate set of notes on the subject under consideration, but on leafing through it found he could not read it. He had to return it with the notes uncopied. 

One day a student remarked that she had taken nearly all of her Bible work with him. He replied, "You're likely warped." 

In an examination he said, "You may have your Bibles but not your notebooks." One queried, "But what if my notes are in the margins of my Bible?" "Then take my

Bible," was the reply. She did, and there seemed to be an added sublimity to that examination. 

A missionary to India recalls the farewell letter she received from him. He wished her a pleasant voyage and assured her that though she might sometimes feel far out upon the sea, she could get comfort out of the fact that she would never be more than six miles from land. "He made a timid Western girl from the mountains of Idaho feel at home," says one. "Three times I was invited to the Hoff home with the students and twice by myself. These visits did something for me. I had come from a district where Brethren churches were few and far between. I had never had much of a general church consciousness. His personal interest in me gave me the consciousness that I was among those who loved the church and gave themselves for it. It helped me to discover that I was somebody and might become still more." 

A student asked him about a call to China. "You have felt the call, and that the door is open for your going," he said. "Suppose you pray now that should it not be the Lord's will for you to go, he may close the door that

has seemingly opened." The advice was followed and the door to a long and useful service remained open. "In the old Hastings Street Bethany was where I first learned to know him," says an early student. "I can see him yet hurrying down the street to the 'elevated' with little Jewish children holding his hands or coat-tails wherever they could get a hold. He always had a special place in his heart for the Jewish people and so much wanted to help them to know Christ." 

A classroom incident is recalled: "One day we had had an especially interesting lecture, and perhaps some of us were sitting with our mouths open when Brother Hoff came in for his turn. He put questions to the class with very poor response. Finally he said: 'I like to have you respond. I like to take my students with me and not have them sit like little birds with open mouths, waiting for a tidbit." " 999 The first time he taught the Practice Preaching course he kept telling the students what a grand opportunity they were having in being able to preach and receive the criticisms of the class, and that he wished he had had such an opportunity. The class told him it was not too late yet. He took them up on it and insisted that they be as free in criticizing his sermon as they were with the others.

His way of handling classroom questions is memorable. 

A student recalls the time when someone asked him whether he believed in dreams. "Yes, but not the mince pie type" was the ready answer.

Another recalls a Friday chapel incident. The student was having difficult problems and requested prayer. In response father started the hymn, 'Tis So Sweet to Trust

in Jesus, including the verse beginning, "I'm so glad I learned to trust him." A precious season of prayer followed.

One writes: "Seldom did we have a class period without at least one good laugh. I can see him dressed in his simple manner sitting behind his desk. Then when he was ready to explain some point he would rise and sit on the desk with one leg wrapped around the table leg and then we knew we were about to receive something worth while. He tried to teach us to laugh without making a sound lest we disturb other classes. I can see him shaking all over like a bowl of jelly and yet not a sound did he make."

A student who lived at Maywood recalls that one time an educator came to see father. That day the student was helping father to haul barn litter out on the farming lot. Father made no apology for his situation but in overalls sat down and talked with the distinguished visitor in terms too technical for the student to comprehend. 

Another recalls that one Sunday morning in church father inquired about his work. "Are you getting hold of your work?" he asked. "Yes," was the reply. "Is your work getting hold of you?" was the next question.

The student puzzled over that one; it was something to ponder.

CHAPTER VII  

The task for succeeding chapters is to lift up some of the main points in father's teaching and the insights which he shared with his students. Effort will be made also to set forth his methods of Bible study and teaching, with the hope that those who were with him in the classroom may get a new impetus to study and those who were never privileged to search the Scriptures with him may get some help and guidance from his teaching procedure.

His faith was grounded in history. I do not know whether he made public statements to this effect or not, but he told me privately that he was confronted with problems and doubts which might well have shaken his faith had it not been for the grounding in historical facts. 

This statement was made near the end of his life, within the last year, I think. It represented his mature judgment. To him, the Bible recorded a history that bore unmistakable marks of God. This history was his anchorage, and its course was to a considerable extent his guide in understanding the way in which God works with men.

He was no longer teaching purely historical courses when I came on the school scene; he had committed them to others. His regard for the historical rootage appeared, however, in his course in Biblical Archaeology. I went through that twice with him, the second time near the end of his teaching career, in order to be sure to get the full contribution he had to make in this field. 

He opened this course with the statement that there is

something very remarkable about the providence of God. 

Things seem to open up according to need. People through the centuries generally believed in the trustworthiness of the Bible account. They did this without outside evidence. All the time there were waiting in 

Bible lands numerous monuments and records. These were covered else they would have been totemized and ruined.

During the last century the trustworthiness of the Bible came into question in many areas. When evidence was needed to substantiate faith the explorer with his spade dug up the hidden things in Bible lands. There they were as Bible contemporaries left them, mute witnesses of peoples that were actors on the pages of the Book. 

Father felt that there was no more striking evidence of providence than this.

Since archaeology is the study of the remains of ancient peoples, Biblical archaeology is the study of Bible peoples and events through the things that can be found in Bible lands from ancient times. Father conceived the value of Biblical archaeology to be fourfold: (1) It gives contemporary evidence to the Bible. (2) It visualizes the nations featured in the Bible. (3) It illustrates and vivifies many scriptural texts. (4) It enables us to get a more definite and correct Biblical chronology. 

Much digging has been done in Bible lands just to get evidence to confirm people's convictions about the Bible. 

Folks who have their minds already made up do not make first-rate interpreters of evidence. Father's statement of purpose is therefore significant. He found confirmation of the Bible in the stones, sherds and rubbish heaps of the ancient Near East, but he sought more than this. 

He sought new light on the Bible and its meaning. He

expected the findings to be not only evidential but also creative. And he found that for which he was looking. 

The Genesis account of the origin of the world and man stands out more clearly on the background of other people's ideas about origins. One of father's striking sayings was, "Our cosmogony [i.e., view of the origin of things] has a God to start with, while others have him hatch out." Polytheistic as most of the origin stories of others are, he nevertheless found in them "an underlying hazy atmosphere of monotheism" which he counted to be due to the "great underlying fact of creation." 

It is not possible to go into details of the course in Biblical Archaeology, but it surveyed things that had bearing upon Old Testament events, peoples and practices. It also covered the geography of certain Biblical localities and the customs of the people. All this helped much to clarify the history of the Hebrews and illuminate their way of living. It broadened the basis for faith and gave perspective for understanding the religion in the Bible.

The inner meanings of history as it conditioned people in their growing religious experience will be considered in the next chaper. The remainder of this chapter will explore the connection that father saw between the prophets and history.

The Hebrew prophets peered into the realm of the Spirit and saw things that were beyond the view of others. They listened to the voice of God and gave utterance to what they heard. Their heads were in the heavens, but they had their feet on the earth. Their insights came as they faced human needs. Their messages were addressed to the people of their day in terms meaningful to their hearers. They did not hesitate to

tackle the knottiest problems of their times in terms of what they conceived to be the will of God. 

Each prophet had his setting in a historical situation. 

He must be interpreted in terms of that situation. His message might indeed reach far beyond his times, but its fulfillment in any given time was conditioned on the response that people made to the message. When he threatened the judgment of God upon sins, his predictions failed if people repented-and he was glad to have them fail. If he promised blessings, those blessings were realized only by the people's acceptance of the provisions upon which the blessings were based. The failure of any one generation or people did not, however, invalidate the purposes of God. God's purposes only tarried for response. The fulness of time was largely a fulness of response to the revealed will of God. God's calendar was a moral one. Such vital connection between history and prophecy was basic in father's handling of the teachings of the prophets. He had little patience with those who work out elaborate dispensational schemes and charts. His philosophy of history was of a deeper sort. 

He did not often risk taking classes directly into the prophetic books. The vivid imagery of these books yields itself too readily to misinterpretation. He began his prophets' classes back in the books of history and studied the rise of prophetism there. When once the function of the prophet for his times and his habitual ways of working were established father was ready to proceed to the books of the prophets. 

We shall follow the development of prophetism in the books of law and history. The first occurrence of the term prophet in the Bible is in Genesis 20:7, where 

Abraham is called a prophet. The point there is that his

being a prophet made him one who could pray effectively. 

The Exodus account of the relationship between Moses and Aaron is enlightening with respect to prophetism. 

Aaron was to be Moses' spokesman, in other words, his prophet (Exodus 4:14-16; 7:1-2). Miriam, the sister of 

Moses, is called a prophetess (Exodus 15:20-21). Moses himself became the first great prophet, though at the beginning he felt he could not speak. God had face-to-face intimacy with him (Deuteronomy 34:10-12). He became the type of the prophetic line and of the greater Prophet to come (Deuteronomy 18:15-22). In verse 18 of this reference the function of the prophet is clearly stated. 

He is a spokesman for God, and he speaks with authority. 

Compare Acts 3:22-23; 7:37. 

The line of prophets really began with Samuel. He was famed for his insight. It was insight that gave the early prophets their reputation as men of God (1 Samuel 

9:6-9). Samuel exercised his gift in behalf of the people.

He anointed kings and pronounced judgment freely upon 

King Saul (1 Samuel 10:1-8; 16: 1-13; 13: 13-15; 15: 10-34).

He was at the head of a band of prophets (1 Samuel 10: 

5-6; 19:20-24). These men were under the power of the

Spirit, but sometimes their conduct approached that of frenzied dervishes. Through the leadership of Samuel the schools of the prophets were given sane and wholesome direction.

Nathan and Gad spoke the will of God to David (2 

Samuel 7:1-17; 12:1-15; 1 Kings 1:1-31). They did not fear to reprove the king for his sins. Other prophets are mentioned in the times of succeeding kings, but Elijah and Elisha were the next outstanding ones. Elijah spoke for God in a few bold appearances in the time of Ahab and Jezebel (1 Kings 17-21). Elisha followed with a

long ministry of constructive guidance in leading Israel out of Baal worship (1 Kings 19:19-21; 2 Kings 1-8). 

This historical survey, together with an analysis of the Hebrew terms used for the prophets-one referring to their seeing ability, the other to their speaking function -gave father the necessary background for the study of the books of the prophets with his classes. These books he then followed through in historical order. Each prophet served his own people in his own day. His mission had an outreach into the future, but the message with all of its implications grew out of principles seen to be operating in the prophet's times. The prophets through the Spirit of God could see those principles and much of their meaning. Father believed that we through the same Spirit can discover enough of these principles by sound historical and exegetical study to see clearly the direction of God's revelation and avoid the bypaths of theoretical interpretation of prophecy. 

Manifestly, we cannot here go through the books of the prophets with father. Perhaps we might look at a sample briefly. We'll take the book of the prophet whom he admired to the extent of naming a son after him-- 

Amos. 

Father used the text of the Bible books as it is in the American Standard Version. The custom of many scholars to lift out certain elements in the prophetic books as later accretions did not seem to bother him. He believed in giving the books a chance to speak for themselves as they stand, not as cut up by critics. So when he came to the Book of Amos he first explored the historical indications as to time, period, and the situation of the author and then dug into the chapters. 

Amos 1:1 gives the historical setting of the book;

chapter 7:10-17 the information concerning the prophet. 

Amos was a rustic herdsman from the rough country between Bethlehem and the Dead Sea. He disclaimed connection with the professional prophets of his day. 

God gave him his message and there was no choice but to speak it. With the keenest of insight and the finest of ability in the use of language, this unschooled prophet became the great champion in Israel of the ethical demands of God.

Amos came as it were as the voice of God roaring like a lion from the mountain of God (1:2). With the power of a master he pointed to one after another of Israel's neighbors, showing that God held them responsible according to the light they had (chapters 1-3). This of course must have gotten strong Amens from his hearers, even when the southern kingdom of Judah was shown to be responsible for its greater light in the law of the 

Lord (2:4-5). But the prophet was aiming at Israel, in whose royal sanctuary he was speaking. All the other pronouncements were but the gathering of momentum to drive home the message of Israel's responsibility (2:63:15). The heartlessness and injustice shown by Israel's ruling class were bringing the nation fast toward destruction under the righteous judgment of God (chapters 4-5). 

Their formal religious practices were of no avail; only thoroughgoing repentance offered hope (5:4ff.; 21-27).

Counting on their ceremonies to bring them God's favor, they were expecting the day of his coming to be one of blessing for them, but as things stood it could be only a time of judgment and destruction (4:12-13; 5:18-20).

Captivity in the East was the calamity that threatened the nation (chapter 6).

The later chapters of the book deal with various as-

pects of the threatened judgment. There was a hopeful side, however. Though the prophet seems not to have expected the nation to repent, he believed thoroughly in the eventual fulfillment of God's hopes for Israel. This was to be realized through a righteous remnant that would be saved out of captivity (9:7-15). Here the prophet reached far ahead into the purposes of God that awaited fulfillment in the gospel age. God is himself righteous and will be satisfied with nothing short of righteousness in his people. There is no favoritism in him that will accept worship on any basis other than sound ethical attitudes. Men in the mass fall before these searching demands, but a few respond and with them lies the future. The power of the God who rules the world will bring it to pass.

Here is a firm foundation for ethical religion, coupled with the faith to hold on to the high purposes of God. 

No wonder father cherished Amos! The historical rootage of the prophets and the permanent validity of their messages here find typical expression. 

There is wisdom in this sort of historical approach to the prophets.

CHAPTER VIII  

Father's Bible study and teaching fell into three categories: The first was exegesis, which for him was the taking of Scripture in units of a book or a group of books, and drawing out the meaning by direct and careful study. 

The second was doctrinal study, which traced topically an idea, a group of ideas or practices through the Bible. 

The character study in his Character of Christ course might possibly be included in the second category, but it was so distinctive that we should perhaps make a third for it.

In all of these types of study he used the inductive method. He was an original and creative scholar and had full right to pass on to others the results of his own study. But he seldom did so. He preferred rather to lead others over the path of discovery that he had followed so that their conclusions might be their own instead of his.

We shall take his doctrinal studies first. This order does a measure of violence to his own method, for he insisted that students study a number of books exegetically before taking up Bible doctrines. Nevertheless, the doctrinal study brings to the fore the principle of progression in revelation which we need in order to understand his procedure. It also shows how he looked to the Book for the direction of his thinking and the answers to his problems. He wanted his doctrine directly out of the Bible. We shall endeavor to see how he got it. 

His doctrinal courses included at various times the general study of Bible Doctrines which was essentially

the tracing of redemption through the Bible, and specific courses like Doctrine of the Holy Spirit, Doctrine of 

Prayer, and Doctrine of the Prophets. The last three will have attention in later connections; the first will give us grist for this chapter. 

Father regarded the primary theme of the Bible to be redemption. His year-long Bible Doctrines course dealt with the historical unfolding of the plan of salvation. 

The natural Biblical divisions into which the study resolved itself were the three dispensations: (1) patriarchal, (2) Mosaic, and (3) gospel. He devoted about four weeks to the patriarchal age, four months to the Mosaic, and three to the gospel. The prolonged attention to the second division was due to the amount of material to be covered and the necessity of thorough grounding in the Old Testament for the understanding of the New. 

As to relative importance he was in the habit of saying that the patriarchal age was the starlight of revelation; the Mosaic, the moonlight; and the gospel, the full glory of sunlight. 

The Scripture material on the patriarchal age consists of Genesis and scattered references throughout the Bible. The Bible begins with God and the world that 

God made. Genesis 1 introduces the power of God's word. He spoke and the world came into being. Creation was an unfolding, step by step, at the word of God. Man was its climax, made in the image of God. 

This image was spiritual and intellectual rather than physical. The physical characteristics attributed to God in the Bible represent man's comprehension of God in terms of his own make-up, and are an accommodation to man's limited understanding and appreciation. God

is spirit. Man is like God and thus different from the animals.

There were obligations laid upon man in the beginning. He was to be fruitful, to administer the created world for human welfare and to obey God's instructions. 

He had opportunity for unlimited growth and progress. 

He had the power of choice whereby he could obey God's will and make wise use of his opportunities. But this power carried with it the ability to sin. Man took the wrong road and that is what brought trouble. 

Sin at its source was disobedience to God. Its results were death, the loss of the highest opportunities, and the suffering of untold toil and trouble. Sin multiplied. 

Besides disregard for God there were soon murder, defiance, idolatry, boasting, evil imaginations, deception, greed, theft, strife and immorality. But God did not forsake man; he put a thread of hope into the very first pronouncement of judgment (Genesis 3:16). He gave opportunity for a new start time after time—a new son (4:25), a new beginning for the race (chapters 6-9), a new family (12:1-3). The flood is to be counted a work of grace more than a measure of judgment. 

Throughout the two and a half millenniums or more of time that Genesis covers, the revelation of God's will was fragmentary yet full enough to bring responsibility. 

Man had a sense of good and evil. There was elemental communication of God's will. People had nature's laws and teachings. Paul indicates that this revelation through nature alone should have led men to recognize 

God's sovereignty and glory (Romans 1:18-32). In refusing to heed its message they were rejecting God. 

There were also other means of revelation for those who were responsive. Providences of God such as the flood

were significant. The lives of godly men condemned unworthy conduct. The patriarchs had fellowship with 

God. There were intuitive revelations, visions, dreams, and angel ministry. All the vision and dream texts father led his classes to examine with care. In Genesis visions and dreams were valued as bringing God's will to men and sometimes bringing warning. Angel appearances were also carefully scrutinized. These were sometimes visions and dreams; sometimes, father believed, actual appearances. In patriarchal days the messages of angels to men brought guidance, comfort, protection, warning and judgment. Angels were the agents of God's presence. There are a number of instances where God is said to have appeared and spoken to the patriarchs. In the light of John 1:18 father felt sure that these were either intuitive experiences or angel appearances.

Prayer functioned in the patriarchal age, sometimes associated with altars and offerings. There were prayers of petition, intercession, blessing, thanksgiving. Four

Hebrew terms are used in connection with offerings: The altar was a slaughtering place; to offer was to slaughter and to cause to go up; the offering was a present. The offerings were usually animals. Offerings expressed gratitude, consecration, and a general devotional attitude. 

They were spontaneous expressions of the heart toward God. Expiation for sin seems not to have been a factor in them.

A covenant was made with Noah but it was largely negative (9:8-19). The Abrahamic call and covenant really started positively the plan of redemption on its long way. Abraham was a pioneer in faith. God called him, blessed him, and promised to make him a blessing to

all the earth (12:1-3). Through trying experiences Abraham learned faith, the basic human response necessary for laying hold of the salvation that God offered. became the father of a family and of the faithful. The family increased in significance until it brought forth the Christ. The faithful continue to increase in the gospel age (Romans 4:9-25; Galatians 3:6-29). 

Father was wont to illustrate the covenants by two lines diverging from a point and crossed midway by a bar, like a horizontal A. The point marked the beginning, the covenant with a family. The bar represented the Mosaic covenant with Israel, now increased to a nation. The open enlargement represented the new covenant, in which all the families of the earth are blessed and the covenant with Abraham finds full realization. 

The procedure in class on the patriarchal age was discussion, a syllabus of texts, and the writing of papers on the original condition of man, sin, the means of grace, theophanies, prayer and offerings.

On the Mosaic dispensation, the Scripture is all the Old Testament after Genesis. The study of this age began with the exodus of Israel from Egypt and the journey to Canaan. In the exodus from Egypt God had a whole people to educate in faith. It was an ignorant and ill-trained people that came out of Egypt. Faithprovoking providences multiplied in Egypt and in the wilderness. There was much murmuring and failure.

A new generation had to be raised up. But finally the 

Promised Land was reached and entered by a people that had belief in God, a law to guide them and a ceremonial system to school them in the principles of salvation and bring them into the experience of it. The exodus experience became a testimony to the vitality of

God's promise to Abraham and an evidence of God's mercy to Israel. It was an experience to build on through many generations. Prophets long used it as an appeal to faithfulness.

Those means of revelation found in the patriarchal age continued in the Mosaic dispensation with a growing enrichment of meaning. The new insights into the meaning of nature such as appear in Psalms 8, 19 and 104 are a good example. The further development of visions and of angel ministry are other instances. A very significant development is the increased access of the Spirit of God to the human heart for revelation, strength and guidance. But the two outstanding contributions of the 

Mosaic dispensation are the law and the prophets. 

The law was from God. The graphic description of the writing of the Decalogue on tablets of stone by the finger of God is a pictorial way of indicating that the law had God's authority. Two New Testament references say that it was given through angels (Acts 7:38; 

Hebrews 2:2). The human agent was Moses, trained in the learning of the world in the court of Egypt and schooled in the school of the Lord in the wilderness and on the mountain. There are three phases of the 

Mosaic law: (1) the Decalogue; (2) detailed applications; and (3) the ceremonial system. 

The commandments functioned in regulating life and developing conscience. See Paul's evaluation in Romans 

7:7-8; Galatians 3:23-25. The commandments were mostly negative. Great principles underlie them. Take the Decalogue for instance. The first commandment is based on the conviction that monotheism is to be the universal religion. The second, that worship is to be spiritual. The third, that attitude toward God and truth

be genuine and sincere. The fourth, that one day in seven be set apart for God. The fifth, that respect prevail in the home. The sixth, that human life be counted sacred. The seventh, that marriage be held inviolate.

The eighth, that property rights be recognized. The ninth, that reputation be respected. The tenth, that covetousness be uprooted from the heart. The details of the law are an amplification and application of principles like these, mostly in prohibitions. 

The ceremonial is the positive side of the law. Exodus 

20:24 is the key verse here. The Hebrew word for altar means to draw near. The verse then means, "If you use proper means to draw near to me, I will draw near to you." This is the inner secret of the offerings. 

Father's procedure in this part of the study was an extensive survey of references in which important terms are used. The concordance publishers should have given him a subsidy. He worked the concordance hard and instilled into his pupils the practice of using the concordance in word study. He was satisfied with nothing short of the original languages. He covered every reference in which every significant word connected with the offerings was used, one hundred seventy-four of one, three hundred two of another, of others two hundred eighty-seven, one hundred eighty-two, two hundred eight-five, one hundred seventy-nine, eighty-five and seventy-six respectively. He spared his classes from going over all of these, but they covered a great many. The 

Englishman's Hebrew Concordance was his guide. Drawing near to God, consecration, expiation, restitution and thanksgiving were elements in the offering system that came out of the study. There followed a survey of the atonement references (one (one hundred thirty-five in-

stances), the forgiveness words and the several terms for grace and favor.

The strong new element that appeared in these offerings of the Mosaic age was the manner of dealing with sin. The substitutionary idea came to the fore. The blood of the slain animal and administering it in behalf of a person signified taking the life from the one and transferring it to the other whose life had been forfeited through sin. In the sin offering man recognized his sin, confessed it, brought it to the altar through his offering, and offered it up, thus obtaining the realization of pardon. The priest officiated as his representative before God.

Another phase of the ceremonial law was the system of regulations about the clean and unclean. Sanitary measures may have been involved to some extent, but the main function was disciplinary, developing conscience and the capacity for self-denial. 

The special days and feasts are also noteworthy. These fitted into both the offering system and the clean and unclean procedure. The sacrifice of the paschal lamb at the Passover carried significance which was to bear fruit in the New Testament.

It was in the Mosaic dispensation that prophetism developed and had its day in Israel. The historical development was noted in the previous chapter. As a means of revelation the prophet saw into the character and purposes of God and spoke God's message to the people. He was God's spokesman. He had visions, dreams and intuitive insight. Angels had place especially in the later apocalyptic forms of prophecy, as in Daniel. The prophets appeared in times of crisis and labored to get the people to follow God's will. They taught,

Only in the later prophets does the individualization begin which presaged the era of personal responsibility and salvation through Christ. This distinction, however, is not to be pressed too far, for religion was a very personal matter with the psalmists and many men of God of old times.

The Old Testament doctrine of salvation in all its phases reaches forward to Christ and finds its climax in him. With respect to revelation, John 1:18 and Hebrews 

1:1-2 are key scriptures. From eternity Christ was the

Word by whom God revealed himself. He became flesh that men in the flesh might truly know him. The revelation in Christ is superior to that of the Old Testament in that it is complete, it brought God to the human plane for vital union with men, it condemned sin and made victory possible, and it is thoroughly sympathetic with men's weaknesses and needs. The revelation in Christ reveals God as Trinity-Father, Son and Holy Spiritjust as the crystal reveals the spectrum. The manner of the revelation in Christ is manifold; it was through the incarnation, the teachings of Jesus, his daily living, his miracles, his sacrifice, his resurrection and exaltation, and his continued ministration through the Holy Spirit. 

The New Testament doctrine of salvation consists of two phases: justification and sanctification. The first has to do with getting clear of sin; the second, with growth in character and service. Father continued the word-study method here, considering the references in the Englishman's Greek Concordance to all forms of

the Greek root that is translated by just, righteous and their cognates in English. The root means, essentially, right or normal. God is the standard as seen in Christ. 

It is applied to Christ and to people who have been brought into the kingdom of God.

A group of four words having to do with reconciliation and atonement were next studied. These involve bringing about a change from enmity to friendship. The change is always on man's side. God effects it in us through the death of Christ. Through Christ men are changed from an attitude of enmity to one of friendship with God and receive the ministry of reconciling others to God. There is also a set of four redemption words that pick up the Old Testament physical, ceremonial and national redemption and carry it to a spiritual significance. Man is redeemed from the penalty and power of sin by God through the purchase price of the blood of Christ. The mercy, propitiation and pardon words feature God manifesting his love and mercy in Christ and through Christ's suffering, and thereby bringing release of man from sin and making the supreme appeal to draw man out of sin. The anger texts in the New Testament, as applied to God, carry less of anthropomorphism than in the Old Testament and more of the righteous indignation that is necessary to the strongest love. The study of the cross and death texts completed the survey of God's side of justification. By virtue of Christ's death on the cross man may have eternal salvation. The shedding of his blood sealed the new covenant, broke the power of death and the devil and brought forgiveness and life to man. It also constituted the greatest appeal to man to accept salvation and give self in service. 

Man's part in justification is faith and acceptance. Study

of the faith references shows a saving faith to be reliant trust in God through Christ, which carries through to the point of setting the mind on things above and committing oneself to him for salvation. It derives its power from the grace of God. It is an act of will in response to evidence brought to man through God's grace. It leads to full salvation. Besides faith, the elements in conversion are conviction, repentance and baptism. When these are properly realized, sin is rid out and the way is open for baptism by the Holy Spirit. 

Sanctification is the growth process in Christian living, the process of making holy. Sin reasserts itself and requires constant handling. It does not belong in the 

Christian life. The virtues of Christ do belong there in the measure that Christ had them. He is the standard. 

By his Spirit the new life is made possible, victory is gained over sin, and the adulthood of Christ's character is approached. The standard, however, is too high ever to be reached in this imperfect world. It always leads 

The Holy Spirit takes our hearts and writes the law of God upon them. It transforms life. Aids toward the holy life are Bible study, prayer, the ordinances of the church, suffering, persecution, and Christian service, fellowship and worship. Thus man is made truly a son of God in both nature and character. on.

This chapter has gotten long, but it covers a big field. 

The deduction is that we cannot speak of the Bible doctrine of salvation as one. It was a very different thing in the New Testament from what it had been in the Mosaic age or back in patriarchal times. Yet there was continuity. It was an unfolding process as revelation progressed and men became more capable of response. The doctrine of progressive revelation was not popular in

the church when father began his ministry. But he knew that here lay the truth, and under much misunderstanding he stood firmly for it. His courageous teaching was no small factor in the change in view that has come about in the church in the past forty years. I am grateful for his insistence upon progressive revelation and his direct method of lifting doctrine out of the Bible itself. His word studies sometimes became tedious, but when they were finished, what we had was Bible, not Hoff. And we had also the method of direct creative Bible study with which to carry on for ourselves.

CHAPTER IX  

Would you like to have father's method of mastering a Bible book? It was very simple. I can give it to you in a few minutes, and you can practice on it for a lifetime, as I am doing.

His method dealt with verse (or sentence) units, paragraph units, chapter units, book section units, and the entire books. He used the American Standard Version that is paragraphed. As a working basis he assumed that the paragraphs were properly made, sometimes, however, changing the paragraph units in the process of study.

His procedure was inductive. He used it himself and led his students over the same path. He began with the verse (or sentence) units, stating as briefly as possible what each in turn says. When the verses in a paragraph were thus analyzed, he looked the paragraph over to see what the main thing was that the paragraph meant to set forth, and what the steps were in setting it forth. 

He noted the subdivisions into which the paragraph thus divided itself and marked them in his Bible with red or blue pencil. He then formulated a proposition setting forth the main thought of the paragraph and relating properly to it the several contributing ideas that his verse analysis had disclosed. When he had thus dealt with the paragraphs in a chapter, he applied the main thought -subdivision-proposition method to the chapter unit. 

He continued this until all the chapters in the book were studied, and then looked at the whole book unit in similar fashion.

When this process was complete, he had an analysis and outline of the entire book down to every paragraph and verse, and it was one that had grown out of the book itself by the student's own thought. It gave a sense of mastery of the book that no commentaries or lectures could impart. One knew what the book was driving at and how it got there. There might be details that were puzzling, but major things could not help being clear and convincing.

I had an experience once that illustrated well the value of the results secured by this method. After having studied the Gospel of Matthew in Bethany with father, I took a Matthew course in the university under a nationally known teacher. One of the questions later in a written examination at the university for a graduate degree was to give without Bible or notes an analysis of the Gospel of Matthew. That assignment was the easiest in the whole examination; I could have written the entire period on it. The Gospel of Matthew was a part of me, and it was not the university lectures and reading that had made it so; it was the firsthand study of the book at Bethany.

Our task in the rest of this chapter is to illustrate this method as applied to the Gospel of Matthew. There ought to be a whole book for it but the few pages available may make clear the procedure.

The first half of Matthew 1 does not yield itself well to verse analysis. The result of such an analysis would be a genealogy not much different from the one we have in the Bible; that would not get us anywhere. We will take verses one to seventeen then by paragraph units. 

The first consists of verse one. A simple statement of it would be: Jesus traced by generations to David and

Abraham. Whether this is the heading of the book or of a brief section is not clear from the verse itself; it is a problem to pigeonhole for further evidence. Father found many such things. At any rate the statement sets out the genealogical interest.

Verses 2 to 6a give successive generations from Abraham to David; verses 6b to 11, from David to the captivity; verses 12 to 16, from the captivity to Jesus. 

Verse 17 is clearly a summary statement, bringing the genealogy to a conclusion. We might call it an analysis of the genealogy of Jesus. The analysis into sections of fourteen generations must be for convenience of use, as in memorization. Actually, a comparison with the Old 

Testament shows that three generations are omitted in the middle paragraph to make the scheme of fourteen work out. Ordinarily we would wait until the end of the chapter to summarize but verses 1 to 17 constitute a clearly defined unit. It seems that there ought to be a chapter division here. The meaning of verses 1 to 17 

may be stated in a proposition: The genealogy of Jesus is traced in three groups of fourteen generations each from Abraham to David, to the captivity, to Jesus. The theme is The Genealogy of Jesus. Interesting points to pigeonhole for further light are why it is Joseph's lineage that is traced and why the relationship of Jesus to him is stated in the peculiar way of verse 16. 

Now apply verse analysis to the rest of the chapter. 

Verse 18. Jesus was begotten of the Holy Spirit. 

Verse 19. Joseph misunderstood the situation. 

Verse 20. His perplexity is removed by an angel.

Verse 21. The Son born is to be named Jesus. 

Verse 22. The peculiar birth of Jesus is in fulfillment of prophecy.

Verse 23. The prophet Isaiah predicted the birth of a son from a virgin.

Verses 24-25. Joseph accepts the situation and co-operates with the plan.

The paragraph falls into these subdivisions: (1) Verse 

18a. (2) Verse 18b. (3) Verse 19. (4) Verses 20 to 23. (5) Verses 24 and 25. Now what is the important thing in this paragraph? Clearly the fact that the child of 

Mary was begotten by the Holy Spirit and destined to be the Savior of his people. The perplexity of Joseph and his final acceptance emphasize the main point. The quotation from the prophet connects the event with God's long purpose. A proposition covering the paragraph might then be: By an angel's message Joseph was brought to understand that Mary's child was begotten by the Holy Spirit and destined to be the long-promised Savior of his people from their sins. A theme would be: 

Jesus Begotten by the Holy Spirit. Incidentally, there is light here on the problem we noted in verse 16. The 

Bible thus illuminates itself.

There is not space to continue this analysis through many chapters. Anyone who wishes can continue it privately to his own profit. If his efforts seem crude at first, he need not be discouraged, for efficiency increases with practice. It is not a procedure to be followed once and for all. Every thoughtful approach to the chapters will bring new insights. But none at all will come until a person applies himself to study. 

Suppose we make our next effort together on chapter five. This is the beginning of the Sermon on the Mount. 

The verse analysis might be something like this: 

Verses 1-2. Jesus sets himself to teach upon a mountain.

Verse 3. He pronounces the blessing of the kingdom of heaven upon the poor in spirit. 

Verse 4. Of comfort, upon them that mourn. 

Verse 5. Of possession of the earth, to the meek.

Of satisfaction, to those who desire right- 

eousness.

Of mercy, to the merciful. 

Verse 8. Of seeing God, to the pure in heart.

Verse 9. Of sonship of God, to peacemakers. 

Verses 10 to 12. Of joy in the rewards of the kingdom of heaven, to those persecuted for righteousness' sake. 

Note that we have run past the paragraph break between verse 9 and verse 10. We have done this because the distinctive beatitude form in verses 3 to 9 carries over into verse 10. It is true that the beatitude of verse 

10 is amplified in verses 11 and 12 as the earlier ones are not. But it still seems better to count verses 1 to 

12 as a single paragraph. Verses 1 and 2 constitute the setting for the whole sermon. Each beatitude is a unit. 

Note the form of these beatitudes. Each falls into three parts: (1) the pronouncement of blessing; (2) the kind of folks blessed; and (3) the explanation as to what the blessing consists of. The people are described not by class or position but by character, conduct or attitude. 

The blessing fits in each case the character or deeds of the person. These verses really set forth, then, the qualities of character and life upon which Jesus set value, and the blessings consequent upon such. There is an element of progression in them. The last named does not come first. The whole process begins with the humility of the first beatitude and leads to the crowning achievement in the last. But of course we do not finish

with one when we come to the place where we can enter into others. The Christian life is a growth in character and an unfolding of blessings. 

A proposition for verses 1 to 12 might be: Jesus in beginning the Sermon on the Mount set forth in the form of beatitudes the kinds of character and action upon which the blessings of the kingdom attend. A theme might be: The Kinds of Folks Who Receive the King dom's Blessings.

We turn now to an analysis of verses 13 to 16: 

Verse 13. Jesus tells his followers to be like salt in the earth. 

Verse 14. They are like light, or a city on a hill. 

Verse 15. Light must shine to fulfill its purpose. 

Verse 16. The follower of Christ must live so as to honor God before men.

This sermon has been called the constitution of the kingdom. It crystallizes what Jesus had in mind for the sons of the kingdom.

Matthew indeed is the book of the kingdom. When you have gone through the book, you will find that chapters 1 to 4 are a kind of introduction; they get Jesus on the scene, prepared, and the preaching of the kingdom started. Further teachings and some narratives lead to the sifting parables in chapters 21 and 22, the scathing rebuke of the scribes and Pharisees in chapter 23 and the discourse on the last things in chapters 24 and 25. The remaining three chapters deal with his betrayal, trial, crucifixion and resurrection.

The framework of this gospel thus consists of five rather extensive discourse sections, most of them compact but the last somewhat broken and not so closely knit as the others. These discourses have an introduction and a conclusion of narrative and are separated by narrative materials. It is a great book. It sets forth the teachings of Jesus as no other book does, and these are the most important teachings to be found anywhere. The book makes the transition from the old dispensation to the new. It sustains something of the basic relation to the Christian gospel that the law does to the Old Testament. The book will reward a careful study by the procedure that father used.

CHAPTER X  

When the Bible says of Jesus, "He called unto him the twelve, and began to send them forth by two and two," there is not much likelihood that the statement will be misunderstood. It is a factual statement. Someone might raise a question whether it is a fact or not, but none would question what it meant to say. Again, when Jesus says, "All things therefore whatsoever ye would that men should do unto you, even so do ye also unto them," there is not much doubt about what he says. 

This is a direct teaching statement. Sects do not spring out of differences in understanding about what the Golden Rule says. Differences may arise about its implications for modern life, but hardly about what it says. 

But the Bible is not made up wholly of factual statements and direct teachings. There are many figures of speech in it. There are similes, metaphors, antitheses, hyperboles, fables, parables, allegories, examples, events used as illustrations, incidents with teaching implications, and other figures. Someone has estimated that fully ninety per cent of Christ's teaching is couched in figures of speech. He made the world of nature and everyday living fairly bristle with spiritual meaning. 

The last book of the New Testament is a picture book from beginning to end. The prophets used a great many figures. It is in the realm of figurative language that differences of opinion are most likely to come. It would be helpful indeed, if people could get sound principles by which to interpret parables and other figures in the Bible. Father was much concerned about this problem

and was pretty sure he had the key to its solution. This key was context.

Not in the regular curriculum but occasionally in summer school he taught a course in the Parables of Jesus. 

In it all the figures of speech in the gospels were listed and classified, and careful study was made of each of the parables. This was one of the richest courses he taught. 

The present chapter will be devoted to his principles of interpretation of parables, with some examples. The study should bring clearly to attention the importance he set upon context in Bible interpretation. 

A parable is an extended simile. Christ was the master parable user. There are only a couple of parables in the Old Testament, none in the Apocrypha and none in the New Testament epistles. Christ had truth in mind and taught it by laying down beside it a story that had some point of resemblance. The story is not likely to apply in all respects. The truth that Christ taught was too profound to be completely embodied in any illustration. But there is some point at which it is enlightening. 

In identifying figurative statements, father's principle was always to take the literal interpretation if it fits, i. e., if the context will allow it. If not, take it figuratively and set about in an orderly fashion to get at its meaning. 

His principles of interpreting parables were these: (1) 

Determine the bounds of the parable proper. We must know exactly where the figurative part begins and where it ends. (2) Then notice carefully what immediately precedes and what follows. Usually an occasion precedes and an application follows. These may give definite clue to the interest in the parable. There is often an abstract statement in the introduction or application

that is a key to the parable. (3) Each parable except one is a unit of thought and must be interpreted only in so far as this unity indicates. When there is a clue in the context, the point of the parable becomes self-evident. 

To load the parable with meanings beyond this point is to strain it. Any parable will break down and become incongruous if so strained. (4) When there is no clue in setting or application, see whether there is not some line of thought running through the parable or some focal point at which the parable centers. If so, this will be the key to interpretation. At any rate the parable must be interpreted in the light of the larger Bible content and in harmony with it. (5) Each parable was addressed to a specific audience but most of them carry principles that are far-reaching and capable of wide application.

The parable in the conclusion of the Sermon on the Mount is a good one with which to begin the practice of parable interpretation (Matthew 7:24-27). There are really two parables here, but since they are parallel and vital parts of the same unit of thought, we shall consider them as one. The first part begins with the word likened in verse 24 and goes to the end of verse 25. The second begins with likened in verse 26 and goes to the end of verse 27. Now, looking at the frame around the parable in the immediate context, an abstract statement is found in verse 24a for the first part and 26a for the second. These statements show who and what the parable is about. It is to characterize one who hears and acts on 

Jesus' words and one who only hears. Verse 24a differs from verse 26a in that the one does and the other does not do what he hears from Jesus. The one part of the parable differs from the other in the presence or absence

of the rock foundation and in the final end. What comes of a person doing or not doing Jesus' teachings resembles what comes of a house having or not having a rock foundation. The house stands or falls according to whether it has a rock foundation; the man, according to whether he puts Christ's teachings into action. That is simple, isn't it?

The third of these parables comes very near being an allegory and may be so used. But as a parable it supplements the other two in making a single strong appeal for a proper attitude toward the lost. 

Suppose we take next a parable that has no interpretation accompanying it, Mark 4:26-29 for instance. The parable proper begins as if a man in verse 26 and runs to the end of verse 29. There is an introduction, And he said, So is the kingdom of God (verse 26a). Jesus spoke the parable and it was to characterize the kingdom; that much and that much only we are told. When father came to such a parable he observed three points: (1) We must not give the parable any interpretation which conflicts with other Bible teachings. In other words, observe the larger Bible context. (2) The immediate context is usually a reliable guide. (3) Examine the picture to see whether it focalizes on any particular interest. 

In immediate context, this is another kingdom parable following the parable of the sower. Examine the parable picture. The interest centers in growth. The growth is gradual and independent of the man who sowed the seed. There is something in the earth that seems to bring growth and fruitage spontaneously. It is mysterious but effective. The grain comes to full ma-

turity without the man having done anything to it since sowing.

Growth in the kingdom must be like that. Perhaps in the context this explains the fruitage of the word in the heart. There is power present that is beyond man. 

We do not know how the growth takes place; we do not need to know. But we can expect a harvest. Courage then, and faith and hope!

Perhaps the parables of the mustard seed and the leaven will offer as good examples of immediate and remote context as we can find (Matthew 13:31-33). The mustard seed parable begins a grain of mustard seed (verse 31). It is a kingdom parable like the one we have just studied (verse 31a). In Matthew it follows the parable of the tares and in Mark the parable of growth that we have just interpreted. Remarkable growth is its focal point. Father pointed out that it is not said that the kingdom is like a grain of mustard seed. Rather, the kingdom is like a grain of mustard seed that grew to be nearly a tree. The smallest of garden seeds makes a bush large enough for birds to lodge in. Though the kingdom seems insignificant in its beginnings, much can be expected of it. This is a faith-building parable. Father found no clue whatsoever in the context that would justify identifying the birds with evil and giving a pessimistic color to the parable. The birds merely emphasize the size of the bush. 

The parable of the leaven follows, with the same sort of introduction and the same context. The picture is that of an inner transformation as the preceding one is of outward growth. There is present a permeating and changing power quite out of proportion to the relative size of the active agent and the mass affected. Father

examined with his class the claims of those who count leaven to be always an evil element. Taking a concordance and noting every instance in the Bible we failed to discover any such universal usage. Figures have wide difference of usage according to context. Sometimes it is the devil who is spoken of as a lion (roaring after its prey); sometimes it is Christ who is the lion (of the tribe of Judah). The context settles the usage. So it is here. It is the permeating quality of leaven that is in mind; there is no reason to drag in any other implication that is not in the context.

We should have space yet for interpreting one of the more difficult parables such as that of the shrewd steward in Luke 16:1-13. It must suffice, however, to call to attention the fact that father's principle of a single focal point will solve the problem in parables like this. 

He was accustomed to say that parables are not made to walk on all fours. They break down if pressed beyond the intended point of meaning. In this particular parable the unethical element is not the point at all; it merely emphasizes the steward's shrewdness. Christians ought to have that much shrewdness in the administration of their financial resources for eternal ends. 

Context, both immediate and general, furnishes the necessary guiding element in interpreting parables. We shall find in a later chapter that it is equally necessary in interpreting prophecy. It is useful in all Bible study.

CHAPTER XI THE BIBLE SPEAKING FOR ITSELF 

Father was eager to have light break forth from the Bible. When it did so he had little to say; he was ready to act. He accepted what he found there with a sincerity and faith that proved contagious. His students caught his spirit.

He believed in giving the Book an opportunity to speak for itself, counting this more important than anything that anybody could say about it. And he believed that the Bible was its own best commentary. These two factors determined his procedure and the tone of his expectation. 

He spent the major part of his study time upon the Bible itself, not upon commentaries. At times he seemed to disparage commentaries. I had an experience with the Gospel of John that called definitely to my attention his method of study and teaching. After having studied

Perhaps as good a book as any by which to illustrate this element is the Gospel of John. John is a gospel of light but it is not an easy book. The purpose of our study of the character of Christ, as father stated it, was (1) from the human side, to see what our lives should be, and (2) from the divine side, to comprehend the fact that through the divine Christ our sins are pardoned, and (3) to make possible the growth of character by appreciation and experience of the power given through the Holy Spirit. 

If you open a harmony of the four gospels you will find it beginning with the Prologue to John. That is where the Character of Christ survey of the four gospels began. As pertains to Christ the Prologue sets him forth

as the Word or expression of God, the climax of all revelation. He is the source of the light of life for every man. Light goes in every direction except as it is hindered; so the light of Christ. The pre-existence of Christ is also taught in the Prologue. 

The material in Luke 1 stands next. Significant points here are: (1) Christ was begotten of the Holy Spirit; he had human form but a divine heritage that we don't have; (2) he was expected to be a great king, everlasting and universal. In the narrative of Christ's birth in 

Matthew 1, the two names applied to Jesus were noted. 

Jesus means Savior. Immanuel means God with us. 

Luke's account of the boyhood of Jesus has character value. The boy Jesus grew naturally. He made symmetrical development physically, mentally, spiritually and socially. The visit to Jerusalem is a sample out of his boyhood. The fact that Mary and Joseph left him on his own indicates his characteristic dependability. 

But here came a clash between interests. He took his first recorded step from the home bounds toward special service in the Father's work. The larger field opened to him and he put the spiritual first. Even as a child, he made first things first. The poise of Jesus is also apparent in this childhood incident. He was already having inspirational experiences at twelve years of age. These elements ought to enter into the experience of every twelve-year-old. Jesus was outstanding, but he set the normal for boys to strive for. It is striking that Jesus received his knowledge of God and spiritual things by learning and by intuition, not by visions or dreams. 

Spiritual intuition is the highest type of revelation; it is the one which we have today. Jesus' patience and his obedience to parents are evident in his return to his home

for eighteen years when he already saw much to do in the cause that was on his heart. 

The goodness of Jesus was recognized by John the Baptist even before he recognized Jesus' Messiahship. 

In the baptism, humility and devotion to God's will are the striking character elements. The endowment with the Holy Spirit must be noted to understand the life of Jesus. He was Spirit-filled. The Spirit came into a prayerful soul. He was recognized as the beloved Son of God.

His submissiveness to Spirit guidance appears in the temptation incident. Jesus knew the Scriptures well enough to have on his tongue's end just the passage he needed as occasion arose. He met the enemy with tact and skill. He didn't choose great Messianic texts although the temptations were in the field of his Messianic mission. He aligned himself rather with humanity. 

He humbly resigned to human limitations and kept his devotion to God. He would accept sovereignty on no basis short of service. He chose the plan of God as his own, and the program of God as his life program. No lesser good would suffice for him. 

It would be interesting and perhaps enlightening to go on with this inductive character study, but enough has probably been given to show the method and spirit of it. The remainder of this chapter should probably be reserved for a synthesis of all this such as father expected of his students in their final paper in the course. 

The first section, if father's interests are followed, is the divine characteristics. It is not likely that any student got through this study without a deep impression of the breadth and scope of teaching in the gospels that Jesus was divine. He was the eternal Word of God

(John 1:1). He was to be a great king (Luke 1:32), Savior (Matthew 1:21), and light giver to all (John 1: 

4-11). He was Son of God, begotten of the Holy Spirit (Matthew 1:20). John the Baptist testified that he came from above (John 3:31). He received recognition from heaven at baptism (Matthew 3:17), at the transfiguration (Matthew 17:1-13), and at the end of his ministry (John 12:28). He made claims for himself such as none but God could make. References in point are John 1:51; 

Matthew 11:27; 10:40; John 6:45-51; 7:16, 28-29; 8:12, 14, 16, 18, 23; Luke 10:22; John 10:1-21, 30; Luke 12: 8-9; 

Matthew 22: 41-45; John 11:4, 25; Luke 16:19-31; John

Matthew 9:18; 14:33; John 9:38; Luke 17:16; Matthew 21: 

15-16; 26:6-13. He was recognized as divine by demons (Mark 5:7). His dignity overawed the soldiers (John

But just as surely as the gospels present a divine Christ, so they present a human Jesus, a man of perfect character, the norm for humanity. He was born of a woman in humble circumstances (Luke 2:6-7). He made a well-rounded growth in childhood and youth (Luke 

2:40, 46-48, 52. He was obedient to parents (Luke 2:51). 

Like other people he became tired (John 4:6), hungry

(Luke 4:2; Matthew 21:18), was tempted (Luke 4:1-13), was limited in certain respects (Matthew 8:10), was disappointed (Matthew 11: 13; 17:17), may not always have been at his best (Mark 7:31-37), was troubled under suffering (John 12:27), felt the need of human sympathy (Mark 14:33ff.), shrank from death (Matthew 23: 36-46), and was tempted to despair (Matthew 27:46).

The most striking characteristic was his poise in those contrasting qualities the undue prominence or deficiency of any one of which would have made an unbalanced character. He was humble, yet maintained dignity. Humility was in evidence at his baptism (Matthew 3:15-16), in his choices in the temptation (Luke 4:1-13), and in the unassuming manner of his ministry (Matthew 12: 

19). He was meek and lowly in heart (Matthew 11:

He taught humility (Luke 14:1-11, 25-35; 18:9-14), accepted financial assistance (Luke 8:3), rode an ass in the triumphal entry (Matthew 21:5), and did a servant's task in washing his disciples' feet (John 13:1-20). Yet with dignity he commanded a following, was victorious over all temptation, and stood up in the face of religious and civil leaders who opposed him. 

Frankness, candor, sincerity and genuineness were manifest throughout his life, yet he used tact in his work. 

His frankness is apparent with Nathanael (John 1:47-50), with the scribes (Mark 2:5-7), with John's disciples (Matthew 11:6), with his own disciples (Matthew 16: 

13-16, 23), with the Pharisees (Luke 14:1-24), with Zacchaeus (Luke 19:9-10), and in regard to acceptance of him (John 7:17). He did not hesitate to put his teaching beside the law (Matthew 5:21-48). His tact was evident

in meeting Satan (Luke 4:

(John 3:1-21), in teaching the Samaritan woman (John 4:7ff.), in dealing with Simon the Pharisee (Luke 7: 

40ff.), and in handling opposition (Matthew 21:23ff.; 22).

He was courageous and fearless under all conditions, but yet cautious, considerate, modest and discreet. He was patient, enduring, and long-suffering, yet just and fair. He was compassionate, merciful, kind, sympathetic and loving, yet stern when the situation demanded it.

He was magnanimous, yet discriminating. He was consistent and self-controlled. Each of these qualities as well as those which follow might well be elaborated with instances and references if space were available. 

Jesus had spirituality and a prayer life that were distinctive. He had keen discernment and was a close observer of nature, events, children and older people. He was appreciative of faith, truth, character, response, and kindness.

There are some traits that came into prominence in relation to his vocation. He taught with authority. He understood the Scriptures. He was direct and simple in method, logical yet intensely practical. He was concrete and graphic in speech, had unusual power of expression. 

He was a thoughtful, tactful teacher, resourceful but not tedious. He used discretion in training and using the twelve, was sociable, approachable, alert for opportunities, but unsympathetic with the insincere. He manifested great administrative ability, was energetic in his work, fearless in duty, maintained composure and selfcontrol in disturbing situations, was compassionate and sympathetic, had a deep sense of responsibility, and was faithful unto death.

The character of Christ indeed offers unsearchable

riches. Little wonder that father, as much as he studied it, was captivated by it. It got hold of him to the extent that one of his students goes so far as to say of him, "Brother Hoff, more than any other man that I have known, reminds me of Christ."

CHAPTER XIII  

If one were superstitious he would find a way to avoid making the chapter that deals with prophecy and prediction number thirteen. But since it is being written on Wednesday and Thursday instead of Friday, perhaps one can proceed. This take-off has meaning in relation to father's teaching. He took prophecy and prediction out of the field of superstition and guesswork and put it on the basis of sound principles of reasoning. As I talk now with those who studied under him, there is nothing that they appreciate more about his teaching than this factor. He gave them a sane view of prophecy and a grasp of principles by which they can think for themselves in this field. My own conviction is that he has saved the church much grief at this point. His teaching, firsthand and through the teachers and ministers trained under him, has kept the church he served from speculative bypaths and guided us down the main road of the spiritual gospel. It has sustained our faith that the gospel is the power of God unto salvation and the Spirit the transforming influence by which men and nations are to be changed. We live in the age of greatest opportunity now and do not need to strain our eyes looking for signs of some future manipulations by divine interposition to bring in the era of promise. The promise is upon us. Hesitancy of Christ's followers to measure up to God's purpose is the biggest drawback to its realization.

What were the principles by which father interpreted prophecy? Briefly two: historical context, and the use

the New Testament makes of prophecy. As we had occasion to see in an earlier chapter, the Old Testament prophets sustained a direct connection with the affairs of their own day. In the early pre-writing prophets whose work is described in the historical books this is evident. It was no less true of the writing prophets, though not always so evident. Our previous survey showed how Amos was related to the people of his day. 

The first verse of the book indicates the historical situation to which he addressed himself. The whole book deals with that situation until the last chapter. There we find material reaching out into the future (Amos 9: 

7-15). In Isaiah the same thing is evident. The book is addressed to the times of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and 

Hezekiah (Isaiah 1:1). Chapter 1 is a strong appeal to that generation. Chapter 2 is addressed to that generation too, but it reaches far into the future (2:2-4).

No better illustration of the twofold interest of the prophets can be found than the early chapters of Isaiah with their reference to current events and the Messianic predictions of 2:1-4; 9:1-7; 11:1-10.

The prophets were great preachers of righteousness and critics of morals and religion of their own generation. To understand their contribution in this respect it is necessary only to discover and keep in mind the historical situation in which they served. Keeping in view this historical anchorage will save the Bible reader from many vagaries of interpretation. The historical student knows better than to let his imagination run to modern automobiles when Nahum says, "The chariots rage in the streets; they rush to and fro in the broad ways: the appearance of them is like torches; they run like the lightnings" (Nahum 2:4). He knows well that

Nahum is talking about chariots in the siege of Nineveh. 

Observing the historical connection is the first step in taking the magical element out of prophecy. It is only good common sense to leave a prophet's statement stand in its historical situation. To be a sane interpreter of prophecy one must then be a thoroughgoing student of Biblical history. This was father's working basis for the study of the prophets.

The historical situation is important also in interpreting predictive prophecy. The prophets spoke in terms that were meaningful to their age. They sometimes spoke of coming judgment or deliverance for their people, often of the approaching captivity and return from exile. History is the guide for us in interpreting such predictions. Some of them, however, reach farther than any historical event in Israel's history. They have a primary literal meaning, and also an evident secondary meaning, which usually turns out to be spiritual in its implications. But even this farther reach of predictive prophecy was usually in terms significant to the prophets' immediate hearers or readers. They spoke of the 

Messiah in terms of the prophet and priest, the religious leaders that their people knew, and a king to carry to fulfillment their disappointed hopes for the house of David. What could be more reasonable than for the new David to make his start where the first one didin Bethlehem? Future hopes for the people took form in expectations of a kingdom of Israel built on lines of righteousness and godliness. The sacrificial mission of the Messiah was cast in terms of the Hebrew altar and sacrifices. Recognition of this historical setting is of immense help in getting at the meaning of predictive prophecy. But father did not trust to this alone.

The guiding factor that father trusted for interpreting Old Testament predictive prophecy was the New Testament use of the predictions. When Paul or some other 

New Testament writer applied an Old Testament text to the work of Christ or the Christian church, that was enough for him. He believed that is where it belonged, not off in the future where some modern chart maker might place it. New Testament usage then was his second principle in interpreting prophecy. 

Father divided Messianic predictions into two groups: those that concern the Messiah and those that concern the people. The Messiah texts follow the three general interests of prophet, priest, and king. Deuteronomy 18: 

15-19 looks for a prophet like Moses. Acts 3:22-26 and 

7:37 apply this to Christ. It no doubt had a limited application in every prophet whom God raised up, but reached its full realization in Christ. Matthew (12: 

18-21) tells us that Jesus applied the ministry of the servant of the Lord in Isaiah 42:1-9 to his own ministry of helpfulness. Luke (4:16-19) reports that Jesus took the anointing text of Isaiah 61:1-3 as the key to his own ministry. There are a number of texts in which prophet is used of Jesus. He was indeed the Prophet. He was also the fulfillment of the priesthood. The Book of Hebrews is our main source here, though he is recognized as the sin bearer in Acts 7:32-33 in fulfillment of Isaiah 

53, and the priestly intercessor in Romans 8:34. It is the royal texts that are most abundant. The prophets abound in references to the expectation of the raising up of a new David. Acts 13:33; Hebrews 1:5; 5:5 apply Psalm 

2 to Christ. Matthew 4:15-16 and Luke 1:32-33 apply 

Isaiah 9:1-7 to him. Acts 13:22ff. carries over the idea of Isaiah 11:1ff. But Christ's kingship was not political

(John 18:36). There is no more reason to expect Jesus to be a literal king on a throne, like David, than to expect him to revive or restore the sacrificial system of animal slaying and offering. His ministry is spiritual in one function as well as the other. The two ministries are put together in Jeremiah 33:20-22; and Jesus fulfilled them both. The Jews tried to make him king in the ordinary sense; Satan also tried this. But Christ set up a different sort of kingdom. He rules wherever his kingship is recognized. His law is love. His sword is the Spirit.

Each of these strains of texts has place for people along Iwith the Messiah. Father was accustomed to start his course in Doctrine of the Prophets with the Abrahamic blessing and covenant texts. Abraham was at the center of the promise, his descendants were embraced by its widening scope, but there was no limit to its ultimate reach; it included all (Genesis 12:1-2; 17:1-8; 18:16-21; 

22:9-19). Stephen found use for this universalism in preaching Christ (Acts 7:2ff.). Christians in turning men from their sins fulfill the Abrahamic covenant (Acts 3:25-26). In the New Testament it is not a blood or family matter but one of spirit and personal religion (Matthew 3:9; 8:11-12; John 8:33-34; Romans 4:11-17; Galatians 3:6-14). There was a line of covenants, the climax of which was the new covenant, that of the law in the heart (Genesis 15:1-21; Exodus 24:1-8; Isaiah 49:1-13; 

Jeremiah 31:15-34). The new covenant came in the gospel age (Hebrews 8:6-13; 10: 16-17; 2 Corinthians 3:1-18).

A large number of Gentile ingathering texts fit in here (e.g. Deuteronomy 32:43; Psalms 18:49; 117:1; Isaiah 

11:10; 52:15; cf. Romans 15:8-13; Isaiah 44:6; cf. Acts 

13:47; Hosea 1:10; cf. Romans 9:25-26; Isaiah 42:6; cf.

Matthew 12:17-21). Joel's prediction of universal prophecy was realized in the Spirit endowment of Christians (Acts 2:28-32). Peter says Christians are "a holy priesthood . . . an elect race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession" (I Peter 2:5, 9). On the priesthood of believers, see also Romans 12:1-2; 

Philippians 4:18; Hebrews 13:15-16.

Thus there is abundant evidence that the larger hope of the prophets finds fulfillment in the conversion of Jews and Gentiles to Christ. The apostles and New Testament writers took the predictive pictures of prophet, priest, king, covenant, nation and kingdom and applied them to the spiritual experiences of the gospel age. We stand in line with the prophetic hopes today as we accept and propagate the gospel of Christ. The features of the old covenant were but figures of the new. People could be led to reach forward to something better and greater only in terms of the best that was in their own experience. That is the way we reach forward to heaven and the future. To expect a restoration of the nation of 

Israel, its kingly line and sacrificial system would be to turn our backs on the spiritual realities in Christ and revert to the experiences out of which the hope came. 

If we follow the guidance of the New Testament writers in interpreting predictive prophecy we will go forward, not backward. Christ extracted the spiritual significance out of the old, and led it to realization in the era of the Spirit. Even the wonders connected with the expectation of the Messianic age in Joel 2:30ff. are interpreted spiritually in Acts 2:16-21.

What then is to be said of the apocalyptic strain-the interposition of God in behalf of his people, the second coming of Christ, the resurrection and the judgment?

Father observed that the spiritual realization of prophetic hopes was no bar to apocalyptic expectations in the New Testament writings and he didn't see why it should be in our case. He believed that the kingdom would reach its consummation in that fashion. He surveyed carefully with his classes all the New Testament texts in each of these lines. He looked for the coming of Christ in glory, not to cow his foes and initiate a millennium but to bring men to judgment and reward (1 Corinthians 4:5; 15:23-26; 1 Peter 5:4). The time of the coming he counted to be dependent on the moral response of people rather than on a divinely predestined schedule (Matthew 24). He leaned heavily on 1 Corinthians 15:20-28 for the purpose and function of the second coming. The practical approach to matters in this field he counted to be watchfulness and working hard at one's Christian tasks rather than speculation. That was Jesus' emphasis according to the gospel record, and father was glad to take it as his own (Matthew 24 and parallels; 

Luke 12:35-40). The resurrection he expected to be of both the righteous and the wicked (Acts 24:15; cf. Daniel 

12:2; John 5:28). In regard to the judgment, he expected God to be the judge, Christ the medium of equitable judgment, man the subject of judgment, and the extent to which man has used his opportunities in appropriating Christ the basis for judgment. 

Something should be said here about father's interpre tation of the Book of Revelation. No detailed statement needs to be made, for father stated his views plainly in his book, The Message of the Book of Revelation, and he also had a chapter on it in Training the Sunday School 

Teacher-First Standard Course. He delighted to take classes through the book, going through it no less than

three times in a term to get a preview, a detailed study, and a perspective. He pointed out that Revelation resembles the New Testament epistles, especially in the early chapters. It was addressed to a line of seven churches located on the Roman road that made a circuit from Ephesus. The considering of these churches in order of their location was evidence to him that the book was addressed to actual needs with pastoral interest. If chapters two and three were to be counted dispensational, as some hold, selection would have been made according to type of problem, not location. Examination of the book justified this inference as to function. 

It was written to give stamina and courage to persecuted Christians and had a direct practical purpose. This does not keep it from having meaning for us too. It rather enriches its meaning. The principles in it are of farreaching significance. One of his examination questions was to choose at least a dozen themes from it for practical discourses.

Though resembling epistles, the book is distinctive in character. There is nothing like it in the New Testament. The nearest approach is the apocalyptic chapters in the first three gospels (Matthew 24; Mark 13; Luke 

21). It is a book of visions like Ezekiel, Daniel and Zechariah in the Old Testament. It is full of imagery that comes from these Old Testament books. He had two primary rules of interpretation for Revelation. The first was to observe the relation to this Old Testament imagery. The symbolical terms, beasts and cryptic numbers in the book need not necessarily carry the same meaning or value that similar ones had in the Old Testament, but the Old Testament-particularly the Book of Daniel -gives a good perspective of imagery and history, a

good context for understanding them. His second rule was that no figurative language in Revelation should be given interpretation that is out of harmony with the plain statements in the rest of the New Testament. For example, the wars in Revelation he was sure could not be literal ones, for the rest of the New Testament represents God's purposes of redemption going forward by spiritual means, not by carnage and warfare. Or again, the thousand-year millennium should not be given literal interpretation that would invalidate such direct statements as 1 Corinthians 15:23-26. He urged much care and humility in interpreting the pictorial language of this book to fit in with the gospel of Christ. 

After a brief introduction in 1:1-8, the first section of the book-1:9 to 3:22-consists of a vision of the glorified

Christ and the application of this vision to the needs of the seven churches. In each of the brief "letters" to the seven churches, the church is addressed, some characteristic is carried over from the vision of the glorified Christ, there is commendation (with one exception), instruction, warning, rebuke, and promise. Chapters 4:1 to 11:19 deal with the opening of seven seals. Chapters 4 and 5 are dramatic scenes in heaven setting the tone for the opening of the seals. The seven seals represent in turn "important facts and phases of the gospel dispensation" as follows: 

promise of victory, victory through sacrifice, assurance of justice, forebodings of justice, promise to the faithful, the second coming of Christ, and the final judgment of the wicked. Chapters 12 to 20 repeat the same general interests with different imagery, bringing out the main actors in the drama. The Jewish mother of the Christ and of the faithful, Satan, the two beasts that probably typify as persecuting powers the Roman Empire and Roman ec-

clesiasticism are dealt with in graphic fashion. The 

Babylon symbolism undoubtedly refers to Rome. Satan and all his followers finally come to their doom and right is victorious. Chapters 21:1 to 22:5 portray the glorious finale. Judgment is past. Eternal glory has come. The finest things of earth on a stupendous scale symbolize the heavenly glory. A strong hortatory conclusion (22: 

6-21) ends the book.

Through father's eyes, Revelation was a spiritual book with a great message of comfort, hope and encouragement. He gloried in the hope of the hereafter. He looked for it to be the fulfillment of all that is good in this life, where sin and selfishness can not mar, and growth can progress to the perfection of Christ in God.

CHAPTER XIV  

This survey of father's Biblical interpretation and methods of Bible study has necessarily been fragmentary. Effort has been made to select trunk lines of interest and the procedures that have largest practical import. I am painfully aware of some serious omissions. 

Little has been said about Paul, for instance-and how father did revel in Romans! Several lines of interest must yet be noted in this concluding chapter, specifically his understanding of the doctrines of revelation and inspiration, and the importance he set upon the Holy Spirit and prayer.

He regarded the Bible to be God's revelation of the way of redemption for the human family, divine in its origin, purpose and content, but human in its language and style. He called it "God's love letter to the human family." The divine communication in it is in harmony with the nature of God-spiritual. The more spiritual man is, the more capacity he has for communing with 

God and receiving the revelation. "Things of a kind understand each other," is the way he put it. Prayer and the intuitive impulse of the Spirit of God upon the spirit of man are normal channels of communication between man and God. Revelation does not ignore these channels; it uses them to the full. 

Men kept records of history both oral and written. 

They made research to ascertain facts. They stepped out on faith to ascertain truth. The Holy Spirit used their abilities and attainments in a marvelous way. The prophets were God's spokesmen and writers in the old

dispensation.

Jeremiah is a good example of a writer at work in the Old Testament (Jeremiah 30:1-3; 36:132; 45:1; 51:60); Luke in the New (Luke 1:1-4). Apostles and other Christian leaders such as Mark and Luke wrote the New Testament. 

Revelation in the Bible was a gradual process covering many centuries, as was noted in chapter eight. Dreams, visions, angel ministry and casting lots were means of making known the divine will upon occasion. But most 

Bible truth came intuitively. Jesus knew the Father perfectly and expressed the Father's will. The plan of revelation is thus complete and adequate for all needs. 

To lay hold of it we must have personal experience of the Spirit of God.

The Bible is an inspired book. Some have taken every jot and tittle to be inspired and infallible. Others have gone to the other extreme and denied any special character and authority to it. The truth lies somewhere between the extremes. Of literalism he remarked casually, "Some people are so literal that they can hear the squeak of the pearly gates. . . . When you run up against people who insist on taking the Bible literally and will not believe in pictures, have them read about straining out gnats and swallowing camels." Of the opposite extreme, he said, "Don't fool with the Bible unless you know more than the other fools do." The Holy Spirit has inspired the Bible not by negating man's talents but by making human personality fruitful. It took inspired men to make an inspired Bible. The Bible has some claims for its own inspiration, e.g., Mark 12:36; Acts 1:16; Romans 

3:2; 2 Timothy 3:16-17; 2 Peter 1:21. Hebrew prophets said, "Thus saith the Lord."

The Bible has come through severe tests. To be valid,

inspiration must appear in all. In the making of duplicate copies of Bible books many thousands of variations came into being, but only a few of these affect meanings appreciably and none obscure any vital doctrine. The process of selecting the canonical books out of the larger mass of those available was another test. A comparison of the Bible books with those that have been excluded sustains our belief that God was supervising the selection. A third test is translation into the languages of the world. Parts of the Bible are now extant in more than a thousand languages and the way of salvation is plain in all. The work of translating has been done faithfully and for the most part, well. God must be as much concerned about one people having his will as about another. We could hardly believe in inspiration if he had deserted the process anywhere along the line. 

The real nature of inspiration appears in the sort of process just traced. It is not a jot and tittle proposition, but one that brings to men effectively the way of salvation. Characteristically he said, "I am not so thankful that the Bible was inspired long ago in Hebrew and Greek as that we right now have an English Bible whose message is inspiring and saves those who believe it." 

Also, "I have read the Bible through many times, and the remarkable thing is that each time I read it I receive new light and discover truths that I had never seen before. This is a proof that it is God's book, not man's.” 

The same Spirit that inspired the writing and transmission of the Bible works in the human heart to make the Bible meaningful and effective. Teachers and preachers of the Word today stand in the process of making God's will known to man. This brings a mighty challenge to seek the Holy Spirit's power and guidance.

It would be too bad to have God's plan of revelation and inspiration stopped just short of its goal in reaching life. "Knowing God's Word," he said, "and having the 

Holy Spirit to give it utterance is what will move the world."

Some of his pointed statements will show the place he gave the Holy Spirit in the Christian life. "Many that have human spirits do not have the Holy Spirit." "When born into the kingdom we are babes, and only through the Holy Spirit do we grow. Alone, we soon get dwarfed." "The Holy Spirit is as necessary for growth and strength to Christianity as sunshine to a plant.” "The Holy Spirit comes in only as there is room for him. If we sin it is because the Holy Spirit does not have room in our hearts.” "It is just as natural for a child of God walking in his steps to be filled with the Spirit as it is for one to breathe. It is just as natural for a Christian to be filled with the Spirit as for a bird to fly or a duck to swim." "As health is normal so is goodness normal. If we are in the Spirit, his yoke is easy and his burden is light. 

Our burdens are heavy when we are trying to work alone." "To have a keen sense of the mystic presence of the Lord all the time and everywhere is a privilege while the Holy Spirit has the right of way." "Ministering and fasting make good ears for the Holy Spirit." "Many times we read in religious papers that folks were elected to office, but not in five years have I seen

the report of a man being filled with the Holy Spirit."

The Holy Spirit is the power of God by which he works in the world. It is by his power that the resources of the spirit world become available to us. The Spirit activated service in Old Testament times. vision that this might become universal (Joel 2:28-32).

John the Baptist came preaching that that day had dawned (John 1:33). The Spirit came upon Jesus at baptism (Matthew 3:16), led him into the wilderness (Matthew 

4:1), and was the power by which his ministry was carried out (Luke 4:14-19). "Jesus was on the live wire of heaven all the time," is the way father said it. Jesus told his followers what the Spirit would mean in their lives (e.g. John 16:12-15). The Spirit came upon them and used them mightily at Pentecost and thereafter (Acts 2). The Book of Acts is sometimes called the "Acts of the Holy Spirit." That book shows what the 

Spirit can do with men, and the epistles show the fruit that the Spirit can yield in human life (e.g. 1 Corinthians 12; Galatians 5:22-24). The Spirit is the power that brings conviction, looses tongues, takes away fear of men, gives boldness in service, makes men liberal, and sanctifies Christian character. It is the good gift that the good Father wants most of all to give his children (Luke 11:13). 

Some quotations will introduce father's understanding of prayer. "Prayer doesn't alter God's plan but it often gets things here in shape so that his plan can operate." "Prayer makes us better men, and that is about as far as nine tenths of our prayers go. It takes so much to get us ready for his blessings."

"If we had more praying, we would have more transfiguration scenes."

When asked how to study and understand the Bible, he replied, "Mostly on your knees." "Put your prayers in such a form that Jesus could sign his name to them and approve them.” "Take an invoice before praying: then you will be better able to pray and your prayer will be more effectual." "Prayer closets are not lazy resorts but places of the busy soul. The busiest spend much time there to 'steam up' for activity." "A lot of our prayers ought to be cut off at both ends and shortened in the middle. Don't say, 'Lead, guide and direct me,' when one term expresses the thought. 

Peter's prayer in Matthew 14:30 came straight from the heart." "How often do we let ejaculatory prayers burst out of the occasion? They are the truest kind." "Any work or play that makes spontaneous prayer impossible should be abandoned forever." "Either our prayers will kill our sins or our sins will kill our prayers." "Pray with a motive single-eyed to God, not with one eye on God and one eye on man." "In public prayer, pray true enough to be heard of 

God and loud enough to be heard of men." "Faith is not believing that God can but that God will." "Nothing strengthens our faith as much as checking up and watching for the answers to prayer."

"What the world needs is not mere intellectual faith but F-A-I-T-H hyphenated by acts." 

It is through prayer that we open the door to the infinite resources of God's grace. Father always enjoyed teaching a course on prayer. He was sure that God's ear has never been closed to man's cry. In spite of the impression that in the old dispensation people had to approach God through a priest, there are many references in the Old Testament to personal prayer. In all ages men have had free access to God. But it was in 

Christ that prayer reached its highest possibilities. He prayed under varying circumstances and taught his disciples to pray. The most glorious prayer was on the mount of transfiguration; the one of farthest outreach the prayer of John 17; the one of deepest feeling the prayer in the Garden of Gethsemane; the one of greatest mercy the prayer of forgiveness on the cross. He taught people to worship in spirit and in truth (John 4:23-24), in sincerity and simplicity (Matthew 6:5-15), and in harmony with God's plan and purpose (John 16:23-24).

Prayer brought power to early Christians (Acts 1:14; 

2:1-2; 4:24-31). Prayer in Christ's name and spirit will bring power to us.

Emanuel B. Hoff- 

We must conclude our survey. the name Emanuel has meaning. God with us! The prophet coined it (Isaiah 7:14; 8:10). It had Messianic implications (Matthew 1:23). Christ put the full meaning into it. What could a child born into a nineteenthcentury American home do with such a name? Father took the name seriously and made it meaningful for those who knew him. His living and his work made God a more immediate reality in many lives. He did this by his spirit and devotion. He did it also by his life-

long vocation. Forty-three years in the Christian ministry; twenty-five in the classroom as a Bible teacher, most of them given to the building up of a Bible training school and seminary for his church! His teaching not only bore the marks of a creative personality and the wit of a keen mind, but carried a soundness of judgment that made it stable and enduring. It did not die with him but lives on to bless the lives of many. 

Survey briefly the scope of the principles we have found: A historical grounding for faith. A growing revelation discovered personally by the individual through inductive study. A method of exegetical analysis whereby the Bible comes to speak its own message and give its own emphasis. An intelligent use of context, both immediate and remote, to derive accurate meanings from figurative as well as direct teachings. 

Letting the Bible be its own best commentary and holding suspended judgment on difficult points until light breaks. A direct approach to appreciation and appropriation of the character of Christ, who is the perfect Revealer of God. A future outlook grounded in historical understanding of prophecy and geared to the spiritual program of Christ and the New Testament. The conviction that we stand in the main stream of God's purpose when we seek to appropriate and propagate the truth of Christ and his gospel of love and forgiveness.

The alignment of life with the purpose and power of God by endowment with the Holy Spirit and the agency of prayer. These are principles that will not soon die. 

Emanuel B. Hoff contributed a viewpoint, an interest, a mood, an appetite and a way of satisfying it. He did this to a greater or less degree for a couple of thousands of Bethany students and unnumbered others. I should

like to live a life as rich in meaning and usefulness as his. 

Forward-looking words adapted from a statement by his son Amos in the memorial issue of Bethany Bible School Bulletin will give meaning to the forward-looking picture on the opposite page: "He has finished his task and many who crossed his pathway are better because he lived. And now that his spirit only is with us, nothing will be more pleasing to him than to see his host of spiritual sons and daughters take up the leadership where he was asked to lay it down. He expects us to surpass him, by reason of our greater opportunities—a challenge which is almost bewildering. A number of times when he contemplated the tremendous opportunities available to young people in modern education, he said a bit wistfully, 'My, if I had only had such a start!' He was true to his day; will we be to ours? His life helps us to reevaluate the power of a life attuned to the purpose of God. "The still small voice of the Angel of Death that whispered, "Thy work is finished,' becomes a clarion call to us, "The work of the kingdom is but begun.' Who is there who will rise up with a vision large enough and an enthusiasm strong enough for the task?" "If we are Christians, we are expected to do things beyond our own strength."
