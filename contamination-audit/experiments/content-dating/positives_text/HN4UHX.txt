The Continued Study Unit 

This is a new educational tool. It represents the cement that binds together the diverse elements or topics which together comprise the Social Science fabric of our educational program. 

These Units must not be thought of as isolated topics which have no connection with the subject matter of textbooks. They do, however, represent a correlated, integrated, and cumulative program. They represent a study of the whole social science field which has resulted for the first time in a solution of the problems involved. 

No textbook can possibly give all that is known about any character, any event, or situation, and yet much more information is needed on every such topic than the textbook affords. In place of printing many paper-bound booklets on various subjects, this program has resulted in a survey of the factual material needed to supplement the textbooks in history, geography, civics and government, and allied topics. Over five hundred topics have been selected as an objective-not that they may all be published in the immediate future. 

Each of the topics selected will be tied into subject material in the textbooks. In this way, additional material on a subject may be added from time to time. A fuller knowledge of the subject under discussion in the textbook is thus made possible. Herein is offered not only factual material correlated with the textbooks, but supplementary reading that is automatically directed to a constructive end. The pupil may be daily exposed to what is really research material, without the labor of digging it out of reference books written for adults. 

Another advantage of the continued Study Unit idea is the possibility of keeping textbooks up-to-date, as well as the opportunity of securing Units on new and modern problems as they arise. In the past, the teacher has been forced to give assigned readings from books which are beyond the mental age of the child. Comprehension was thus impeded and the scope of a pupil's activities was limited. 

To load a textbook with such desirable material merely destroys the continuity of the text, with the result that the pupil's interest in needed facts is destroyed by his interest in the more supplementary phases of a subject.

Each one of the Units in this series, then, has been definitely planned to meet and to bridge a gulf that may not be covered in any textbook.

The Ohio River Gateway to the West FOREWORD 

Most rivers take their rise in hills or mountains, then the little stream grows larger and larger by receiving the waters of other streams until the main trunk is of size and value. This is not the case with the river which we call the Ohio. Near the western line of Pennsylvania the 

Allegheny from the north and the Monongahela (mō-non-gă-hé-lă) from the south meet and lose their names the Ohio is born, a strong and navigable river from the start. 

For a thousand miles, from Pittsburgh to Cairo (ka'-rō), Illinois, the Ohio takes its curving way westward to the Mississippi. No other waterway draining the sunset slopes of the Appalachians (ap-ă-lach' ĭ-ăn) can compare with it. Its basin takes in one-fifteenth of the United 

States, and one-fourth of the water that the great Mississippi pours into the Gulf of Mexico comes through the mouth of the Ohio. 

In all the thousand miles of its course, moreover, there is no considerable descent to interfere with navigation except a two-mile rapid at Louisville, which, because of its rareness, was called the Falls of the Ohio. Even in the old days before the Government built dams to improve the channel of the river, there were stretches of many miles wherein it was hard to perceive any current, so easy and gentle was the river slope. 

No wonder that the Ohio, as well as several of its tributaries, the Great Kanawha, the Muskingum (mus-kin'-gum), the Scioto (si-o'-to), the Wabash, the Cumberland, and the Tennessee offered from the very beginning of settlement a fine opportunity for man's travel. 

More than that, the Ohio presented the one great river road westward from the Appalachians to the Mississippi. The emigrant who took the easier path by water rather than the toilsome journey by land in order to reach the "Great River," even though he used the Kanawha, the Kentucky, or the Big Sandy river as his route, had to come to the Ohio at the end. Thus in the development of our country, in its growth westward, the Ohio could not fail to be one of the notable avenues of adventure. We shall see how it was woven into the story of the growth of the United States. (ix)

CHAPTER ONE 

Rivals for the Ohio 

A Closed Road: Strange to say, for many years after the Mississippi was well known to those rovers, the French, the Ohio lay practically unexplored, although it was nearer the Atlantic coast. New 

Orleans guarded the Mississippi entrance; there were little settlements and forts along the "Great River," even near the mouth of the Ohio, but the Ohio itself lay within its hills and its forests, shut off from the world and unsettled by any but the Indians.

The English, of course, still shut off on the west by the Appalachian mountain walls, found enough to do in developing their colonies strung along the ocean, and in watching the French and the Indians. Why, however, did not the 

French make a highway of the Ohio, especially as La Salle, according to the best evidence, paid a visit to that river? 

The reason for the astonishing failure of the French to frequent the Ohio waters was given in the early history of their exploration of America, when Samuel de Champlain and his two or three white companions used their death-dealing muskets to defeat an Iroquois war party in favor of the Hurons. 

Never in the years that followed had the Iroquois tribes forgotten that injury. 

Along the southern shore of Lake 

Ontario lay the various "nations" or tribes that formed the Iroquois 

Confederacy of the "Long House."

These steadfastly took the English side against the French, and stood sentinel so faithfully that the French found it much more comfortable to go westward by the 

Ottawa River which led them within reach of Lake Huron. Thus the French, while they became well acquainted with the larger and more remote of the Great Lakes-Huron, Michigan, Superior-had little to do with Lake Ontario and Lake

Erie. The road down the Ohio, as may be seen by looking at the map, was shut in the faces of the French, and the English were not ready to use it.

The Fog Begins to Lift: Not that the Ohio was at all unknown, but there was no map of it in early days; the Indians, however, knew it well and they had described it to the whites. Every official, both 

French and English, was well aware of the general location of the stream, but only through hearsay.

For the white man it was a river of mystery. (11)

Finally, the Delaware and the

Shawnee Indians, pushed out of their lands in eastern Pennsylvania, crossed the mountains to their friends north of the Ohio; and hard on their heels came the Pennsylvania traders with their strings of pack-horses. By the trail along the

Juniata (joo"ni-ǎt'ă) River, and by other less important paths, they spread all through western Pennsylvania and on into the Ohio lands. 

By dozens and scores they came; they built strong log cabins in the principal Indian villages to store the furs and skins which they took in exchange for the liquor, blankets, guns, powder, lead, and trinkets which they brought from the East. 

Still the colonial governments took little interest in the "Mysterious River." 

However, as Pennsylvania and

Virginia became richer and more thickly settled, far-seeing persons began to think of the time when settlement must spread across the mountains, and when these rich lands along the Ohio, of which they had been told, would be worth a great deal of money. Few of the 

Pennsylvanians were interested, because their colony was making profits from the Indians and did not care to have settlers break up that trade. The Virginians, on the contrary, bestirred themselves.

In the year 1747, a number of 

Virginia gentlemen, among whom were two half-brothers of George 

Washington, Augustus and Lawrence it was from Lawrence that George later inherited Mount Vernon-formed the Ohio Company and asked King George to grant them a little patch of ground, just half a million acres, on the south side of the Ohio not far below the "Forks" where Pittsburgh now stands. The company promised to settle a hundred families on the land within seven years.

Matters moved slowly in those days, but in less than two years the King granted the company two hundred thousand acres. Before the company had time to make any moves in consequence of this permission, word went to the French in America, who at once prepared an expedition to show that the Ohio, with all its tributaries, belonged to them.

Céloron Buries His Plate: One 

July day in 1749, an imposing fleet of canoes, twenty-three of them, each big enough to hold a dozen men with their baggage, paddled along the southern shore of Lake 

Erie. In the canoes were twenty regular soldiers, one hundred eighty

Canadian recruits, and thirty Indians. Commanding this force was an old soldier, Pierre Joseph Céloron, sieur de Blainville (often wrongly written Bienville) and in his keeping was a heavy little box. 

The French landed on the lake shore at a point where Lake Chautauqua in Pennsylvania was not far

Rivals for the Ohio 13 away. Of course their Indian guides had told them just what to do. 

After carrying canoes and baggage across the "portage" to the lake, they paddled down to its mouth, where a creek issued which took them to the Allegheny River. As far as they were concerned this was the Ohio. "Ohio" is an Indian name; the

French spelled it "Oyo," pronouncing the "y" like "e," but there had been so much said about the beauty of the stream we now call the 

Allegheny, that the French also called the river they were seeking "La Belle Rivière" or the Beautiful River. When Céloron's men, after two weeks of toil since they had left Lake Erie, came out into the Allegheny, the priest of the expedition wrote, "Finally, overcome with weariness, and almost despairing of seeing the Beautiful 

River, we entered it." At that spot,

Warren, Pennsylvania, is now built. 

Here Céloron opened his heavy little box and from it took a leaden plate, about a foot long and twothirds as wide, with an engraved inscription saying that France renewed her claim to the Ohio, "and all the streams that flow into it." 

He buried the plate under a red oak at the junction of the creek and the river. Then he nailed to the tree a tin plate bearing the fleur-de-lis or royal lilies of France.

Danger to French Influence: 

Céloron pursued his journey downstream. At the mouth of each important tributary he buried a leaden tablet and put up his tin sign.

Six tablets in all were thus buried; three of them were later found and exist today. As he passed along, the commander found a number of 

English traders-six at the Forks, and ten at Logstown a little farther on. He had them brought before him, but contented himself with ordering them to go home at once with letters telling the governors of Pennsylvania and of Virginia that no English trader had any right to be in the valley of the Ohio.

The last tablet was left at the mouth of the Great Miami River, now in Ohio. Here he turned away from the Ohio to reach a certain Indian village which he had heard was a nest of the hated traders. 

For thirteen September days his men forced their canoes up the shallow stream. When they came to the Indian town, Céloron found that his information was correctthere were the traders and their log cabins. At times fifty traders made this place their headquarters. Céloron warned the chief of the town to send all the traders away. The chief promised, Céloron departed overland for home, but the traders proceeded to make their cabins into a fort!

When Céloron reached Montreal again, he sent an alarming report

to his government. The lands along the Ohio, he said, were swarming with English traders, who, because 

England "ruled the waves," were able to sell blankets, guns, powder, and rum to the natives at prices much cheaper than the French could offer. As far as he had gone, the Indians were all friendly to the English. If Canada did not make a great effort, this Ohio country would be lost to the French.

CHAPTER TWO 

The English Hold the Ohio

English and French Build: In order to carry out their plans, now that the English King was ready to grant them land, the Ohio Company built a storehouse where Cumberland, Maryland, now stands. 

Here a trail led from Will's Creek, where it flows into the Potomac, up to the Forks of the Ohio. They engaged Christopher Gist, a noted. woodsman and surveyor, to make a journey into the Ohio Valley and report on the best place to locate their two hundred thousand acres. "Go as far as the Falls" (where 

Louisville is situated) "if you need to in order to get good level land," said the Company, “but we would rather go clear to the Mississippi than take mean, broken land." 

Gist returned with a report that the richness of the soil was excellent, and the Company hired 

Nemácolin, a Delaware Indian, to blaze the trail clearly from the 

Potomac to the Forks. A branch from this trail led to the Monongahela or Redstone Creek, where 

Brownsville now stands. Here they erected Redstone Fort, which figures prominently in the history of the Ohio.

The French made the next important move. An expedition of three hundred men went to the present site of Erie, Pennsylvania, and built Fort Presqu'ile (pres-kēl') meaning "peninsula." A long peninsula, as you may see by the map, there incloses a large bay for a harbor. They then cut a road southward for eighteen miles to Rivière aux Boeufs (riv'-e-ayre-obuff') or "stream of the buffaloes," now called by an easier but still appropriate name, "French Creek."

There, where Waterford now is, soon stood Fort Le Boeuf. 

Fort Du Quesne: This was too much for Governor Dinwiddie of Virginia. His colony as well as 

Pennsylvania claimed the Forks. 

He sent George Washington, guided by Christopher Gist, to Fort Le 

Boeuf in order to warn the French that they were trespassing. He prepared to have a fort built at the 

Forks. On the way to deliver the message, Washington took a good look at the place for the fort, and wrote in his journal that the "point" would certainly be highly important as a key spot for military operations and for trade. Was he right? 

Washington returned with a polite but firm "No!" from the 

French. Scarcely had Dinwiddie's poor little log fort at the Forks taken shape when a force of five (15)

hundred French, with cannon, appeared before it. The English numbered forty-two and were glad to be allowed to depart in peace. On the foundation they had built, Fort 

Du Quesne (dū kān'), named after the governor of Canada, held command of the three rivers. It had log walls sixteen feet thick, and with its projecting bastions and its deep ditch was considered very strong.

General Braddock soon arrived from England to capture Fort Du 

Quesne. Working its slow way up

Wills Creek, his army reached the Monongahela. So long did it take 

Braddock that the French had time to call together their Indian allies from far-off regions; yet when the savages were collected at the fort, the French commander reported that they preferred "cooking and counciling to fighting." At last Braddock came within a few miles of 

Du Quesne. 

The French commandant decided that he would have to surrender; but one of his captains, a determined fellow, resolved to give the

British a hot reception as they crossed the Monongahela. If the redcoats lost some men, they would allow better terms of surrender, and perhaps would delay until help came to the French. Everyone knows how, to the great surprise of the French themselves, Braddock fell into the ambush, and suffered a defeat that was as bad as the defeat of the British by Andrew 

Jackson in the battle of New 

Orleans. Fort Du Quesne was saved to the French for three years.

Fort Pitt Defies Pontiac: In 1758, however, General John Forbes, "Head of Iron," came pushing along over the Pennsylvania mountain ridges with an army more than three times as large as that which Braddock had led. With him was 

George Washington, who had escaped death as by miracle at Braddock's defeat. This time there was no way of stopping the British advance. The French blew up their ammunition, threw their cannon into the river, and fired the fort. Then part of the whites of the garrison departed for Canada up the Allegheny River, others went south on the Ohio, and the Indians took the trail to their homes in the West. 

William Pitt, the great Prime

Minister of England, sent word that the fort should be restored to good condition without delay. "It is necessary," he said, "in order to maintain the undisputed possession of the Ohio." It was rebuilt as 

Fort Pitt, and stood unattacked while the English continued the war until at the end of five years (1763) the French lost their possessions on the continent of North America.

The French and Indian War, as the colonists called it, ended thus, but a fierce Indian war led by the chief Pontiac began at once. Of the English forts within the present

17 boundaries of northern United

States, only two defied the wily and furious attacks of the redmenthose were Detroit and Fort Pitt.

The three hundred settlers who had made "the Forks" their home crowded into Fort Pitt, and the soldiers of the garrison bravely resisted the siege by the Delawares and Shawnees. The rivers were more enemies than friends, for they rose and almost washed away the stockade at one place. Moreover, the

Indians dug caves in the banks, from which they rose unexpectedly to take shots at any careless white man. This was most annoying, but the garrison secretly built a big bateau or flat-bottomed boat, strong enough to carry a small cannon.

They launched it quietly, rowed along the shore, and swept all the caves with their fire so effectively that the Indians tried that trick no longer. Yet, in the end, only the forced march of troops under Colonel Bouquet (boo-kā') saved the inhabitants. In 1765 Pittsburgh was laid out in streets as a real town. 

The Ohio Boundary: With peace declared, the King thought of a scheme to keep a strict boundary line between the "Indian country" and the American colonies. He forbade the settlers to make homes beyond the headwaters of the rivers that flowed into the Atlantic. In this way he hoped to stop all disputes and fights between the red and the white men. The matter dragged along until the English 

Government grew impatient and ordered Sir William Johnson, Commissioner of Indian Affairs, to hurry up and see that the boundary line was fixed. 

Sir William, who had married into the Iroquois "nation" and was high in favor with their "Long 

House," called these six tribes to a council at Fort Stanwix in New 

York, not far from his own house at Johnstown along the Mohawk.

The Iroquois claimed dominion over the tribes in eastern Ohio, who had very little to say about any bargains made. The treaty that resulted from this council fixed the Ohio River as the boundary to hold back the settlers, and gave the region called Kentucky, south of the Ohio, to Virginia.

Now the Virginians came floating down the Ohio "in shoals," as one traveler said, and landed on the south shore. Few of them settled along the river, as that was too dangerous; they usually went a day or two's journey into the interior to join the whites already there, many of whom had come overland. 

The land companies began again to organize. Sir William Johnson was in two of them, along with Benjamin Franklin's son William, who was governor of New Jersey. Benjamin acted as the London agent for one of the companies. 

Another company was formed by forty-nine wealthy Virginians,

among whom George Washington was numbered. Washington, who was much interested in the developing West, made a journey down the Ohio as far as the mouth of the Kanawha to see these fertile lands for which he had fought. All these land companies died during the Revolutionary War, but, while they lasted, the thought that they would persuade the English Government to break the Ohio River line established by the Fort Stanwix treaty brought hundreds and thousands of eager land-seekers to to the banks of the river. 

Among these men were many rough and lawless characters who frequently or constantly trespassed upon the Indian lands, hunted the 

Indian game, and even shot Indians at sight. Naturally, the Indians became more and more determined to keep the line of the Ohio as a sort of fortification. The 

Shawnees, the Senecas or Mingoes (western Iroquois), the Wyandots or Hurons, and a great many of the Delawares, united in a league of hatred against the whites who would not respect treaty boundaries. 

The Explosion Comes: Every thing was ready along the Ohio for a grand explosion, and the explosion soon came. Both Pennsylvania and

Virginia claimed the land on which 

Pittsburgh stood, now a thriving village; a Virginian, Dr. Connolly, at this time had seized Fort Pitt and held it in the name of Lord 

Dunmore, Virginia's governor. Connolly had a bright idea: in case of serious trouble with the Indians, Virginia would send out troops to the fort, which would give him a better hold on the disputed section. 

In the spring of 1774, the year before our Revolutionary War began, Indians attacked, near Fort

Pitt, three men employed by a trader, killed one, wounded another, and took their goods. This gave 

Connolly his chance. He sent out letters to all the nearby settlements, saying that they should prepare for an attack by the Shawnees, who indeed were the most troublesome savages along the river.

There was a settlement on the 

Ohio at Wheeling, now in West

Virginia, and at the settlement happened to be Michael Cresap (cres'-ăp) who led a band of reckless followers. When Connolly's letter reached Wheeling, Cresap's frontiersmen set up a war post and danced around it like Indians, whooping and striking their tomahawks into the post. Just then word arrived that a canoe containing some Indians was coming down stream. These proved to be a white man, a Delaware, and a Shawnee, all in the pay of the robbed trader, trying to see if they could not get back some of their employer's furs. 

With part of his men, Cresap, apparently careless who these Indians might be, took a canoe and pushed out into the river, while

The English Hold the Ohio 

19 some of his followers hid in the growth along the shore. Stevens, the white man, seeing a canoe filled with excited paddlers, dressed somewhat in savage fashion, thought they were hostile Indians and turned toward the Virginia shore. As he came near, Cresap's concealed men took careful aim and shot Stevens' two Indian companions dead. Next day this same party pursued other redmen who had passed down the river, and shot several.

Dunmore's War: These and other unprovoked killings stirred the Indians to immediate revenge. They, too, had murdered without special cause, but they now went on the warpath just the same. The frontiersmen begged to be led against their enemy, and Lord Dunmore was quite willing to be the leader. 

He planned that two strong divisions of men should be raised and meet at the mouth of the Kanawha, which spot was called Point Pleasant. Dunmore would bring the northern division, while the southern division would be headed by 

General Andrew Lewis, a brave man, but not an especially skillful leader. 

While Lewis' frontiersmen were mustering, Dunmore arrived with fifteen hundred men at Fort Pitt. 

He changed his plan, and sent word to Lewis that he would camp farther upriver, then go overland to the Scioto (si-o'to). Ignorant of 

Dunmore's new thought, Lewis marched down the Kanawha to 

Point Pleasant and camped between the two rivers with his eleven hundred fighters. Here he received 

Dunmore's letter and decided to cross the Ohio the next morning; but the Indians had their own plans. 

Cornstalk, the greatest chief of the Shawnees, had tried to prevent his tribesmen from going on the warpath. Now that there must be a battle, however, he was ready to meet the foe. The best defense often is attack. Cornstalk led a force of warriors from the Scioto region toward the camp of Lewis. 

If he could destroy that division, he would then surround and destroy Dunmore's men. It was well planned.

The Point Pleasant Fight: When the Indians arrived at the Ohio, they saw the whites camped on the opposite shore without any special guards or fortifications. Cornstalk took his warriors some miles upstream; they made rafts and crossed during the night. Now they could pen the whites into the triangle between the rivers. At dawn, as the hunters went out to provide food for Lewis' men, shots rang out. 

One hunter fell dead; others fled back to their mates, with the shout, "Injuns! I seen five acres of ground covered with 'em, as thick as they could stand!"

In the undefended camp a drum beat and the men sprang up from their blankets. Two strong parties

went out to scout for the enemy. 

They found him. The sun came up to the barking of hundreds of rifles. 

Colonel Lewis hastily summoned men to cut down trees for a breastwork across the triangle, while those not needed ran to help their comrades. The battle was on. 

Two lines, one of Indians, the other of whites, more than a mile long, faced each other. Sometimes the enemies were so close to each other that they could have fought by throwing stones, yet the fighters were mostly hidden from each other behind trees and rocks. At times, nevertheless, hand-to-hand combat raged with knife and tomahawk. "Ho!” cried the Indians, tauntingly, "where are your fifes? They do not whistle." Indeed, the bordermen were so busy that the only whistling came from bullets. At intervals there rose the deep voice of Cornstalk, calling to his braves, "Be strong! Be strong!"

Thus went on the fight until noon without much apparent advantage to either side. With every hour, however, the Indians lost a little confidence and the whites gained it. Finally the frontiersmen began to push forward and the savages gave back until they reached a spot so well protected that the whites could advance no farther.

Here the Indians amused themselves by shouting, "We have as many warriors as you; tomorrow we'll have two times as many, then you will see"; but on the morrow they were gone. Under shelter of the darkness they had crossed the 

Ohio once more. 

The whites counted their loss. 

Seventy-five had been killed or mortally hurt, and twice that number merely wounded. The Indians carried off as many dead as possible or threw them into the river to the whites found the bodies of more prevent their being scalped, but than thirty. Each side could claim success. Certainly the Indians had suffered less than the whites, but for a time they felt little desire to fight again. During the next two years the Ohio borderers had peace, while settlers came in fast. Dunmore's War" gave the Westerners a much needed breathing spell and prevented the tribes from immediately falling on the rear of the colonies as they engaged in their struggle with the British.

CHAPTER THREE 

Clark of the Ohio

Young Man on a Journey: We may now begin to see events along the Ohio through the eyes of that remarkable and inspiring young leader who has justly been called "Clark of the Ohio." In June of the year 1772, two years before "Dunmore's War," several land hunters set out in a canoe from 

Fort Pitt. One of them was a tall, athletic young fellow not yet twenty years old, with intelligent face and a crown of red hair. One of his fellow-travellers described him as "a young gentleman of Virginia inclined to make tour of the new world." 

George Rogers Clark did come from an old Virginia family with property and friends of high standing. One of his schoolmates was 

James Madison who afterward became President. George, however, had little interest in Eastern affairs.

Already he had become a man in thought and action, who saw his future in the western country to which Virginia laid claim. He would go down the Ohio to seek his fortune. 

The canoe party halted awhile at 

Wheeling, which Ebenezer Zane with his two brothers had settled three years before. This lay at the end of a trail from Redstone (or 

Brownsville), on the Monongahela, which shortened the journey down the Ohio. Instead of following the winding rivers first north, then south, past part of the region now included in the West Virginia "Panhandle," travelers by this trail could cut straight west from Redstone 

Creek to the mouth of Wheeling 

Creek. This settlement of the Zanes was then the only exclusively white community on the river below Fort Pitt. 

One hundred and twenty miles from Pittsburgh, having had no adventure except that of sleeping on hard gravel stones along the shore, the travelers steered their canoe into the Great Kanawha, and 

Clark returned home, overland. A couple of months later, with two 

Negro slaves, and with his father to start him off right, George came again to settle on a "bottom of fine land about forty miles below 

Wheeling." A bottom was the level ground where the hill-slopes retreated from the riverbank. 

Settler and Surveyor: George had two strings to his bow. Like that other George (Washington), who was so much interested in the West, he had mastered the art of surveying, which an educated mind could learn well enough in a few (21)

months, at least for all necessary purposes of that region. There was plenty of work available in that line. During the fall and winter he found considerable employment "running lines" for claim seekers.

By summer of the next year the rush of settlers had gone a couple of hundred miles past George's cabin, and he was offered a good sum of money for his land and his corn crop. 

Perhaps Clark did sell; at any rate that winter he took up a "tomahawk claim," marked by "blazes" or chips chopped off the tree trunks, at Grave Creek, now called Moundsville¹, from its great Indian-built mound, below Wheeling. By spring he had gathered a band of land seekers who were to meet at the mouth of the Little Kanawha and settle farther down on ground that Clark had picked out and for which he would do the surveying. This young giant of twenty-one was a natural leader of men.

A hunting party sent out by these whites, when they assembled, was attacked by the Shawnees. Giving up their ideas of down-river settlement, the band offered their services to Michael Cresap, and Clark was present when Cresap's followers, as previously told, shot the Indians. 

All timid settlers fled back from

West Virginia to the Monongahela, and those who remained were sheltered in blockhouses.

"Let Virginia or Congress do it," said the Kentuckians. This was an excellent suggestion, but Harrison gave Clark no men, no money, no supplies to carry it out. He wanted Clark to rule the waves of the Ohio, but how was that to be done? Clark, however, did manage to have one galley built at the Falls, under the protection of Fort Nelson. The galley was nearly seventy-five feet long, with forty-six oars and four small cannon. When the galley was launched, she was sent upstream to the mouth of the Licking. Her crew was not complete, and Clark called for volunteers from the neighborhood.

The Indians forgot their first friendly feelings and resented the "improvements" that had been made. They slaughtered the game recklessly so that the whites should not have it. By the time autumn came, wild animals, once so plentiful, had grown scarce. Winter came early that year, and so much ice choked the Ohio that no boat moved on it until March. Many of the settlers passed weeks with very little meat, and with no wheat bread, for the flour gave out. The next summer's corn crop was excellent, but on the first of October came a frost that made a great deal of it unfit for food for either man or beast. After these early trials the town prospered greatly. At first it had been called "Adelphia" or "the brothers," but during the summer of 1788 the colonists decided to give it another name. Marie Antoinette, the Queen of France, had been a friend of the American cause during the Revolution, and had treated 

Benjamin Franklin, our ambassador to France, with great kindness. It was agreed to combine her two names and to call the town "Marietta." 

The settlers loved the beauty which trees bestow upon towns. 

While they had to cut down many giants of the forest, they also left many standing and planted other varieties, such as the elm, which were not commonly found in the 

West. A rule of the "directors" made it compulsory for each landowner to set out at least fifty apple trees and twenty peach trees within three years. The result was that 

Washington County, in which Marietta is situated, became the greatest fruit county of Ohio, and held that place for more than sixty years.

Kentucky's Suffering: In the year 1790, Judge Innes of Kentucky wrote to the Secretary of War describing the dreadful suffering of the 

Kentucky settlers from the Indians.

Inspite of the fact that there were now thirty thousand people south of the Ohio, the savages from the north continued to raid them. "During the last seven years," Judge 

Innes said, "fifteen hundred persons have been killed or carried away.

Immense value of property has been destroyed and countless horses stolen. We are losing faith in our Government."

It was true. In spite of the little settlements which had begun north of the Ohio, that north bank was still called the "Indian shore." There had never been a great many Indians living on the Ohio itself, and now almost all had withdrawn to the interior in order to escape the revenge of the Kentuckians; but they were still a dreadful menace in both a lesser and a greater way. 

Beside the raids which the young "show-off" braves so often made across the river, gangs of Indians, fifty or a hundred together, lay in wait along the Ohio to leap out on any emigrants who landed, and often forced white prisoners, by cries and appeals, to lure the boats within reach. The bloody fights, the suffering, the torture recorded in the frontier chronicles of the Ohio would fill many volumes. 

The Indian Danger: As the In dian villages gradually removed farther from the Ohio shore they came nearer and nearer to the forts which the British still held in defiance of the treaty of 1783 which ended the Revolutionary War. The officers at those forts encouraged the redmen to keep back those evercoming American settlers.

The five or ten thousand warriors who hung like a threatening thundercloud between the Lakes and the Ohio had made up their minds that, as they said, "the white man shall not plant corn north of the Ohio." 

They decided that the treaties signed by various tribes, giving away land, were not to be respected.

The only treaties that mattered to them were those of 1768 at Fort

At last Rapp came to America, found a spot not far from Pittsburgh, and settled on it with nearly eight hundred of his German followers.

These peasants were poor and ignorant, but honest and industrious. They looked up to big, bearded Rapp as their absolute master and dictator, and let him fix all the rules of the community. 

While he did not exactly forbid marriage, Rapp looked upon it with little favor. His Germans, however, being more prosperous in 

Pennsylvania than ever before, were quite willing to do as he said.

Rapp, therefore, called the village Harmony. 

Ten years of experience in Pennsylvania showed Rapp and his adopted son Frederick, who helped him in his management, that the situation of Harmony was ill suited to marketing their produce. It was twelve miles from navigable water and the roads were exceedingly bad. A community cannot prosper unless it can sell; therefore Rapp and Frederick began to look for another place. In newly-settled

I am come to this country to change society from an ignorant and selfish system to one which shall remove all causes for contest between individuals."

His plans gained great publicity. 

All people interested in social improvement were discussing the New

The Results of the Experiment: 

Many fine and noble characters were numbered among the thousand persons who met the "Boatload of Knowledge" at the landing on the Wabash that January day, but there were also a great many who wanted to live at some one else's expense. New

The Slavery Controversy: About 

1830, the slavery question began to be a troubling one in the United States, and nowhere did it become more of an issue than along the 

Ohio. That great river, as it stretched for about a thousand miles, formed a line between slaveholders and non-slaveholders. On the north of the stream the Ordinance that created the Northwest Territory forbade slavery forever in the States that were to be made out of that region. 

True, in the harvest season, the farmers of Ohio hired each year about two thousand slaves from the planters of Kentucky and of West

Virginia (which then was a part of Virginia); but when the harvest had ended, the slaves went back across the river. Slave owners came across the Ohio and brought with them their human property, but the slaves did not remain in the land of the free. The rapidly growing city of Cincinnati always had plenty of Southerners living in her hotels and visiting her business establishments, and it depended largely on the trade which came. from "below the Ohio"; yet even 

Cincinnati had many inhabitants who thought slavery was absolutely wrong.

The Underground Railroad: For more than thirty years before the 

Civil War men in slave States, for money payments, and men in free 

States, out of kindness of heart, had helped escaping slaves to put as much distance as possible between themselves and their masters. It is said that in 1831 a certain fugitive

After some delay, the owner and his helper found a boat, and set off in pursuit. They saw the Negro reach shore, and thought they had him; but when they began to search, they could find no trace of the fugitive. 

In much ill-humor, the master came back into Ripley. "Where's your slave?" inquired some one. "That fellow must have gone off on an underground road," growled the owner. As there was a strong antislavery feeling in Ripley, the story caused great amusement. People laughed for many a day about the "underground road." Presently, when railroads became common, the system of hiding slaves and forwarding them far from the border was known as the "Underground Railroad." 

At Parkersburg and at Marietta the arrival of a party of fleeing slaves was announced by the hoot of an owl. At the signal a boat would come from the Ohio side to bring over the runaways. Today on the Ohio

Growth of Manufacture: And now, in the days since the war between the States, what has the march of time brought to the "Beautiful River" from which Indian and frontiersman, wigwam and blockhouse, flatboat and keelboat, have disappeared forever?

The growth of manufacturing, the development of natural resources, have changed many a stretch of the long stream.

Let us begin at the spot we call the head of the Ohio, where two rivers end and one is born. Of 

Fort Pitt only one little brick structure remains, but Pittsburgh has grown great and powerful, the center of the greatest iron and steel manufacturing district in the world. 

The three river valleys furnish ways for railroad lines, leading down the Allegheny from the oilfields and down the Monongahela from the coalfields. Not only railroads, but fleets of barges also, bring the coal and coke to the roaring blast furnaces and steel plants which line the rivers above and below Pittsburgh. The steel products of these plants, coal for other plants still farther down the Ohio, sand and gravel for concrete, all products of rather low value and heavy weight, go downstream in fleets of barges, fastened together like great rafts, measured in acres instead of square yards, and pushed by puffing sternwheel steamboats. 

At East Liverpool, not far below the Pennsylvania line, the coneshaped ovens and tall stacks of pottery factories stand out in the landscape, and these need coal brought by the river route, whose slow but sure transportation is cheaper than that by railroad.

Portsmouth, much farther along the 

Ohio, has important potteries also, and in Cincinnati is a famous establishment whose jars and dishes and vases take their place in museums as true works of art.

Up and down both sides of the narrow valley spread more plants, making themselves useful though certainly not ornamental.

Cincinnati and Louisville: Now we see Cincinnati, the second largest city in Ohio, standing where the Ohio makes a great bend north into the Corn Belt. Because the corn made possible the raising of fine fat pigs, the pork-packing establishments of Cincinnati so prospered that the "Queen City of the West" was often called "Porkopolis." The people of Cincinnati did not mind that title much, for the prosperity which came with the ear of corn and the pig enabled them to build a fine city, a center of music and art.

The old pontoon bridge here, the first to span the Ohio, thrown across by General Lew Wallace at the time of the Confederate danger, was replaced in the year after the

Not a great many miles below the slopes of Cincinnati is Louisville, which, as a necessary stop for all river travel, could hardly fail to become a place of great importance. It is that today, a city on the border, doing business with both North and South. Especially is Louisville a wonderful tobacco market, where buyers come to bargain for the Kentucky product. Unity At Last: Nevertheless, we can now appreciate, from these studies, the past and present importance of the Ohio River. First, it stands in our history as a pathway of settlement and of empire.
