FURTHER ADVENTURES WITH GRIP STRENGTH TESTS 

In 1934* one of the authors reported results of grip strength tests, on a single individual, repeated daily or weekly for six months. The fidelity with which the effective powers of forearm muscles reflect changing bodily conditions was strikingly revealed by this record. Since then three related incidents have reached his attention. The first was the investigation, by several 

Boston University students, of diurnal changes in the effective strength of forearm muscles and modifications of effective power produced by changing food habits. These experiments have not yet been completed, but enough has already been learned to prove the almost indispensable value of strength tests in physiological, or at least in industrial, educational and physical conditioning, research. Reports of a few preliminary experiments are included hereafter to demonstrate the possibilities of this line of investigation. 

The second incident was the return to active interest in testing of the subject who offered himself as the human guinea pig for the first series of grip tests reported in 1934. This individual proposed to the writer a study in fatigue tested by grip strength during a series of squash-racquet games on a single afternoon. Results were so unexpected and so startling that supplementary experiments soon followed.

The third incident was the text book comment of a well-known physiologist, whose latest work carried, in a section on measurement, the amazing statement paraphrased below:

Muscular strength tests later became popular, the dynamometer beng used to test the power of certain groups of muscles and the result employed as an index of general condition. . . . Since convalescents from infectious disease are limited in their exercise, not by what muscles can do but by the strength of the heart, the general opinion today is that strength tests do not permit us to draw satisfacory conclusions regarding general physical efficiency. . . .

The inference too is clear and quite as extraordinary: that physiologists and physical educators may safely ignore strength tests as measures of physical condition. For the comment paraphrased above was nowhere else modified or supplemented.

This opinion and inference are called amazing in view of the incontrovertible facts that (a) hundreds of industrial researchers,† civil service examiners,

The present writers exhume the above comment on strength tests as prima facie evidence of the difficulty of securing casual attention to, or even fair treatment of, one of the most challenging recent developments in the measurement of health, physical fitness, physical or organic vigor. Perhaps what follows will assist in preventing others from making similar errors.

It is hoped that the new evidence of the utility of strength tests given in this publication will induce many industrialists, physiologists and educators, skeptical of their value, to capitulate to their revelations. It is also hoped that others, who now so profitably use strength tests as adminisrative measures to classify children in school or camp, and measure results, will be stimulated to plan and execute strength testing experiments on their own initiative and in diverse directions. Those who do will be profoundly affected. . . . (It is important to note that these experiments use men as subjects. Women are likely to react very differently. In fact, women lose 15-20 per cent of effective muscular strength just before menstruation begins. At its close they have regained half of their losses. Two weeks later they are normal. 

This complication renders enormously more difficult the prediction of girls and women's physical performances in sports or any other function using muscles. Far from invalidating strength tests, however, the facts (a) that physical powers vary in accordance with menstrual states and (b) that strength scores vary likewise, serve perhaps to render the latter even more important for girls and women than for boys and men, as practical measures to determine numerous policies for both groups and individuals.

The grip strength curves of Mr. Etonixod are shown on Chart IV. To our great surprise his right grip strength (he is a right-handed player) after fading during the first three games, rose steadily and remained higher than at the start of play until six P.M. when it dropped steadily until the time of retirement. 

Contrarily, Etoxinod's left grip, after a brief fading, rose rapidly to equal his right grip, and then dropped steadily throughout play and later. At the 8th game a cramp developed in the left forearm, whenever Etoxinod was tested, depressing scores considerably lower than would have been recorded otherwise. 

How would a physiologist explain these phenomena? A layman would expect the right grip to have declined with the fatigue of exercise and, if anything, the left grip to have increased. The contrary facts indicated that the right forearm muscles were merely warmed up by exercise. (Etoxinod has an unusually strong grip and holds the racquet loosely except when actually striking the ball. He has been active in sports most of his life.) Muscular action increased the flow of blood through tissues and lymph was circulated: muscles were constantly flushed with cleansing and vitalizing fluids. 

Contrarily, the left forearm muscles were relaxed. As the body gained in tone from general exercise the strength of the grip increased. But soon fatigue products appeared: the left forearm muscles, the least active voluntary muscle group in the body, were clogged with lactic acid, and became steadily less efficient. Finally, the concentration of fatigue products became so great that certain muscles rebelled against calls on them for voluntary activity during grip tests: cramps resulted. 

A more definitely physiological explaination follows: "With continued exercise the lactic acid present in the active muscles gradually passes by diffusion into the blood and is delivered to other parts of the body. This is probably the cause of general fatigue after long-continued moderate exercise. 

It has been found that during the exercise of some muscles the venous blood from other muscles, in the resting state, may contain less lactic acid than the arterial blood coming to them. These resting muscles contain a ready store of intermediate carbohydrate and assist in the recovery of the active muscles. 

When the exercise is continued the resting muscles may become heavily loaded with fatigue products due to poorer circulation than the more active muscles. The vaso-motor system operating through the constrictors and dilators assists in bringing about this condition." 

These conclusions were apparently supported by strength trends during recovery. As the evening progressed, lactic acid became diffused throughout the body and moved into the right forearm muscles which, cooling and relaxing, lost power. Concurrently, left grip strength increased, as the concentration of lactic acid in inactive muscles was reduced. 

Thus did a single experiment upset a preconceived notion concerning the probable effects on grip strength of exercise and general bodily fatigue. But

The effects of fatigue now became apparent in two ways. Playing Opponent Y (the best of the three) he began missing strokes with discouraging regularity. His grip scores declined steadily, even the right grip falling off rapidly. During the last game he hardly moved from the back court. 

Comparing the curves for this day with those for others seems to confirm several previous observations for Etoxinod. (a) Right grip increases and remains high until general fatigue becomes fairly severe. (b) Left grip drops steadily from the time true fatigue begins. (c) Left grip decline proceeds in fairly well-defined waves. (21) Are these waves due to chemical processes-raising the production of adrenalin for example? 22 Or are they primarily psychological? (d) Grip strength increases or declines in a fairly steady curve until fatigue becomes pronounced, when pulsation in power becomes quite marked. (23) Can this pulsation be eliminated by training, or is it due to changes in attention or concentration during the games just preceding the tests? 

Our case for left grip strength as a better barometer of actual immediate condition than right grip strength might be fairly clear had we not tested the opponents, and remembered subject C's record (see Chart II). Their records, carried also on Chart VIII, constitute conclusive evidence of great individual differences in response to almost identical experimental conditions. 

Opponent Z responded almost as did Y in his previous exercise. But Opponent X declined precipitately in right grip while gaining extraordinarily in left grip, raising it in fact over 10 per cent higher than he had ever done before. 

Apparent reasons for these phenoma were two. First, X carried tremendous enthusiasm and power to every stroke. He took the first game easily while

Etoxinod relaxed somewhat. Second, his concentrated efforts continued. (24) 

Did he lose right grip strength because of his mental concentration and consequent unremitting grip on the racquet? (25) Was the left grip rise a reflection of steadily increasing emotional and physiological resources? (26) 

To what extent was the flow of adrenalin stimulated? 

Finally, observe the reversal in the direction of Y's curves (in Chart VIII) from those in previous tests. Chart VII shows results when he was under the pressure of superior opposition. His grip strength dropped rapidly at the outset and remained on the lower level with only a slight later rise when not injured. But when playing an exhausted opponent his grip strength, after a brief drop, rose rapidly. (27) Is this opponent impairing his performance by tensing all muscles when meeting strong opposition? He has been so advised by instructors months before. (28) Did the grip tests reveal a psychological attitude reflected in effective muscular power? 

VII 

That muscular strength tests-particularly using the manuometer, which is inexpensive, easy to handle and which requires relatively little expenditure of energy to operate are potentially tremendously valuable techniques of physiological research seems amply demonstrated by the foregoing report. Apparently grip strength, which the manuometer (hand dynamometer) measures with a high degree of validity and reliability, reflects bodily conditions with considerable accuracy. Apparently bodily changes during any particular day have considerable effect on effective muscular powers. Apparently the average individual is most effective physically at approximately 11 A.M. and 3 P.M. 

Apparently there are many and various individual differences both in grip strength and in fatigue. Possibly the grip of the hand least used in sports is a better health barometer than the one most used. Apparently the effects of painful, if minor, injuries reduce effective strength and general condition to a marked degree. Apparently skill becomes erratic as fatigue sets in, and the tendency to err is reflected in pulsating muscular power. Apparently intense concentration on play-or work?—against superior opposition only further harms the player—or worker?- -: he would do better if he cared less or were driven less! At least his physical powers would wane less rapidly. Apparently, as general endurance increases from day to day and week to week, grip strength declines less rapidly, and over a shorter range.

Of course no conclusive generalizations can be drawn from such brief and meager data. But, for alert personnel directors, industrialists, educators and physiologists, certain values are already fairly certain. (1) Tests of muscular strength may prove invaluable to employers not only in selecting for employment the most capable applicants, but also in checking their condition from hour to hour or day to day or at greater intervals. (2) Strength tests may prove indispensable to physiologists in determining proper eating and rest habits. (3) Strength tests may be far more significant to parents in checking the daily health and growth of children than any other or combination of other measures now available or proposed. (4) Grip tests are likely to prove invaluable to athletic coaches in the selection of players and substitutes for any game using large muscles and in deciding when or whether to return tired or injured players to games. Correlative applications in industry will quickly suggest themselves to employers and personnel directors. (5) Psychologists can hardly afford any longer to ignore strength tests. 

The S-R bond or reflex arc usually ends in a muscle cell! (6) Physical educators are likely to learn or confirm more about their pupils' physical needs, and possibly their psychological needs, through repeated strength tests than by most procedures now used or known. Physical education is by definition education which at least, and always, involves large muscle activity. It would seem to be only the most elementary logic to conclude that tests of muscular power are as fundamental to physical education as tests of intelligence are to mental education. Actual practice or experience indicates that the relation is much closer in physical education than in mental education. (7) The grip test alone may well become a highly valid measure of physical endurance. Let the subject repeat the test every ten seconds and record his scores. When scores are plotted, the shape of the resulting curve, or the number of repetitions before he loses one-third of his grip strength is likely to be a highly valid measure of general endurance. (8) Every new experiment with strength tests further confirms the validity of the P.F.I.* as a measure of physical fitness. The experiments described in this report are perhaps not the least significant of a long array of studies. (9) If grip tests reveal so much what will other strength tests-of back, legs, arms-show? Physiologists are likely to find in strength tests a technique of measurement more stimulating and revealing than any of us can now imagine. VIII 

Need the authors of this report protest their own awareness that the experiments reported herein are deficient on a dozen counts? They know it well. 

Our chief purpose is to stimulate others as well as ourselves to conduct the hundreds of confirmatory and new experiments suggested by these first shots almost in the dark.

We wish to point out to experimenters, however, the necessity of taking account of the nature of each experimental subject, about whom should be known, if research is to be significant, and among other items, his or her sex; weight; age; S.I.; P.F.I.; I.Q. if possible; race; personality rating; physical habits; and a detailed history of activities just preceding experiments. It is important, too, to discover whether acquainting the subject with his scores at the time of testing has any effects on performances. This is the case with subjects new to testing. It must be less true for experienced subjects, but how much less?*
