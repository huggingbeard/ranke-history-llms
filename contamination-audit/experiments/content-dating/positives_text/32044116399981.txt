CHAPTER I: OCCUPATION 

The necessity for the military occupation of Germany for a time, at least, after the surrender of her armed forces is a foregone conclusion, except for the unlikely eventuality of a successful democratic revolution before the defeat on the field of battle, and the setting up of an orderly government which would be prepared to cooperate with the Allies.1 This is conceded even by those who demand non-discriminatory treatment for Germany. Moreover, even if a genuinely democratic government should be set up which was capable of administering political authority, it would still need the protection of an occupation force to prevent the militarist-expansionist elements from regaining power. In fact, many anticipate that occupation will not be confined to Germany but will be extended through most if not all of German-occupied countries in Europe. Opinions from Norweigian, British and Canadian, as well as American, sources to this effected are here cited. "For purely military reasons it will be necessary to keep the Allied forces under arms for a considerable time when the war is over. And it is well that this is now being generally recognized. Few persons in the allied countries can fail to admit today that it was a disastrous mistake not to occupy Germany after the last war....The same thing must not happen again when this war is over. Even the calmest and most objective observers cannot fail to see this point."2 ff "The defeat of Germany is bound to be followed by an extensive military occupation. But this occupation should be effected as an operation of war, says Carr, who also points out that an army of occupation in the winter of 1918-19 could have enabled the new German government to maintain order. In contrast to this is the method adopted in those areas of Italy that have been occupied by the British and American troops. 

General Eisenhower, as commander-in-chief of the Allied forces, established the armistice conditions, which have never been made public, and the territory is being administered by the A. M. G. (Allied

The purpose of the occupation, it is universally agreed, would be to restore and maintain order, to restore transportation, and to distribute relief-food, shelter, clothing and medical-supplies and to supervise disarmament. What other functions and responsibilities should be added to these, whether they should include also punishment of war crimes, supervision or direction of long-range planning for Germany's economic life and re-education, depends upon agreements to be reached regarding the. treatment of Germany.

Another most question is whether the Allies or United Nations should set up a civil government as well as a military government in 

Germany. Kingsbury Smith reports: "It is felt that as soon as Germany has been completely occupied, a supreme allied military and civil government should take over. The government would control the administration of Germany;; supervise the total disarmament of its air, land and sea forces; and direct the decentralization of its industries and general readjustment of its economic life."3

This course contemplates an Allied government of Germany which will continue for a considerable period. This is also the view of the 

Vansittart school of opinion previously mentioned. On the other hand, those who take the opposite view believe that in order to enlist German cooperation, without which there will be no hope of a real peace, civil administration must be loft as far as possible in German hands, protected, of course, by the military forces of occupation.

If there is to be a civil government as well as a military occupation, what should be the relation between them? Should the military or the civilian authorities be given the final power? 

This is a highly controversial question that has been heatedly discussed ever since the allied forces entered North Africa. 

This question is discussed by Benjamin Akzin, who believes that about the worst of all possible solutions would be a mixed military and civilian occupation regime. "In the circumstances," he says, "it will be better to choose between an essentially military administration, with civilian officials attached in advisory and subordinate capacities, and a fundamentally civilian regime, with commanders of troops restricted purely to military function." His choice is: "at first,...one of military govemment, with supreme authority vested in military commanders, and with subordinate military officers responsible for general administration in districts and localities. Civil- ian officials would a ct mainly in an advisory capacity; they might also be called in to perform special and technical tasks, especially of relief and rehabilitation, but clearly under the authority of the military administration."1

The United States Army has stated its position on the point clearly. Brig. General Cornelius W. Wickersham, Commandant of the School of Military Government in Charlottesville, Virginia, told the American Society of International Law that the commanding general of the occupying forces becomes automatically the military governor and has the supreme authority.2 Under him is a staff officer in charge of civil affairs. He called attention, however, to the fact that, the officers in charge are administrators; matters of policy are decided by the 

President or State Department. Control by the army, he emphasized, lasts only during the period of military necessity and until a civil govemment can be reestablished or organized.3 Similarly, "in the words of Under Secretary of War Robert F. Patterson," says Motherwell, "military government 'is limited, in point of time to the period of emergency that is closely tied to the conduct of the campaign against the enemy. 

4When military necessity ceases and civilian government can take control, government by military authority comes to an end.' 

This military doctrine holds that military government shall be supreme and exclusive for a period limited in time. The opposing view is that it should be limited in scope, almost from the very beginning. 

Certainly the military must be the judge of strict military necessity. 

But what is done in the first few months of the armistice may fix the mold of subsequent reconstruction for years--and fix it perhaps awry." ".

There is a successful precedent for such a policy in the organization of the International Force set up under the 'auspices of the League of Nations to maintain order in the Saar district of Germany during the plebiscite of 1935.1 This international force was composed of contingents from Great Britain, Italy, Holland and Sweden.

A proposal has been made by Louis Adamic in "Two-Way Passage", that second or third generation German-Americans should be used for the civilian personnel on the ground that these would be able to constitute a bridge between America and the land of their origin, and that they would thus be in a position to aid in the re-education of Germans in democratic ideals. On the whole this proposal has not been enthusiastically received, though it has some warm adherents. Whatever their nationality, the training of the personnel for the various tasks that go with occupation is being given special attention. The training of the civil affairs officers at Charlottesville, includes instruction in Army organization and staff functioning, public administration, the principles and experiences of military government, international law and the intensive study of the principal enemy countries, including the language. They will be assisted in the field by the necessary experts who will be trained at a number of institutions and by junior assistants obtained from the military police carps.

CHAPTER II: RELIEF AND REHABILITATION 

The problem of relief and rehabilitation for the greater part of the continent of Europe was the first of the post-war problems to be made the subject of serious consideration and action by national governments as well as by private organizations. The United Nations 

Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (known as UNRRA) is also the first piece of United Nations machinery to be formally established. 

It was automatically created by the signing of the agreement by representatives of forty-four nations at the White House on Novomber 9, 1943.

This act, followed by the meeting of the Council of UNRRA at Atlantic City on November 10, was the culmination of three years of planning and activities. As early as August 1940 .

CHAPTER III: PUNISHMENT OF WAR CRIMINALS 

The punishment of Germans guilty of atrocities and other acts contrary to the accepted laws of war toward the citizens of the occupied countries or toward prisoners of war has been the object of special study and concerted official action. Not only is there unanimity among the Allied governments in their intention to bring war criminals to justice, but it has been expressly stated by their spokesmen that henceforth among the major purposes of the war must be included the exaction of 1 retribution for the cold-blooded execution of innocent people. 

An Inter-Allied Commission on War Crimes in London has been assembling evidence. Furthermore, a United Nations Commission for the Investigation of War Crimes, while not yet officially organized, has already held two meetings in London and, according to the British Foreign Office, has agreed upon its final arrangements. Conference issued as one of its documents a statement signed by 

President Roosevelt, Prime Minister Churchill, and Premier Stalin re garding atrocities and the treatment to be accorded to those responsible for them. 2 The importance attributed to this question is attested by the fact that the first joint official declaration made after that of January 1, 1942, creating the group of the United Nations, was addressed to this subject. APPENDIX Report of the Subcommittee on the Trial andFPunishment of War Criminals 

Responsible leaders of the United Nations have charged the forces of the Axis powers with war crimes and have announced their intention to punish, "through the channel of .organized justice, "those guilty of or responsible for such crimes, "whether they have brdered them, perpetrated them or participated in them." The Axis has been warned that war criminals will be required to "stand in courts of law in the very countries which they are now oppressing and answer for their acts."

Whatever shape this project may ultimately take, it is of the utmost importance that punishment be administered "through the channel of organized justice." In the present report your subcommittee attempts no more than a brief statement of some of the principal problems and of suggestions for their solution. 

1. The problem of most immediate urgency, and one basic to the entire project, is the assembling of evidence. Justice cannot operate on the basis of mere hunch or vague charges of atrocities. The work of assembling, appraising, and organizing the evidence can and must be done now. It is vital that this work be done accurately, comprehensively, and expeditiously. 

Both military and civilian officials of the United Nations in a position to obtain evidence should be carefully instancted to record and report in authentic detail concerning the place, time, and nature of alleged wrongful acts, the identity of alleged wrongdoers, the identity of injured persons, the identity and availability of possible witnesses, the preparation and preservation of adequate records, etc. Procedures for recording and reporting should be as nearly uniform as may be practicable. 

No pertinent item of apparent authenticity should be overlooked. 

It is assumed thatthis vital work will go forward under the supervision of and in response to encouragement from the United Nations Commission. Unless this work is done thoroughly and well, the entire project may be frustrated. 

2. On the basis of the evidence which is being assembled and assessed, it will be necessary to determine with considerable precision the categories of wrongdoers who are to be charged and tried. Mere dragnet procedures are rarely satisfactory and will certainly serve no useful purpose in the present circumstances. The plea of superior orders or of justified retaliation should not be permittedto establish broad immunities and the restricted operation of such pleas should be anticipated and carefully defined. It will be well to concentrate upon the more heinous offenses and upon those who can be shown to have been substantially responsible therefor. This implies considerable selectivity in the determination of categories. Efforts should be directed toward the successful prosecution of the more serious offenders rather than towerd the mere prosecution of large numbers. It should be ever in mind that the objective is not revenge but vindication of the processes of organized justice.

3. is the various types of evidence are assembled and the categories of offenses determined, it will be necessary to anticipate problems likely to arise in connection with the rendition of offenders. In anticipation of the unconditional surrender of the Axis powers, the procedures of demand and rendition should be precisely and uniformly established, It is certain that efforts will be made to facilitate escape and frustrate rendition. Such efforts should be anticipated and forestalled. Simple, uniform, and expeditious procedures should be in readiness and should be vigorously executed with such measures of force as may be required. The problems of refuge in third states should also be anticipated and the United Nations should be in agreement with respect to limitations upon efforts to obtain asylum and with respect to the measures which will be taken where an ill-founded right to give asylum is obviously desirable. 

4. With the evidence assembled, the offenses determined, and the offenders surrendered, it will be necessary to agree upon the tribunals which are to try the offenders. Problems will arise with respect to the nature, constitution, place of sitting, jurisdiction, and procedure of such tribunals. The Axis has been warned that offenders will be required to stand trial "in courts of law in the very countries which they are now oppressing." The warning is well conceived and significant. It would appear to negative the idea that international courts need be specially constituted to conduct the trials. Most of the more heinous offenses have been committed in territories of one or another of the United Nations, against the security of the United Nations, or against nationals of the United Nations. If the trials are conducted, at least in the first instance, before civil or military courts of the United Nations, we shall have the advantages of experienced tirbunals, constituted of experienced personnel, having authority by virture of the place of the wrongful acts or the objects of the wrongs committed, and equipped at the outset with established legal procedures. 

With such resources available, we may well restrain the impulse to set up tribunals ad hoc for a special and limited purpose. In the interest of uniformity, however, and as a safeguard against excessive judgments inspired by the bitterness which total war has naturally engendered, consideration may well be given the suggestion that the United Nations agree to constitute a single international tribunal ad hoc which should be authorizedto undertake advisory review upon sufficient showing of excessive penalty or of error of fact of law which has resulted in manifest injustice. 

5. It is apparent that trial "in courts of law" and "through the channel of organized justice" means trial according to law. The resources of international law are at hand and trials should be had pursuant thereto. It has long been an accepted principle of international law that a belligerent may punish with appropriate penalties members of the enemy forces within its custody who have violated the laws and customs of war. In some measure such laws and customs have been reduced to conventional agreement, as in the rules annexed to the Hague Convention Concerning the Laws and Customs of War on Land. principles of criminal law generally accepted among civilized nations are also a proper source of international law. If doubts arise, there should be prompt agreement upon a restatement of what the pertinent law has been and the procedures and penalties which it permits. Cautĥon The

44should be exercised to exclude anything which may fairly be de nounced as legislation after the event. As previously emphasized, the aim must be vindication of law and justice and not mere revenge. 

6. Since experience with procedures of this kind has hitherto been fragmentary and relatively unsatisfactory, and in view of the im portance of maintaining all procedures undertaken on a high and humane level, it may be desirable that the United Nations agree. in advance on certain general principles governing the penalties to be imposed. 

Obviously the penalties should be determined with due regard for the enormity of the offense and the responsibility of the offender. Unification of penalties through the device of advisory review, as sugcested in paragraph 4, supra, may provide auseful safeguard, but it willavoid misunderstanding and inspire confidence if agreement on essentials can be announced before the proceedings are instituted. Within broad lines and without attempting to prejudge cases, this should become possible as soon as the vital work of assembling the evidence is sufficiently advanced. 

7. As repeatedly emphasized throughout this report, the trial and punishment of war criminals should be before civil or military courts of law, should be conductedaccording to law, and should have as its primary purpose the vindication of the rule of law to which the United Nations are committed. As regards the element of intent, broadly conceived, there should be no difficulty. The Axis leaders have long since warned us of the kind of war which they intendedto wage. Formalized vengeance can bring only an ephemeral satisfaction, with every probability of ultimate regret; but vindication of law through legal processes may contribute substantially to the reestablishment and decency in international relations. 

Respectfully submitted, of order

Edwin D. Dickinson, Chairman

At the opening of the General Disarmament Conference in Geneva in 1932, when Chancellor Bruening was head of the German delegation, he offered to accept a disarmament treaty which made no immediate change in the status of German armaments provided it set a term to these disarmament clauses of Versailles--of five years, or even ten. 

This was what was called "equality of treatment. Ultimately equality in armaments was envisaged. If the French had been willing to accept this offer, a treaty might have been concluded which would have carried strict provisions for supervision and control, but they were not. This experience suggests that the permanent system of general security envisaged by the Atlantic Charter, looking forward to eventual equality among nations, may well be essential unless Germany. is.to be kept permanently disarmed by force.

How is the disarmament of Germany to be supervised and enforced?

This is a highly technical subject to which little attention has been given in recent publications or public utterances except to assign the task of executing the measures of disarmament to the forces of occupation presumably under the authority of the victorious Allies or the United Nations as a body.

It may be useful, however to point out that work on the technical aspects of disarmaments was completed by the General Conference on the Reduction and Limitation of Armaments (usually referred to as the 

Disarmament Conference) held in Geneva, February 2,1932 to April 14, 1935. The conference failed to achieve a disarmament treaty not because of the technical difficulties but because there was no agreement on the necessary political conditions." This technical basis is therefore ready to be applied, subject to the necessary attention to subsequent developments in military strategy, either to the unilateral disarmament of Germany or, later, to an international agreement on armaments and collective security. Among the separate draft conventions awaiting only the political basis for agreement are that on supervision, including the establishment of a Permanent Disarmament Commission; that on the prohibition of chemical warfare; that on publicity of national defense expenditure, and on the manufacture of and trade in arms, amunition and the impliments of war. Its provisions, so far as they applied to purely national control, were later incorporated into the Neutralit y Act, setting up the National Munitions Control Board, and the method has been tested with success, according to official reports. Economic disarmament. 

Total disarmament goes much further than merely destruction of armaments and demobilization of personnel. The Treaty of Versailles also prohibited the manufacture of and trade in arms, except for certain arms in supervised factories, while the manufacture of many categories, such as tanks, submarines, and airplanes was prohibited altogether. Current proposals for restricting manufacture of arms in themselves are much more drastic than the Versailles provisions. 

Their objective includes a large measure of economic disarmament in order to insure against the possibility of German military rearmament in the future. They cover not only the prohibitions of the manufacture of mnitions but the complete elimination of German heavy industry, or alternatively, its control by an international commission or trusteeship; the control of the use of certain strategic materials necessary for the manufacture of armaments and the control of German foreign trade.

International Police Force 

Closely related to the question of the disarmament of Germany and its enforcement, as well as to the more general objective of the prevent ion and repression of future aggression, is the organization of an international police force. The sentiment in favor of such a force, is sufficiently widespread, often supported by official spokesmen, that the question arises as to how possible German participation would affect the problem of armament control. 

Here it is necessary to be clear as to what is meant by an international police force, whether an agency of inspection, supervision and control or a military force for the preservation of order and peace. If it is the former, set up to enforce German disarmament, the question is automatically answered in the negative. Similarly, if it is the latter, there can be no question of German participation during the period of her unilateral disarmament. Upon the establishment of the permanent system of general security, however, envisaged by the Atlantic Charter, the Moscow Declaration and the Connally Resolution [S. 192] the question does arise whether Germany should be allowed to contribute her share to the international armed force, if such a force is established and/or to share in its administration. CHAPTER VI REPARATIONS 

The case for reparations against Germany after the war has been prejudiced by the experience of the Allies after the last war.

The economic provisions of the Treaty of Versailles, including reparations, are commonly regarded as its worst feature. It is these provisions against which Lord Keynes's criticism was directed when he characterized the Versailles settlement as "a Carthaginian peace. "1 Today no one attempts to defend them. Whatever may be thought about reparations in principle, there is little difference of opinion on the proposition that the mistakes of the Treaty of Versailles regarding reparations must not be repeated. 

The arguments against reparations of this type and magnitude, taking into account past experience, are: First, that they are impracticable, as shown by the fact that up to 1932 the Germans paid back in reparations no more than they received in loans, mostly from American business men, and then repudiated the loans; and that all attempt at collection was dropped by the agreement of 

Lausanne in 1932; Second, that they prevented economic recovery and led indirectly to the German inflation, thus arousing more bittemess during the post-war period than any other part of the Versailles Treaty, and therefore militated against efforts at reconciliation; Third, that any sum so large as to put a whole nation in debt for several generations, which any adequate reparation would be, upsets the economic balance of the whole world. These arguments, however, apply chiefly, and perhaps exclusively, to reparations in cash, which were demanded after the last war. There is a case for the demand that a nation responsible for aggression should make restoration for damage done and property destroyed, as far as possible, provided payment can be made in another medium than cash, i.e. goods and services. It is generally held, however, that the demand should be reasonable and the time of payment limited. The following excerpts quoted from statesmen, historians and economists bear on these points. "Aside from the loss of life, the cost of the war to the victorious nations will be more than the whole national wealth of the Axis Powers. Therefore, there can be no real reparations.... The first World War proved that no considerable sums could be collected in any event...The fact is that there cannot be any continuing intergovernmental debt of consequential amounts between governments in either reparations or loans....Certainly experience shows that no nation can be punished as a whole and leave any hope of lasting peace. ...We can have peace or we can have revenge, but we cannot have both."2

CHAPTER VII: RE-EDUCATION OF GERMANY 

If the test of a successful post-war policy towards Germany is that it shall contribute to reconstruction and the hope for a peaceful world, then the re-education of Germany is fundamental. 

The success or failure of the new peace settlement, and with that the future of mankind, will depend on the success or failure of the re-education of Germany."1 This means that the thinking of the German people must be reoriented from expansionist ambitions, from imperialism, from the doctrine of race and the glorification of war, back to pride in intellectual achievement, to respect for the individual, and for the rights of other nations, and to the spirit of cooperation--in short to the ideals of demo cracy and the four freedoms. 

It is not necessary to wait until after the war to begin the educational process. In the broad sense of the term, it is being carried on now by radio from Washington, London and Moscow, wherever there are German prisoners of war, and in every other way where contact with the German people is possible. After the occupation takes place, when the radio, the press, the cinema and other avenues of communication come into the hands of the Allies, the situation can be immediately utilized to continue these services that are now known as psychological warfare. First of all the German people will have to be brought up to date on the facts of the war; they may be told why the United Nations are fighting and what kind of a world they hope to have come out of the peace. In this way Germany's intellectual issolation may be broken down and the first steps taken by way of popular education toward indo ctrination with the ideals of democracy. The soul of a people can be changed. But here agreement stops. Opinions differ on the degree to which re-education is possible, the means by which it is to be brought about, and by whom, and the time that must be allowed for the process to achieve results.

The convinced Nazis, then, may not present too formidable a problem. But the spirit of German militarism did not arise.. with Hitler, and the German school system, from elementary schools to universities, has been a hot bed of aggressive nationalism.

It is the effect of this education, this rampant nationalism, that must be eradicated. Moreover, one explanation given for the failure of the republic is that Germans have never had the training of selfgovernment, do not understand democracy and probably do not want it. 

It will require a long, slow process of education before they are ready fully to embrace democratic principles. 

Lord Vansittart says the cure will have to be drastic and largely self-administered; that without a fundamental change of soul no other cure can be permanent. He believes it will take a generation at least. 

Dr. Friedrich W. Foerster, a former German educator, who was exiled from Germany some twenty years ago because he exposed official violations of the Versailles Treaty, puts the period of regeneration at twenty-five years, during which the Germans will have to be kept under restraint.

Granted that a change of ming can be achieved through a process of re-education and that steps to this end should be taken by the United Nations, the question arises over methods. Shall a new education be imposed from the outside or shall re-education be left to the Germans themselves, perhaps to the proven anti-Nazis, provided a sufficient nucleus can be found after the victory? What should be done about text books and teachers? And, most important of all, will the measures popularly considered necessary to insure against further aggression--military defeat, occupation, disarmament --produce a psychological atmosphere that will render re-orientation of the spirit impossible?

CHAPTER IX: ECONOMIC FUTURE OF GERMANY 

The various proposals for the economic treatment of Germany necessarily have been considered as an integral part of the general policy. They will be brought together here for the purpose of getting a picture of Germany's economic future in the light of the proposals made.

At once the controversy arises, as in every other field, regarding the use of discriminatory treatment. Here a difference is to be noted as compared with the political field, For whereas all who believe in a policy of discrimination of any kind apply it to the solution of political problems there is a large block of opinion in favor of generous, even equal, economic treatment of the Germans, combined with support of the policy of unconditional surrender, military oc- ..cupation, punishment of war criminals and total disarmament. Professor J. B. Condliffe, now of Yale University, expressed the judgment of an economist who, as author for several years of the World Economic Survey of the League of Nations, has also had a wide international experience. "After this war, the peace must be politically hard, but it ought to be economically generous. 

The Royal Institute of International Affairs, in a group study on the Problem of Germany, interprets the Atlantic Charter itself as by implication having made the choice between force and cooperation in the economic field, through articles four and five, pointing out "a clear risk of conflict" between its economic and disarmament clauses which might present a dilemma between prosperity and security. "Cooperation with Germany in the economic and social field...would mean that no discrimination is made against the Germans of a kind to affect their prosperity as individuals; more positively, that the economic welfare of 

Germans as individuals, as of their neighbors and ourselves, is an object of our policy."

In a very few years after the crisis of 1873, German grain growers not only lost their markets but also became a high-cost area threatened by constantly cheapening American wheat even in their own home markets. CHAPTER X: 

SOLUTION OF THE GERMAN PROBLEM IN TERMS OF INTERNATIONAL ORGANIZATION 1. Introduction 

If we assume that the treatment of Germany will be determined not on the basis of either punitive justice or vengeance but in the interest of an enduring peace in Europe and the World--a policy which is in harmony with the Atlantic Charter--it can be seen from the foregoing analysis of proposals that the Allies are still locked on the horns of a dilemma. "The key point of a successful post-war treatment of Germany is the reconciliation of two apparently irreconcileable propositions: (a) that the German people can never again be trusted not to abuse their strength by trying to trample on weaker neighbors; and (b) that any settlement which discriminates against and refuses to trust the German people contains the seeds of another German war."1 

It is the emphasis on the one or the other of these propositions-sometimes to the exclusion of the other that has led to the different approaches to the problem. One way of resolving the dilemma, of which examples have been given, is to confine discriminatory treatment to the German state while offering opportunity and even equality to the individual citizen, that is, stern political combined with generous economic treatment. This seems to be the line taken in the Atlantic Charter and by the Anglo-Soviet Treaty of May 26, 1942. It obviously involves the risk of antagonizing those Germans who put the interests of the State ahead of those of the individual, thus rendering peaceful cooperation more difficult. This risk is clearly seen by the advocates of the policy but they consider it less than the risk of further acts of German aggression.

The other solution is the international solution--to make Germany a member of a larger unit, a European or world or ganization, or both, and therefore subject to its controls. This plan also has its risks, not of 

German non-acceptance but of German disloyalty. Such disloyalty would have to be guarded against by guarantees as watertight as could be devised. The advantages urged for the latter solution are first, its permanency, and, second, that it would hold out to the Germans the promise that after the probationary period of transition all discrimination would be removed by the fact that obligations and controls would apply equally to other member nations. This solution is not necessarily inconsistent with the former (but is rather its complement, carrying it to the logical conclusion) for many of the proposals, already listed involve some kind of international organization or authority. Moreover, it is not inconsistent with the Atlantic Charter or with the Moscow and Teheran agreements, and is implied, if not definitely supported, in the recent utterances of many of the Allied statesmen.

Its boundaries are not fixed. 2 There is too much diversity between the various European countries: differences in their internal structure, some being democracies while other are monarchies or even dictatorships; differences in their economic policy, and in their traditions and religion. Adding to this original diversity the intensification of nationalism due to the war and the resulting new emphasis on independence and national so vereignty, there little prospect is seen of obtaining the consent of the various European governments to the necessary limitations of sovereignty involved in the establishment of a federation, whether it be political or merely economic, in the form of a customs union.
