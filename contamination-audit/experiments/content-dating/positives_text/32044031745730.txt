SIR: About a year ago Major Bishop informed me that his association, the Research Organization on Military Education, had received some money which could be used for the purpose of studying the status of military training in the colleges. In view of the fact that it was a much discussed question in the schools at the time, I decided to assist him by sending out a questionnaire to R. O. T. C. graduates. 

About 16,000 questionnaires were mailed; more than 10,000 returns were received. All the people who received these blanks were graduates in the period extending from 1920 to 1930. Coming from those who have had experience with the course the returns speak for themselves.

A STUDY OF THE EDUCATIONAL VALUE OF MILITARY INSTRUCTION IN COLLEGES AND UNIVERSITIES INTRODUCTION 

The educational value of military training has been a matter of debate since the earliest appearance of such training in the college curriculum over a century ago.

Since the World War, the debate has become somewhat more pointed. 

Criticism has expressed itself in organized opposition to certain aspects of such training, especially to military education as a required part of a college education. Thus far there have been relatively little reliable data bearing upon the question, and therefore the discussion has been characterized by bias on both sides. 

It is possible that at some later time objective tests of the value of various types of military training may be available, and can be satisfactorily applied as a better basis for determining the value of this subject as a component in the training of a well-educated man. 

In the absence of such objective tests, the most helpful data on which to premise reasonably sound conclusions would appear to be a wide sampling of the opinions of those who have actually undergone such training and have had the opportunity to evaluate it in terms of what they regard as its effect upon their own lives. That such evaluations may be faulty in individual cases is recognized. However, considerable preponderance of opinion pro or con as to the effect of a common experience may reasonably be given weight in an inquiry to determine the character of the reaction resulting from such an experience, whether it be military training or of any other sort. 

It seems also that the opinions of those who have actually undergone a given experience may be assumed to be of more positive value as a basis for conclusions than opinions expressed by those who have not themselves undergone the experience, reactions to which are being studied. On the foregoing premises this study has been undertaken. 

The curriculum of the Reserve Officers' Training Corps of the senior division consists of a 4-year course, divided into basic and advanced courses, each of two years' duration. The work of the basic course consumes 96 hours per academic year, or 3 hours per week, of which 60 hours are devoted to practical exercises out of doors when the weather 1

permits, while 36 hours are used for theoretical instruction in the classroom. The primary purpose is to teach the fundamentals of military tactics, and, through disciplinary training, to develop organization and teamwork. The basis upon which the course is offered is entirely a matter for institutional decision. At present the course is required of freshmen and sophomores at 80 of the 126 institutions maintaining senior units of the Reserve Officers' Training Corps. 

The advanced course is entirely optional and operates on a schedule of five hours per week for both junior and senior years. In addition, the trainees must pledge themselves to attend one 6-week training camp during the summer following completion of their junior year. 

The aim of the advanced course is to give specialized instruction in that branch of service chosen by the candidate for commission, and to afford practical training in leadership and command. The course is more technical in nature than the basic work, which consists largely of tactical training.

The following study reports the results of a questionnaire sent only to graduates who completed the basic and the advanced R. O. T. C. courses.¹ In view of their experience with the entire R. O. T. C. curriculum they should be well qualified to evaluate educationally the course as a whole. However, it is realized that those graduates who took only the basic course, either voluntarily or as a requirement, may have an entirely different estimate of the value of military training, and of the wisdom of including it as a curriculum requirement. It is not expected that conclusions can be drawn covering so controversial a question as optional versus required training without a special study of the views of this group. It is hoped that this study may be followed by an inquiry to college graduates who took the basic course, voluntarily or as a requirement, but who did not elect the advanced training.

METHODS AND PROCEDURE OF THIS STUDY 

A questionnaire containing 8 principal questions was sent out to 16,416 graduates of the 1920 to 1930 classes, inclusive, of the 54 institutions selected, accompanied by a letter from the Commissioner of Education, urging compliance. The names and addresses were gathered from college catalogues, alumni association offices, and the Adjutant General's files. The extent of the inquiry among graduates of a specific institution was largely based on the size of the institution and the number of R. O. T. C. units maintained. The exception to this was the smaller college, whose entire graduate list was usually covered. MILITARY INSTRUCTION DETAILED ANALYSIS OF OPINIONS 

In addition to the replies summarized in the foregoing table, it has been found desirable to include a more complete discussion of each question based upon a large number of expressions of opinion that could not be tabulated in the simple form of "yes" or "no." These opinions have been carefully analyzed with the purpose of giving a more complete interpretation of the attitudes of the graduates. 

1. In your opinion, has the R. O. T. C. military course of study a definite educational value of its own?—According to the data received, 97.1 per cent of those who replied to this question are agreed that military training has a definite educational value, apart from its purely military aspects. The intensity of this conviction varies according to the individual, the institution such individual attended, and the major course of instruction he pursued. The graduates view the training as rounding out the college program, whether it be academic or technical, by emphasizing things which make for personal efficiency. They point out that the success of college men in large industrial organizations is accelerated by the practical type of training in principles of organization, cooperation, and leadership, which is given by the course.

Military training is depicted as providing the student with a coordinated and balanced course in human relations, which combines a practical period of apprenticeship with several years of theory, demonstration, and application. The outdoors is pictured as the laboratory for the disciplinary and applicatory training; the classroom for the study and solution of military problems. A considerable number point out that the tactical decisions involved in these problems call for analytical treatment, skill in judgment, and accuracy in decision. It is contended that such training makes for a disciplined mind, has a practical application to the daily tasks of life, and is in line with advanced educational practice. 

The course in military history giving the broader aspects of American military policy appears highly regarded from an educational standpoint. According to the replies, the events of history are accurately treated and critically examined as a means of demonstrating the effect of preparation and leadership upon success. 

A number advanced the thought that education should concern itself with the physical, as well as with the intellectual, and stressed the fact that military training affords physical development to the mass rather than intensive exercise to the few. The summer camp period was stated as being especially efficacious in accomplishing this physical improvement. Proper posture, outdoor recreation, systematic exercise, a knowledge of sanitation and hygiene are attributes of military training believed to be of educational value.

2. Did the R. O. T. C. contribute anything important or unique to your education?-Among the opinions of the 94.9 per cent who gave an affirmative answer to this question are noted the following: Military training developed confidence, coordination of mind and muscle, and contributed practical training in leadership. The disciplinary exercises taught courtesy, respect for authority, and the value of individual effort to mass accomplishment. The training contributed to an interest in national affairs and to a conviction that there are duties and obligations of citizens in time of peace. It gave an appreciation of the importance of health, neat appearance, and erect carriage to individual progress. It contributed an ability to speak clearly and coherently before a group of men. 

Among the opinions of the 5.1 per cent who gave a negative reply to the question we find the following observations: The R. O. T. C. course contributes nothing to those not interested in the training. 

Its value is to the Nation and not to the individual. The training is unique but not important, and takes valuable time that might be better devoted to one's educational objective.

C. course with respect to content and organization, compare with other courses given at your institution? -The replies representing 36 institutions, rate the quality of the military course "Average," with respect to content and organization, compared with courses given by the other departments. The advanced R. O. T. C. course by itself is rated "Average" to "Above average"; the basic course between "Average" and "Below average"-more generally the latter. The Army instructors are rated "Above average" in personality and beneficial influence upon the students, but only "Average" in pedagogical ability. 

The preponderance of replies from graduates of the technical schools tends to rate the comparative quality of the R. O. T. C. course lower than do graduates of the academic institutions. The tendency toward a lower rating is also noticeable in the returns from graduates of the medical units. These two groups of graduates attribute the lower rating to the more elementary character of the basic course. 

The 2-year elementary or basic course receives general criticism that its disciplinary standards are not sufficiently strict, and that its quality suffers as a consequence. The point illustrates a limitation of military training courses in civilian institutions as compared to those in institutions which are essentially military colleges where students live in company barracks and operate under a 24-hour military schedule. In the nonmilitary colleges, students are under the control of the military department three hours per week in the first and second years and five hours per week in the third and fourth years.

Graduates of some institutions stated that the comparative standing of the R. O. T. C. course was injured by the ease with which passing grades were obtained, and that it was rare for a student to run into scholastic or disciplinary difficulties solely on account of military science. Although reports from several other institutions presented an opposite situation, the preponderance of replies indicates that the scholastic ease with which students pass through the R. O. T. C. is a retarding influence upon its academic rating. It is not conducive to the prestige to which graduates believe the R. O. T. C. is entitled for it to be known as a "snap course.' 

The consensus of opinion of those who dealt with the relationship of the staff of the military department to that of the academic faculty was that the frequent rotation of professors of military science and tactics, who have shown exceptional talent for this type of detail, is a policy not advantageous to the R. O. T. C. from the standpoint of institutional recognition. 

4. From your own experience was the time you spent on the training justified by the results obtained?—From the affirmative responses which represent a large majority, we find the following viewpoints: Military training taught both in theory and practice the principles of cooperation, organization, and the art of command. It developed selfcontrol, dependability, and orderly habits. The democracy of the training made for solidarity in the college body and resulted in

friendships not otherwise possible. The financial aid was a material help in meeting college expenses. The course provided a much needed variety to the academic subjects. It provided more practical knowledge than any other course. 

From the 5.1 per cent negative responses received, we note the following: Military training intruded itself into student schedules already burdened with requirements. The basic R. O. T. C. course was not applicable for credit toward a degree, nor was it made difficult enough to justify serious attention. The work of the basic course was repetitious, sketchy, and dull, and of little value to the majority upon whom it was forced. It was a waste of time. 

5. In your opinion, did military training aid or make easier the development in your own life of one or more of the following qualities or characteristics: Leadership, Initiative, Orderliness, Disciplinary value?—The replies show that four-fifths of the respondents credited their military training with favorably affecting their ability to supervise and to cooperate with others. Approximately three-fifths credited its disciplinary influence as a factor in their development by enabling them more readily to adjust themselves as employees, while a slightly smaller number indicated that the training aided in initiative and orderliness. Additional qualities checked were confidence, health, decision, and patriotism.

In accompanying statements, the general opinion expressed was that training in the art of command breeds confidence and initiative and gives to a young man in the first trying years following graduation an advantage over the nonmilitary man. 

6. In what way, if any, has the military education you have received been of economic value to you since graduation?—According to the evidence, such economic benefits as may result from military training are largely of an intangible kind. The majority of the replies stated that the training is an effective force making for character, physical fitness, and the ability to get things accomplished, and that the possession of such qualities is an aid to individual progress. A goodly number rated the R. O. T. C. as the course that had proven the most valuable to them in every-day life. A handful stated that their military education had proven an economic detriment. 

Forty-nine and three-tenths per cent indicated that the training had improved them physically; many asserted that a proper posture was an economic asset.

7. In your opinion does the R. O. T. C. course of instruction tend to produce a militaristic attitude inimical to world peace?—Ninety-three and six-tenths per cent of graduates replied to this question negatively and stated very definitely that actual education in the destructiveness of war is conducive to a sane patriotism and an enlightened attitude toward peace. They asserted that a working familiarity with modern military weapons acted as a sobering offset to any romantic conception of warfare. They pointed out that the 

R. O. T. C. is a training and not a state of mind. It was their view that the military instructors on the whole had shown the repulsive side of warfare and had presented their material from the standpoint of its emergency application rather than from the standpoint of assuming the inevitability of war. This group asserted that the extent of its militaristic thought, as influenced by military training, was a belief in military preparedness as a security measure. 

The 6.4% who replied affirmatively to the question, indicated that students assume that they are being trained for the next war and that the very expectation creates a positive belief in the inevitability of war. This group believed also that such training keeps alive a militaristic spirit at the moment when the United States is advancing the Kellogg and other pacts for judicial settlement of all international disputes. It would be far better, some thought, to teach youth to think in terms of world-wide economic and political development rather than in patriotic terms which view our neighbors with alarm. 

8 (a) and (b). Judging by your own experience, do you favor the

R. O. T. C. as a required subject for the first two years of college or would you make the entire course optional?-Eighty-one and two-tenths per cent of 9,554 replies favored two years of required training. This majority argued that disciplinary training is now neglected in the

home and that the R. O. T. C. exerts a stablilizing effect upon youth at a time when it is most productive of good. While granting that a number of students disliked the ideas of drilling or subjecting themselves to disciplinary restraint, the majority asserted that such students could advance no serious reasons against it. They felt that those who most needed the training physically would be the very ones not to take it if the course were made entirely optional. Many stated that while they had opposed required training as undergraduates, they now support it as alumni for the reason that its disciplinary and educational benefits were not realized until after graduation. 

The point was often raised that those who received their education partly at Government expense owe to the Government an obligation to be reasonably familiar with military tactics to the end that their services may be of prompt use to the country in time of stress. 

The minority, who favored an entirely optional course, stated that a stronger R. O. T. C. would result if the enrollment were limited to those genuinely interested. It was said that required military training blinds a student to the inherent good contained in the training, and creates a negative attitude that adversely affects the morale and progress of those who are interested. It was argued that the government receives little benefit from the considerable effort and expense spent upon indifferent or unwilling trainees. This opinion asserted that it was against the modern spirit in education to force courses upon students that were unrelated to their educational objectives. 

For the sake of a clearer understanding of the replies to this question, it may be stated that the matter of whether freshmen and sophomores shall or shall not be required to undergo disciplinary military training 3 hours per week during the academic sessions has been widely discussed, and has become a problem in several institutions. It should also be noted that while numerically the alumni by a ratio of 4 to 1, favor military training as a curriculum requirement, as groups they support the "status quo" basis at their own institutions. The only exceptions comprise the replies received from the alumni groups representing DePauw University, University of Cincinnati, University of Pennsylvania, and Georgetown University-all optionalthe majority of whose R. O. T. C. graduates favor required training.5 

Although the subject of optional training is legitimately debatable, it is an institutional and not a general problem. Much of the excited discussion on both sides according to the replies, originates off the college campus, and that which permeates its confines is said to be instigated largely by outside sources. Although the land-grant colleges, with the exception of the University of Wisconsin, continue the compulsory requirement, they individually have very different views as to those upon whom it shall be made compulsory. 8 (c). Judging by your own experience, would you abolish the R. O.T.C. from collegiate institutions?—The replies show that 98.5 per cent of those replying to this questionnaire view military and academic education as complementary, and are emphatically in favor of the continuance of the R. O. T. C. in educational institutions. In their opinion the abolishment of this activity would be a decided step backward.

The graduates do not view student dissatisfaction with the R. O. T. 

C. seriously, and observe that unfavorable attitudes frequently change to support as the purpose of the training becomes more clearly revealed. 

They believe that attacks upon the training are largely due to unfamiliarity with its objectives and are too often based upon superficial observation of the campus drill rather than upon classroom procedure. A considerable number stated that were there no military objectives they would still favor the inclusion of the R. O. T. C. or some similar activity in the college curriculum as a training in citizenship and as a medium for character and physical development. 

One and five-tenths per cent favored the abolition of the R. O. T. 

C. course on these grounds: (1) That it would advance the course of world peace; (2) the expense does not justify the results obtained; (3) military training does not rightfully belong in an educational curriculum; (4) there would be sufficient time to give intensive military training after the emergency has arisen.

SUMMARY 

1. The volume of opinions drawn from more than 10,000 college graduates who completed the R. O. T. C. course in military science and tactics gives full recognition to the educational values derived from the course. This is apparent from the standpoints of both general education and discipline and educational subject matter. 

The course is recognized for its utility in developing right habits of mind and body and qualities of character that are fully as useful in everyday experience as they are when applied to military objectives. 

2. The R. O. T. C. is especially recommended by graduates because it has brought to them a more definite and serious recognition of a number of the more important duties and responsibilities of a democratic citizenship. 

3. Graduates of the R. O. T. C. have come to feel that some college authorities and faculties have not given sufficient recognition to the R. O. T. C. as an educational instrument, largely through a

failure to perceive many of the educational values that have been developed through its agency. 

4. Although graduates recognize on the whole that military education is equal in quality to that which is academic or professional, they feel that the R. O. T. C. course could be strengthened by better pedagogical training on the part of the teachers of military science, and by their placing greater stress upon the study of principles rather than upon military technique. 

5. The graduates would strongly oppose the abolition of military training from institutions of collegiate grade, and are of the opinion that the contribution made by the R. O. T. C. to a young man's general education is sufficient in value to warrant the continuance of the course as a curriculum requirement. 

6. Ninety-three and six-tenths per cent of the 9,636 replies attest that R. O. T. C. training does not create a militaristic attitude in the minds of those who have experienced it, but that it does furnish graduates with a sense of individual responsibility toward national welfare and security.
