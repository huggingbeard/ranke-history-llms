CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY 

The past decade has witnessed a number of significant developments of great importance to California forestry. Important among these developments was the work of the California Forestry Study Committee created by Chapter 1086, Statutes of 1943, comprised of two members of the Senate, two members of the Assembly, the Director of Natural Resources, and the Chairman of the State Board of Forestry. 

The recommendations of this committee provided the foundation for a series of laws relating to forestry and forest practices, which were enacted by the Legislature in 1945, covering a definition of the State's responsibility for fire protection, the enactment of a Forest Practice Act, forest insect and infestation control, acquisition of state forests, authorizing the Division of Forestry to engage in range improvement projects and a reorganization of the State Board of Forestry on a staggered term basis.

This legislation enacted in 1945 entirely changed the character of the work of the Division of Forestry essentially from that of a fire protection agency to that of a land and resource management and regulatory agency. 

Most of the management and regulatory functions, however, are in connection with privately owned lands so that these activities are exercised in cooperation with the owners and users of the forest, range, and watershed lands of the State which are not in government ownership. 

The joint interim committee was not continued by the 1945 Legislature. However, a Senate interim committee was created by Senate Resolution No. 151, Statutes of 1945, which submitted recommendations supplementing those of the original committee but requiring little additional legislation on forestry.

Upon completion of the work of the 1945 interim committee, the study of forestry problems in California was continued in the Public Lands Committee, created in 1949 and continued in 1951, a committee delegated, among other things, to study the results obtained through the forestry legislation enacted in 1945 and 1947.

This Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands cooperated closely with the Assembly Agriculture Subcommittee on Forestry, Grazing and Public Lands, and on November 6 to 8, 1952, the two committees scheduled a hearing in conjunction with the State Board of Forestry to review forestry problems, programs and progress. The information contained in this report summarizes the testimony developed at this hearing. 

SUMMARY OF FINDINGS 

The principal findings of the Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands relative to California forestry, programs, and progress are sum marized as follows: 

1. In general, the basic forestry legislation enacted in 1945 provides a sound basis for operations of the State Division of Forestry. (7)

2. Fire protection continues to be the primary function of the State 

Division of Forestry. This activity requires the greatest proportion of personnel and funds budgeted for this division. 

3. The fire plan of the Division of Forestry is based upon the principle that the "forest values" (timber, range and watershed) being of interest to all citizens, should be segregated from all rural and structural values having only local interest in the matter of fire protection and prescribes a precise allotment of fire protection forces in accordance with need without regard to fund sources or local pressure demands. 

4. The fire plan is a method of arriving at the solution of fire protection and control problems for a "normal maximum" condition, but is not assured to be adequate to handle the worst situation that might develop. 

5. The principle objective of the Forest Practice Act is to provide for continuous production of forest products. The act has been amended to clarify technical points and provide more effective administration and enforcement, but no basic change has been made since the enactment of this legislation in 1945. 

6. The Forest Practice Rules authorized by the Forest Practice Act are primarily designed to provide: (1) adequate leave of seed trees at time of logging, (2) minimum damage to residual stands, (3) adoption of strict fire prevention practices, and (4) prompt aggressive action on all fires on or near operating areas. Some confusion results from conflicts between the provisions of the Forest Practice Rules and the fire laws with respect to required fire protection equipment. 

7. Some confusion exists in the provisions for cutting under the 

Forest Practice Rules and the Minimum Diameter Cutting Law, Sections 4850, 4851-4854 of the Public Resources Code. There is some evidence that some owners and operators are circumventing the Forest Practice Rules through the abuse of the statutory provisions. 

8. Farm forestry advisory service was initiated in California during 

1940 with the establishment of two demonstrative units by the Federal Soil Conservation Service. This service is now an activity of the State Division of Forestry. The State now has six service foresters. 

9. New forest industries such as those processing wood fiber products provide an opportunity for utilization of slash material and other by-products of timber operations. These expanding industries also add to and stabilize the economy of the State and local communities. 

10. Forest insects and disease are among the greatest threats to a sound and adequate forest economy. California is currently losing about one-half billion board feet of merchantable timber per year to widely scattered insect and disease infestations. 

11. The progress that has been made in reforestation of cut-over and other timber stands destroyed by fire and insects is not impressive. 

Part of the reason for the lack of progress in restocking the timber croplands is the lack of knowledge of proper techniques of reforestation under California climatic conditions. Much more research is needed for all forestry problems.

12. The acquisition of large state park and national forest areas in counties with large forest areas such as Del Norte increases the tax problems of the local units of government particularly when there is need for increased governmental services due to expanding timber operations. 

13. There is a need for re-examination of the provisions for taxation of timber stands. This is due in part to recent developments which require application of the provisions of Section 12 of Article 

XIII of the Constitution. 

14. The policies and programs of the U. S. Forest Service are important to both the timber operators and the general economy of the State inasmuch as approximately one-half of the forest lands in California are owned by the Federal Government. Two of the most important problems are the timber-sale policy and the access road program. 

RECOMMENDATIONS 

In general, the Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands finds that the impact of population and industrial growth in California has greatly increased the resource management and protection problems of our wildland areas. Where once a handful of people lived and worked in a few sawmills and logging camps, today there are thousands. Timber harvest has more than doubled in the last six years. New slash areas have grown from a few scattered thousand acres to 200 thousand acres per year. 

Recreational use of these same lands has increased many fold. Today practically all major timber owners and operators are planning and developing for continuous forest production and a permanent industry. 

They are each year spending great sums in better harvesting methods, leaving seed trees and residual timber blocks, falling snags, making fire breaks, helping prevent and control fires-investing for the future. 

Rancher-timber owners are looking to the woods as a steady or periodic revenue-producing resource. 

To support this faith in the future and to assure the prosperity of our mountain areas the public also has a responsibility. These wild lands are used by the public as mountain playgrounds and sportsmen's areas. 

This type of use creates serious fire risks that are beyond the control of the private owners and establishes a burden that the public must share. 

There are other risks of insects and disease that know no boundary lines. Here again the public must help protect the present and future resource by aiding in controlling serious infestations. 

Too little is known about our soils' productive capabilities, about the best silvicultural practices to follow in different timber types, how to secure maximum growth, how to economically secure full restocking of cut-over and denuded forest lands under California's severe climatic conditions.

Much research and field experimentation is needed in these and other related fields. The work now being conducted by private and public agencies in these problems would perhaps be more effective if there was a coordination in the many programs and an allocation of the financing in proportion to the agencies benefited. Coordinated long range planning and objective programing will be of potential benefit to the State as a whole.

The principal recommendations of the Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands with respect to specific forest problems are as follows: 

1. The Public Resources Code should be amended to provide for the fire protection requirements now existing in the Forest Practice Rules. 

The provisions of the Forest Practice Rules requiring fire protection tools, equipment, and facilities should be standardized and be made an amendment to the Public Resources Code. 

2. The State Board of Forestry and the State Fish and Game Commis sion should cooperate in the formulation of policies relating to hunting seasons so the fire risks will be minimized in high value timber areas.

All state agencies dealing with matters of fish and game management and hunting should cooperate in the formation of uniform policies for the protection of all natural resources. 

3. The State Board of Forestry should establish a clearly defined policy under the provisions of Sections 4850, 4851-4854 of the Public Resources Code and adopt rules and regulations to be followed in processing applications for clear-cutting permits.

Rules and regulations should be coordinated so as to prevent evasion of the provisions and intent of the Forest Practice Act; clarification and reconciliation should be made. 

4. The Public Resources Code should be amended to provide specific authorization for the operation of the service forestry program of the State Division of Forestry. 

The Public Resources Code should be amended to provide for a service forestry program to be administered by the State Division of Forestry. 

5. The State Forest Nursery Program should be expanded to provide adequate planting stock for reforestation purposes and the Public Resources Code should be amended to provide for a more realistic price structure for sales of seedlings and planting stock. 

The State Forestry Nursery Program should be expanded to provide the necessary planting stock for reforestation purposes, and the Public Resources Code should be amended to enable the sales of such seedlings and planting stock on a basis of realistic and simple methods of cost determination. 

6. The Forest Insect Control Law should be amended to include forest diseases and provide for more prompt and aggressive control action. 

The Forest Insect Control Law should be amended to include forest disease control and provide for more prompt and aggressive control action. 

7. The State should continue the wild land soils surveys which were initiated by the five-year program 1947-1952. 

Wild land soil surveys, which were initiated by the five-year program during 1947-52, should be continued to the final completion of such a survey of all wild lands of the State.

CHAPTER II DEVELOPMENT OF FORESTRY IN CALIFORNIA 

The State of California took official recognition of forestry problems long before the federal Forest Service was organized. The first State 

Board of Forestry was appointed in 1884, some 20 years before the organization of the U. S. Forest Service. 

The state activities in the field of forestry, however, were not very extensive in these early years. This was because the relative demand for timber was less and because of the tremendous supply of virgin timber easily accessible. Timber cutting prior to 1849 was so limited that it had little effect on the forests. 

The California Forestry Study Committee estimated that the total lumber production in California prior to 1901 amounted to less than 25 billion board feet. Statistics compiled by the State Division of Forestry show the periodic annual timber production since 1899 as follows:

These data show that the number of active sawmills in California increased 461 percent, from 142 in 1940 to 796 in 1951. The greatest rate of increase in the total occurred from 1940 to 1945, although the total almost doubled from 1945 to 1951. The total number of active primary wood processing plants for 1952 is estimated to be approximately 1,300, which includes sawmill, veneer and plywood mills, pulp plants, shingle and miscellaneous forest products plants. 

Statistics show that the greatest increase in the number of sawmills was in the North Coast District where the number of mills increased over 1,200 percent. The greatest increase in the number of mills in this district occurred after World War II. 

The Central Sierra District had the next highest increase of about 500 percent. The number of sawmills more than doubled in the Northern 

Forest District from 1940 to 1945, but decreased slightly during the postwar years. The number of sawmills increased in the other administrative districts, but the total number of mills in the three southern districts is small as compared with the number in the three northern districts.

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT 

The increase in the number of sawmills and the increase in lumber production since 1940 are the results of several changes in the economy of the State. The phenomenal growth of the population of the State of 

California both before and after World War II is one of the most important factors stimulating an increase in demand for lumber products. 

The location and growth of defense industries in California not only increased the demand for lumber for defense projects, but also was a factor in the large immigration of population which increased the demand for housing and other building requirements. 

A third factor was the change in price levels or dollar values. Prior to the war some owners of timber in the northern and other parts of the State could sell their timber for 50 cents per thousand board feet and others couldn't give it away. The increase to the present average of over $17 per thousand board feet paid for public timber is due to both increase in demand and the change in price levels. 

Another important economic factor is the relative position of California as a lumber producing state. The data in Chart 2 show that California is now the second largest lumber-producing state in the United States. 

Oregon is first, having surpassed Washington in about 1938 in the amount of lumber produced. These data also show that the Pacific Northwest is declining as a heavy producing section and that the California forest resources are now being used to meet the heavy demand for lumber and lumber products. 

Part of the reason for the increase in the total lumber production in California is an increase in demand for what has formerly been termed the lesser or "inferior" species of trees. The two largest lumber-producing areas in the State traditionally have been the pine forests and the redwood forests. This is an important factor affecting both the amount of lumber produced and the logging practices used in the lumber production. During early logging in these regions, the lesser species were in little demand. However, improvements in cutting and manufacture and the situation developing during World War II gave impetus to a more extensive use of these so-called "lesser species." The former lack of demand for the lesser species has been fortunate for the owners of such timber in that they now have a valuable resource in those areas in which it was not harvested. 

A report on the history of lumbering in the United States presented at the joint hearing, November 6 to 8, 1952, by W. D. Pine, Farm Adviser, Humboldt County, states, in part, as follows: "To the east and north is the one-time great lumber state of Maine, and how fortune smiled on it is quite a story. During the early timber harvest in Maine, white pine was the only timber of value. "All pine areas had been logged with the peak year around 1909. 

During those days the loggers took only the good pine, leaving the fir, spruce, and hardwood as of no value. They left cull pines which made excellent seed trees for reforestation. Then they moved west for bigger and better trees. As a result, the State of Maine now has 

17 million acres of forest lands, and 98 percent of this land is in private ownership. Maine is sure to continue as a great pulp state.

MAJOR FOREST PROBLEMS IN CALIFORNIA 

The California Forestry Study Committee summarized the forest problems in California as follows: 

1. The cutting of old growth in such a way as to maintain productivity of forest land and the prolongation of the life of the old-growth supplies. 

2. The reforestation of as much as possible of that part of the worst area of cut-over lands that seems reasonably certain of favorable response to planting or seeding. 

3. The reduction of losses by forest fires, both in old-growth timber and second growth, and of further damage by fire to the growing capacity of non-reforested cut-over lands. 

4. The reduction of losses by insects and disease. 

5. The providing for a continuity of State forest policy. 

The philosophy which actuated these recommendations has continued as a guide for the policies of the State Board of Forestry in the development of wild-land management since that date, according to W. S. Rosecrans, a member of the California Forestry Study Committee and Chairman of the State Board of Forestry. As stated by him at the joint hearings of the Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands, May 17, 1952, and 

November 6, 1952, the salient points to this philosophy are as follows: "(1) We were convinced that neither the state nor the federal governments could impose a system of forestry. "(2) We believed that the progress for wild-land management should be based on education and cooperation. "(3) Compulsion is only effective when used against a small and recalcitrant minority. (4) Programs for wild-land management must be developed at the grass roots. "(5) The people directly effected must be convinced and their cooperation secured." 

THE STATE DIVISION OF FORESTRY 

The broad authorizations of the 1945 legislation based on the philosophy outlined above provide the basis upon which the state division now operates. The new legislation brought about decided changes in the functions and activities of the State Division of Forestry.

The principal function of the State Division of Forestry prior to 1945 was fire protection. Other activities performed on a small scale were the 

State Nursery and blister rust control. 

The new functions added by the 1945 legislation included reorganization of the fire protection activities, administration of the Forest Practice Act, the acquisition and management of four state forests, range improvement in cooperation with ranchers, conservation, education, insect control, and watershed research. The programs and results of these new activities are discussed in other chapters of this report. 

The organization of the State Division of Forestry as of December, 1952, is shown in Chart 3. Essentially, the organization is a central office staff and a line organization in the six administrative districts. The location of the administrative districts is shown in Chart 1.

CHAPTER III FIRE PROTECTION 

Fire protection, historically, has been the major purpose and activity of the work of the State Division of Forestry. Although what has been termed the basic forest legislation enacted in 1945 added a number of new functions and activities, fire protection continues to be the primary function of the Division of Forestry. 

Although forests are renewable resources, forest fire protection is essential for the use and perpetuation of the existing resources, both old-growth and second growth stands. The Division of Forestry estimates that the timber cropland in California amounts to about 15.1 million acres which is about equally divided between private and public ownerships. Using present day limited information, these lands contain about 

212 billion board feet of available young and old-growth timber, more than half of which, or about 114 billion board feet, is privately owned. 

About half of our timber acreage is still uncut.

The past two decades have witnessed a number of improvements in the fire protection activities of the State Division of Forestry. Prior to 1932, the initial attack on fires was made by local citizens and private protective agencies, augmented by such small "pick-up" crews as could be mustered by public agencies. Many fires which could be quickly controlled today were, under this lack of initial attack organization, many times increased to considerable proportions. 

The present system of organized initial attack state suppression crews dates from 1932 when hourly paid pickup labor was supplanted by a number of small mobile crews. According to the State Forester, some 350 men were hired at $25 a month with board. These crews were employed on a seasonal basis with funds from emergency sources rather than through the regular support budget. 

THE 1940 FIRE PLAN 

Prior to 1939, the Division of Forestry had no real organized fire plan on paper. In that year, a selected committee met with state rangers throughout the State to develop their first comprehensive plan of assignment of crews, vehicles, lookouts, dispatchers and administrative personnel to accomplish what was considered adequate forest fire protection. 

The two major results of this initial fire plan were: (1) Recognition of the principle that "forest values" (timber, range and watershed) being of general interest to all citizens, should be segregated from all rural and structural values having only local interest in the matter of fire protection, and (2) the fire plan prescribed a precise allotment of fire protection forces in accordance with need without regard to fund sources or local pressure demands. As a result of the adoption of this fire plan, the fire crew organization became an established support item in the budget and a definite fire season was established for budgeting ( 21 )

purposes. After Pearl Harbor, a special session of the Legislature welcomed the 1940 Fire Plan and appropriated $4,000,000 to implement a large portion of it. 

The State Forester emphasizes that the fire plan is looked upon as a method of arriving at the solution of fire protection and control problems rather than as a collection of hard and fast details of operation. 

The plan recognizes the effectiveness of trained, well equipped specialized crews, the prime importance of rapid initial attack and aggressive follow-up for control at minimum size with minimum resource loss. It also recognizes the need for local cooperation from landowners and local working groups.

The fire plan is set up as a flexible instrument to be adapted to rapidly changing conditions, population movements, new roads, transportation and communication advances. It is recognized that the fire problem will continue to change periodically and that an area of present low fire occurrence may present considerable difficulty in the future if that area is changed by roads, increased usage or other factors of hazard and risk. 

The Division of Forestry does not assume that the fire plan is adequate to handle the worst situation but is a plan for what might be termed the "normal maximum" fire condition.

FEDERAL-STATE-LOCAL RESPONSIBILITY 

The fire protection activities of the Division of Forestry have been performed in cooperation with the local and district fire protection groups and organizations. The Federal Government, through the Weeks' 

Law (1911) and the Clarke-McNary Act (1924) contributed to the protection of timber and watershed values on a partial reimbursement basis.

At first, there was little distinction between rural and "forest values.'' 

The State, through the State Forester, could cooperate in any "manner he deemed wise," providing some other agency made a contributing cooperative gesture. Industry and citizen groups often came forward to aid in specific projects. 

The 1940 Fire Plan provided the basis for the clarifying legislation of 1945 which sets forth the relative spheres of responsibility for the Federal, State, and local government. Under the present laws the Federal Government is: (1) Entirely responsible for federal lands of every category; and (2) A financial contributor on those private and state-owned lands of primary timber and watershed values. (Clarke-McNary Act 1924.)

The State has primary financial responsibility on nonfederal, unincorporated lands: (1) Producing or capable of producing forest products (Zone 1); or (2) Which might suffer excessive erosion or loss of water for irrigation, domestic, and industrial use (Zone 1); and (3) Which are principally range lands contiguous to the above described lands (Zone II).

The State may further extend its protection system for the "prevention and suppression of forest fires or any other fires on any lands within

a county, municipality or district," if such agency makes an appropriation therefor and the State Forester accepts the terms of agreement to accomplish the work. (Section 4006, Public Resources Code. Other basic law requires that the directors of the Departments of Natural Resources and Finance shall approve such agreements).

Local governments provide fire protection facilities in varying degrees decided by local conditions. Some counties have county-wide fire departments; whereas, fire protection facilities in the unincorporated areas of other counties are provided by special fire districts. 

With few exceptions, the local fire protection facilities in the unincorporated areas exist primarily to fight structural fires. A bulletin of the State Division of Forestry states that the attitude of the division toward fire districts is that they should exist primarily to meet a local, and especially congested, need for structural protection rather than brush or timber protection. Although the division field men cooperate with and assist the special fire districts, there is no uniform system of agreements between the State and fire districts because of the wide variation among the districts.

COUNTY AGREEMENTS 

The State Forester has considerable discretion in the manner in which the State provides fire protection for the lands within its sphere of responsibility. Section 4000.2, Public Resources Code, provides that the 

State Board of Forestry shall declare which lands of the State require fire protection as an area of "state-wide interest. "The State Forester must then arrange for such protection, either by direct action, or by contracting with some other agency in a manner prescribed by law. 

The counties having contracts for fire protection with the State are classified as "inside" and "outside" depending upon the type of contract. The agreements with the "inside" counties provide that the State 

Forester, through his organization, shall practically relieve the board of supervisors of all of their direct responsibility for fire protection in the unincorporated areas of the county. The "inside" counties are, by far, the most numerous of those having fire protection agreements with the State. These counties reimburse the State for the fire protection services provided the rural or unincorporated areas. 

Six counties: Kern, Los Angeles, Marin, San Mateo, Santa Barbara, and Ventura reverse this process and contract with the State to provide fire protection to the lands in these counties that have been declared by the State Board of Forestry to be of "state-wide interest." The State reimburses the counties for such fire protection services. These counties are termed the "outside" counties.

The agreements with the "inside" counties provide that the State will direct an officer-in-charge to handle all "county" and "state" problems and facilities as one single operating unit. The officer-in-charge will do all things necessary to represent the State's interest in the timber and watershed lands, and at the same time he will make expenditures agreed upon to protect structures and crops on behalf of the county. Counties are requested to assume local obligations which should not be paid for by the State. This occurs when counties pass special ordinances requiring considerable time in administration on the part of regular state employees. FIRE PREVENTION 

Most of the testimony presented at the joint hearing November 6 to 8, 1952, on the subject of fire protection dealt with problems of fire control and only incidentally touched upon the problem of fire prevention. It was generally recognized that the subject of fire prevention was of sufficient importance to warrant a special hearing on that subject alone at a later date.

Slash areas create new and serious fire problems. Under an old-growth forest the fuels retain some moisture, and sun and wind conditions are modified. On the other hand, cut-over lands have an accumulation of logging debris and the greater exposure to sun and wind results in a much drier fuel. This local fuel and climatic change makes cut-over areas more susceptible to fire incidence, accelerated rate of spread and greater difficulty of control.

That is why most forest practice rules require the falling of snags, partial slash cleanup and other fire prevention and suppression practices. 

Slash accumulations, therefore, present critical fire hazards and represent one of the major unsolved problems today. Prospects for utilization of much waste material appear to be possible in the future. In the meantime, many of the proposed methods for slash removal are controversial.

This is a transitional condition that we must go through in converting a bountiful virgin timber stand to a managed second growth timber crop.

From 1932 through 1946 the incidence of man-caused forest fires steadily increased in California which is not surprising when consideration is given to the many phases which influence the risks. Those factors include great population expansion, new rural and mountain industrialization, greatly increased travel, and receational use of the wild-land areas. 

Immediately following World War II when experienced manpower returned, it was possible to initiate an analysis of what was happening and what must be done if forest fire losses were to be minimized. From available through inadequate information, it was concluded that 50 percent of the man-caused fire problem lay with local people, about 30 percent with the traveling public, and 20 percent from miscellaneous causes.

Plans were formulated for two direct lines of action: 

1. Fire prevention that could be accomplished by the protection agencies working with local people through education, hazard reduction, and law enforcement. 

2. A state-wide educational program involving the efforts of industry, state-wide organizations and groups, and other governmental agencies.

Implementing this plan, the law enforcement policy was strengthened, additional emphasis was placed on hazard reduction and on education. At this time the state-wide fire prevention committee was founded and cooperative educational programs were developed to use all available media. 

State administrative aids to fire prevention, the "Fire Cause Analysis

Chart" and "Forest Fire Prevention Action Program Chart" were de veloped and put into use. 

These charts show: a. Cause by agent, by type of person, and whether local or nonlocal. b. Location by type of person, whether local or nonlocal and cover type classification of areas involved. c. Action by field personnel in all phases of forest fire prevention.

It has been possible by the use of this information to direct fire prevention activities toward those causes and locations where the greatest reduction in fire incidence and resource loss can be expected. This form of attack on a most complex problem has proven effective as is borne out in the downward trend of man-caused forest fires. 

Following the enactment of the Forest Practice Act in 1945 and the subsequent adoption of minimum forest practice rules, industries and organizations have contributed materially to the expansion of an effective fire prevention-education program, the success of which is reflected in fewer fires and less resource destruction. 

Contributions by the lumber industry through cooperative organizations, individual operators, and cooperation with governmental agencies are too numerous to list, except to enumerate in passing the following: a. Air patrols in three counties on a cooperative basis with the protection agencies and the local lumber industry for better detection and reporting of forest fires. b. An organization in the Redwood Region devoted almost entirely to fire prevention educational activities with organizations at a local level. c. An organization in the Pine Region with the same objective is in the formative stages. d. An undetermined number of industry-paid patrolmen during critical fire periods and during hunting seasons. e. Better than 85 percent compliance with the forest practice rules. 

Effective costly and voluntary contributions are not limited to the lumbering industries since other private industries, such as railroads, gas and power companies, telephone companies, oil companies, moving picture producers and many others are also waging educational, hazard reduction, and personnel training programs in the field of fire prevention. Newspapers, radio and television organizations have contributed significantly.

The state-wide forest fire prevention educational program has resulted in a coordinated campaign with close cooperation between the various protective agencies. Members of the California Forest Fire Prevention

Committee have contributed through the various media available to thein to an extent where forest fire prevention is increasingly becoming a 66 'watch word" in most areas of the State.

So far lightning fires are not preventable. Prompt detection and rapid suppression is the only solution for this type of fire. However, the falling of snags on all timber operations, more roads, partial slash disposal and cooperation from ranchers, timber operators and others in discovery and initial attack have materially reduced losses from this source. Experimental work in the field of rain making and lightning storm dispersal is being followed closely in hopes that ultimately something can be done to prevent or reduce the number of lightning fires. 

Through the continued efforts of private timber ownership and federal and state agencies in presenting the fire danger due to the concentrations of hunters during critical fire-hazard months, the Organized Sportsmen have been made aware of the necessity of cooperation by the sportsmen and landowners. Sportsmen of California, stated that sportsmen's organizations were urging later opening of the deer seasons which are now held in the months of greatest fire hazard. As an organization, they are cooperating with the landowners and protection organizations in the ever-growing job of fire prevention.

Another action urged by the sportsmen's groups is the use of cooperative hunting areas provided for by Section 1159 of the Fish and Game Code. The Department of Fish and Game sets up an area of 5,000 acres of adjoining owners who band together to establish the cooperative hunting areas. Written permission is required. Area boundaries are signed and patrolled. Trespass laws are enforced by the Fish and Game Department and the landowner may charge a fee of $2 per day per hunter. Such cooperative areas have been successful for pheasant hunting in the Sacramento Valley and are cited as an instance of well-managed multipleuse of private lands.

Although notable advancement has been made in all phases of forest fire prevention there is yet a long way to go. Only through good planning, continued cooperation, personnel training, development and use of better educational tools, vigorous application of effort, and due diligence to the problem can forest fire losses be minimized. The task is to "Keep California Green and Golden" so we may continue to enjoy a bountiful harvest and use of our wild-land resources.

CHAPTER IV FOREST PRACTICE REGULATION 

The Forest Practice Act was a part of the basic forest legislation enacted in 1945 that enlarged the activities of the State Division of Forestry from those of fire protection to a management and regulatory agency as recommended by the California Forestry Study Committee.

Regulation of forest practices by the State of California under the provisions of the Forest Practice Act differs from the regulatory measures and policies of the U. S. Forest Service in that the regulations established by the State of California are applicable to the owners of private land whereas the regulation exercised by the U. S. Forest Service is applicable to the national forest areas in public ownership. 

This distinction is important since about half of the commercial forest land in California is owned by the Federal Government and the other half is in private ownership. The establishment of sound forest practices which are in the public interest is not particularly difficult when such practices are applicable to forest areas owned by the government. An entirely different problem is presented when such regulations are applicable to privately owned lands.

As discussed in Chapter 2 of this report, the principles which guided the California Forestry Study Committee in recommending Forest Practice legislation were based on the recognition of the need for cooperation with forest land owners rather than the imposition of a system of forestry by the State. The Forest Practice Act, therefore, provides for the appointment of a five-man Forest Practice Committee representing different types of ownerships and operations for each of the four forest practice districts established by the act. These districts are shown in Chart 

4. The forest practice committees have the responsibility of developing and recommending forest practice rules for their respective districts to the people and to the State Board of Forestry. 

The forest practice rules were described by the manager of the Shasta Forest Company, R. A. Colgan, as "A code of logging and protective methods for the operations of a forest area. "Professor Emanuel Fritz of the School of Forestry of the University of California describes the operations of the act as follows: 

"There is now a greater awareness of the economic and cultural significance of our forests. The importance of keeping our forest lands continuously productive and of keeping the forest industries permanent is now widely accepted by industry and the public. This has come about through an interested Governor, a capable and dedicated Board of Forestry, a hardworking, competent and sagacious State Forester, a sizeable group of progressive lumbermen, and a favorably altered economic situation. *** "The Forest Practice Act of 1945 was passed without the opposition expected by some. I still look upon it as the best initial state ( 30 )

law affecting cutting practices that could have been offered. It recognized the newness to many operators of the idea of leaving seed trees; it required only the minimum of practice needed; it recognized that the logger on the ground was the man best able to write practical measures and that after he wrote the rules authorized in the act they needed only the approval of the State Board of Forestry to give them the force and effect of law. It thus provided for self-government. It provided for local differences of forest and operating situations and for the fact that there may be more than one way of accomplishing the purpose of the act by authorizing alternate cutting and protection plans and, finally, it recognized the desirability of making haste slowly. "As a result of this wise, American-like approach, no one felt that he was having forced upon him rules made in one office by a public official. *** Actually, on the bulk of the forest lands, the state requirements are being exceeded by a good margin. What may be even more important, there has been engendered a cordial and cooperative spirit between the forest industries and the state agencies."

COOPERATIVE ACTION 

The California Forest Practice Act is based upon the cooperative approach in the regulation of private forest cutting practices. The philosophy back of this recommendation was discussed in Chapter II of this report.

California was one of a group of states which adopted laws regulating cutting practices in the mid 1940s. The California act was patterned after the Maryland Forest Conservancy Districts Act of 1943. 

The provision for local advisory committees, common to both the Maryland and California acts, recognizes the need for cooperation in the formulation of the minimum forest practices to be required. This principle also recognizes the diverse conditions found in the various forest areas of the State. 

Forestry research, discussed in more detail in later sections of this report, has demonstrated that sound forest management requires consideration of local conditions conducive to forest growth and regeneration. These conditions are not uniform throughout the State. It has also been found that soil and other necessary factors may vary from acre to acre. This discovery is comparatively recent but is beginning to receive general recognition by the forest industry. 

The cooperative approach and the recognition of diverse local conditions recognized by the California Forest Practice Act are the principal and significant differences between the California act and the forest practice laws of other western states.

THE FOREST PRACTICE COMMITTEES 

The primary function of formulating the forest practice rules starts with the work of the forest practice committees. Four such committees have been established as follows:

Redwood Forest Practice Committee; 

North Sierra Pine Forest Practice Committee; 

South Sierra Pine Forest Practice Committee;

Coast Range Pine and Fir Forest Practice Committee. 

The work of the forest practice committees does not cease with establishment of the forest practice rules. These need change to keep pace with changing conditions, new forestry knowledge, and progress in the forest industry. The members of these committees, therefore, must hold frequent meetings and inspect the forest operating conditions in their districts. 

One of the incidental problems of these activities is that, at the present time, the forest practice committee members do not receive compensation for the many hours of time and expenses incurred in official meetings and travel. The State Board of Forestry recommends legislation to allow expenses of all committee members while on official forest practice committee work.

Each forest practice committee consists of two representatives of timber-owner operators, one nonoperating timber owner, one farmer-timber owner, and a staff member of the Division of Forestry representing the State Board of Forestry. Although the forest practice rules for each of the four forest districts may vary because of different industry conditions, the procedures for formulation and adoption of the rules are uniform. 

The first job of each forest practice committee was to analyze the forest problems and to devise rules of conduct for operators to follow which would reasonably protect and promote the continued forest growth and production in each forest district. After the rules were formulated in tentative form they were discussed at public hearings held at key points within the district. The rules promulgated as the result of the public hearings were submitted to all timber owners of record in the district. 

Upon receipt of favorable ballots of two-thirds or more of the timber owners, the rules were submitted to the State Board of Forestry for approval as required by law.

I think one of the changes made was a mistake and should be rectified by future Legislatures. The original premise of the act was that these rules would not become law until they had been passed on by two-thirds, representatives of two-thirds of the private forest acreage. The way the amended act provides, under these new rules, is that we must hold hearings before we adopt any amendments and then on the basis of what we have decided after holding those hearings, we present our recommendations to the State Board of Forestry. The State Board of Forestry makes up its mind and it may approve the rules and they automatically become law after publication. I think it was a mistake to remove the provision that the amendments as well as the original rules be submitted to a vote of the people who are going to be bound by it. 

The reason that was done was that it was an unwieldly process to make sure you had contacted by mail every timber owner and given him a chance to vote. I think that problem might be solved by legislation providing that the forest practice committees and the Division of Forestry and the Board of Forestry should agree on certain minimum acreages in each district that had to be heard from favorably before the new amendments became law. I haven't thought the thing all the way through but I think it would be a much better program if we found some way to carry on in the amendments the original premises.

MINIMUM DIAMETER CUTTING 

One of the current problems closely related to the Forest Practice Act is the duplications that sometimes exist in the forest practice rules with the provisions of the Minimum Diameter Cutting Law, Sections 4850, 4851-4854 of the Public Resources Code. This law prohibits the cutting "for conversion into lumber any sound live coniferous tree less than 18

inches in average diameter" at a point six inches above the ground without a permit from the State Forester. The law stipulated that such a permit "shall" be issued if on the basis of facts known, or data, information, and records available that either of the following conditions exist: 

1. The cutting of trees would improve forest growth. 

2. The land is to be devoted to purposes other than forest growth. 

The Minimum Diameter Cutting Law was a necessary forerunner to some of the regulations now contained in the forest practice rules. 

Three main problems are associated with the administration of the 

Minimum Diameter Cutting Law: (1) the agricultural clearing provi sion is being used more and more as a subterfuge to permit the cutting for conversion into lumber of below legal size trees, (2) the law does not prohibit an owner from cutting small timber obstensibly for piling, pulp, and fuel products and then using the butt logs for conversion into lumber, and, (3) the law is somewhat a duplication of minimum diameter and conversion into other use controls of the Forest Practice Act and results in confusion with that program. 

Early in 1951 the division conducted a survey of all the permits approved by the State Forester prior to that date covering a five-year period, 1946-1950. The results of this survey revealed the following: 

Some 254 applications to cut small conifers had been approved embracing 49,003 acres of forest land. Eleven of these permits were approved for purposes of improving forest growth on 1,112 acres, while the balance were for conversion to purposes other than forest growth. 

Analysis of the latter permits revealed the following: 

1. On 29 percent of the acreage approved the applicants had actually executed their declared intentions. 

2. On 36 percent of the acreage the owners had not exercised their permit to cut small trees but still contemplated carrying out original cutting objectives. 

3. On 27 percent of the acreage the owners failed to cut small trees and had abandoned their plan of conversion from timber to other uses for the land. 

4. On 8 percent of the acreage the owners had removed the small trees and had abandoned their plan of conversion. 

This survey indicated a relatively small amount (8 percent) of the area was logged to small diameters without following through intent of conversion to another use. A good deal of the area left uncut (63 percent) was primarily because of inability to sell the small timber. Although a detailed resurvey of the area covered in 1951 has not yet been made, indications are that some of this timberland has been cleared to take advantage of improved market conditions in recent months.

During the past year and a half since making this investigation approximately 15 applications have been received monthly in contrast with an average of four per month prior to that time. This increase is due in part to: (1) permits now are being obtained where previously the owners cut without them; and, (2) partly to a more widespread knowledge of the fact the permits are relatively easy to obtain under the law; and more important, (3) better realization value from small trees and a shortage of readily available cheap stumpage.

While there may be little need for alarm state-wide solely on the basis of forest acreage taken out of timber production under Sections 4850, 4851-4854 of the Public Resources Code, the problem is accelerating rapidly. It must be realized that insufficient time has elapsed particularly in the most recent survey to adequately determine whether the permittee has abandoned thought of "conversion to other use." Recognition of the problem is necessary in order to forestall further abuse of the law and to meet a potential threat that can have serious effect upon the forest resource of the State. The number of permits approved during the past 18 months has increased 360 percent over the rate of approval made during the 1946-1950 period. 

In some of the Mother Lode counties the problem is indeed an aggravated one. Where portable mills are active and small operators are unable to readily purchase cheap logs there is a tendency to induce rancherowners and other small timberland owners to apply for clear cutting

permits. Presently this problem is being met in the field by an aggressive follow-up on each application. The division has endeavored to eliminate misuse of the law by (1) examining all areas proposed for clear cutting and making recommendations as to desirability or feasibility, (2) attempting to "sell" timber management rather than conversion where reasonable to do so from an economic standpoint, (3) requiring detailed information from the applicant as to steps, other than logging, to be taken to convert the areas to another use, and (4) requiring applicants to submit a notarized sworn statement of their intentions. 

However, in the last analysis once an applicant has submitted a sworn notarized statement of his intentions to convert the land to another use, the State Forester has no alternative than to issue a permit. Close administration of this law and education have been successful in persuading prospective applicants to refrain from cutting small trees on some 47,731 acres (35 percent of the acreage originally applied for) of forest land during the past six and one-half years. 

The point of especial concern is the fact that on all areas checked in the latest survey, where logging had been completed, some 47 percent of the area has apparently been abandoned for further treatment after cutting the undersized timber. If this relationship continues to hold on areas yet to be logged, it is certainly apparent that a large share of the applications are either subterfuge or at least poorly considered ventures. 

It thus proves to be a good escape from the requirements of the forest practice rules.

Some suggestions, therefore, have been made to repeal Sections 4850, 4851-4854 of the Public Resources Code in order to eliminate a possible loophole for evasion of the forest practice rules. These provisions of the law serve a very necessary purpose where bona fide use of land for purposes other than timber production are desired by the owner. It is generally agreed that the State should never take the position that it can dictate to a citizen that he shall grow timber or any other crop on his land. The objective should be to preclude circumvention of the forest practice rule requirements through the abuse of the statutory provisions. 

THE FUTURE 

The above review of the activities of the forest practice committees and the administration of the Forest Practice Act by the Division of Forestry indicates that much is being done on a cooperative basis to insure continuous production of the privately owned forest lands in California. 

Both the principle and the administration of the Forest Practice Act appear to be sound.

When the Forest Practice Act was under consideration by the California Forestry Study Committee, some doubt was expressed as to the necessity for this type of regulation. The success of the Forest Practice 

Act should forestall any great pessimism as to the future of California forestry as it appears at the present time. The optimistic approach was expressed by William B. Berry, Forest Consultant, as follows: "I feel quite often that much of the approach to many items on your agenda for these next two days has been based on the assumption that our California forests are being destroyed and that a

terrible day of reckoning is approaching. I do not believe it. I hope that you legislators do not believe it either. "The situation in California forests has never been better. We have amazing forest resource in good condition, not perfect condition, but in good condition. To drive through and fly over California's forests is an exhilarating and stimulating experience. The record written in the trees and forest lands, particularly in the young stands, belies the pessimism and condemnations of 20 and 30 years ago. Areas popularly labeled devastated 20 years ago-those areas now bear beautiful and valuable young stands and harvests that are now popularly labeled destructive are leaving reserves of advance growth far in excess of the practices two or three decades ago. It is very true of the average. "Even the scars from many terrible and senseless fires in the forests have healed much better and much faster than we had expected a few years ago. This is true however bad continuing hazards have become. "The vast systems of roads, both public and private, is a prepaid premium to insure forest protection from fires, insects and disease, and this road system is building an annuity in increased value for both existing and potential growth. Fortunately, the physical and economic trends acting upon this great resource are also quite good. Whatever temporary adjustments may come along, we are at this time building sound forest industries on foundations of excellently growing forests. We are awakening new consciousness in forest values. We are opening new areas of inaccessible forests, creating new values in secondary species, increasing the efficiency of yields in forest products, attacking the problem of waste, for example. We are improving systems of salvage and of forest protection. We are developing the wherewithal to harvest timber crops in shorter growing cycles."

CHAPTER V SERVICE FORESTRY The Forest Practice Rules discussed in Chapter IV of this report are but one phase of the program of the Division of Forestry to promote the growing of trees as a crop. The forest industry associations also encourage continuous forest production by adoption of forest practices which are more stringent than the minimum practices provided by the forest practice rules. The establishment of industry sponsored tree farms and advisory services provide other programs for assuring continuous forest production.

Economic factors are largely responsible for the cooperative efforts being undertaken to assure a constant source of timber products in this State. As stated by C. L. Morey, representing Fiberboard Products, Incorporated: 

"The battle for good forest practices is being won nation-wide largely because of one fact * * * I refer to the fact that good forestry is good business and as long as that is true under our free enterprise system, you will not see our California forests laid waste." 

The part played by the forest industries is illustrated by the testimony of Ralph Hodges, California District Forest Engineer, Western Pine 

Association. Mr. Hodges stated in part that: "The association's own (forest practice) rules, which were adopted in 1932, served as a pattern for the present state standards. 

Such rules are needed as minimum standards in keeping forest lands productive. The Western Pine industry believes in public and private cooperation in continuous forest production and for increasing timber supply. It supports forest practice rules of proven necessity for the common goal in keeping lands productive. It recommends that timber landowners go beyond the forest practice rules in order to get maximum production of forest crops. There are increasing owners doing this, and they have adopted many different advanced practices and are continually finding ways to grow and harvest trees more profitably. Already one-fourth of industrial-owned lands have been certified. California leads all western states in the pine region in number of tree farms. Intensive forestry practices are not matters for legislative action. The pine industry would prefer to see forest practices exceeding the state law minimum on private lands as a result of normal incentives and desire for increased production.

SOIL CONSERVATION SERVICE 

The first farm forestry advisory service provided by governmental agencies was initiated in California in 1940 with the establishment of two demonstration projects by the U. S. Soil Conservation Service. Federal activities of this type were transferred to the U. S. Forest Service in 1945. 

As will be noted later, the Federal Government has ceased to provide advisory services directly to the farmer or timber owner but does assist the states in providing such services by subsidizing part of the costs through the Department of Agriculture's Production and Marketing 

Administration. 

Between 1940 and 1945, however, the Soil Conservation Service did provide specialized forest advisory service for members of soil conservation districts. For example, George Volz, Chairman, South Sierra Pine

Forest Practice Committee, testified as follows: 

"I would like to state briefly a few remarks regarding the assistance which I have received from the farm forestry program. My first assistance was received through the farm forester connected with the Soil Conservation Service. Quite a complete program was laid out. The timber was cruised, and through the use of the increment borer, they determined the amount of board feet that was growing per acre per year and laid out certain plans for harvesting this crop. 

---

The Soil Conservation Service now maintains three foresters in California who work in a liaison capacity with the forestry profession but do not provide direct services to landowners as was done in the case of the testimony cited above. The Soil Conservation Service and the Production and Marketing Administration work with farmers in the planning and application of soil and water conservation measures. Technical farm planning assistance is provided through the SCS and incentive payments to farmers for applying conservation practices are made by the PMA through its agricultural conservation program. As stated by John Barnes, State Conservationist for the Soil Conservation Service: 

"The maintenance of a good forest cover on farmers' forest lands is essential for their protection and for the production of needed forest products. We find that farmers need the same high quality technical advice on the management of their timber crops as they need on other farm crops. Our field men are continuously asked by farmers where such farm forestry assistance can be secured. Few private foresters are available for helping these scattered farmers with their problems. Also, the cost to an individual farmer of hiring a forestry specialist is often more than he feels he can afford, particularly the beginning stages of his interest in farm forest management. "The State Division of Forestry has demonstrated through its service foresters that it can give the type of forestry assistance farmers, and other small woodland owners need. There is need for a larger staff of these trained men to whom our men can refer farmers who are ready to adopt sound woodland management plans. 

This need is particularly great in the northern coastal counties and the forest counties bordering the Sacramento Valley. We feel that this assistance should be provided woodland owners if the current exploitation of these lands is to be checked and their productivity maintained. "In other words, the Soil Conservation Service concerns itself primarily with those features of land use that will control erosion and help the farmer with those particular practices aimed to control erosion and conserve soil, and refer them to specialists in forestry for their forestry problems and specialists in law for their legal problems. 

STATE FOREST ADVISORY SERVICE 

The Senate California Forestry Study Committee adopted the following recommendations in 1947: "We recommend that the Division of Forestry be enabled to employ 'forest advisors' to give advice and guidance in farm forest management to the end that the owners of small timber tracts may derive the maximum returns and benefits from them."

This recommendation was based on the realization that about one-half of some 8,000,000 acres of timber cropland in private ownership is owned in tracts of less than 5,000 acres in size. These 4,000,000 acres are owned by over 18,000 different individuals or firms. About one-third of this acreage is owned by some 15,000 farmers.

The State Forester cites these statistics as an indication that the major share of the success of any forestry program in California will depend in a large measure on what is done on these lands. The state forest practice program has accomplished some good in the matter of small ownerships; but the biggest obstacle lies in the fact that unless the small forest owner can see how he himself is to benefit, he is often tempted to liquidate his forest resources and attempt to convert those lands to other uses. 

In order to meet the demonstrated need for expansion of the advisory service to forest landowners, which began with the Soil Conservation Service, the State in cooperation with the forest service established a "service" forestry program in 1945. The number of "service" foresters was increased to six in 1948. 

Prior to 1951, this activity came under the Federal Norris-Doxie Act but now operates under the provisions of the Federal Cooperative Forest Management Act of 1950. Although the program is conducted in cooperation with the Federal Government which contributes some $8,000 annually (about 15 percent of the total cost) this activity is principally state financed and is administered by the Division of Forestry. 

The objective of the "service" forestry program of the Division of Forestry is to extend in-the-woods technical advice to farm and other forest land owners on the establishment, protection, and management of forest land and on harvesting, utilization, and marketing of forest products for the purposes of sustaining and improving the productivity of forest land in farm and small ownerships. The service is not restricted to rigid boundaries, but an attempt is made to spread it throughout the major portion of the commercial forest land area of the State. Accordingly, the "service" foresters do not furnish detailed services in the form of elaborate management plans, cruises, and type maps to a few individuals confined to an intensive demonstration project. The amount of service provided is guided by the following principles: (1) Give only that amount of service that can be given to other interested owners. (2) Stress the education and training angle so that the forest owner learns to make rough cruises, mark timber, scale logs, etc., instead of depending upon the "service" forester. This almost precludes any work with nonresident owners unless they can spend time in the field with the "service" forester. (3) Show those owners that have large enough properties to afford it, that when sufficient forest values are involved, a consulting forester or cruiser could be profitably employed. It has been our experience that the forest advisory program stimulates rather than competes with such private services. 

Six full-time men and part-time services of other technicians are employed in this program throughout the timbered counties of the State. 

These men are generally restricted to this one activity, but at times they may be utilized in other work such as fire control, training fire control personnel in use of maps and aerial photographs and survey of fire damage, range improvement, forest practice regulation, and other forest management fields. However, this is more than offset by forest advisory services performed by some qualified assistant and associate rangers, and other technically trained men.

Some of the accomplishments of the "service" forestry program of the Division of Forestry are indicated in the report of work done under this program as shown in Table 9. In addition the "service" foresters perform valuable services in relation to permits for clear cutting. Generally, requests to cut trees below the legal minimum diameter limit are referred to the "service" forester for field inspection. In cases where conversion of the land to a productive use other than forest growth is doubtful, the "service" forester makes a definite attempt to show the owner its potential as timber growing land. In many cases this results in the conservation of the most favorable areas as timber sites for the production of timber. These contacts have proven of great value as an aid in selling sound wild-land management to people who seldom are reached by other means.

The service forestry program is one of the most important and basic steps in selling forest management and good forest practices in California. 

When a service forester makes a satisfactory contact with a land owner often the problems of enforcing forest practice rules and forest and fire laws are solved as well. It is common to see a rancher who is an expert manager of his crops and herds completely unaware of the value of his timber.

To improve this program recommendations were made that: (1) The State immediately expand its present program with an additional two service forestry positions assigned to Northern California. (2) Reclassify service forestry positions from assistant forest technician to forest technician as the employee becomes trained and proficient in his work. This will bring continuity to the program in which personal contact and intimate knowledge of an area are essential in order to sell forest management to forest owners. FOREST CONSULTANTS 

There are approximately fifteen resident consulting foresters in California. In addition, some from other states have California clients. These professionally trained foresters provide sound technical advice and service related to various types of forestry problems, including timber cruising, timber sales, and management of forest lands.

It might appear at first glance that the various advisory forestry programs of the Division of Forestry and other agencies might interfere with the private services offered by the consulting foresters. This, however, is not the situation in California inasmuch as the advisory programs are educational and stop short of intensive management services. When such services are required that the owner himself cannot do, "service" foresters refer the timber owner to a list of consulting foresters who are familiar with the forest areas and problems involved in the particular case.

One of the consulting foresters, Myron S. Wall, Jr., summarized the advisory service programs and the relationship between the service foresters and the consulting foresters as follows: "First, the advisory service has done an excellent job with its limited manpower since its inception in 1940. "Second, it is of paramount importance that the state force already engaged in this work be increased to give it the necessary manpower to meet the tremendous work load imposed by the necessity of contacting the hundreds of landowners in need of this advice. "Third, the functions of free governmental forestry advice are to contact, introduce and demonstrate; while those of the private consultant are to formulate the details and implement the program. "Fourth, present relations between the governments and private enterprise in this field are good because there is a general understanding between them of the functions of each. "Fifth, federal participation is to be commended so long as no attempt is made to divert authority from the state level."

FARM ADVISORS AND EXTENSION SERVICE 

The relationship of the service foresters of the Division of Forestry to the farm advisors of the Agricultural Extension Service of the University of California was the subject of considerable discussion at the joint hearing, November 6 to 8, 1952. The State Forester explained that activities of the six "service" foresters were somewhat similar to those of the farm advisers but in an entirely different field. 

With the exception of Sonoma and Tuolumne Counties, the farm. advisers are not professionally trained foresters. Some farm advisers, however, are actively interested in forestry and forest problems particularly those in counties with relatively large timber areas. The farm advisers in these counties cooperate closely with the "service" foresters where such are available in addition to receiving advice from the Extension Forester's office at the University of California.

One of the farm advisers who has had no professional training in forestry who is actively interested in forest problems is W. D. Pine of 

Humboldt County. Reference was made in Chapter II of this report to the brief summary of the lumbering industry in the United States presented to the committee by Mr. Pine. This summary was part of a nationwide survey made by him upon the recommendation of the ranchers and at the request of the Board of Supervisors of Humboldt County in cooperation with the College of Agriculture Extension Service of the 

University of California.

The objective of this survey was to determine the extent to which timber or cut-over lands should be used for timber production or converted to other agricultural crops. As explained by this farm adviser, the fir timber of Humboldt County had no commercial value prior to 1940 and was considered to be a "nuisance" to range landowners. These range landowners were getting rid of their fir and attempts were being made to convert logged-off land to agriculture. As stated by Mr. Pine: "Stumpage prices were going up and loggers were going back to harvest timber previously left standing which was not then of sufficient value to warrant cutting. Under the law of California, if the area to be logged were declared to be used for agricultural purposes after logging, the trees could be cut and nothing remain but brush and debris. This presumably was to be followed by fire to make way for agriculture. In a good many instances the law was used to sell the timber even though the land was never intended to be used for agriculture. "This situation prevailed until the spring of 1952 when the Agricultural Extension Service began to have inquiries for advice on forestry on range lands. We had worked with cooperators on seeding burned-over areas but knew very little of forestry practices, except what we learned from the rancher himself. Range landowners were

1 beginning to feel are we making a mistake with the present economic situation of timber in trying to convert timber growing land to agriculture? Also, what is going to be the effect on property taxes after our present economic bubble has burst?”

In conducting this "Timber Survey," the author contacted small and large timber owners, livestock producers, private tree farm owners, corporation tree farm managers, commercial concerns, and public officials. 

These interviews were conducted over a period of two months mainly in the Pacific Northwest, the Great Lakes States, and the Southeastern States.

After describing the climax and decline of lumber production in other states, the author summarizes his findings of present conditions and states in part that: "The majority of people in Humboldt County, and for that matter the entire State of California, have not yet realized the value of their timber and what it means to their future economic stability. They still think in terms of inexhaustibility." 

Another significant finding was that "the advance in technology is rapidly taking care of the reduction from old growth to the use of second growth." Other findings were as follows: "Private capital is purchasing millions of acres of land and setting it aside as tree farms. *** In the Nation there has been an increase of 165 percent in lands planted to trees over the past five years. "There are more trained foresters employed by private industry than either the state or federal governments. "There are 119 nurseries operated by private, state and federal governments for the growing of seedling trees in the United States. 

During the years 1945-50, these nurseries have increased from 59 to 119, turning out approximately one-third billion seedling trees per year; but California is still very slow in the development of this program. "In some states, forestry is considered of great public interest, and bond issues have been voted to keep forest lands in perpetual production."

The conclusions of the author of this "Timber Survey" with particular reference to Humboldt County are as follows: "In many instances disregard for our Forest Practice Act makes our position somewhat comparable to that of the Great Lakes States and other sections 50 years ago when they were cutting timber resources without plans or regards for the future. "If they had the knowledge of forestry as we know it today and had taken advantage of this knowledge, they would have avoided the calamity in which they found themselves. "Humboldt County has the opportunity now to avoid these mistakes, if people here become aware of what may be expected at the end of the present cutting operations of old timber. "A program should be started immediately to acquaint people with the facts so that at the end of the present harvest (estimated

to be 15 to 20 years) we shall be well on the way to having another crop to replace the original stands." 

As a result of these conclusions, the author presented a number of recommendations to the timber owners of Humboldt County. The services of the State Division of Forestry were described as being particularly helpful. Some excerpts from these recommendations are as follows: "If you have second growth redwood, Douglas fir, or hardwood, it may not be much value at the present time-but it will-protect it, and give the best trees a chance to grow! It will reward you with dollars. "If you have a forestry problem, consult technical advice *** your State Division of Forestry will be interested and helpful in your problem. "Do not declare timber about to be harvested as being on agricultural land unless you are satisfied it is agricultural land and can be used as such. State laws give you this privilege, but it has been abused in many stances. Consult your local State Division of Forestry officials for advice on laws before cutting. "Insist on a soil classification survey for the county comparable to the survey completed for Mendocino County and made by the 

State Division of Forestry and the Forest Experiment Station at 

Berkeley. "Give additional support and cooperation to your State Division of Forestry in prevention of fires. Information gathered from other sections emphasizes that fire is the greatest enemy of our forest enterprise. If used properly, fire is an aid in some reforestation; but used improperly, it causes destruction." 

THE SMALL WOODLAND COUNCIL 

The above description of the extensive timber survey made by the Humboldt County Farm Advisor illustrates the educational nature of the activities of the farm advisors of the College of Agriculture Extension Service of the University of California. As far as forestry problems are concerned, this report dramatically describes the need for technical advice from professionally trained foresters. 

As noted above, the Farm Adviser who made this report for Humboldt County was not a professional forester. His findings, however, were confirmed by Woodbridge Metcalf, an Extension Forester, on the staff of the Extension Service, University of California for approximately 30 years. This Extension Forester, while on sabbatical leave from the university covered the same sections of the United States as those described by Pine and confirmed his findings as follows: "I think we can take some satisfaction in the productivity and the satisfactory condition of our California forests and try to prevent much of our forest land from getting into the condition that so much of the forest land of the United States is in, because I think if anybody goes to these other parts of the country you will find that there are literally millions of acres of waste land and some of the biggest economic problems that these states have such as Minnesota with some six to eight million acres of waste land, Wisconsin and Michigan the same, Ontario with a million and a half acres in

the Niagara Peninsula of what is supposed to be some of the finest farm land in Canada, waste land. These problems take many, many decades to solve."

The Agricultural Extension Service of the University of California has two Extension Foresters, W. Metcalf and R. F. Grah, who work in cooperation with the State Division of Forestry and perform educational and promotional services in the field of forestry for Farm Advisors and their clients. One of the coordinating and promotional services recently organized under the auspices of the University of California is the Small 

Woodlands Council. Extension Forester Metcalf explained the objectives,. 

membership, and organization of the Council as follows: "The objectives: to obtain good management on California woodlands; second, to serve as means for the coordination of the work of all agencies involved in the management of small woodlands; third, to develop a uniformly high degree of knowledge and competence among all personnel working in small woodland management; fourth, to gather, prepare and disseminate in usable form technical information related to the management of small woodlands; fifth, to promote demonstrations of improved woodland management practices; sixth, to foster research related to the problems of small woodland management. 

"Second, membership: University of California School of For estry, dean of the school and one additional staff member concerned with woodland management; second, University of California agricultural extension service, the extension foresters; third, the United 

States Soil Conservation Service, staff men dealing with woodland management; fourth, United States Forest Service, Region Five, staff men in state and private forestry; fifth, the California Range and Experiment Station, representative from the forest management research staff; sixth, California Division of Forestry, forest management staff men; seven, consulting foresters, two men from different parts of the State engaged in consulting work with small woodland owners; eight, Redwood and Western Pine Associations, one representative from each tree farm certifying committee; nine, such alternates, consultants, or additional regular members as may be invited by the above listed organizations to participate in the council. “Third, organization: The council shall function in a coordinating and promotional manner, allowing each participant full freedom of action in carrying out council recommendations in the manner best suited to the objectives and mode of operation of his parent organization. There shall be a chairman, which shall be selected from the membership of the council to serve as executive head of the organization for a period of one year. The chairman shall call and preside at meetings of the council and shall appoint such committees as are necessary to carry on the work of the council. A secretary shall be selected from the membership to serve concurrently with the chairman. He shall keep record of the actions of the council and shall keep council members informed as to its actions."

The educational and promotional objectives of the Small Woodlands Council, therefore, are designed to acquaint the owners of relatively small timber areas with the various technical services that will enable them to follow efficient management practices for their timber resources. 

One of the first steps in this direction is the preparation of a series of 

Woodland Management Guide Sheets on the following subjects: erosion and forest soils, growing coniferous seedlings, tree pruning, selective cutting, thinning, Christmas trees, log scaling, planting forest crops, direct seeding, insect control, and forest product markets. These will be used by all agencies and organizations in this educational work. Other coordinated publicity, educational and demonstration projects are contemplated.

CHAPTER VI FOREST PRODUCT INDUSTRIES 

As discussed in earlier chapters of this report, general economic conditions influence and, to a large extent, motivate the policies and pro grams of the State Board of Forestry and the State Division of Forestry. 

The expanding forest production in the last 10 years, for example, increased the problems of fire protection and enforcement of the provi sions of the Forest Practice Act. 

In this connection, the problem of slash removal has been discussed as being one of the important problems for which no adequate solution has been found to date. Slash and related forest materials have frequently been called waste products of logging operations. This term is becoming a misnomer because of the increasing number of industries that are being developed to utilize such material other than by sawmill and veneer processes.

One of the newer types of forest product industries in California is that which processes various products from wood fibers. The Forest 

Product Laboratories of the U. S. Forest Service in Wisconsin and 

Oregon have provided a foundation for the development of such industries. Some of the possibilities for wood fiber industries were summarized by William G. Van Beckum, Pacific Lumber Company, as follows: "Economics, of course, is a very important consideration in dealing with the various types of raw material. In addition to the raw material available from the forest, we have very plentiful supplies from existing forest products manufacturing plants. These are excellent sources of wood raw material, particularly since the wood from these operations is by-product wood on which money has been spent to fell it and to transport it to the mill. More often than not, this by-product wood goes to waste. Here, of course, we must recognize that such wood is advantageously used in many instances as fuel, nevertheless, the fuel needs taken care of, there still is an abundance left over. Many of the wood fiber plants around the country today are operating entirely on by-product wood generated in sawmills, plywood mills, and other wood processing plants. "In considering wood fiber industries we must also be aware of capital investments necessary for plants to produce these fibers; then, too, we must gauge, as accurately as possible, the market potential for wood fiber and products derived from it, as well as the extents and limits of our participation on this potential brought about by geographical reasons. In giving thought to wood fiber production for various uses, we are obliged to run the entire gamut from such comparatively crude wood fiber uses as chicken litter right on through to the chemically produced pulps that are used in paper making and the still more highly refined wood fiber pulps that are basic raw material for the manufacture of such fineries as plastics, cellophane, rayon fabrics and the like. WOOD FIBER MARKETS AND BETTER FOREST PRACTICES HENRY VAUX, School of Forestry, University of California 

"I have been asked to speak about the relationships between wood fiber markets and better forest practices in California; and in view of this, the purpose of my statement is to call attention to wood markets as one of these numerous factors which influence better forest practice and suggest some of the problems of developing that sort of market here in California. "In 1946, 95 percent of all the wood cut for commodity use in California was for sawmill logs. Most of the remainder was for veneer logs, shingle bolts and similar products where minimum specifications are even more strict than in the case of saw logs. This means that logs have had to be at least 10 inches in diameter, at least 12 feet long, and contain one-third to one-half sound wood volume in order to be acceptable for sale or use in California. Moreover, marketability has been very different for different species. Our saw log markets pay relatively high prices for redwood and pine, but some of our most common woods, such as white fir, bring much lower returns. "To the logger, working in old growth stands, this means that it is necessary to leave a considerable volume of wood lying on the ground because no one will pay him to utilize it. On the average, about 15 percent of the sound wood volume in the stems of trees cut for commodity production in California is thus left to rot in the form of these logging residues. In the representative old growth stand, where the present harvest may remove 30,000 board feet of saw logs per acre, over a thousand cubic feet of sound wood per acre may be left on the ground,

50 al 1 Most of this will be in the form of small trees, sections of tree stems too short to make a log, or logs which are big enough but which contain too little sound wood. At present levels of cutting, it is estimated that more than 100,000,000 cubic feet of wood is thus removed each year from California's stock of standing timber without contributing in any way toward the economic well being. Each year more wood goes into these logging residues than is now destroyed in California by fire, insects, and all other natural enemies of the forest combined. Sometimes these logging residues are called logging waste, but that is a misnomer. The big bulk of the logging residue is in sizes, qualities, or species of material for which there is no current market. It would actually be wasteful of time and energy to remove it under present conditions. Thus logging residues are a problem largely because of the narrowness of our market for wood. 

If, as is true in many other sections of the country, we had markets for sound material four inches in diameter, and four feet long, much of this logging residue could be put to useful purposes. What such a market development might mean for California forestry may best be seen by looking at other sections of the country, where the quality limitations on the wood market are not so narrow as they are here. Throughout the 

1 southern United States the market for saw log material is supplemented by a very strong demand for pulpwood. Material as small as 4 inches in diameter and 4 feet in length is there commonly accepted for pulpwood use. The evidence of the strategic importance of such wood fiber markets for better forest practice is widespread. "In states like Louisiana and Mississippi, for example, only a little over half of the wood consumed is of saw log quality or better. A fifth of the entire market volume is pulpwood and another fifth is for fuel wood, distillation wood, and similar material where the purchaser wants sound wood fiber and is not concerned with other quality characteristics of the raw material. "The benefits from this sort of market are reflected on many forest properties both large and small throughout the south. In 1946, the Forest 

Service found that 17,000,000 acres of privately owned land in the southern states was being cut under practices which distinctly improved the productivity of the forest. In the absence of diversified markets this area would have had to be handled under much less efficient methods of cutting. The fact that there is much more intensive cutting practice in the south than there is in California reflects the difference in wood markets in the two areas to a considerable degree. A market for pulpwood, or other wood fiber, is important to forest practice because it gives a forester a far greater degree of flexibility in his treatment of a forest stand. Without it, his treatments must be rigid for all his work must be done within the rigorous quality specifications for a saw log. With it, he can take much fuller advantage of silvicultural knowledge, of the basic productive powers of the forest stand, and of economic opportunities for forest production. Such markets would permit much closer utilization of material cut in our old growth forests, with resulting advantages in reduction of slash, in increased economic returns; it would permit us to expand by as much as one-sixth the economic activity supported by our forest resources without any necessary increase in the draining of those resources. Moreover, it would make possible new types of improved forest practice in both young and old stands; for example, although the pines are the most

prized species in the mixed conifer forests of the Sierra, they are often less aggressive competitors than the firs with the result that cutting favors transformation of mixed stands of pine and fir into relatively pure stands of the lower valued fir. "Present markets intensify the problem because they make it necessary to leave many small standing trees which may be predominantly fir. 

Moreover, large firs are often defective and hence unmarketable, yet they are such prolific seed sources that they may literally overwhelm prime regeneration. If a pulpwood market were present, both the old defective firs and many small firs could be removed from the stand economically. 

The net result would be to make forest conditions much more favorable for development of a pine forest in the stand and thus to check the trend toward elimination of pine growing stock from our mixed types. "Another sort of beneficial effect is to reduce the time needed to grow a forest crop. For example, in California, a forest owner who has some productive well stocked second growth timber now 50 years old, finds that the average tree in his stand is now less than 12 inches in diameter. With only a saw log market available, cultural or improvement cutting is not feasible in such a stand at present. If such market conditions continue, his trees will average about 16 inches in diameter in another 40 years at which time he could probably make a profitable partial cut. But if a pulpwood market, taking material down to four inches in size, were available now, this second growth owner would benefit in three ways: First, he could cut some of the small trees now on a stand improvement basis. This would provide him with some current cash income, obviously a very great advantage to the financial stability of his business. Second, much of this cut would come from trees which will die out as a result of normal competition during the next few decades. In other words, he would be able to salvage a substantial volume of wood which is completely lost in the present markets. Third, by removing part of the trees and reducing the level of competition in the forest, the trees that remain will grow considerably faster. As a result, he may then expect his trees to reach an average size of 16 inches in 35 years instead of the 40 years previously required. Even such a modest reduction in the number of years an owner must wait for his crop is important in a business like forestry where the long period required for the crop to mature is a factor which discourages some owners. "Up to this point I have been emphasizing the fact that if we had a more diversified market for wood in California, it would stimulate better forestry in a very practical and effective way. But what are the odds that such a market will appear? Is a pulpwood market something that we may reasonably expect to develop and actively work to promote, or is it merely wishful thinking to talk in such terms? Some progress toward market diversification has already been made. Between 1946 and 1950, the consumption of wood for pulp in this State increased from about 300,000 board feet to almost 50,000,000 board feet per year. This increase resulted primarily from the installation of new fiber using plants at Ukiah and Antioch. More recently, further expansion resulted from the opening of a hard board plant at Anderson. These expansions have had a significant effect on promoting better forest practice in certain areas. The new plants use large quantities of materials which are too poor in quality for the

sawmills and the plywood mills, and the prices they pay for wood discriminate less between species. This recent development is most encouraging, but we should recognize that it has only scratched the surface of the problem. In aggregate, fiber use still accounts for less than 2 percent of all commodity drained from the forests of the State. "The favorable effects, particularly upon the utilization of young growth stands, are still confined to relatively small geographic areas. 

Very great further expansion of the market for low grade wood is needed before most of our forest landowners can take advantage of such opportunities for better forestry. There is little doubt that consumer markets for pulp and fiber products will provide the necessary basis for needed expansion. Consumption of paper, paper board and other pulp products in the United States has risen steadily and dramatically over the past half century. It is now about 12 times what it was in 1900. Per capita use has grown from less than 60 pounds per person per year in 1900 to almost 400 pounds today. Since 1920, the expansion of pulp use has been closely related to expansion in total national output of all goods. Factors in world markets for pulp, of which we are a substantial importer, seem likely also to favor further growth. Hence, demand for paper and paper board will probably continue to grow so long as the economic productivity of the Nation rises. Both population trends and industrial developments suggest that California's demands for paper and board will expand at least in proportion to the nation-wide trend. "As already noted, potential supplies in the form of a presently unused wood resource adequate to meet the needs of such expanded demand are also here. Utilization of half the logging residues now accumulated in the course of saw log production, plus an equal volume of wood cut from small or defective trees, would provide a supply of pulpwood of about a million cords per year, enough to make about 80 pounds of paper and board per year per person now in California. Such levels of pulpwood use are thus well within the absorptive capacity of existing or foreseeable 

California markets. "Expansion in the California wood fiber industry will not, however, come about automatically as a result of the existence here of the necessary potential demands and supplies. Substantial problems of industrial development must first be solved. These problems involve broad and complicated questions of economic and industrial growth. It is precisely because the problems lie outside of forestry and yet are vital to it that I feel this matter deserves your attention. One group of basic obstacles to expansion to our wood fiber market lies in some everyday problems of business organization and development; such as, the level and structure of transportation rates, labor and capital costs as compared with those of competing regions, problems of industrial water supply and effects of tax policies both on the establishment of new industries and on the feasibility of forestry. "In the final analysis, the indirect influence of factors such as these may well prove as important to California forestry as the more direct effects of public forestry programs. It is obviously impossible to suggest specific action needed to overcome these sorts of obstacles to the market development we have been discussing. They will ultimately be overcome through natural forces of economic growth. But it is equally clear that

a very great number of activities of state and local government will influence this desired market development indirectly. If we can recognize these effects and take them into account in deciding questions on such matters as tax policy, we might accomplish a good bit in improving the economic environment on which good forestry ultimately depends. "The second set of obstacles is primarily technological. Under currently used methods of pulp manufacture, tremendous quantities of pure water are required. For a bleach craft mill making a hundred tons of pulp per day, about the smallest practical size, forest products laboratory estimates that 6,000,000 gallons of processing water are needed to operate for one day. In the north coastal area of the State, I am told that water is now available to meet requirements of this magnitude for a pulp industry. But the bulk of our forest area lies in areas where water is scarce. Moreover, wood is expensive to haul over long distances; so plants fairly near the woods and hence where water supplies are not a potential or actual problem, will be needed if the available wood is to be marketed economically. The role of water supplies may thus be critical in determining the extent to which effective fiber markets can be developed in California. "On the one hand is the problem of water pollution. Much study has already been given to this matter and some solutions have been found; but each new or prospective pulp plant must still face pollution as a major problem. A separate and unsolved question is how to utilize fiber without such heavy consumptive use of water. Purification of effluence to avoid pollution may also permit re-use of much of the processing water, but this, in turn, is costly and may be less economic than to locate where water is cheap and plentiful. "It is thus apparent that there is plenty of room for developing fiber using methods which will be more economical in their use of water. But if this is to be achieved, a substantial program of research into how this may best be done seems clearly needed. The pulpwood paper industry is justly famous for the advanced level and technical ingenuity of its research program. Public agencies have also made splendid contributions to improve pulpwood paper technology, but so far as I can learn, only a small amount of research is now directed specifically at re-using water. 

Undoubtedly the problem is of much greater significance in California than anywhere else in the Country. So this is a specific research problem where we, in California, need to assume the leadership and where successful development of new techniques can contribute significantly to more effective use of our timber resources."

CHAPTER VII FOREST REGENERATION AND FOREST PEST CONTROL 

The objective of continuous forest production of the Forest Practice Act is also the objective of other activities of the Division of Forestry and the forest industry. These activities include the promotion of forest regeneration, a control of forest insects and disease. As stated by the 

State Forester: "The regeneration of California's forest lands that have been denuded by fires, insect attacks, and improper logging is of much concern to foresters and forest landowners. This concern is not limited to lands lost to forest production up to the present time, but will continue to be a concern as additional lands become deforested by fires, insects and other causes. 

According to the Division of Forestry, over 2,000,000 acres of medium and high site (sites having the best possibilities of reforestation and growth) of the total of some 15,000,000 acres of forest land in the State are considered to be stocked to less than 20 percent of capacity. More than four-fifths of these 2,000,000 acres are privately owned. In addition there are over 1,000,000 acres of private and public lands of poor site quality that have inadequate stocking. These poor site lands probably never can be restocked economically.

In addition to these poorly stocked lands, it can be assumed that the problem is growing every year as more acres are lost due to destruction by fire and pests. It is estimated that California may be losing as much as one-half of 1 percent, or about 8,000 acres, of its timber cropland area annually to these causes.

NEED FOR RESEARCH 

Considerable testimony was presented at the joint hearing, November 6-8, 1952, to show that the progress made to recover these losses is not impressive. W. S. Rosecrans described this poor record as "the really sad history of artificial forest regeneration in California." Part of the reason for the lack of progress in restocking the timber croplands is the lack of knowledge as to what should be done and the proper procedures to be followed in the reforesting of the various forest areas. 

The State Board of Forestry recently reviewed the need for artificial forest regeneration and passed the following resolution: "Failure to secure adequate reproduction following cutting or fire has caused many thousands of acres of good timber cropland to become idle and an economic loss to the State and the owners thereof. 

While California's forest practice regulations are specifically planned for the purpose of securing the reproduction of a good stand of trees, there is still much to learn about natural regeneration and much more about artificial forest regeneration. It is therefore proposed that the State Board of Forestry call a conference of selected representatives of the forest industry, the University of 

California, the California Forest and Range Experiment Station, ( 59 )

United States Forest Service and the State Board of Forestry. This would be a comparatively small conference of active key persons to operate without publicity and to consider the various problems involved in forest regeneration, both from the standpoint of good silvicultural practice and sound economics. "Among its purposes would be to arrange for concise summary of the present knowledge on the subject, agreement among the research agencies concerned for the prosecution of this research and steps for the application of results as rapidly as they are reached." 

The chairman of the State Board of Forestry commented upon the adoption of this resolution as follows: "Now, this would be, in effect, a preliminary stock taking to guide future operations and policies in forest regeneration. Now, these hearings, which are being held currently on these vital problems of wild-land management, are going to set the stage for operations and appropriations and programs probably for the next decade, and therefore, the hearing today on forest regeneration and developing this program will develop many ideas which should be followed for the next few years in really getting some effective work on forest regeneration. This step which the board takes is a preliminary step of stock taking. In posing this problem before the Legislature and before the people of California, I think we will do well to emphasize its importance and to emphasize the fact that if we are going to make tree farming a reality we have to get a stock of trees on the land. 

And then, we have to try to improve the quality of that stock." 

THE FOREST REGIONS 

California has five distinct forest types, the pine, redwood, Douglas fir, fir and mixed conifer (pine, Douglas fir, fir as shown on the timber croplands map, Chart 5). Distinctly different problems of forest regeneration are encountered in these forest types. 

THE PINE REGION 

Successful tree farms exist in each. The problem of artificial regeneration in the pine and fir forests, however, is much more difficult than in the redwood forest areas. This difficulty in the pine forests has been experienced in Washington and Oregon as well as in California. Some of the problems in the pine region were summarized by R. Roseberry, Diamond Match Company, as follows: 

"My discussion of regeneration problems primarily applies to the pine region. Even where logging only meets the minimum forest practice requirements there is seldom need for artificial reforestation because there are nearly always many trees left below the diameter limit. Some of these are: where fire has killed all trees and removed any seed source; where the forest has been opened up to brush invasion and so made natural re-establishment of trees very slow. 1. Is much of it a site high enough in growing capacity to merit the expense. 

2. Could the money be spent better on existing high site forested areas to get them in maximum production. The policy should be to get the best areas in full production first.

"Rodent control without seeding when a good seed year occurs has shown good results and rodent repellant seed also offers possibilities. "Seed bed preparation by scarifying the ground has proven effective in some instances. "Airplane applications may prove superior for seeding and rodent control after more experimentation. "To help keep our company lands fully stocked we are trying to find an economical answer to reforestation. Fortunately, it is not a major problem as most of our cutover lands are fully stocked and nature wastes no time in seeding openings that occur. However, in burned areas and in some timber types where natural reforestation is completely lacking or slowed down by the encroachment of brush it is necessary to seed or plant to keep the lands in full production. "Planting is too costly so we are experimenting with seeding and rodent control. We have had some success with seeding in past years but must find a way to economically obtain the desired results on large areas. At the present time we are treating areas for rodent control that have a good natural seed crop and trying various methods of distributing seed along with rodent control on areas that have been burned. Some of our neighboring companies, as well as the 

Forest Service, are also experimenting along these lines and it is hoped that we will have the answer to some of our reforestation problems in the near future.”

THE REDWOOD REGION 

The relationship between natural reseeding and artificial regeneration in the redwood region was discussed by A. Merrill, Hammond Lumber 

Company, as follows: 

"On Hammond Lumber Company lands, two methods of logging virgin timberlands are utilized, both of which are so planned that natural regeneration of the harvested areas will be accomplished. 

I might point out that on practically all year-round operations in Humboldt County and Del Norte County in the redwood region, both of these systems of logging are employed with various modifications. On our current operations between 85 percent and 90 percent of the timber harvested is cut on a tree selection basis and is logged by tractor. On these shows, all larger, over mature and decadent trees which will produce lumber are removed, making possible an accelerated growth on younger fast growing trees. Redwood smaller than 42 inches at stump height and Douglas fir smaller

16 than 30 inches are left standing. On one of our larger operations we leave all redwoods smaller than 48 inches standing. The stand resulting normally contains between 10 and 30 trees per acre and provides a seed source to reforest the holes left by the cutting of merchantable timber. "We have inventoried more than 52,000 acres of cutover lands in Hammond ownership and in practically every area where adequate seed trees were left in good condition following logging, successful natural regeneration of trees has taken place. Where repeated fires have destroyed both seed source and reproduction and on certain areas where heavy brush has captured the site, adequate natural regeneration has been long delayed or completely prevented. "During the winter months when wet weather makes tractor logging impractical on our broken terrain, a high lead logging system is employed to maintain log production. About 10 percent or 

15 percent of the acreage cut over each year is logged by this method on our lands. A carefully planned and well constructed truck road system extended many miles ahead into the virgin timber not only provides flexibility in selection of stands to leave and stands to cut but also permits location of high lead setting laid out in narrow strips well back in the virgin forest. Each high lead setting is about 

800 feet wide and averages about 30 acres in size. About 10 or 15 acres are clear cut with the remainder covered with seed trees. Every year, by modification of our logging methods, a smaller clear cut area is left-more of the setting is covered with seed trees. The seed source surrounding the clear cut area is left standing until full restocking has taken place. "Now, to determine whether natural regeneration is accomplished on such clear cut areas, we have conducted reproduction studies on a series of the first high lead setting operated after this strip logging system was adopted. Each year our logging department has perfected high lead logging on staggered settings both as to operation and seed source planning and therefore the conditions for restocking on the settings examined are generally less favorable than on the more recently logged areas. "Generally, a period of from 5 to 10 years, following logging or slash burning, is required to determine whether a cutover area will inadequately restock from natural seeding. Our findings are based on the amount of reproduction produced by only three seed years and are therefore tentative, but we found that 47.5 percent of the clear cut areas had already restocked with reproduction, fully restocked, and that the average acre within these settings on the clear cut area carried 500 trees. By species, 63 percent of the reproduction was redwood, 22 percent Douglas fir and the remainder, other whitewoods. All indications point to good natural restocking of these high lead settings with a passage of a few more years. "As a further check of natural seeding, seed traps were set out on the settings examined. These seed traps were constructed of plywood in the shape of a funnel with the open top four feet square. 

Seed traps, as the name implies, are laid out to get a sample of the amount of seed that is disseminated from seed sources naturally.

The quantity of seed caught in the traps is a very conservative indication of the amount actually disseminated over these clear cut areas because: "1. Three-quarters of the traps were placed in areas believed least likely to reseed. "2. Most of the Douglas fir and some of the redwood cones had opened before the traps were placed in position in November. "Based on results obtained thus far, which are incomplete- I might add we are continuing all these experiments-we believe that at least a pound and a half of seed, containing 178,000 seeds, are sown over each clear cut acre each year. This should be sufficient seed to produce adequate stocking in one good seed year, provided that conditions for germination and first-year survival are not excessively unfavorable and if the rodent population is not heavy. "We are led to believe that most of the seed is being consumed by rodents, mainly mice, which are known to eat almost their own weight each day. We have further investigated this factor by trapping and find the rodent population is exceedingly heavy. Rodent control of the logged settings, just prior to seed fall, should result in better restocking and at an earlier date. In cooperation with the 

U. S. Fish and Wildlife Service, we are conducting a rodent poisoning experiment this winter over an area of some 500 acres.

THE STATE NURSERY 

An adequate source of seed and seedlings is a necessary prerequisite to a successful reforestation program. Natural reseeding is one of the objectives of the Forest Practice Act. As discussed above, however, planting is also needed in some areas. 

The small nursery maintained by the Division of Forestry is inadequate and improperly located as a source of seedlings for extensive reforestation programs. Both Washington and Oregon exceed California in the number and extent of both industrial and state nurseries. 

The California nursery law requires that planting stock and seed to to be used for public planting, other forestry purposes such as erosion control, and commercial conifers in small size lots be sold at "cost of production." According to the Division of Forestry, this provision has a number of disadvantages. First of all, the double accounting of costs is unrealistic in that the division is required to determine the growing cost of only a portion of the stock of the aggregate production. 

The state nursery program has been operated in cooperation with the Federal Government under Section 4 of the Clarke-McNary Act since July, 1951. The purpose of this legislation is to assist the states in providing incentive planting to farmers and forest landowners. The federal appropriations permit the U. S. Forest Service to underwrite losses incurred by the state nurseries in the production of planting stock for all forestry purposes. This federal aid, however, does not meet the entire deficit. The California State Nursery, for example, incurred a loss of $2,836 during the last fiscal year in growing and selling stock which supposedly should have been sold at "cost of production. 

An other unsatisfactory provision of the present law is that although it provides for nominal prices as incentives for planting of commercial conifers in large quantities, it makes no similar concession to farmers who

1 12 wish to make windbreak and erosion control plantings, nor does it recognize the needs of forest landowners wishing to make smaller planting trials of woodlot, Christmas tree, or small forest plantations. 

Forest tree seed collection is also an important adjunct to a reforestation program, not only to provide seed for nursery needs, but to permit seeding trials. Advantage has to be taken of good seed years which occur infrequently to build up supplies in seed banks. The Division of Forestry has used its fire control and technical personnel to collect seed each year. 

Because of other necessary fire control duties which peak about the same time as seed collection time, the division has probably reached its peak in collection results through this procedure. Although over a ton of clean seed was collected last year, the needs of the division's nursery program will require the purchase of seed from commercial seed collectors. 

Some changes in the present law and other steps to improve California's reforestation program are recommended by the State Forester as follows: 

1. Even at the sacrifice of growing planting stock for public planting purposes by private persons; the nursery law needs elimination of "cost of production" sales of some types of stock. Competition with commercial nurseries should be avoided, but incentives by means of offering planting stock for forestry purposes at nominal prices should be provided. Such prices should be flexible to meet the needs of having them established by the State Forester and approved by the State Board of Forestry. Ready availability of suitable planting stock will go a long way in getting industry and forest landowners to increase their efforts in trial plantings and help solve the many problems. 

2. Research and trial reforestation projects should be accelerated by the federal and state forestry agencies, and by industry working independently and cooperatively. 

3. Technical personnel should be provided to the Division of 

Forestry so that adequate supervision can be had as branch nurseries develop. As it now stands the branch nurseries receive only remote direction from the state nursery at Davis and have to depend on the use of fire control foremen at the camps for supervision. One specialized technically trained man at each branch nursery not only could oversee the nursery activities but also direct the use of inmate personnel in trial reforestation measures on nearby private or stateowned land.

FOREST INSECTS 

The need for reforestation programs is due in part to the losses from insects and disease. The annual drain on California's timber resources from forest insects and disease, although difficult to determine accurately, is estimated to be a half billion board feet. Insects alone caused a loss four times that from fire during the past year. 

The State Forester reports that forest insect infestations are widely scattered and endemic in nature so that control is not always economical or practical. The insect pests we now have are native and although they cannot be completely eradicated, they can be kept under reasonable

control. Concentrated and epidemic types of infestations require prompt and aggressive action if losses are to be held within reasonable economic limits.

California, however, has not experienced severe insect infestations that have visited other states. The pine beetle causes the most damage in this State. Other western states have been plagued by other types of insects. Idaho, for example, had a sudden outbreak of the Douglas-fir tussock moth about five years ago which necessitated aerial spraying of 413,000 acres at a cost of nearly $650,000 to prevent the destruction of 1 to 1 billion board feet of fir timber. Shortly thereafter, an epidemic of spruce budworm in the Pacific Northwest necessitated aerial spraying of 2,750,000 acres at a cost of about $2,750,000 to protect valuable fir timber stands.

The insect surveys and control programs that have been in effect in this State are the result of research which has been almost exclusively a federal undertaking in California. The need for continued research in this field was expressed by Waller H. Reed, representing the Western 

Pine Association, as follows: 

"It has long been evident to the California Forest Practice Committee (Western Pine Association) that the research program on forest insects of the Bureau of Entomology and Plant Quarantine must be greatly strengthened if we hope to get on top of insect outbreaks. These outbreaks are now moving in on us with increasing intensity and loss. We feel this research work can be intensified and strengthened by the addition of some new men but mostly by a program in which the State forestry organization, the Forestry School Faculty, the federal forestry agencies and private foresters assist the bureau in digging out needed facts. It is our feeling that the answers will come quicker if more persons involved are searching for them. "The pine industry would like to see developed a state-wide forest research program on insects and diseases under the general leadership of the State with the assistance of other interested groups. The programs in this and other states should be coordinated and augmented by the Federal Government." 

The need for continued research was also emphasized by Charles B. 

Eaton, Forest Insect Laboratory, Berkeley, as follows: "In summary, the forest insect situation in California, although markedly better than that in some other parts of the west, is not one about which we can afford to be complacent. Surveys to detect or appraise new or recurrent outbreaks, control to prevent or minimize damage, and research to determine how to cope with insect problems are the basis of the present program. Surveys and control are being developed through the cooperative efforts of public and industry foresters, with technical guidance from the Federal Bureau of Entomology and Plant Quarantine. Research is at the present the weakest link in the program, and should be greatly strengthened."

FOREST DISEASE 

Although the problems of forest insects and diseases are somewhat related, certain aspects of forest disease problems are different from those of insects. In the matter of forest diseases, California is primarily concerned with the control of white pine blister rust. It is an introduced fungus of European origin that attacks all white pines including California's important sugar pine.

White pine blister rust is peculiar in that it requires an alternate host plant of gooseberry or currant in order to complete its life cycle, and it can spread only short distances from host plants back to pines .This feature of the disease permits control in local pine stands through elimination of these host plants in and near any area of sugar pine. 

According to T. H. Harris of the Bureau of Entomology and Plant 

Quarantine, "Blister rust control can hardly be separated from the larger field of pine management. We are much further ahead in the technical control measures of blister rust than we are in the knowledge of how to grow sugar pine. The principal problems that face foresters today are: where to grow sugar pine economically; next, how to secure adequate reproduction of the species after logging; and third, how to attain maximum growth of the species throughout the life of the stand." 

The State of California and the Federal Government have cooperated since 1942 in a program of blister rust control on state and private lands. 

Present annual appropriations by the State are $168,000. 

In 1949, blister rust control areas in California totaled 1,500,000 acres. 

Field surveys applying the economic standards and new facts discovered by research have selected about 650,000 acres suited to sugar pine management. About half of this acreage is privately owned. The program of blister rust control in this State is as follows: about 75 percent of the 650,000 acres has received initial control treatment, and 40 percent follow-up treatments, about 17 percent, or 110,000 acres, is on a maintenance basis requiring only a small amount of periodic clean up. 

The State Forester reports that blister rust is severely damaging young sugar pines in the northern part of the State outside of control areas and is rapidly intensifying. Blister rust is being controlled cooperatively on the 325,000 acres of privately owned land by the State, the Federal Government, and the private owners. Continued cooperation requires enabling legislation to clarify the legal position of the State in this cooperative venture. The discussion in previous chapters of this report has indicated the need for forest research on practically all of the problems that were discussed at the joint hearing, November 6-8, 1952. Most laymen attending a forestry hearing of this kind for the first time are amazed at the statements of professional foresters that they actually know so little and need to know so much more about the problems of forestry in this State. 

One of the reasons for this situation is the rapid growth of the forest industries in recent years due to the increased population and resultant increased demand for lumber and other forest products in this State. 

New problems arise as the tempo of the industry increases. As stated by 

Dean Fred S. Baker, School of Forestry, University of California: 

"For example, in the preceding sessions of this hearing you have noticed the number of times that sound straight-forward questions have been put to the men sitting here and they have been answered, shall we say, a little bit vaguely? Well, this is no reflection on the mental capacities of the men who have been here at all-if these questions had been put to me, you would have heard something worse than vague, probably. But it just does illustrate the kind of things that we need to know and know better, the simplicity of them and the numerous nature of those questions. There are altogether too many of these practical and relatively simple questions that are suddenly coming up, that require no great laboratories, no great investments, but a more careful thought along this line of forest research *** so, the point I wish to stress is that the problems are numerous and are increasing at a tremendous rate as forestry is developing into a form of land use, rather than merely the harvesting of a ready-made natural crop."

One of the problems that requires an immediate and practical solution is that of the increase of Scotch Broom, particularly in El Dorado County. Scotch Broom, Cytisus Scoporius, is spreading rapidly and threatens to take over much of the range and timber land in the Sierra and the Sierra foothill area. A recent survey shows that 27,738 acres are now infested in El Dorado County alone; about 3,000 acres of this being on the national forest. The balance occurs largely on private timber and range lands. From the original infestation at an elevation of about 

2,500 feet, it has now spread and is found growing very vigorously at all elevations from 500 to 6,500 feet, and is now being found as far west as Folsom and east around Fallen Leaf Lake in the Lake Tahoe area. 

This problem was presented at the joint hearing, November 6-8, 1952, by Lowell Mobley, Agricultural Commissioner, El Dorado County, who suggested the need for three types of research for control of this shrub: (1) exploration of the possibilities of biological control, (2) search for the best material for chemical control, and (3) methods of application (74)

that would give economical control especially in heavily overgrown or inaccessible areas. According to Mobley: "Scotch Broom is a very serious threat to all types of agricultural as well as timber production. While it is considered, when in full bloom, very beautiful by many it is smothering out most competitive plants including forest seedlings to such an extent that it is highly improbable that any reforestation will occur once the broom has become established. It moves very rapidly into logged-over areas, usually soon forming a dense stand. In addition, it, growing so vigorously and dense and being highly inflammable, increases the fire control problem very materially and also increases the damage by fire to existing forest trees in case of wildfire or controlled burn. 'While a very hot fire will kill mature plants, new seedling growth is stimulated by the fire so that in a matter of a couple of years the stand is heavier and more vigorous than before and most of the remaining competitive plants have been killed. While most, if not all, native plants are relatively slow in taking over recently cleared or burnt-over lands, broom, being more aggressive, takes over and prevents the return of native brushes such as we need in chaparral succession. 66 The principal forest research agencies in California are the California 

Forest and Range Experiment Station of the U. S. Forest Service, the 

College of Agriculture including the School of Forestry of the University of California, and the forest industries. The Bureau of Entomology and Plant Quarantine of the U. S. Department of Agriculture is allied with the experiment station.

The members of the faculty of the School of Forestry serve half the time as professors and half the time as members of the experiment station under another title. Other members of the faculty of the College of Agriculture engage in research in related forestry problems such as soils, grasses, and range improvement projects. 

The State Division of Forestry does not carry on forestry research as such. As described by Dean Baker: "The major lines of work that are involved (in the Division of Forestry) are of an administrative nature. They handle the great matter of fire outside the national forests; they handle the forest practice rules; and they also are having a growing opportunity to work in the state forest, the main one being the Jackson Forest in the redwood country. * * * "So you can't say that they are flatly in an administrative sort of work where they can't use their heads in thinking about why things happen, and the problems, and the way of solving those problems. It doesn't mean that they need to have great laboratory facilities and things of that sort, but they do need to have time to think about the thing from every sort of an angle. They should not be so busy and so overburdened with their administrative work that they have no opportunity to think things through, to develop policies, and to solve the problems just by good common sense." 

The State Division of Forestry, therefore, is in an excellent position to coordinate the work of the forest research agencies in the State. This

is one possible solution to the situation related above by Dean Baker where practical answers to relatively simple problems are not now readily available.

WILD LAND SOIL SURVEYS 

One example of the type of research projects that can be initiated and coordinated by the Division of Forestry is the wild land soils survey recently completed in Mendocino and adjacent counties. Funds appropriated by the Legislature for this purpose were used by the Division of Forestry to contract with the California Forest and Range Experiment Station for the actual field surveys. 

The proper use of cutover or burned forest lands, whether for continued timber production or other agricultural use, depends in large part on the soil characteristics. There has been a long standing need for basic information on the upland soils in California just as there has for soils information on other agricultural lands. The 1945 report of the California Forestry Study Committee recommended that "As soon as possible the State should undertake a study of land classification to determine factually which lands should be dedicated to timber growing, which to livestock raising, and which are too poor or unsatisfactory for either pursuit." Any land management program must first respect the soil as a permanent source of wealth. 

The Legislature initiated a program for forest and soil surveys to be conducted with annual appropriations for a five year period. That survey applied to the wild lands outside the national forest areas and comprised a total of 10.7 million acres of timber and intermingled timber and range lands. The survey was completed in 1952 at a total cost of $373,000. The timber survey work was completed on the 10.7 million acres, but the soils survey was limited primarily to Mendocino County. 

The final product of the timber stand survey was the preparation and publication of detailed timber stand maps which contain information on vegetation, timber types, age, density, site growth, and drain. These data were obtained from aerial photographs and field sampling methods. 

Each map sheet covers about 37,000 acres at a scale of 2" equals one mile. 

Similar maps showing soil and species composition were prepared from the soil survey for almost 2.7 million acres, consisting of the wild lands of Mendocino County and adjoining portions of Humboldt, Lake, and Sonoma Counties. These maps show vegetation species, their sprouting characteristics and browse value, soil series, and soil depth, and the suitability of the soils as classified for timber and grass production. 

This wild land soils survey of Mendocino and adjoining counties is described in detail in the 1952 report of the Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands. That report also cites the application of these data to the management program of the Mendocino National Forest on which the forest service has made a similar soils survey. The soils survey data and the large scale maps provide very useful information not only to public agencies, but also to forest and range land owners, assessors, and all people concerned with land productivity. 

According to the State Forester, there is an urgent need to reactivate the inventory of these forest soils resources in the wild land of the State. 

Although there are some 35.8 million acres of upland soils in California in need of soils classification, a major portion of which is federally owned,

to the priority of effort recommended by the Division of Forestry is that of some eight million acres that lie in the northern coast ranges and upper foothills of the west slope of the Sierra Nevada. This is a long range program. The Division of Forestry, however, recommends a continuation of the program which ended in 1952 with a budget request of $77,000 for the 1953-54 Fiscal Year.

WATERSHED RESEARCH 

One of the neglected resources of California is the watershed areas. 

Large areas of public domain and forest land are set aside for power site developments or other uses related to water supply, yet nothing is done with relation to watershed management. 

This problem was presented at the joint hearing, November 6-8, 1952, by E. N. Munn, forester residing in Contra Costa County. The following extracts from his testimony summarize his views as to the additional supplies of water that can be obtained through watershed research and watershed management: "Let us look briefly at the situation. In the Central Valley are some 4,000,000 acres under irrigation on about 60,000 farms, four times the area in number as in 1900. The potential irrigable area of the valley is around 10,000,000 acres. The Bay area imports a half a million feet of water annually from the Sierra. By the year 2000, only 

50 years away, it will need 1,000,000 acre-feet. For full development of the water resource of the valley, something like 20,000,000 acrefeet of storage will be needed. Plans in existence today are for 40 major reservoirs and many small ones; these will use up just about all of the available water storage sites in the valley. Just about a year ago, the Governor stated that plans for the conservation of water are imperative in our State. Deductions and research elsewhere have indicated that the yield of water can be increased from forest lands and its quality improved. "Foresters, however, have just about completely neglected the water resource. To my knowledge, there is no announced forest policy for this State as to the manner in which its forest lands should be treated in relation to the water resource. There appears to be no declared intent by state legislative or land managing agencies as to the sponsoring or undertaking any specific program on forest lands leading directly to the proper function of the watersheds. Fire control is perhaps the sole exception. "To my knowledge, no public forester in this State has spoken out boldly and sharply as to the need or the opportunity for management policies and practices that would increase the production of water from the forest and watersheds of the valley. Also no public or private forester, agency, or institution, has spoken out or taken determined or positive steps, or has diligently sought funds for an active campaign to increase water yields, or to halt excessive erosion processes on the forest lands of the State. No head of any forest managing or administrative agency has a watershed management expert as his direct adviser on water policy or on watershed management. Where an agency employs any such man, he is in a supportive capacity and has other duties than attention to watershed problems. The head man, however, has technical advisers for recreation, fire, wildlife, timber, and so on, to help determine these policies and practices, but not for water.

No land managing agency, public or private, has yet shown any marked appreciation or need for research in the Central Valley on the relation of the timber areas to water production. No research agency or institution in the State has seriously undertaken projects that would determine quantitatively how greatly the production and quality of water can be increased or decreased by the type of forest or species favored or discouraged in forest management, or by the size or character of the trees, by the density or openness of the stand, or by the practices used in harvesting the forest crop, by the growth or use of ground cover by domestic or wild animals, or by any other kind of forest practice. If any such project has been undertaken or carried out, no great importance appears to be attached to it sufficient in kind or degree to capture the imagination of any land managing agency. "Is it really possible to increase the yield of water from forest lands? The answer is unmistakably yes. Let us look at just one phase. Riparian vegetation grows along streams, around lakes and meadows; plant roots are in water almost continuously; they drink heavily-they are the "Old Soaks" or the drunkards of the forest.

There is perhaps 20,000,000 acres of such vegetation; it drinks from 

1 to 10 acre-feet per acre with an average of perhaps of around 5 acrefeet per year. Earlier we have indicated the amount needed for full development of the water resource in the Central Valley was 20,000,- 000 acre-feet. Now, the million acres at 5 acre-feet equals 10,000,000. 

Should it be possible to save only one-tenth of this amount, 1,000,000 acre-feet, through treatment of the riparian forest, we could save enough water to irrigate 500,000 acres in the valley; or we could save enough water to meet the projected demands of the Bay area for imported water. Such saving, gentlemen, ain't hay.' At a value of $5 per acre-foot, this saving would be worth $5,000,000 each year. 

Yet with this opportunity in sight, there is no research to determine what methods should be used, their feasibility or their cost. "There are many other ways in which the water yield can be augmented through forest practices. Added amounts of water can be made available by increasing the snow storage, by reducing evaporation, by delaying snow melt, by increasing ground water storage and the like. Time prevents discussion, but savings are significant and immeasurable."

Several million dollars have been spent by the Department of Public Works, Corps of Engineers, and the Bureau of Reclamation in planning I engineering projects, determining water production, and surveying the storage sites and canal routes; but no specific studies have been made of 

I watershed conditions behind these projects. As stated by Mr. Munn: "No one knows much about the erosion conditions, where erosion exists, how much, or what is the cost? No one knows about the amount of sediiment delivered in the streams and reservoirs, whether it comes from public or private lands. *** No data are available, or being collected on the quality of water from different watershed or forest conditions; i whether the time of delivery of water to streams is being changed by current forest practices; whether flood heights and volumes are being

increased or decreased; no analysis of accumulated hydrological data has been made to determine the effect of change in forest conditions and produce guides to forest policies and practices." 

The solution to these problems, according to Munn, requires two types of research: (1) research looking into ways and means of enhancing the water resource through forest practices; and (2) a survey of conditions, watershed by watershed, including analysis of pertinent data throughout the State, as a basis for policy and program determination. Both types of research should be concurrent. No state agency has any plans or organization for such research projects at the present time. 

Cooperative agreements between the forest industries and the State is one method proposed by Munn to finance the needed watershed and other forestry research. Under this plan, the Division of Forestry would act as a coordinating agency. The forest industries and others are permitted by present tax laws to deduct contributions for scientific purposes.

CHAPTER IX FOREST TAXATION 

All of the subjects discussed in earlier chapters such as continuous forest production, utilization of all forest materials, reforestation and forestry research are essential to successful private forestry in California. 

The major obstacles to successful forestry are losses due to fire, insects, disease.

Some observers would also add taxes to the list of possible obstacles to successful private forestry. On the other hand, adequate local governmental services such as schools, roads, and protection to persons and property are also essential to a successful forest industry. 

The three types of tax problems discussed at the joint hearing, November 6 to 8, 1952, were: (1) exemptions of publicly owned land from taxation, (2) distribution of federal forest receipts, and (3) the method of taxing privately owned forest lands. Some of these problems are more important to some sections of the State than to others depending upon the character of the local tax base. 

STATE PARK EXEMPTIONS 

The 1951 report of the Senate Interim Committee on Public Lands called attention to the effect of large, extensive ownership of public lands on the tax resources of certain counties. The County Assessor of Del 

Norte County, Mrs. Arlene Spann, testified that 34 percent of the total land area of Del Norte County was subject to taxation with the balance of 66 percent being exempt due to being owned by the federal, state and local governments.

State-owned land in Del Norte County is particularly important because of the extensive acreage in state parks. According to the assessor, this total area of state park land in this county is approximately 15,000 acres and consists of valuable stands of mature redwood. Most of this acreage was acquired in comparatively recent years. Del Norte's tax base was decreased from $11,193,016 in 1930 to $4,935,567 in 1945. As stated by the county assessor, this decrease of some $7,000,000 was due to "the acquisition of our choice timber stands into state parks and the 

Federal Northern Redwood Purchase Unit.' 

New industries and a reappraisal of taxable property with an increase as much as 150 percent has resulted in a 1952 tax base of $15,600,352. 

The reassessment of timber values would cause the loss in the tax base from 1930 to 1945 to increase to an estimated $15,000,000 at the present time. 

The assessor also stated that: "In spite of the reappraisal this year, Del Norte County had the highest increase in their tax rate of any county in the State. Many modest homeowners are paying $30 per month property tax for only the home and small lot." ( 81 )

The increase in the tax rate is due to the expanding need for local services, particularly schools, required by the increase in population from 4,000 to 15,000 in a few years time. This increase in population, percentage-wise, is one of the largest of any of the counties in the State and is due to the expanding timber industry in this county. 

The loss of a substantial portion of the tax base such as that experienced by Del Norte County is particularly burdensome at a time when the increased population requires additional governmental services. For example, the cost of schools increased from $31,604 in 1930 to $295,330 in 1952.

One of the solutions proposed by the county assessor is that the State make payments in lieu of taxes for the state park lands. Another solution is that the amount of lands owned for state park purposes be reduced and returned to private ownership and thereby be restored to the tax rolls. The assessor maintains that park lands extending four or five miles on either side of the highway are inaccessible to the public and are therefore not necessary for park purposes.

DISTRIBUTION OF FEDERAL FOREST RECEIPTS 

One of the problems related to the ownership of forest lands by the Federal Government is the distribution of the forest receipts. The Federal Forest Receipts Act provides that 25 percent of the receipts from national forest sales of stumpage and other forest products shall go to the counties in which the national forest is located. The federal act also provides that the county revenue from this source shall be used for roads and schools. California law, however, provides that this revenue shall be divided equally between the road and school funds although the federal law does not specify any particular division for these purposes. 

In California, the 50 percent of forest receipts for school purposes goes to the county school service fund and can be used in any manner that the superintendent of schools deems necessary. 

According to the County Assessor of Del Norte County, the taxpayer, in many cases, is not getting the best use of this money and states that "it is vitally important that this money be placed under the control of a body of people instead of one person. "This assessor proposes that such revenue be concentrated on school construction so that building requirements can be met as they arise at a substantial saving in interest costs. 

For example, one bond issue in the amount of $118,000 was voted for one area in Del Norte County. The interest on this bond issue for 25 years is estimated to be $99,000.

Other testimony suggested that the need for roads in some areas was greater than the need for additional school funds at particular times. 

It was suggested, therefore, that the forest receipts be subject to the boards of supervisors for allocation to the roads and schools as needed in the respective counties receiving this source of revenue. 

AD VALOREM TAXATION 

The third type of forest tax problem presented at the joint hearing, November 6 to 8, 1952, was that of the present method of taxing timber and timber lands. Although national forest lands are not subject to taxation, the counties, as discussed above, receive 25 percent of the

receipts of sale of forest products from the national forests. The principal source of such sales is the old growth timber stands in the national forest areas.

The method of ad valorem taxation of timber in private ownership varies according to the extent of old growth and cut-over lands. The land, in either case, is subject to taxation as assessed by the counties, usually at a very nominal price per acre. 

Old growth timber is assessed by the county assessor in addition to the land value in most counties although some counties still assess timber on a flat-acre basis. Siskiyou County is one of the counties that recently changed over from a flat-acre basis of assessment to a flat base on landwhether cut-over, residual, or old growth-plus volume by forties of saw timber. According to the Deputy County Assessor, Frank Lathrop, this changeover was made in 1950 with satisfactory results which have added a very substantial amount to the tax base and equalized the timber assessment throughout the county.

Residual or new growth timber, however, has not been subject to ad valorem taxation since the adoption of an amendment to the California Constitution in 1926. This amendment is now part of Section 123 of 

Article XIII which reads as follows: 

"Section 123. Fruit and nut-bearing trees under the age of four years from the time of planting in orchard form, and grape vines under the age of three years from the time of planting in vineyard form, and all immature forest trees which have been planted on lands not previously bearing merchantable timber, or planted or of natural growth, upon lands from which the merchantable original growth timber stand to the extent of 70 percent of all trees over 16 inches in diameter has been removed, shall be exempt from taxation, and nothing in this article shall be construed as subjecting such trees and grapevine and forest trees to taxation; provided, that forest trees or timber shall be considered mature for the purpose of this act at such time, after 40 years from the time of planting or removal of the original timber as above provided, as a board consisting of a representative from the State Board of Forestry, a representative from the State Board of Equalization, and the county assessor of the county in which the timber is located, shall by a majority thereof so determine." (Amendment adopted November 2, 1926.)

Although the language of the Constitution, the endorsing arguments, and the general tax literature, states that growing timber shall be "exempt from taxation, some authorities contend that Section 12 of Article XIII does not exempt such timber from taxation but merely defers such taxation. W. R. Schofield, of the California Forest Protective Association, for the past 30 years has been in close touch with the whole problem of timber taxation, and in 1931 the State of California employed Mr. Schofield to make a report for the Tax Research Bureau on timber taxation for submission to the Legislature. At the committee hearing, Mr. Schofield commented: "There hasn't been anyone who has been able to refute much of my report, if anything, and I would say this: The historical background and methods of taxing timber would continue today much

the same as my report of 1932-perhaps in a little different variance of values-but it is still consistent. "Timber in the State of California is land. There are three categories of property taxation: land, improvements and personal property. Timber is land. The courts have recognized the fact that there can be a horizontal division of land as well as a vertical division of land. In other words, let me explain: You can divide the timber from the land, and it can be separately owned, the same as you would divide a section of land into two half sections. The courts recognize the fact, then, that there is a division horizontally. However, even in the category of timber ownership only, it is still land. "Timber is taxed now as then on an ad valorem basis, the same as any other land in the State. In the early days, it was customary to consider the over-all value existing upon the land, both in the timber supported by the land and the land itself. Therefore, the taxation, or the assessment, was made on the basis of an acreage of so much timberland. There wasn't too much pressure upon the assessor to refine values; therefore, it took the normal going value of all lands. Agricultural and grazing lands were pretty much alike. As the years went by, there was an attempt to differentiate between timber-bearing lands and other lands. As a result (some counties were in a position to hire it done and some counties were not) a county cruise was made, where they hired outside authorities and experts. They obtained a cruise of the then existing timber on the lands. There were other counties in which the major timber owners of the counties were asked to supply to the county assessor the volumes of the timber which they owned. This volume usually was in the form of a bracketing of minimum and maximum stand volume, rather than a specific volume, for an acre, for individual forties or for sections of land. "On that basis, the assessor applied a value which still, today, is not questioned by the courts in his determination of value. It is very difficult; unless you can prove fraud, you won't have much redress in the courts trying to set aside the determination of the value by the assessor. Then, as mentioned by Mr. Nelson, in 1926, the constitutional amendment, Section 123 of Article XIII of the Constitution, was adopted. The purpose of the Legislature's adopting it at this time was because it thought, and perhaps rightly so, that it might bring about a certain attempt on the part of the holders of cutover lands to hold them for the growing of timber and not have them revert to delinquency and be lost as a tax base. It was thought to be a good forestry measure and it was a good forestry measure. There were no requirements of the owner to make any application; however, the assessors, upon notification by the owner that a piece of land had been logged, placed the land in the cutover land category. 

Some of this land was converted to grazing and to higher use than retained for the growing of timber, and, consequently, those that had potentially higher values-such as bottom lands which would grow exceptionally good grass-were given an increased basic value applied to good grazing land. However, that which was left purely for the growing of timber took a very nominal fee. "Now, you have talked about the exemption of timber. There is no exemption of timber in the State of California. The Legislature of

the State of California, in passing Section 12 of Article XIII of the Constitution, recognized that for at least a period of 40 years there was no value on growing timber when 70 percent of the original growth had been removed. That is not an exemption. The Legislature says there is no value; therefore, in assessing land that has growing timber on it that is 40 years or less in age, the assessor can assume nothing else than that this growing timber has no value and ‘ad valorem' means 'in accordance to its value.' So, therefore, there is no exemption. And let's get that out of our minds. So many people think there is some concession, or exemption, being given to timber ownership when there is not. "After the period of 40 years, the Legislature, in its wisdom, recognized the fact that perhaps it had no value even after it was 40 years of age, because they said that for the purpose of assessment it shall be determined whether the trees have maturity and value by a committee composed of the representative from the State Board of Equalization, the representative from the State Board of Forestry, and the county assessor in which the land exists. "Now, we have had the question come up, 'Well, it has no value, but it still is being cut and marketed under the age of 40 years. 

Under the unbalanced economic and other conditions that existed in recent years, it is true that there has been a market for this smaller timber. It hasn't created too much of a disturbance in the basic roll, basic assessment roll of the county. It does mean, however, that somebody has made a little money on some property that perhaps should have had an assessment on it since it did command a value and was sold. But let me say to this committee that at the hearing of the Board of Equalization in Humboldt County it was established at least to my satisfaction and, I think, to the board's satisfaction-not only by representatives of the timber industry but representatives of agriculture and grazing, that they recognized the fact that this young, growing timber had no value. And economics will take care of that situation from here on out, I think. It is then a question of when does it have a value, when after it is over 40 years of age; when we don't know. Then it becomes assessable, but before that time there is no exemption. "We have had much discussion also in bringing another phase of timber taxation which exists throughout the United States. Something that is a matter that needs very, very considerable amount of study and something that cannot be jumped from state to state simply because some other state has used it. I made reference yesterday to the fact the reference to the New Hampshire law. I defy anybody in the Legislature, or anybody in this room, to take the New Hampshire law and put it into operation in accordance with the laws of the State of California and apply it to our growing timber and make a sound tax measure out of it. We have a report that was recently gotten out by the Federal Government about yield taxes and the author gave the different states that have the yield taxes and the various forms of yield taxes. We have heard people talk about yield taxes and severance taxes, using them synonymously. 

Let me give you a very simple definition: a yield tax is an income tax at the time of severance, as opposed to a severance tax, which

is an excise or a privilege tax on a private owner's trees when cut. 

Now, those of you who are familiar and conversant in the field of taxation know that there is a considerable difference between an income tax and an excise tax. "Under the conditions in California with the large amount of virgin timber still standing, our market is primarily, almost completely, as yet in the form of a product known as lumber. We haven't gotten into the stage of conversion to cellulose, to any great extent, and our primary commodity is lumber. Therefore, any determination of value should be predicated upon lumber until such a time as there is a greater predominance of another commodity from the forests other than lumber. That should be recognized, and we should be very cautious, and the Legislature should be very cautious, in approaching the question of taxation of timber in any other manner than the present ad valorem tax, or trying to make any remedial measure which will put on the tax roll for a year, maybe, a small and very infinitesimal amount of young growth timber that might be cut by some foolish operator who doesn't know the true value of its being able to be carried until it reaches maturity. "Let me point out to you this fact: that we have in the State 

Legislature, primarily in the State Senate, a tax committee, an interim committee studying taxation headed by Senator Hulse. They are doing a good job. They are exploring every phase of taxation. I say that any other part of the Legislature, an individual legislator, or anyone else, should not introduce legislation changing the type and manner of taxation of timber, until the tax program has been thoroughly worked out by the Interim Committee on Taxation, so that all phases of property and taxation shall be covered. Otherwise, you are going to create a disproportionate taxation of timber. Timber has, over the years and many, many years, gentlemen, paid taxes. They have paid on the basis of value established by an assessor who considered that value correct in accordance with the values of other properties; therefore, timber has paid its just share of taxes through the years. "When it comes to the marketing, there is only one harvest of that timber in a generation and this is taking place in your timber with the current prices on stumpage and the fantastic prices paid for stumpage-people think there is an enormous profit being made and that there is a lot of tax escapement of timber on account of the high prices the owner is getting. All you need to do, if you can use a pencil and use an interest table, is to put down the original value of that land when purchased and carry that over the period of years that it has been held and in which taxes have been paid, the amounts of taxes that have been paid, and add the accumulation of a reasonable compound interest. You will find that the value reached now on that timber is about equal to what they are getting in the so-called "high prices" of timber. Gentlemen, it wasn't many years ago that many large timber companies and large holders of timber lands were forced to allow their timber to go on the tax delinquent roll. Some of them never redeemed them. Some of them quitclaimed their property to the State of California after paying years and years of taxes. So there you have an exceptional condition. There is no other commodity

that is a product of the soil that takes so long to reach the selling date, and then it is only one sale. "I think that we have perhaps a condition which must be adjusted. 

There is a diversity of opinion amongst the timber owners themselves as to what type of taxation there should be. We even have an honest conviction on the part of some timber owners that there should be no tax on timber. Now, that sounds fantastic; however, if you reckon it with the fact that the value of the timber brings in other values which become taxable, that perhaps during the periods of years when it has not produced any return, it shouldn't be taxed because it is contributing to the tax base through the other avenues of assessment and taxation. I don't advocate that, but it is believed by some and honestly so, and it has merit. There are others who think that we should have a yield tax or a severance tax. Those things are things which would have to be determined by legislative action in a constitutional amendment before it could be accomplished, and I think you gentlemen of the Legislature are familiar with the make-up of the State of California to know that at least under present conditions the timber people wouldn't have to bother at all about fighting anybody who introduced a severance tax or a yield tax, because there are other people in the State of California who carry the battle for us for nothing. They are interested, too. I think we must approach it in the manner which has been accepted by the Board of Forestry, in the approach of a committee comprised of those who are interested and concerned with the timber tax situation, to explore every avenue and possibility in our present existing laws for any way in which we can maintain a balance and a condition of taxation so that there is no escapement but that there is a reasonable bearing of taxes by timber bearing lands. And then, on their recommendations, decisions can be made. "Then, again, referring to the Senate Committee and the new state-wide committee that is studying the over-all tax problem in the State of California, we must not disturb the balance without equality.

APPENDIX "A" FOREST SERVICE TIMBER-SALE POLICY, PROGRAMS AND PRACTICES IN CALIFORNIA STATEMENT PRESENTED BY B. H. PAYNE ASSISTANT REGIONAL FORESTER, DIVISION OF TIMBER MANAGEMENT U. S. FOREST SERVICE, SAN FRANCISCO 

As an aid to better understanding of the subject, let me quote briefly from the basic enabling act of the Forest Service, which authorizes the sale of timber from the national forests. This act is the Act of June 4, 1897, and I quote: "For the purpose of preserving the living and growing timber and promoting the younger growth on national forests, the Secretary of Agriculture, under such rules and regulations as he shall prescribe, may cause to be designated and appraised so much of the dead, matured, or large growth of trees found upon such national forests as may be compatible with the utilization of the forests thereon, and may sell the same for not less than the appraised value in such quantities to each purchaser as he shall prescribe." 

While this is the head of the legislative authority for sales of national forest timber, there are other key items in that and subsequent acts which modify and complement the basic sale authority. Most recent action along this line is the amendment authorizing sales without advertisement up to $2,000 in value which has been limited by law to $500 until this year. Some other important laws affecting timber sales are: 

Knutson-Vandenberg Act, authorizing collection of deposits in addition to stumpage from purchasers for use by the Forest Service in replanting or improvement of future stands of timber on sale areas. 

The Granger-Thye Act, authorizing collection of deposits for slash disposal and extra protection. This act also authorizes certain forms of cooperative effort between the Forest Service and purchasers or users. 

And there is a Sustained-Yield Forest Management Act, and even an act authorizing the export of national forest timber from the state or territory where grown. 

There are, of course, other legislative acts, but I would like to go back to the premise that the "Secretary of Agriculture, under such rules and regulations as he shall prescribe." 

The current regulations of the secretary affecting timber are contained within 15 typewritten pages. They are 29 in number, and although they were overhauled and re-issued about three years ago, they have remained remarkably consistent over the years since they were first issued. They constitute what you might say is the charter under which the timbersale business is conducted by the Forest Service. 

Perhaps one of the most basic is Regulation S-3, which provides for disposal of national forest timber according to management plans. For management plan purposes, national forests have long been subdivided into management units known as working circles. Their boundaries are wwww ( 96 )

not fixed by law but by administrative determination. They generally follow topographic features and attempt to enclose areas of national forest land having a common outlet or manufacturing or marketing area. 

Major purposes of management plans are to bring together information on character and volume of timber, character and condition of the land, growth capability of the area, status of development of the area and needs for additional roads. From this basic information is developed the allowable rate of cutting based upon the principle of sustained yield or continuous harvest. 

Management plans vary in scope with the intensity of management possible. For many areas in California there is not available sufficiently detailed basic data to justify preparation of full-scale, formalized management plans, therefore we have adopted a stopgap measure known as the Streamlined Management Plan. Copies of one such plan are available for the committee and to interested parties as an example of this minimum type of management plan. It was prepared on the national forest by the men in possession of the on-the-ground knowledge of facts and conditions. It provides a useful working tool for the district ranger and the supervisor in the daily conduct of their business. 

One of the most important jobs accomplished through such planning is the preparation of a short-term cutting budget. By that is meant determination of where and what timber to offer for sale in the next three to five-year periods. This determination must be based upon the relative condition of the timber, current economic conditions, amount and kind of road development needed and its relationship to the remaining timber stand and other minor factors. In setting up such a cutting budget use is made of base map and an overlay on which the relationship of proposed sale areas may be graphically related to needed or existing roads.

Using the cutting budget, forest officers can proceed with preparation of specific sales offerings, to reconnaissance or location surveys of roads needed.

Prospective purchasers are also interested in knowing of the general program in advance of specific sales offerings in order that they may be better able to plan their own operations. And they are invited to seek this information from the local sources. 

In the preparation of our cutting budgets, we continually strive to develop a balanced sale program that will achieve the management objectives and most nearly meet the needs of all classes of forest users. 

While calculation of the allowable annual cut furnishes a guide or limitation, it is still necessary to determine where to cut and how much in a specific sales offering. The extremely rapid change in lumber-price structure that took place here since World War II and the accompanying market uncertainties led purchasers and forest officers alike to a preference for a sale program comprised almost entirely of short-term sales; that is, annual sales or sales of duration not beyond two or three years. 

Rapid increase in number of customers, shortage of manpower, lack of planning funds, and other factors contributed to prolong this pattern. 

While it is not now intended to shift rapidly from a short-term sales program to one of exclusively long-term sales, it is hoped that in the

interest of efficiency of our own efforts and achievement of a better balanced program, that the expansion of the national forest sale program can be accomplished by inclusion of a more normal and desirable proportion of larger and longer-term sales. 

From the standpoint of policy, it is our desire to bring the cut from national forest lands up to their full capacity, working circle by working circle. The current program falls considerably short of that. In Fiscal 

Year 1952, for example, the cut from national forest lands in California was 666 MM. board feet. If all of the national forest working circles were being operated at capacity, they could provide a cut of 1,180,000,000 feet.

In spite of the fact that the cut is at a rate far below the potential, cutting is not evenly distributed. There are, roughly, 85 working circles in the region. As of November, 1951, there was either no cutting or a negligible amount on 23 of these 85 working circles. At this point I would like to interject the fact that in determining the allowable cut on our working circles, in the virgin stands we deliberately cut, or provide for the cutting, of about one and one-half times the growth of that area in order to get over the management unit in a shorter time period and harvest the mature and frequently the deteriorating timber. 

Lack of accessibility and market have been primary factors in delaying our over-all objective, of cutting to capacity. Mr. Byrne will follow with a statement in some detail about the size and scope of the road program necessary but there are other factors which have contributed to delay. 

We have not yet resolved satisfactorily the problem of rights of way across private lands to reach all national forest areas. In fact, in some cases we have even been delayed because of inability to obtain rights of way across national forest land due to objections by claimants of unperfected or undeveloped mineral claims.

The availability of manpower and funds to make and administer sales is a problem that will probably always be with us. We have effected savings in costs by adoption of short-cut methods in our sale program, but the real need is for intensification of management because of the increase in values involved. There is always room for improvement in our operating efficiency; but, generally speaking, an increase in business is going to mean a commensurate increase in total costs of the job. Congress has recognized the volume of business in appropriating funds for timber use in the past, and it is expected that we will not be hampered in expansion of the sale business by lack of funds so long as we can continue to demonstrate reasonable ability and need. 

We presently expect the cut for this fiscal year to be in excess of 700,000,000 board feet. With reasonable progress in the field of access road development, management planning and advance sales preparation, we hope to be in position to increase the rate of cut approximately 50,000,000 board feet per year over the next five or six years. 

In addition to the policy of increasing the cut to full capacity, we are keenly aware of the need to extend management practices to all timber areas as quickly as possible. The ideal, of course, is to cut from each area, each year, a volume of timber equal to the growth produced. If that could be achieved, the problem of eating the cake and still having it would be solved.

The actual operation of the sales program is conducted under a number of regulations of the Secretary of Agriculture, as provided by law, one of which deals specifically with authorization. The Regional Forester here in California is authorized to make sales up to 50,000,000 feet of timber in one transaction without reference to higher authority. This is the maximum delegation of authority permissible under the regulation. 

Likewise, each supervisor and district ranger has an authorization to make sales up to a specified size. The maximum size which may be delegated to a forest supervisor is 10,000,000 feet and there are two forest supervisors in this region who have that maximum authority. While authority to make, which means approve sales, is limited by regulation, a supervisor can prepare and recommend sales of any size. Those larger than his authorization must be approved as to logging plan, cutting requirements, prices and other pertinent features prior to advertisement. 

Before offering timber for sale it is the obligation of the approving officer to see that provision is made for practicable fire-prevention measures and methods of cutting which will preserve the residual timber, promote young growth, avoid damage to the soil and watershed, and secure as complete utilization as existing markets and requirements of users will permit. To satisfy this obligation requires considerable advance sale preparation, including on-the-ground examination for development of a logging plan, slash-disposal measures, road locations, and to secure data for use in developing cutting condition and requirements to be included in the contract of the sale.

In order to meet another requirement of the basic legislation, each sale must be appraised to determine the market value of the timber. Appraisal of standing timber by the Forest Service is a job that can be aptly described as simple in theory and difficult in practice. 

In theory an appraisal consists of determination of selling price of the end product (usually lumber). From that is subtracted the estimated costs of producing lumber. The resultant margin is then divided between profit and risk on the one hand and stumpage prices on the other. 

In determining selling prices and cost estimates, we make use of considerable detailed information collected in confidence from timber operators. These data are carefully analyzed and averaged where possible to furnish a guide based upon experience. From this guide and knowledge of the sale area, the appraiser prepares his cost and selling price estimates. 

In the early part of the Forest Service program of timber disposal, where new installations were generally required to furnish a market for timber, the so-called investment appraisal system was used to assist in determination of an appropriate margin for profit and risk. This method has not been abandoned and is still in use as an occasional check calculation. Times have changed considerably, however, and now a majority of national forest timber is purchased by individuals or companies already having established manufacturing facilities. Under this atmosphere, a variation of the overturn appraisal system is used whereby the results of comparable sales are considered in determination of the margin of profit and risk required for the operation of national forest timber in a normally efficient manner.

An additional measure, used primarily here in California, is a price adjustment procedure whereby changes in market prices for the finished product are reflected, to some degree at least, back to the stumpage price.

This adjustment procedure was developed by the Forest Service at a time when the market outlook was poor as a measure to reduce marketing risk. 

The appraisal of standing timber is a task which requires experience and a high degree of competence and good judgment. It is far from an exact science but the job is exacting and one to tax the skill and judgment of any forester. The large number of sales made in the past and the number presently being made furnish a considerable background of experience. Experience is a requisite to development of informed and sound judgment.

Discussions with prospective purchasers is another opportunity for checking of basic data and the judgment involved in its application. For this reason alone, we welcome the opportunity to test the metal of our men by discussion of the details of appraisals of national forest timber. 

While we feel that some disagreement as to prices is a normal relationship between buyers and sellers of any commodity, such differences of opinion can usually be reduced by constructive discussion. 

Sales of timber are awarded to the highest qualified bidder after advertisement. Qualification consists of demonstration of reasonable financial and technical ability to carry out the terms of the contract. 

For all practical purposes, the sealed bidding procedure received servicewide application until 1947. Since that time, use of oral auctions has been authorized at the option of the regional forester. In this region, primary use is made of sealed bidding procedure because it seems best suited to existing circumstances. Under sealed bidding procedures, a prospective purchaser can make an independent, dispassionate determination of the worth of the timber and bid accordingly. On the other hand, oral bidding permits so-called "home town" industry to better evaluate outside competition and to meet it if possible. Decision as to method used is based upon management considerations primarily and is made working circle by circle, rather than sale by sale. The region's policy in this regard was revised as recently as last April and like other phases of policy, is subject to continuing review in the light of changing conditions. 

Once a sale is awarded, the contract signed, the Forest Service's responsibility becomes one of timber marking, measurement, and over-all contract administration. Here good sale preparation pays off. For if a sale is well prepared, boundaries marked, road locations determined, and cutting plans well formulated, there is a minimum of opportunity for uncertainty or misunderstanding, either on the part of the administrator or the purchaser. 

Incidentally, while each timber-sale agreement follows an approved pattern, one purpose of sale preparation is development of a contract which is specific and appropriate for the area in question. 

Timber-sale contracts are drawn to permit the harvesting of the timber on a reasonably practicable and economic basis and with recognition of its requirements given in determination of minimum acceptable stumpage prices. It is also designed to protect the basic resources of soil, water, and the residual timber.

In national forest timber-sale contracts, the phrases "to the satisfaction of" or "in the judgment of the forest officer in charge," are found in a number of places. This is a deliberate attempt to avoid impracticalities which stem from too much rigidity or centralization. It is at once the

strength and perhaps one of the weaknesses of a decentralized organization. It is recognition, however, of the fact that all forest land is not uniform and that it is impractical to try to establish detailed rules or specifications for every phase and every acre of the job. We have, instead, furnished field officers with guidelines within which they are free to move about, to use their ingenuity, and to meet the situation on the ground. 

In this phase of our business, as in others, there is a constant need for an on-the-job training program to develop more competence and ability. We are proud of the record our field men have set in the way they have accepted additional responsibilities. Concrete testimony to this point is offered by the increased sales load they are carrying and by the number of former forest officers who have accepted more responsible positions with industry at higher pay. 

Let's see where we stand now in progress on the doing part of the job: the total cut of timber from national forests in California from 

Fiscal Year 1909, which was about the start of cutting, to date is 11 billion board feet, valued at 53 million dollars at the prices paid for it. 

In the past three fiscal years the cut has been 13 billion board feet, valued at $24,000,000, or an average price of $13.65 per thousand. 

The timber-sale business is financed by appropriations from the Congress. Timber use funds for the past three fiscal years alloted to this region have averaged $290,500 per year and amounted to $871,500 in total, for that three-year period, or about $0.50 per thousand board feet for the timber cut.

While the sale business is financed from appropriations there is some direct return from the stumpage values. Only a portion of this return is directly to the Forest Service. These are: 

1. Twenty-five percent of timber-sale receipts to the State for distribution to the counties in lieu of taxes. Last year, Fiscal Year 1952, $2,995,000 was returned to the State of California for distribution to the counties from the sale of national forest timber. This amount is equal to $0.39 per acre of commercial forest lands in national forests in California. 

2. Another return is under the Knutson-Vandenberg Act, whereby a part of the payments for stumpage was deposited for replanting and timber stand improvement to keep cutover lands productive. This amounted to $840,000 in Fiscal Year 1952. These funds are for use of the Forest Service for replanting and improving the future stand of timber on areas cut over. They are tied directly to individual sale areas and if not expended for the purpose intended must be returned to the Treasury. 

3. Another return is in the form of 10 percent of receipts from all sources which are returned to the Forest Service for expenditure on roads. This amounted to $1,300,000 last fiscal year. This, in turn, is used largely to develop or make accessible additional timber. 

The returns, measured dollarwise, are substantial. It is important to measure them in such terms in order to demonstrate the fact that the timber-sale program of the Forest Service is a business and subject to measurement in terms commonly accepted in business.

While there has been considerable reference herein to the legislative authority which constitutes a charter for the timber-sale business, I do not want to leave an impression that our policies are, therefore, rigid or inflexible.

Sales programs are initiated by the individual forests and many are completed entirely under decentralized authority. Policies are subject to change as factors affecting them change. 

Forest officers are ready and willing to discuss plans with any interested party and to give all possible recognition to their viewpoint. They are also willing to discuss any feature of the sales program including prices and conditions of sale. Such discussions facilitate coordination with the overall public interest in the development of a sound and workable sales program. Complete agreement is not expected in all cases; it is our sincere hope that we may reach a better basis for mutual understanding and for a continually improving program of timber sales from your national forests in California.

While stressing the willingness of forest officers to discuss various phases of the timber-sale program with interested users, we should not lose sight of their responsibility in terms of the broad public interest. 

To be most effective in carrying out the timber-sale program, forest officers must maintain a continuous awareness of the long-range aspects of the program. They must continue their interest in more complete utilization of our natural resources. They must act to perpetuate the supply of timber because of its importance to the economic welfare not only of the Nation, but of the local communities in which they live. But perhaps above all, as the land managers they must, and I assure you that they do realize that the timber harvesting program must not destroy the basic resources of soil and water, and they must conduct it in a manner that will mesh with the wise use of all the resources of the national forests.

Probably the soonest that we can ever hope to accomplish this job is about five years, and a reasonable program could be worked out in that period.
