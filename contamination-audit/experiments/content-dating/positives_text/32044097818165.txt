REPORT OF THE WATER RESOURCES COMMISSION RELATIVE TO FLOOD CONTROL WORKS IN THE WATERSHEDS OF THE CONCORD, SUDBURY AND ASSABET VALLEYS. 

JANUARY 27, 1960. 

To the General Court of Massachusetts. 

Under the provisions of chapter 75 of the Resolves of 1959, the Water Resources Commission was instructed to make a study relative to flood control works in the watersheds of the Concord, Sudbury and Assabet valleys, including in the study the subject matter contained in House Document No. 1890, which reads as follows: 

RESOLVE PROVIDING FOR AN INVESTIGATION AND STUDY BY A SPECIAL COMMISSION RELATIVE TO FLOOD CONTROL WORKS INCLUDING FLOOD PLAIN ZONING, ETC., IN THE WATERSHEDS OF THE CONCORD, SUDBURY AND ASSABET Valleys. 

Resolved, That an unpaid special commission to consist of one member of the senate to be designated by the president thereof, two members of the house of representatives to be designated by the speaker thereof and one member each from the board of natural resources and the board of water resources to be designated by the governor is hereby established for the purpose of making an investigation and study relative to the drawing up of a comprehensive plan for the development of flood control work including flood plain zoning in order to insure the maintenance of a steady flow of water at all times, the development of recreational and reservation facilities and such other related matters as they may deem expedient in the watersheds of the Concord, Sudbury and Assabet valleys. Said commissions shall consider in their investigation and study any and all pertinent federal legislation or plans that may involve or include the foregoing area as well as any federal aid that may be available for any program that may be developed. 

Said commission shall be provided with quarters in the state house or elsewhere, may hold hearings, may travel within the commonwealth, and may expend for legal, clerical and other assistance and expenses such sums as may hereafter be appropriated therefor. Said commission shall report to the general court the results of its investigation and study and its recommendations, if any, together with drafts of legislation necessary to carry its recommendations into effect, by filing the same with the clerk of the house of representatives on or before the fourth 

Wednesday of January, nineteen hundred and sixty.

The above Resolve was approved on June 29, 1959, but no funds were made available. The Commission has, however, made a study of the problem, the scope of which is limited due to the lack of adequate funds.

Considerable data were furnished by the members of the Commission from the files of the departments of Commerce, Public

Preliminary field surveys of the proposed flood control reservoirs on the Assabet River drainage area considered in this study were made by the Department of Natural Resources. The first was concerned with the recreational potentials of the reservoirs and the second, conducted by the Division of Fisheries and Game, with fish and game development. Information was also obtained from the Soil Conservation Service of the U. S. Department of Agriculture, the Fish and Wildlife Service and the Geological Survey of the Department of the Interior. 

All of the members of the General Court representing the towns in the Assabet River valley and the officials of the cities and towns in that valley were invited to attend the public meeting held by the Water Resources Commission in the Town Hall at Hudson, Massachusetts, on the evening of August 10, 1959. In addition, the Director and Chief Engineer of the Water Resources Commission has held a number of conferences with municipal officials in conjunction with the study.

A very comprehensive report on the Sudbury River made by the 

Sudbury Valley Commission was printed as House Document No. 

2351 in March, 1950. After a ten-year period many of the basic problems still remain in the area. Some of these have become more acute due to the expanded population of the area. Most of them have a definite relationship with competing water usage. 

The complex water problems of this area become evident to the public only when there is either an insufficient flow of water or too much water. The "Diane Storm" of August, 1955, re-emphasized the flood control needs in the area.

SCOPE OF THE STUDY. 

The study included the following items in regard to the flood problems and related water resource problems in the valley: 

(1) Evaluation of the plan of the U. S. Soil Conservation Service for the installation of eight small flood control reservoirs on the tributaries of the Assabet River. (2) Study of a plan for operation of the existing reservoirs on the Sudbury River and its tributaries for flood control purposes. (3) Study of the problem of flood plain zoning.

SUMMARY. 

The details of this study are contained in the Engineer's Report which follows this section.

In general, the results can be summarized as follows: (1) The installation of the eight flood control reservoirs on the Assabet River, with construction funds to be provided by the U. S. Soil Conservation Service, will result in a reduction of $108,000 of annual flood damage in the towns on the lower Assabet River. (2) Fish and game improvements can be integrated into the project at four of the proposed flood control reservoirs. (3) Recreational facilities of regional significance can be developed in conjunction with two of the reservoirs, while recreational facilities which would be ample for the use of the town in which the reservoir is located could be developed at one reservoir site. 

(4) Under the supervision of the Water Resources Commission informal operating arrangements on a trial basis have been made so that the reservoirs on the tributaries of the Sudbury River now managed by the Metropolitan District Commission and the Department of Natural Resources can provide flood benefits. The reservoirs are operated so that a minimum of 1,500 million gallons of reservoir capacity during the three summer months and 2,600 million gallons of capacity for the remainder of the year is kept available for flood storage. If they were operated in this manner at the time of the Diane hurricane in August, 1955, the flood damage in the town of Framingham would have been materially reduced. (5) Population in the SuAsCo area (the Sudbury, Assabet and Concord rivers drainage area) was estimated to be 168,000 persons in 1955. The rate of increase of population in the area for the period 1940-1955 was 38.8 per cent as compared to 15 per cent for the Commonwealth for the same period. With normal expansion the population of this area will be in excess of 300,000 persons in the year 2000. (6) A portion of the drainage area forms a part of the Metropolitan District Water Supply System. A number of the towns are

ENGINEER'S REPORT. 

Herewith is submitted to the Water Resources Commission the engineering report relative to the flood control and related water usages in the Sudbury, Assabet and Concord River valleys, commonly known as the "SuAsCo" area. This study was authorized by the Legislature with the passage of chapter 75 of the Resolves of 1959. The study was limited due to lack of funds and personnel. 

INTRODUCTION. 

The SuAsCo area is a very complex one to deal with insofar as water usage is concerned. The valley, since it was first settled in the early Colonial days, has had an ever-changing water usage, reflecting the continual changes in its economic life. The area is within easy commuting distance of three of the large cities in the Commonwealth, Boston, Worcester and Lowell. A large portion of the valley was used in the early development of the Metropolitan Water System.

A number of legislative investigations have been made of this area but the most recent and comprehensive report, done by the Sudbury Valley Commission, is contained in House Document No. 

2351, published in March, 1950. Your Chief Engineer wrote a portion of that report.

In preparing this one an effort was made to avoid duplicating material in the previous report insofar as possible. Many of the problems on water usage described in the previous report still continue. These problems have become more complex because of the industrial expansion and population growth in the area. Some changes have occurred during the last decade which have resulted in improvements in the water situation, but other changes have created further problems. These changes will be noted under the various subheadings of this report.

The Diane hurricane of August, 1955, focused attention upon the flood problems of the SuAsCo area as this storm resulted in stream flows higher than previously recorded at the river gaging stations.

The water problems of the area can be condensed into a simple statement of: recurring cycles of too much water and too little water. 

DESCRIPTION OF THE AREA. 

The Concord River is a tributary of the Merrimack River, discharging into that river at Lowell. The Concord River has a drainage area of 405 square miles. This river is formed by the confluence in Concord, Massachusetts, of the Assabet River, with a drainage area of 176 square miles, and the Sudbury River with a drainage area of 165 square miles. The dominant feature of the lower Sudbury River valley and the Concord River valley above the Talbot Mill dam in North Billerica is that it has a very flat stream gradient and extensive marshes adjacent to the river. 

The Assabet River has a steeper and more uniform gradient which provides for more rapid runoff of flood waters as compared to the more sluggish Sudbury-Concord rivers. There are four reservoirs located on the Sudbury River which are operated by the Metropolitan District Commission for water supply purposes. Four other reservoirs originally constructed by the Metropolitan District Commission as water sources have been abandoned as sources of water supply and are now operated by the Department of Natural Resources as recreational areas. The mill ponds formed by the Saxonville Dam and the dam above the Fenwick Street bridge in Framingham are operated for industrial purposes. There are three industrial dams located on the Assabet River and the Talbot Mill Dam is located on the Concord River in North Billerica.

FLOOD CONTROL. 

The floods associated with the Hurricane Diane in August, 1955, again focused attention upon the need for flood control in the SuAsCo area. This was the most destructive flood of recent record and caused an estimated damage to industrial property, roads and bridges, residences and agricultural land of nearly two million dollars in the SuAsCo area. 

Damage centers were located in Northborough, Hudson, Gleasondale, Maynard and West Concord on the Assabet River; Ashland, Framingham, Saxonville, Wayland and Concord on the Sudbury

River; and Concord, Bedford, Billerica and Lowell on the Concord River.

A study of past records show that in recent years there have been a number of hurricane-type storms in the late summer and autumn with resulting damaging floods, plus floods of lesser magnitude resulting from early spring rains combined with melting snow. 

A study of rainfall records indicate that heavy rainfalls may occur in any month of the year. Figure No. 6 shows the amount and occurrence of monthly rainfalls in excess of eight inches at Ashland. 

A study was also made of the frequency and month of occurrence of flows on the Concord River in excess of 2,000 cubic feet per second. During the past sixty years flows of this intensity have occurred in nine months of the year, only February, October and

December being free from flows of that magnitude. 

The distribution of such flows is shown in Figure No. 7.

As a result of the Diane storm, a watershed work plan was pre pared by the Middlesex and Northeastern Worcester Soil Conserva tion districts with technical assistance for flood control provided by the Soil Conservation Service of the U. S. Department of Agri culture.

This was done under the authority of the Watershed 

Protection and Flood Prevention Act (Public Law 566-83rd Con gress). Considerable assistance in the collection of data in the preparation of this plan was given by a voluntary group, the SuAsCo 

Watershed Association.

These proposals were reviewed by the Water Resources Commission with the following results:

Sudbury River Plan. 

The proposal for making available 4,755 million gallons of capacity in six of the Sudbury River reservoirs, three operated by the Department of Natural Resources and three operated by the Metropolitan District Commission, was not considered feasible. As stated above, flood storms occur in practically every month of the year. To make available that amount of storage capacity would result in lowering the water levels to such an extent that it would be difficult to carry on the recreational uses of the three reservoirs under the jurisdiction of the Department of Natural Resources. It would similarly be detrimental to the operation of the Metropolitan water supply reservoirs for these reservoirs would be drawn down during the season of greatest water demand. Furthermore, the flood control benefits would be primarily in the town of Framingham above the 

Saxonville dam.

The proposal made by the Soil Conservation Service was revised by the Water Resources Commission after considerable study of the operation of the reservoirs. A plan of operation was evolved which would make available 1,500 million gallons of flood storage capacity

[Apr. during the months of June, July and August, with 2,600 million gallons being made available for the rest of the year. These reservoirs are now being operated under this plan on a trial basis, with their operation being coordinated by the Water Resources Commission.

Assabet River Plan. 

The work plan for the SuAsCo area provided for the construction of eight small flood control reservoirs on the tributaries of the Assabet River. These reservoirs would be located in the towns of 

Westborough, Shrewsbury, Northborough, Berlin and Marlborough.

They would provide for 5,600 acre feet of flood water storage, which is equivalent to 1,830 million gallons from a drainage area of 22.5 square miles.

The Soil Conservation Service estimates that the project will cost nearly $1,150,000. Under the provisions of Public Law 566 the 

Federal government will pay 100 per cent of the cost of construction of the structures, including engineering costs, but non-Federal interests must pay for the acquisition of land and the annual maintenance and operation. The Federal cost would be approximately $950,000, based upon the preliminary study. This project would result in major flood protection benefits to the towns of Northborough, Hudson and Maynard, with minor benefits to the other downstream towns bordering the Assabet River. There would be no appreciable benefits to the towns along the Concord River. The estimated annual flood benefit would be $108,285.

Many persons who have studied the problem of flood control feel that the installation of reservoirs for flood control prevention must be accompanied by a program of flood plain zoning. Otherwise, further encroachments upon the streams below the reservoir sites will restrict the channel of the stream thereby making the reservoirs of little future value. In addition, the so-called wetlands, including swamps and marshlands, have considerable value in some of the drainage areas as natural flood control reservoirs to

The value of the wetlands has been demonstrated repeatedly in the Concord-Sudbury rivers marshlands. For example, in the Diane storm the peak flow recorded on the Assabet River at Maynard on 116 square miles of drainage area was 36.6 cubic feet per second per square mile. The flow measured on the Concord River in Lowell was only 11.1 cubic feet per second per square mile on a drainage area of 405 square miles. Furthermore, there was a 3-day lag between the occurrence of the peak flow on the Assabet River and the Concord River gage, a distance of only about 16 miles. This is shown in Figure 7A, the hydrograph for the Diane storm.

Enabling legislation for flood plain zoning was first enacted in 1954 when a clause was included in the zoning enabling act to become section 2 of chapter 40A of the General Laws. This clause reads as follows:- 

A zoning ordinance or by-law may provide that lands deemed subject to seasonal or periodic flooding shall not be used for residence or other purposes in such a manner as to endanger the health or safety of the occupants thereof.

Since this enabling legislation was enacted, five municipalities in Massachusetts that we know of have adopted flood plain zoning. 

Two of these communities are located in the SuAsCo area, the towns of Concord and Wayland.

The Department of Public Works, under the provisions of chapter 91, is empowered to prevent encroachments within the high water mark of a stream. However, there is no definition in the law as to what is meant by high water. For administrative purposes that 

Department has interpreted it as meaning the normal channel of the stream. Thus, it does not prevent encroachment upon periodically flooded land areas. While the steps taken by the towns of 

Concord and Wayland are satisfactory ones insofar as their own communities are concerned, there is a need, however, for the establishment of river basin flood plain zoning for the action taken by one town in establishing its zoning or area lines may have a detrimental effect upon a downstream municipality.

Accordingly, legislation has been drafted for inclusion with this report which would permit the establishment of flood encroachment lines on the Assabet River valley, based upon taking a multiple of the mean annual flood determined after engineering studies. The

- R 

[Apr. proposed legislation would permit the Water Resources Commission to establish actual encroachment lines, after an engineering study and public hearing. Naturally, there will be occasions when it is. necessary to encroach upon the flood plain zone established, for example, the installation of a highway across the flood plain zone. 

Provisions are included in the proposed legislation that any future encroachments would have to be licensed by the Department of Public Works in the manner set forth by the provisions of chapter 

91 of the General Laws. 

A program has been suggested for establishing conservancy districts. This has been done by placing certain wetlands or open spaces in a conservancy zone. Dover is the only town in Massachusetts that has done this to date.

The preservation of some of the wetlands in a drainage area such as the SuAsCo area, if done on a river basin basis, would reduce the magnitude of future floods and, at the same time, conserve water for the drier seasons of the year. The use of conservancy districts needs to be investigated further.

The Sudbury River Valley Commission, in its report of March, 1950, introduced legislation for the acquisition of land in the Sudbury and Concord River marshes, but the legislation was not acted upon favorably. The preservation of these areas would still have considerable flood control value. There has been some acquisition of meadowlands by public and private agencies for fish and wildlife development and, in some cases, for the preservation of open land. 

Preservation of these lands is of value in the reduction of the magnitude of floods.

Stream Classification.

In considering the dry weather conditions of the three rivers of the SuAsCo project, it must be borne in mind that 27.68 square miles of the Sudbury River are currently diverted for the supply of water to the Metropolitan District. The flows in the upper reaches of the Assabet River, during dry periods of the year, are relatively low which complicates the proper dilution of the effluents from the 

Westborough Sewage Treatment Plant. Down the valleys at the meadows near the confluence of the Concord and Sudbury rivers, the flow is reduced considerably by the use of water for agricultural purposes, with the result that in a dry year like 1957 the flows available to the town of Billerica for water supply purposes were critically low.

When most of the Framingham and Natick sewage was diverted to the Metropolitan Sewerage System by June, 1957, a considerable improvement resulted in the condition of the Sudbury River immediately below the former sewage disposal plants of these municipalities.

Northborough has no sewerage system at the present time, and with increased flow at this point there is a slight improvement in the river until Hudson is reached but not enough to expect more than a future Class "C" classification. There, the effluent of the Hudson

Sewage Treatment Plant enters the river which, as noted in Part III of the report, is in need of complete rehabilitation or replacement. 

The condition of the river below Hudson must therefore be considered, at present, in Class "D". However, with improvement of the sewage treatment in Hudson, it will be possible to improve the classification to Class "C". From Maynard to its confluence with the Sudbury River, the Assabet River must be classified at present as Class "D" during extreme dry periods. But, its average condition should permit a Class "C" classification in the future when the upstream condition has been improved.

LOW STREAM FLOW PROBLEM. 

As stated in the beginning of this report, the problem that is probably even more serious than the flood problem is that of making provision for a more ample flow in the rivers during the drier seasons of the year. Originally 92.74 square miles of the drainage area was utilized for water supply purposes and the water was diverted through two aqueducts to the Boston Metropolitan Area. At the present time, however, many of these sources of water supply, as noted above, have other uses and water is only diverted from the 

Sudbury Reservoir and Framingham Reservoir No. 3, which drain a total of 27.7 square miles. Water from the Wachusett Reservoir on the south branch of the Nashua River is brought through an aqueduct to the Sudbury Reservoir.

During 1957, an unusually dry year, the average inflow from the 

Wachusett Reservoir to the Sudbury Reservoir system was 56 million gallons per day, while the outflow from this reservoir system to the Metropolitan District was 31.6 million gallons per day, a net gain to the drainage area of 24.4 million gallons per day. The records show that an average of 30.5 million gallons per day was wasted into the Sudbury River. However, this is not as favorable a picture as it would seem, for much of this wastage of water was not during the period of greatest water demand. 

As stated above, there is an increasing need for water for domestic water supplies, irrigation, recreation, and for the dilution of treated municipal and industrial wastes. All these demands are at their peak at the same time during the dry season of the year. 

This is a very vital problem in this area in which there are not

[Apr. many additional reservoir sites available for the storage of water for release during dry seasons. There are some sites that might be developed, but further study is needed. Suggested legislation is being appended to this report for continuing the study with emphasis on providing a more ample flow in the rivers during the dry season. The study should include a survey of the groundwater resources in order that groundwater can be utilized to partly satisfy the increasing domestic water supply and irrigation needs.
