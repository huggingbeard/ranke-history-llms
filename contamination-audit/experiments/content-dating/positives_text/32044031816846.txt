Background 

Introduction 

The problems which stem from the birth of children out of wedlock extend to society as a whole. One manifestation of these problems is the amount of tax money spent each year to support such children through the Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) program. In order to recover some portion of those funds, Congress, in 1975, enacted Public Law 93-647, adding Title IV-D to the Social Security Act. 

This law requires jurisdictions participating in AFDC to undertake the establishment of paternity for all children receiving aid and born out of wedlock, and to provide such services to nonaid mothers upon request. Upon the establishment of paternity, fathers can be ordered to contribute to the support of their children, if able, thus reducing the amount of public money expended on child support.

In addition to the benefits to society, there are social and financial benefits to the child and his father. For example, through the establishment of paternity, a child may obtain the right of inheritance which traditionally was available only to children born in the context of wedlock. The child's father, on the other hand, may be afforded the right of custody if the mother dies or if she wishes to place the child for adoption.

The immediate effect of the law has been the commencement or upgrading of paternity determination programs throughout the United States.

Jurisdictions which expended little or no effort in this area prior to IV-D are training personnel to handle incoming and, in many places, a large backlog of paternity cases. Jurisdictions that have long recognized the need to ascertain paternity for children born out of wedlock, are concerned with improving their existing programs by utilizing advanced scientific procedures, and are encouraging the enactment of supportive State legislation.

The United States Department of Health, Education, and Welfare (HEW), Office of Child Sup port Enforcement (OCSE), the agency responsible for the enforcement of Public Law 93-647, subsequently commissioned a number of studies in order to examine and evaluate the needs of jurisdictions with regard to the paternity determination process. Based on the findings of these studies*, the University of Southern California's Center for Health Services Research was awarded a grant for the purpose of creating a handbook describing the techniques and procedures necessary for accurately and efficiently ascertaining paternity. The present document is the result of that project, and is designed to be of service to both newly and well-established IV-D offices.

Objectives and Format of Handbook 

The primary purpose of this handbook is to provide technical information on paternity case processing to IV-D agencies so that they may: 1) evaluate a paternity case to determine the possibility of establishing paternity, 2) adjudicate paternity in the maximum number of cases, 3) process paternity cases in the most cost-effective manner, and 4) ascertain paternity accurately.

The handbook is designed to be utilized as a working document, emphasizing: case processing techniques, interviewing and legal filing procedures, scientific testing methods, forms, and case evaluation criteria.

The first seven sections of the handbook, which correspond to the seven stages of paternity determination, are directed to those responsible for processing paternity cases on a daily basis, including interviewers, investigators, and clerks. The types of items included in the questionnaires facilitate the gathering of relevant information, with issues relating to welfare rights and rights of privacy taken into consideration. Sample legal forms and their descriptions offer suggestions that are specifically directed to the IV-D attorney. 

The final section describes the administrative techniques required to establish and maintain a successful paternity determination unit (e.g., staffing and record keeping).

The seven process sections describe why, when, and how to use each procedure to facilitate a rapid, accurate, and efficient determination of paternity. The summary at the beginning of each

Paternity Determination Process Overview 

This handbook presents a description and analysis of the processes which occur up to and including the point at which the paternity of a child born out of wedlock is either legally adjudicated or deemed impossible to pursue. The immediate objective of the paternity determination process is the accurate ascertainment of paternity in as many cases as possible and in the most effective manner. While the details and circumstances of paternity cases vary, any given case will contain one or more of the following seven critical stages: 

1. Identify and open the case (Open Case) 

2. Obtain allegation from mother (Allegation) 

3. Locate the putative father (Location) 

4. Confront the putative father (Confrontation) 

5. Verify the contested case (Verification) 

6. Adjudicate paternity (Adjudication) 

7. Evaluate and close case (Case Disposition) 

The successful, accurate, and rapid completion of the tasks involved within these stages, described below, increases the number of paternity cases established in a given jurisdiction. 

The Seven Critical Stages Identification and Opening of the Paternity Case 

A mother (or caretaker applicant) who has insufficient financial resources to support her child may apply to a local branch of the State Welfare 

Department or Department of Social Services (IV-A office) for Aid to Families with De pendent Children (AFDC). To determine the amount of need, the application for AFDC requires the mother to indicate the names and ages of her children, as well as her marital history. If the IV-A eligibility worker at the welfare office determines that the mother was unmarried at the time of her child's birth or conception, the case is classified as a 'Paternity Case.' 

Federal Regulations require the AFDC applicant/recipient to cooperate with the local IV-D agency in an effort to establish the child's paternity. In such cases, the IV-A agency will send a case referral sheet to the IV-D agency (Form# Pat. 2). The ineligible applicant is referred directly to the IV-D office since this office is required by law to provide its services to welfare recipients and non-welfare recipients alike. 

Allegation of Paternity

The paternity determination process begins with the paternity caseworker interviewing the mother (or caretaker) of the child and the completion of the Initial Confidential Paternity Questionnaire (pp. 13-15). The mother is also given an information sheet (Form# Pat. 1) which explains the process of paternity determination to the mother and requests her cooperation. The interviewer should also discuss with the mother the psychological benefit to the child of knowing who his father is, and the economic benefits the child may receive (inheritance rights to father's estate, Social Security benefits, life insurance, etc.). The results of this interview may be: 

1) no allegation of paternity, 2) one allegation of paternity, or 

3) more than one allegation of paternity. 2

The lack of an allegation of paternity may stem from the mother's unwillingness or inability to identify the father. In some cases, e.g. those involving casual acquaintances, the mother may be unable to name or identify the father. With sufficient documentation, certain reasons for noncooperation (p. 20) are acceptable and do not jeopardize the AFDC grant award.

Unwillingness may be due to fear of the father's recrimination, or the fact that the child was conceived as a result of rape or incest. If, however, the mother is trying to conceal the identity or location of the father to protect him from incrimination and possible damage to his reputation, she may be considered uncooperative and therefore subject to removal from the assistance payment.

As a supplementary means to aid the interviewer in determining the woman's cooperation, the woman may be asked to take a polygraph test or psychological stress examination (PSE).

While these verification techniques (described on page 16) do not establish paternity, the results may indicate whether or not the woman is intentionally being deceptive, and therefore uncooperative.

In many cases the allegation of paternity will be easily obtained and confined to a single man and the mother will volunteer his name and whereabouts. In some cases, however, the mother will have been involved with several men during the probable period of conception and her allegation may include more than one putative father. 

Whether the outcome of the case at this point results in single or multiple allegations of paternity, IV-D personnel should attempt to locate and confront all of the possible fathers. 

Since this point represents the end of the allegation stage of paternity determination, it is appropriate for the IV-D staff person to decide if this case can or should be processed through the location stage. For certain reasons indicated on the Paternity Case Disposition Checklist (see reasons B-1-B-4, Form# Pat. 37) the determination of paternity may not be warranted. If so, the Checklist should be completed and other procedures described in the "Case Disposition" stage should be carried out. If a decision is made to proceed with the case, and the putative father is not present in the IV-D office, the steps of the location stage should be initiated. 

Location 

The mother is usually the best source of information-e.g. full name, address, Social Security number-necessary for the location of the putative father. Depending on the amount of information she supplies, authorities may telephone or write the putative father to request an interview or, in some cases, directly contact the man and serve him with legal process. If the man cannot be contacted on the basis of the supplied information, location information may be sought from his recent employer, friends and/or relatives, or the State or Federal Parent Locator Services. Possible results of these location efforts include: 1) 2) an active case in which the man is located and subsequently agrees with or denies the allegation of paternity, or an inactive case in which immediate locate efforts have failed and thus make the probability of establishing paternity very low.

If the whereabouts of the alleged father are unknown after attempts to locate him (pp. 2134), or the alleged father resides on a Federal

Indian Reservation or outside the State and paternity complaint processing cannot be obtained from the responding jurisdiction (URESA), it may be necessary to close the case. On the other hand, if the man can be located, confrontation procedures should be initiated. Confrontation

A putative father whose whereabouts are known will receive a written notice to appear (Form# Pat. 15) in the IV-D office for an interview. If he appears, the implications of the allegation are explained to the putative father, his response is entered, and a case assessment is made.

If paternity is admitted, he may be asked to sign a confession of judgment, (Form# Pat. 19) to be formalized by a court order (Form# Pat. 

20). If the man denies paternity with sufficient documentation, such as proof of non-access, and the authorities are satisfied that he is not the father, charges against this man may be dropped and the case referred for an additional interview with the mother.

On the other hand, if the putative father's denial lacks credibility, or if he is unsure he is the father, he will be served with legal process, and court procedures will be initiated. In most jurisdictions, service of process is based on a civil complaint to establish paternity (Forms# Pat. 17, 18), and/or a show cause hearing. In some jurisdictions, however, a criminal complaint is filed

and a warrant issued. Some jurisdictions serve the putative father with civil process immediately upon location, thereby eliminating the interview step. (While there are no legal restraints against filing a complaint prior to interviewing the alleged father, an interview may preclude further action on the basis of an admission or sufficient reason to dismiss the allegation.)

Whether this initial confrontation takes place in court as the result of the filed paternity complaint or in the course of an interview, the legal implications of the paternity charge will be explained to the putative father. He will respond by: 

1) admitting paternity, or 

2) denying paternity, or 

3) stating he is not sure that he is the father.

If the putative father fails to appear after being served with civil process, a Default Judgment may be entered against him (Form# Pat. 

21). The Default Judgment establishes paternity; usually a court order for child support is entered against him as well. In other jurisdictions a bench warrant may be issued when he fails to respond to an order to show cause. 

If the individual denies paternity or declares uncertainty at the preliminary or pretrial proceedings, the matter will be set for trial. A high percentage of paternity cases are settled prior to trial. After the filing of the complaint, the IV-D attorney may wish to file a Request for a Tem porary Order of Support (Form# Pat. 22). If awarded, child support payments will be held by the court pending final disposition of the action. 

A temporary order usually tends to increase all parties' desire to proceed rapidly with the case. 

Verification of Contested Case 

At this point in the process, many of the cases will have been resolved either by stipulated judgments, default judgments, or they will have been rendered inactive. It is a relatively safe assumption, then, that most of the remaining cases will be contested and will involve additional discovery and verification procedures (pp. 66-90) before the case can be resolved.

The first step within this stage is to reinterview the mother using the Paternity Questionnaire- 

Contested Case (Form# Pat. 25). At this inter view it is appropriate to inquire about any and all facts which support her allegation of paternity. 

The IV-D staff person should then try to corroborate the information supplied by the mother, by contacting witnesses, and by obtaining letters, medical bills, motel receipts, and other evidence relating to the mother's account.

The IV-D attorney may wish to file various motions for fact finding (discovery) reasons. 

Completed interrogatories (Form# Pat. 26) are very helpful to the attorney because they supply him/her with information from the putative father concerning his relationship to the mother and child and enable a more complete case assessment.

One of the most enlightening means of discovering facts in a paternity case is blood test analysis, and since paternity is a biological relationship, it is important that the verification process include the use of advanced blood testing technology. One result of blood testing techniques (pp. 73-90) is the exclusion of a man as a possible natural father, in which case no further action against the alleged father is warranted. 

Blood tests which do not result in exclusion are frequently of use, since sophisticated blood test results may indicate a high probability (90% and above) that a given man is the father. This may induce the man to admit that he is the father, followed by the procedures necessary to obtain a formal court order. 

In short, the outcomes of the verification stage may be: 1) 2) 3) dropping of paternity charges against the alleged father, or alleged father's admission of paternity, or alleged father's denial of paternity charges.

If charges are dropped and the case closed, the Paternity Case Disposition Checklist should be completed. If the man admits paternity, a Confession of Judgment and Order may be entered. 

If the putative father continues to deny paternity and the IV-D staff believes they have enough evidence to pursue the case, it will proceed to a formal adjudication in a court of law. 

Adjudication of Contested Case 

The paternity process is directed to the establishment of paternity in a court of law. Although the vast majority of cases (as high as 90%) should be resolved short of trial, the process from initial interview of the mother to court order is directed by the jurisdiction's legal requirements of proof.* * The forms provided in the handbook illustrate civil paternity pleadings in a typical jurisdiction.

The mother, or complaining witness, is usually represented by a IV-D attorney who produces witnesses and other evidence to prove that the man alleged to be the father of the child is the natural father. If the paternity determination trial is handled as a civil proceeding, the attorney must establish only a preponderance of evidence to determine the child's paternity. However, if the paternity trial is treated as a criminal proceeding, evidence must be presented to prove paternity beyond a reasonable doubt.

Based on the evidence and testimony presented, and in concert with the civil or criminal proceeding requirements, paternity is adjudicated. The judge or jury, depending upon the requests of the defendant and the law of the jurisdiction, arrives at a verdict which either: 

1) finds the putative father to be the natural father, or 

2) finds the putative father not to be the natural father.

In most cases when paternity is established, a child support order is also entered. 

Closing the Paternity Case 

A paternity case is "closed", either permanently or temporarily, when paternity is formally established or when, for one or more of a number of possible reasons, pursuit of a putative father is stopped. Depending on the outcome of the establishment process, the closed paternity case becomes a child support collection case (if a support order has been issued) or becomes inactive.

Completion of the Case Disposition Checklist 

(Form# Pat. 37) with the appropriate information will facilitate communication of the paternity case outcome to the IV-A office, and will provide for a quick review of the status of a case. A copy of court findings and orders (if any) should also be included in the file of a paternity case. 5

Opening the Case 

A case involving an issue of paternity is opened for public assistance purposes in the public agency providing AFDC assistance payments. A non-welfare case could be referred from a variety of sources, but would be formally opened as a paternity case in the IV-D office. When the case is identified as a paternity case, it is transferred to the IV-D office. It is at that time that the case is "opened" for the purpose of paternity review, a folder is prepared, and the IV-A Referral Form (p. 12), if available, is reviewed by the IV-D department prior to the scheduled appointment with the mother.

Information to Mother-Paternity Case The following information sheet is designed to be given to the mother of the child whose paternity has not been established. It presents a summary of the procedures (as described in this Handbook) which may be employed in order to establish paternity. It also informs the mother of what her role will be in that process and what will be expected of her at each of the various stages. 

In jurisdictions where there is no delay in referral from IV-A to IV-D, the sheet may be given to the mother at the initial IV-D interview. 

Where delay is unavoidable the sheet should be provided at the earliest possible moment-either by the IV-A Eligibility Worker or by mail. The 

IV-D Interviewer will wish to supplement the printed information with additional data concerning the benefits which may accrue to the child on the basis of the legal establishment of paternity and of the mother's responsibility under IV-D to cooperate in the identification, location and ascertainment of paternity of the man she believes to be the father of her child. Penalties for failure to cooperate should also be explained. Since potential rights of children differ greatly from State to State, they are not included in this model form. 

The IV-D agency may wish to include at least some examples of those rights in its own information packet.

The information sheet provided here assumes that the child is the plaintiff in the action to establish paternity. Where the mother, the State or a legal subdivision brings suit (see Paternity 

Complaint, pp. 40-47 infra), modifications should be made accordingly.

In areas where there is a large population of non-English speaking people, the IV-D office should provide information sheets in the appropriate language(s).

This office will represent your child in a civil case to establish his/her legal paternity. Allegation of Paternity 

Critical to the allegation stage is securing a truthful allegation of paternity from the mother. To do this, several steps are recommended: 

1. Conduct initial interview with the mother to obtain an allegation of paternity. (See pp. 13-15.) 

2. If needed, verify the veracity of the mother's allegation of paternity or lack thereof. (See pp. 16-19.) 

3. Determine mother's cooperation, and if uncooperative, begin non-cooperation proceedings. (See p. 20.) 

4. If appropriate, close the case. (See reasons

B-1, B-2, B-3, B-4, B-5, and B-12 on the Paternity Case Disposition Checklist, p. 111.) 

Initial Confidential Paternity Questionnaire

The Initial Confidential Paternity Questionnaire is the first form which the IV-D staff person completes after the case has been identified as a paternity case. Its purpose is to supply the IV-D staff with information regarding the man whom the mother alleges to be the father of her child.* 

Note: If the mother has more than one child without his/her paternity established a separate Initial Confidential Paternity Questionnaire should be completed for each child.

The questionnaire provides the means to facilitate rapid location of the putative father, identify the conception period, and rule out any presumptions of legitimacy. This form should be completed by the IV-D worker in a private interview with the mother. Where evidence indicates that the case will not be contested the more detailed questions of the Paternity Questionnaire (Contested Case) p. 66 should not be asked. Before the questionnaire is completed, the mother should be told that she will be asked to sign the completed form. This will tend to increase the veracity of her statements. In short, on the basis of a thoroughly completed Initial Confidential Paternity Questionnaire, a IV-D staff person should be able to contact an alleged father and confront him with the salient details of the mother's allegation of paternity.

Polygraph Test and Psychological Stress Exam 

The polygraph test and/or the psychological stress examination are techniques employed by a number of child support enforcement agencies, pri- ⚫marily to assess a mother's allegation of paternity. 

They are not essential to the paternity determination process, and are used routinely only in a minority of the nation's jurisdictions. Those jurisdictions which do employ polygraph testing usually do so on the basis of the reputation of the polygraph operator. A majority of jurisdictions do not employ these techniques for reasons which range from statutory prohibitions to local policy. 

Nevertheless, a IV-D office may wish to consider the use of the polygraph test or psychological stress exam in order to determine the necessity or feasibility of employing them in the paternity determination process. The following information provides a background to be used in conjunction with statutory and case law on this subject. 

Polygraph Test 

Neither the polygraph nor the psychological stress exam determines truth or detects lies; they are instruments that measure and record certain physical and physiological phenomena. The principal behind polygraph testing is that recognizable and measurable physiological responses result from emotional stimuli associated with lying, and that the polygraph instrument can detect these altered physiological rates. The instrument measures respiration rates, cardiovascular activity and galvanic skin response. Drastic alterations in a person's breathing patterns when attempting to be deceptive are translated into electrical impulses which are subsequently recorded on a continuously moving sheet of paper and then interpreted by a polygraph examiner.

Psychological Stress Exam 

The psychological stress exam (P.S.E.) is composed of a set of questions that the individual answers out loud. An instrument records the answers and measures and graphically displays certain specific, stress-related components of the human voice. Superimposed on the audible voice frequencies are inaudible frequency modulations whose strength and pattern are thought to relate inversely to the degree of psychological stress within the person. These involuntary frequency modulations result from minute vibrations, or a microtremor, of the muscles of the voice mechanism during attempted deception. 

Potential Usefulness

When utilized in the paternity determination process, it is important to remember that the results of both testing procedures do not establish paternity, but rather elucidate whether or not an individual is truthfully recounting a given event to the best of his ability. The potential usefulness of the polygraph instrument and psychological stress exam is to identify those mothers who are attempting to withhold or are falsifying information concerning the whereabouts and identity of the putative father. The results are also useful in convincing a putative father that he is the only possible father of the child in question. 

Specific Application

During the paternity determination process these testing procedures may be used at several points. The first time the instrument might be used is after the woman has made or refused to make an allegation of paternity. At this point the predetermined questions should focus on the identification and present location of the putative father, circumstances surrounding their first meeting, and exclusiveness of their relationship during the conception period. The person performing the test should be a licensed, experienced examiner who adheres to recognized examination techniques. Because willingness to submit to these tests is usually at the mother's discretion, it is unlikely that a child support office would have the resources to employ such an examiner. Therefore, if these procedures are to be utilized, contractual arrangements with local law enforcement agencies to perform such tests should be considered. 

Occasionally, these testing techniques may be employed when the putative father denies having had sexual relations with the mother during the conception period. In this instance, such tests would usually be ordered by the Bench, provided the parties so stipulate. At this point, it becomes very important for the examiner to document his testing procedure and chain of evidence techniques so that the results will be admissible as evidence. Mother's Cooperation 

While a non-aid mother is under no obligation to establish the paternity of her child, by law every applicant for, or recipient of Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) is required to cooperate with IV-D officials in identifying and locating the parent(s) of a child for whom aid is requested or who is included in the public assistance budget computation, or establish good cause for refusing to do so. The applicant/recipient's responsibility extends to cooperating with 

IV-D officials to 1) establish the paternity of a child for whom aid is requested, and to 2) obtain support payment from the absent parent. 

According to section 45 CFR §232.12, cooper ation on the part of the applicant or recipient of 

AFDC requires: 

1) Appearing at the offices of the State or local agency or the child support agency as necessary to provide verbal or written information, or documentary evidence; 

2) Appearing as a witness at court or other hearings or proceedings; 

3) Providing information, or attesting to the lack of information, under penalty of perjury; and 

4) After an assignment has been made, paying to the child support agency any child support payments received from the absent parent which are covered by such assignment.

Although the statute gives high priority to the child's right to support, inheritance, and knowledge of his/her father's identity, it also provides an exception to the cooperation requirement where the applicant or recipient has good cause for refusing to cooperate as determined by the State AFDC agency in accordance with standards established by HEW. HEW's standards for determining good cause for refusal to cooperate are contained in 45 CFR 232.13. 

These standards are intended to: (1) protect the child and his/her caretaker relative from potential harm as a result of the child support process, and (2) maintain the integrity of the Child Support

Enforcement program under Title IV-D. 

If a mother who receives AFDC refuses to cooperate, the welfare department (IV-A) must be notified. The IV-A office determines the validity of any good cause claim, and, if good cause is found, also determines whether or not proceeding without cooperation would be against the best interests of the child. If good cause is not claimed or if it has been determined that good cause does not exist and the mother remains uncooperative, this lack of cooperation must be thoroughly documented and IV-A must be notified. If a non-aid mother does not cooperate, the case should be closed immediately. 20

Location of Putative Father 

If a decision has been made to proceed from the allegation stage, the next step in the process is to locate the putative father so that he may be confronted with the allegation of paternity and served with process, if a complaint is filed. Location of the putative father may be time consuming; however, the procedures and resources identified on pages 21-34 may reduce the amount of time and increase the number of successfully located putative fathers.

It may be necessary to close the case after all location procedures have failed. This decision should be made after consulting reasons B-6,

B-7, and B-8 on the Paternity Case Disposition 

Checklist, page 111.

When the mother does not have the putative father's address, or an alleged father is unavailable at the address given by the mother, all feasible steps should be taken to ascertain his whereabouts and make contact with him. These steps may include telephone calls to past employers made by the paternity caseworker in order to verify employment information provided by the mother, and may culminate in the cooperation of the Federal Parent Locator Service. 

The prima facie purpose of location procedures is the contacting of the alleged father to confront him with the paternity charge and to serve him with legal process, if necessary. Care should be taken to distinguish between an uncooperative vs. an unavailable putative father. An unavailable putative father would require IV-D staff to employ techniques (pp. 21-34) to locate him, whereas an uncooperative putative father should be served with process immediately. In order to keep the process as efficient as possible, the primary location procedures should focus on obtaining from the mother as much information as possible regarding the alleged father's residence and place of employment. Only after location attempts based on this data are exhausted should the more extensive State and Federal locator services be employed.

Confrontation of Putative Father

The objective of this stage is to confront the putative father with the allegation of paternity, and if possible to adjudicate an uncontested or default case as quickly as possible. To achieve this will require the IV-D staff person to: 

1. Request putative father to appear in the 

IV-D office so that the mother's allegation may be discussed with him prior to filing a paternity complaint. (See p. 36.) 

2. If possible, interview the putative father and offer him the opportunity to admit paternity. (See p. 39.) 

3. If appropriate, close the case. (See reason

B-9 on the Paternity Case Disposition 

Checklist, p. 111.) 

4. Prepare and have paternity complaint served to putative father if he fails to appear or appears and contests the allegation. (See pp. 40-47). 

5. Prepare and secure signature of putative father on confession of judgment if he admits paternity. (See pp. 48–54.) 

6. Enter default judgment if complaint is not responded to within 30 days. (See pp. 5558.) 

7. If allowable and appropriate, prepare and file Request for Temporary Order for Support and accompanying documents. (See pp. 59-65.) 

8. If appropriate, close the case. (See reasons

B-12 and B-13 on the Paternity Case Disposition Checklist, p. 111.)

If the putative father is located in a State other than that of the child, an action under the Uni form Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act 

(UR ESA) should be filed.

Notice to Appear Form 

The Notice to Appear form is a letter sent to an alleged father requesting that he appear at the child support office to discuss the mother's allegation of paternity. This notice is sent by the 

IV-D office after the mother's allegation of paternity is recorded and before the filing of an actual complaint.

In order to preserve confidentiality and avoid embarassment on the part of the putative father, the IV-D agency may wish to keep the Notice to Appear letter very general. The envelope of such a letter should show the address-not the name of the agency as a return address. The stationery may mention the name of the agency, but, if possible, should not call attention to the child support enforcement division of the office.

Compliance on the part of the putative father with the request to appear facilitates a rapid adjudication of paternity when he is willing to admit paternity and enables the paternity caseworker to assess the case in which the man denies paternity before the actual filing of the paternity complaint. 

If the man who contests paternity presents convincing evidence which indicates that he is not the father, then the paternity caseworker can refrain from filing an unnecessary and costly paternity complaint against this particular man.

In jurisdictions where there is close collaboration between the IV-A and IV-D offices or when there is a large backlog of paternity cases, it may be useful to have the IV-A office send the Notice to Appear provided they can ascertain the name and address of the putative father during their interview with the mother. Under these conditions the immediate mailing of the notice by the IV-A office would eliminate a delay which would otherwise occur if the IV-D office had to wait to mail this form until after they had talked with the mother. 35

Interstate Paternity Actions Federal regulations (45 CFR §303.7) require that paternity matters be pursued even if the father is out of State, and that a IV-D agency in one State must establish paternity or assist a IV- D agency in another State in the establishment of · paternity. Among the means available to the 

IV-D jurisdiction in the State where the mother and child reside are the use of "long arm statutes" and the initiation of actions under the Uniform 

Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act (URE- 

SA).* 

Long Arm Statutes 

"Long arm statutes" are commonly employed by all States in cases where the court of one State asserts jurisdiction over a party in another State on behalf of a party in the first State. The use of "long arm statutes" is provided for by the Uniform Parentage Act, as proposed by the National Conference of Commissions on Uniform State 

Laws (Section 8). To date, five States have en acted this Act in whole or in part, including:

California Hawaii Montana

North Dakota 

Washington

This approach provides for the establishment of jurisdiction over the absent putative father by the State in which the mother and child reside, pro- Ivided that the actual act of sexual intercourse occurred within that State. Before employing this procedure, the IV-D attorney should consult

State statutes and judicial attitudes in order to determine the availability and feasibility of "long arm statutes❞ for paternity determination.

Apart from the provisions of the Uniform Parentage Act, it is still possible that any State's "long arm statute" may serve as sufficient ground for asserting jurisdiction in any interstate paternity action. The IV-D attorney may therefore wish to consider pursuing action in this manner if statutes and case law allow, and if remedies such as 

URESA are not available. 66 * Interstate paternity jurisdictional issues are discussed in detail in 'Asserting Jurisdiction Over Nonresident 

Putative Fathers in Paternity Actions,” Martin R. Levy, 45 University of Cincinnati Law Review, pp. 207-217 (1976).

URESA A more common approach is an action under URESA. This extends jurisdiction to the State in which the putative father resides for the establishment of paternity and enforcement of a support order. As amended by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws in 

1968 (Section 27), the determination of paternity against an absent parent residing in another State may be undertaken when the obligor refuses to pay support on the grounds that he is not the father of the child. To date, the following twentyfour States have enacted the "paternity" provi sion of URESA:

A URESA action in which paternity is an issue may or may not be handled by a responding jurisdiction, even where the paternity provision has been enacted. Some jurisdictions will proceed as in any other case, setting the matter for hearing and subsequently for trial if paternity is denied. 

Many courts, however, will set a paternity 

URESA for hearing and if the father denies, will remove the matter from the court calendar and take no further action.

States which have not enacted the 1968 amendments may refuse to file a responding URESA unless the child was born in wedlock, or a support order already exists. In any event, it is advisable to initiate a URESA action even if there is a low perceived probability that the responding jurisdiction will take action. The possibility that paternity may be adjudicated either through voluntary admission or trial, with a subsequent order for support, is sufficient reason to file an initiating 

URESA in all cases.

Communication Between Jurisdictions 

Inter-jurisdictional understanding increases the possibility of establishing paternity across State lines, and understanding may be enhanced in the following ways: • Responding jurisdictions should inform initiating jurisdictions when they receive inaccurate or insufficient information. 37

When a responding jurisdiction is procedurally unable to take action, the initiating jurisdiction should be informed in detail as to the reasons why action was not taken and what the acceptable procedures are. • The responding jurisdiction should notify the initiating jurisdiction immediately regarding what action has been taken and the results thereof.

It is also essential that initiating jurisdictions take information from responding jurisdictions into account when filing a URESA action. When an action is filed with a jurisdiction for the first time, the initiating jurisdiction should supply all the information it feels necessary to establish paternity, requesting that the responding jurisdiction inform it of any additional needs or procedural corrections that need to be made. 

When initiating a URESA with a potential paternity issue, many jurisdictions will simply allege paternity and only when contested, provide allegations of the specific facts relating to the proof of paternity. While this approach may "work" in a number of circumstances, particularly in those States which have not enacted the paternity provision in their URESA laws, it will probably enhance interstate cooperation if complete case information is included with the URESA petition. 

The inclusion of such items (if available) as: an affidavit from the plaintiff regarding her relationship with the alleged father ⚫ a certified copy of the birth certificate • photographs of all parties ⚫ a complete and accurate description of the alleged father statements of witnesses and any letters or writings which address the issue will aid the responding jurisdiction in locating and confronting an alleged father. Sufficient evidence sent with a petition may facilitate voluntary admissions, where possible, and avoid costly trials. 

It may also increase a judge's willingness to accept a paternity case, even in States where such action is not specifically provided for under URESA.

When and if a paternity case goes to trial in the responding jurisdiction, the initiating jurisdiction should cooperate fully in providing any requested evidence, including drawing and shipping blood samples from the mother and child if necessary. The initiating jurisdiction should also inform the responding jurisdiction immediately of any changes in case status which may occur. 

Finally, it is recommended that when paternity is established and a support order entered in the responding jurisdiction, the initiating jurisdiction should receive a complete copy of the order, including findings of fact and the manner in which support payments will be collected.

Paternity Complaint 

The paternity complaint names a specific man as the defendant in a paternity case and initiates civil proceedings to establish paternity. Upon service of summons*, the man is required to respond in court, within a statutory time, to the allegation made against him. Failure to so respond may result in the entry of a default judgment. 

A complaint will be filed when: 1) the man is willing to stipulate to paternity, or 2) when he contests the allegation, or 3) when he fails to answer the informal requests to respond to the allegation. After a failure to appear for interview, or a denial of paternity by the putative father, a complaint should be filed unless his reasons for denial can be clearly corroborated. 

Although legal requirements vary from jurisdiction to jurisdiction the following information is recommended for inclusion in a paternity complaint (parentheses indicates optional).

Confession of Judgment, Voluntary Agreement, Stipulation 

A voluntary agreement of paternity is signed when the father does not wish to contest the mother's allegation and is willing, if able, to provide child support. Such a procedure may be employed in the initial stages of the process (e.g. when a mother appears with the father for her initial interview, or when the father appears at the request of the district attorney's office), or it may be introduced later to halt proceedings and commence adjudication. In any event, it must be followed by a judgment and order signed by the court.

Some States have enacted consent judgment statutes which permit the father to sign a consent. This consent is filed with the court and signed by a judge and requires neither a hearing nor appearance by the parties. This procedure provides the attorney with the opportunity to establish paternity in the fastest and most efficient manner possible. Since many men named as fathers will be willing to agree to the allegation, it facilitates the adjudication of such admissions in the shortest possible time. It further provides the man who is willing to admit paternity with the opportunity to do so without having to obtain counsel, respond to complaints, or appear in court. This will avoid his court costs and fees for which he would otherwise be responsible if he lost the contested court case. 

If the putative father is willing to stipulate paternity, an agreement is reached on the amount of support. An agreement for entry of judgment must be executed and filled and a judgment entered.

Corroborate Mother's Information

After a denial of paternity by the putative father, and a second interview with the mother, it may be desirable to use corroborative techniques to further determine the veracity of the facts presented by the mother. Such corroboration includes verification of factual details presented by the mother, blood testing and interrogatories.

Experienced investigation work frequently clarifies the details of the mother's story and will eventually convince a judge or jury that she is telling the truth. Such corroboration would range from verification of the location of a hotel or motel, substantiation of the conditions on alleged date of conception and any and all of the evidence which lends believability and support to her story. An investigator, searching for such support, should be familiar with the elements of proof of a case as set forth in the Paternity Com plaint (p. 40), the sample Interrogatories (p. 

68), and the Trial Outline (p. 91). 

Interrogatories to Defendant

Interrogatory and deposition is available as a fact finding procedure prior to trial. The form included here is for interrogation of the defendant; similar questions could be used in deposition taking. Questions may be varied according to the particular case. 

It will benefit both court-oriented and administrative-oriented jurisdictions to develop and maintain sets of "stock" interrogatories for cases of disputed paternity. Blood Testing 

Blood testing determines whether the putative father's blood factors could have combined with the mother's to produce those of the child. It is therefore a technique which should be employed whenever a man denies paternity and cannot provide substantiating evidence for his claim. Possible outcomes of blood testing include: 1) the exclusion of the putative father; or, if he is not excluded, 2) a calculation in per cent of the probability of paternity. The chance of exclusion for a truly innocent man increases with the addition of factors being tested. Similarly, the probability of inclusion also increases as the putative father is not excluded by extended factor testing.

Traditionally, results which exclude a putative father are conclusive and result in case closure.

Probability results, on the other hand, are not conclusive, since even a probability of 98% leaves 2% of the male population as possible father. The latter are nevertheless considerably more useful in prosecution, since, when combined with other substantiating evidence, they may make a man more certain that he is in fact the child's father, thus leading to an admission. Furthermore, where statutes permit their admissibility in court, probability results provide additional strong evidence for proof of paternity. 

Within the paternity determination process, blood tests may occur either before or after a formal complaint has been filed. State statutes dictate whether or not a court order is necessary and who may request blood testing. Many 

States include provisions for entering into evidence the refusal of any party to submit to blood testing. Where permissible, tests administered prior to the commencement of formal proceedings are most useful in that they may serve to eliminate costly trials either through exclusion or by convincing a man who feels he might be the father that he is the true father.

Procedures to be performed by the IV-D 

agency with regard to blood testing include: 

1) Obtaining a court order, when necessary, for blood testing 

2) Arranging an appointment for mother, child, and putative father at laboratory where samples will be taken 3) Instructing the laboratory taking samples and (if different) the laboratory performing tests of the jurisdiction's identification and result reporting requirements.

Blood Test Laboratory 

When arranging for blood testing, the IV-D agency should bear in mind that while blood testing is a scientific means of establishing paternity, it is valuable only when sufficient testing is done to eliminate a reasonable part of the element of chance. The following criteria have been established by a joint committee of the American 

Medical Association and the Family Law Section of the American Bar Association, for acceptable levels of paternity blood testing:

It is not the intent to recommend in all medicolegal problems of disputed parentage that the entire set of tests is mandatory. It is often possible to establish exclusion with the basic blood group systems ABO, Rh, and MNSs. When these basic tests do not allow exclusion, extended testing should be done using the Kell, Duffy and Kidd systems to increase the mean probability of exclusion to the 72% level (63% for

Blacks). In the event no exclusion is produced at that stage, additional testing using the HLA system or the enzyme or protein systems (if necessary, by referral) should be done to raise the mean probability of exclusion to at least the 90% level.

Ideally, the laboratory used should have the capability of raising the mean probability of exclusion above the 95% level. If a putative father has not been eliminated by the time 95% of the male population has been eliminated from consideration as possible fathers, the likelihood that he is indeed the father is very convincing, since the statistical tables used in determining mean probability of exclusion assume that any average man had equal access to the woman in question whereas in most instances this is highly unlikely. 

Thus, these calculations are giving the putative father the benefit of the doubt, and when this is explained to him, he is much more likely to be convinced that he actually is the father. 

It is up to the IV-D agency to establish the level of blood testing desired. A favorable result, i.e., confession of judgment, default judgment, or judicial determination of paternity, is much more likely to occur if the agency requires that sufficient test systems be used to produce at least 

90% mean probability of exclusion. For the few dollars difference in cost, 75% of which will be reimbursed by the Federal government, the results should be significant.

Closing the Case 

Closing a paternity case in which paternity has not been established is a very important procedure in a IV-D office, due to the possibility of that case being re-opened at some later date. 

That is, if paternity has not been determined, the mother, child, IV-A, or IV-D office may stumble upon new evidence and therefore wish to re-open the case. When this occurs, the old. case file will most likely be requested and utilized. The old case file should therefore contain an easily identifiable sheet which summarizes the past results of the case. The Case Disposition 

Checklist (Form # Pat. 37, p. 111) is intended to suffice as this sheet. 

If paternity has been adjudicated, and the case transferred over as a child support "pay" case, it is highly probable that the case file will be consulted for a determination and enforcement of the support order. It is therefore of utmost importance that all court orders are copied and placed in the IV-D office. The IV-D office must also take responsibility to notify the IV-A and any appropriate law enforcement agencies of the outcome of the case.

Paternity Case Disposition Checklist 

The IV-D office needs to keep an accurate record of the disposition of all paternity cases referred from the IV-A office, and for this purpose the Paternity Case Disposition Checklist is provided. When completed, it will afford internal control and case studies information to the IV- D administrator and his staff, and will serve as a paternity case disposition memorandum to the 

IV-A office. It indicates the reasons why a case should become inactive-due either to the jurisdiction of paternity or to some factor which makes it impossible to proceed.

One copy of the form should be filed with each case so that the IV-D administrator and staff will have immediate access to case status. An additional copy may be sent to the IV-A office since case disposition may affect the AFDC grant. 

The paternity case worker should be thoroughly familiar with the reasons for rendering a case inactive, so that decisions may be made efficiently and effectively with regard to which cases warrant continued time and energy. Utilization of this checklist should facilitate making and documenting such decisions.

The child support office may wish to consolidate case disposition information on a single form with IV-A to IV-D referral (p. 11 supra). 

It is included at this point in the Handbook to emphasize the role of the information required to evaluate the feasibility of pursuing a given case, and to indicate the type of data needed for rapid review of the status of all cases processed by the IV-D office.

Administrative Considerations in 

Planning and Implementing the

Paternity Determination Program 

The preceding sections of this handbook describe in some detail the forms and procedures available to effectively operate a paternity determination effort. This section highlights some of the administrative concerns that must be addressed if the successful implementation of a new, more effective system is to be made. Administrative considerations will vary among jurisdictions based upon the size of staff involved, resources allocated to the area, and the jurisdiction's history of focus on paternity establishment. It will be necessary, then, for each jurisdiction to adapt the suggestions that follow with regard to their own needs. The basic purpose of this section is that a transition to a new paternity determination system can be seriously jeopardized by failure to adequately plan for program implementation. 

Planning and Implementing the 

Paternity Program

An outline follows describing procedures which may be used to implement or revise a paternity determination program. The procedures consist of two stages: planning and implementation. The purpose of the planning stage is to inform all personnel and departments of tasks to be accomplished and responsibilities to be assumed. While to some the techniques outlined may seem unnecessary and redundant, it has been found that failure to notify and prepare all personnel directly or indirectly affected by the new program results in problems at a later time. Adequate training of old and new staff is necessary during the implementation phase if the program is to function smoothly. It is suggested that consideration be given to ongoing evaluation of the system during the early months of operation. Modifications may be designed to improve upon the system as operational experience increases.

Managing the Paternity Program 

The administrator of a local IV-D child support office has the ongoing task of ensuring that those cases in which paternity has not been adjudicated 114

are processed in the most efficient manner possible. To do this, several areas require constant attention. These areas, and the considerations involved, are presented here in order to assist the administrator in establishing an efficient paternity office. Staffing 

Administrative considerations in the area of staffing include overall approach and specific job descriptions, caseload ratios, and staff training. 

In order to determine the most efficient approach to staffing, consideration should be made of the physical and financial resources available, relationships among critical governmental agencies, the size of the caseload, and the geographic and demographic nature of the jurisdiction.

Two basic approaches to staffing are possible: horizontal and vertical. Horizontal refers to organizing the child support office staff in terms of the functions performed. Thus, a person classified as an interviewer would handle that task regardless of the specific type of case. An investigator would be responsible for investigating any kind of child support case ranging from Welfare Fraud to Non-payment of Support to Paternity. The horizontal approach is most suited to situations where the number of any given type of case is small and it would be inefficient to allocate staff time to one type of case on a fulltime basis. This includes the smallest jurisdictions, where one or two people might be responsible for the entire field of child support, as well as jurisdictions with populations spread over a large area in which centralization and specialization is impractical. 

The vertical approach requires specialization within the child support office and is most useful where there are large numbers of particular types of cases and facilities for centralizing the specialists in one location. In such a jurisdiction, a "Paternity Unit" may be established in which the function of each person will be related to the ascertainment and adjudication of paternity. Attorneys, investigators, interviewers, and clerks work together as a team, and all paternity cases are directed to their attention. This approach facilitates keeping accurate records on specific types of cases as well as increases the speed of handling cases due to the staff's familiarity with the process.

Regardless of the staffing approach, the administrator must ensure that personnel working on paternity are handling the optimum number of cases. The administrator may wish to undertake a study in order to determine at what point efficiency declines, due to too many or too few cases per staff person. In a horizontal approach situation, the final decision will concern the amount of time allotted to child support personnel to pursue specific kinds of cases. In a team/ vertical approach, the administrator must ensure that the number of people, with the proper classifications, comprise the paternity unit in light of caseload requirements. 

Regardless of the kind of overall approach to staffing, the administrator must ensure that he has the right kind of people performing the necessary tasks. To make this determination, the administrator should refer to the following job descriptions which are based on the need to complete the tasks involved in legally adjudicating paternity. The central focus is the tasks; therefore, combinations of duties (e.g. interviewing and investigating) are possible, and in some cases, desirable. Where this is the case, due to small paternity caseloads or financial limitations, the IV-D office administrator must ensure that the persons involved are familiar with the complete spectrum of information necessary to adjudicate paternity as well as the techniques and procedures used to obtain and support that information. 

Where increased caseloads and funds permit, specialization should be pursued along the lines herein described.

Paternity Case Preparer Tasks: 

To obtain from the mother of the child born out of wedlock the name, address, occupation, and other identifying data of the man she believes to be the father of the child.

To evaluate the mother's allegation with respect to veracity, cooperativeness, and completeness, and to recommend appropriate action.

To contact, interview, and confront the alleged father with the paternity charge.

To evaluate alleged father's response to the allegation.

To ensure that proper forms are completed if the man admits paternity.

To obtain facts corroborating the allegation if the man contests. This may include arranging blood testing for all parties, the pursuit of witnesses and other evidence, and arranging for interrogatories to be administered.

To evaluate corroborating evidence with the attorney and recommend appropriate action. 

To ensure that all forms necessary for presentation in court are completed. 115

To provide status reports to mother and routine governmental agencies upon request.

Skills: 

The paternity case preparer must be familiar with the entire process of paternity determination, including the reasons for pursuing paternity. 

He/she must be able to explain the importance of adjudication to both the mother and the alleged father in order to encourage their cooperation. 

He/she must be familiar with the possible contingencies involved at the various stages and be able to respond appropriately. He/she must be able to assess the merits of a case at the various stages and to distinguish those with a high probability of successful completion from those in which chances for adjudication are slight. He/she must be able to coordinate activities with the IV-A unit and with the prosecuting attorney, and to successfully interview clients. 

Paternity Investigator

Tasks: 

To obtain information regarding the probable whereabouts of an alleged father. 

To obtain evidence related to the allegation of paternity. This may involve contacting witnesses and obtaining documents such as birth certificates, hospital bills, motel receipts, etc.

To obtain corroborating evidence supplied by the mother after the paternity charge has been contested.

Skills: 

The paternity investigator must be familiar with the resources available for locating an absent parent. He/she must be aggressive and imaginative in pursuing leads and obtaining information. 

He/she must be able to assess the value of the evidence based on a knowledge of the needs of a paternity case. Clerk/Secretary

Tasks: 

To accurately complete legal forms (admissions, complaints, depositions, judgments, etc.) and other forms involved in paternity determination.

To type and send letters to mothers and alleged fathers requesting interviews, and to businesses and governmental agencies for assistance in location of absent parents.

To maintain a file of all forms, correspondence, and other information relative to the case. 

To maintain a system of case and civil process follow-up.

To answer routine questions by mother and local government agencies.

Skills: 

The clerk/secretary of a paternity office must combine normal secretarial abilities with the ability to accurately and neatly prepare legal documents.

IV-D Attorney Tasks: 

To make decisions regarding legal actions to establish paternity. 

To prepare the paternity complaint, default judgment, confession of judgment. 

To review interrogatories and participate in all pre-trial hearings.

To represent the plaintiff in the paternity determination trial.

To oversee the efforts of the paternity case preparer and paternity investigator and make sure the effort to establish paternity is being carried out in concert with the legal burden of staff. Skills: 

The IV-D attorney must be familiar with the child support and paternity determination statutory and case law. He/she must be able to try court and jury cases and argue various motions at pre-trial hearings. He/she must be prepared to work with local judiciary and bar to make the paternity process more effective and efficient.

He/she must have a general knowledge of welfare law. 

Paternity Unit Administrator

Tasks: 

To administer the IV-D program including supervising the operation of the ongoing paternity determination program and rendering technical assistance to all IV-D staff involved in the paternity determination effort.

To interact with all inter- and intra-office requests for information or clarification about the paternity determination program.

To train new and existing staff on the process and procedures for paternity determination.

To develop a liaison with all cooperating local, State and Federal agencies and survey local resources concerning their ability to assist IV-D staff in the establishment of paternity. 

To implement an overall plan among the county department of social services, prosecuting attorney and representatives of the court in order to effectuate the establishment of paternity. 116

To maintain adequate statistics, and fiscal and operational records.

To limit the intake of cases to the number that can be worked by staff. 

Skills: 

The paternity unit administrator should be highly experienced in the paternity, welfare and child support areas. He/she should also have had several years of administrative responsibility. If the administrator is not an attorney, he/she needs to be familiar with legal proceedings and comfortable as an overall team or paternity unit leader. It is also the administrator's responsibility to ensure that new staff are properly trained and that others are kept up-to-date in the area of techniques and procedures. Staff should be educated in the process of paternity determination and law relating to paternity determination. Opportunities for review and for continuing education should be provided. For this, the administrator should make maximum use of State and Federal training programs as well as conferences in the area of child support.

Information Systems: Forms, Statistics, Files 

In order for information to be obtained and transmitted as quickly and completely as possible, the administrator must ensure that the forms and procedures employed are logical and efficient.

Consideration must be taken to include in the forms all of the information needed at a particular stage of process, and that requests for data which is extraneous or in violation of the mother and putative father's rights is eliminated. Procedures must be created and maintained which facilitate the flow of information between personnel involved. Constant reevaluation should be made of forms and procedures employed, encouraging feedback from workers who use them on a dayto-day basis. Conditions to be taken into account include: • Are forms readily understandable? • Do forms request all pertinent information? • Do procedures follow logically and efficiently? • Does information flow easily from stage to stage? • Is a separate form necessary or can steps be combined?

The area of paternity determination has traditionally been plagued by a lack of statistics. Recognizing that accurate statistics are necessary to establish and support social policy, Congress and HEW require IV-D jurisdictions to maintain accurate records on the numbers and kinds of cases opened in the field of child support. The local administrator, according to the guidelines of a particular State's plan, must maintain and supply such records as required. Consideration must also be made of the particular statistical needs of a jurisdiction.

The successful and efficient ongoing operation of an office (and maintaining funding levels) depends on being able to identify, isolate and eliminate problem areas, and accurate records assist in the identification and documentation of such areas. Statistics will also be necessary for determining the staffing and financial needs of a jurisdiction. Types of data which may be helpful include: • Number of child support cases opened, • Number of paternity cases opened, • Number of confessions signed, • Number of defaults entered, • Number of cases proceeding to trial, • Number of adjudications of paternity, Number of cases dismissed, and 

Number of blood tests performed. (Note: In order to be comprehensive, statistics kept must include amount of child support awarded and collection records. For the purpose of determining the efficiency and needs of the paternity determination process, collection records should be divided according to types of cases as well as for child support in general. These statistics will be invaluable to the administrator when he/she requests additional resources as he/she will have documentation of the costs and benefits associated with the paternity determination effort.)

Careful consideration must be given to organization of paternity folders. In order to readily identify paternity cases, it may be useful to designate paternity folders so they are distinct from other IV-D cases. This may be accomplished through the use of a different color file tab for paternity cases.

Material inside file folders should be maintained chronologically. Essential forms (such as the paternity complaint, confession of judgment) might be printed on colored paper or in some other way coded for easy identification from the myriad of paper contained in the paternity folders. 

All forms such as questionnaires and checklists should be completed and initialed by the IV-D staff person. This will ensure that the form is 117

legible and complete and will enable the IV-D staff person to immediately follow up on any leads or other information provided by the respondent. 

The administrator will also have to determine if some of the forms should be bilingual.
