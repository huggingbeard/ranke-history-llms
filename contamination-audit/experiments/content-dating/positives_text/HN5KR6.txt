THE CASE OF THE DEADLY DROPS 

Major Timmins' visit to Vienna Towers was fraught with excitement. First the strikebreaking elevator operator taking him upstairs was murdered and the Major himself was slugged.

After his recovery, he went on to the apartment of Charlotte Ellert, a middle-aged lady who wore high laced shoes to keep her legs shapely. And a few minutes later Charlotte was killed by a bullet out of nowhere.

Almost all of the building's tenants were possible suspects: the righteous reformer Mr. 

Porter; the smoldering Southern belle Eula

Tallant; Cannon, who spent his spare time waving poles at pigeons; Vernier, an astrologer with an astounding knowledge of magic. It was an intricate puzzle, and this account of the Major's efforts to solve it is an exciting and absorbing tale.

When I was halfway from the corner to the doorway of the 321 building, I saw who was arguing with the doorman, and I hurried. I knew that Major Timmins was quick-tempered and shortwinded, and I knew that one of the tenants had been slugged the night before. 

Looking the length of Vienna Towers, you couldn't help feeling the solidity and safety and dignity of the place. It was a whole city block of sixteen-story apartment buildings, brick cliffs broken by setback terraces and penthouses. One of the terraces added an odd pastoral touch to the scene; a man up there was waving a long pole at a flock of circling pigeons.

It was hard to realize that things were anything

but solid or safe or dignified inside there since yesterday, but they weren't. They were shaky and jumpy and full of sloppy compromises. A little city in a state of siege—that's what it was.

I came to the door of the 321 building and took Major Timmins by the arm. Under his overcoat his thick shoulders heaved; he was fighting for breath. 

He looked around and gave me a quick handclasp without bothering to remove his scuffed pigskin glove. Everything about the major was a little scuffed and threadbare, but scrupulously neat. "Damned insolent scum!" he panted.
