PUBLIC HEALTH ASPECTS OF WATER RESOURCE DEVELOPMENT IN THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA Prepared By The Public Health Service U. S. Department of Health, Education, and Welfare Introduction 

Water has been implicated in disease transmission in every part of the world. All water resource developments entail health considerations, both during and after construction. 

Unless proper safeguards are instituted, the water facility can spread sickness and death instead of economic and social benefits. Adequate preventive measures against water-borne and water-bred diseases and toxicants should be considered in the early planning of water resource development and should be "built into" each new project. 

Water is a well-spring of life and is man's most universally used material. Almost every facet of our lives is dependent on water. It is used for power, navigation, irrigation, recreation, and industrial processes. It reaches into every home and mouth. 

The abundance of safe water is essential for good health and prosperity.

Water and Health 

The pestilential effects of water-borne and water-bred diseases have been felt on every continent and nearly every country on earth. High on this list because of their virulence, prevalence, or ease of communicability are cholera, typhoid fever, the dysenteries, malaria, yellow fever, schistosomiasis or

bilharziasis, filariasis, dengue, dracontiasis, onchocerciasis and infectious hepatitis.

Water as a Conveyor of Infectious Organisms 

The contamination of water with disease-producing organisms has been one of our greatest threats to health. The United States has had several outbreaks of cholera, the last one in the 1870's. 

Cholera epidemics are still recurring in India, Thailand and Pakistan, especially in the larger metropolitan areas. Cholera can virtually be eradicated by the provision of safe water for human needs. 

Typhoid fever and the dysenteries have also caused widespread illness. Relatively recently, in Rochester, New York, a waterworks employee turned a wrong valve letting untreated river water flow into public mains. Within a few days there were 

30,000 cases of illness which were attributed to contaminated water.

The potential danger of infectious hepititis was clearly illustrated during the water-borne epidemic in New Delhi, India, in 1955-56.

Typhoid fever prevailed throughout the United States until health officials insisted that community water supplies be purified.

This development in water supply occurred only a few years before 1900; the first filter plant had been built in 1875. 

At that time and for some years thereafter the United States fought typhoid fever as intensely as it is fighting heart trouble and cancer today.

Filtration and chlorination of water for domestic use were employed after it was recognized that water quality as well as quantity had to be considered in developing water resources.

Water as a Breeding Area for Disease-Carrying Insects 

Water is a natural breeding medium for disease-carrying mosquitoes. Among the insect-borne diseases which have been geographically far-reaching are malaria, yellow fever, dengue and filariasis. At the present time they are not problems in the United States. However, encephalitis, an exceedingly serious mosquito-borne infection, is now becoming a primary concern. It

One important related illustration of the health aspect of water development was the construction of the Panama Canal. First attempts to construct the Canal had failed, not for lack of engineering skills but because of yellow fever and malaria. was not until the mosquito's role in the transmission of these diseases was known and preventive measures were strictly enforced to bring them under control, that construction could proceed. 

All possible preventive measures were utilized such as draining and filling of water breeding areas, treating the breeding places with larvicides and the screening of sleeping quarters. This meant the difference between success and failure. 

Another illustration is the development of the Tennessee Valley Authority. Shortly after its creation in 1933, TVA employed a staff of technicians in the fields of medicine, engineering and biology to develop a malaria control program for its great chain of reservoirs. Preventive measures included water level management, shoreline maintenance, and clearing of reservoir sites. Water level management is a built-in feature which made it possible to strand drift and flotage material above the water line, limit marginal vegetation and interrupt mosquito production by cyclical fluctuation. Malaria in the area was not only substantially reduced in the 1930's under circumstances which otherwise would have caused it to increase,

but was eventually eradicated in the 1940's when residual spraying of DDT was employed.

Experience in the U. S. has also demonstrated that increased irrigation development has increased such mosquito-borne diseases as encephalitis when not properly guarded against in program development. Irrigation canals and ditches and flooded land have caused a serious insect vector problem. Irrigation of new lands in the northwest and western sections of the United States has been responsible for excessive mosquito production, particularly Culex tarsalis which is capable of transmitting encephalitis. Major weaknesses of irrigation systems which contribute to this problem, consist of: incomplete project drainage, inadequate disposal of irrigation waste water and leakage from canals and laterals. Adequate corrective measures are being instituted in existing projects wherever they were not adequately covered in initial planning. 

Water as a Breeding Area for Disease-Carrying Mollusks 

Schistosomiasis or bilharziasis is a very serious problem in tropical countries and is transmitted through the snail which is an intermediary host for the etiologic agent. However, in the United States it is a relatively minor nuisance called "swimmer's itch" which is experienced principally in the Great 

Lakes area. The outstanding example of a "man-made" disease problem is the greatly increased preponderance of bilharziasis in the irrigated areas of Egypt following the building of the Aswan Dam.

This disease can be prevented by water pollution control measures such as the sanitary disposal of human feces and urine and by relocating nearby villages away from the proximity of impounded waters to minimize contamination from human sources.

Molluscicides can also be used to eradicate snails. However, the presence of snails per se without the concurrent human contamination does not contribute to the spread of the disease. 

Water as a Conveyor of Toxic Chemicals 

The presence of toxic chemicals such as detergents and insecticides in water supplies is becoming an increasingly difficult problem in the United States. This is due primarily to the industrialization and increased preponderance of new chemicals utilized in industrial and agricultural processes. 

Promiscuous use of insecticides such as DDT for agricultural purposes complicate the problem because eventually these chemicals carried by water run-off from the land, find their way into the waterways. Steps are being taken to improve our methods of water analysis and purification so as to detect and eliminate all toxic substances from community water supplies. 

Water Resource Development Water Storage Reservoirs 

In the United States water was impounded primarily for domestic purposes until irrigation began to be practiced on an extensive scale in the dry, western portion of the country. 

It was not until the population began to mount and industry to grow that difficulties were encountered in obtaining enough water for the greatly increased uses, compounding the need for developing new water resources and conserving the supply. 

Reservoirs to store water and make it available where and when needed were the first answer to this shortage problem. first they were single-purpose reservoirs--a reservoir for municipal supply, one for flood control, another for electric power generation, and so forth. At

These storage reservoirs were built to meet the demands of the moment; their location was determined largely by suitability of site and proximity of final use without consideration of total river valley water potential, and future water requirements. 

More and more water was needed to satisfy the increasing number of irrigation farmers, and a rapidly increasing population. 

As remaining inexpensive dam sites for diversion and storage of water grew fewer, larger structures became necessary. More capital was required for water development, beyond the capacity of private individuals, companies, or even State governments. 

The government of the United States then entered the picture, in navigation and flood control at the beginning, then irrigation and power generation, and finally municipal water supply. 

Urbanization and industrialization are accountable for the increasing demands for water. Community water supplies are providing water, not only to the homes but also for industrial processes, air conditioning and firefighting. The use of water in the cities exceeds 150 gallons a day per person--10 to 15 times the amount once needed by rural inhabitants lacking the conveniences of modern life. Requirements for flood control, power, irrigation, navigation, recreation and other uses are competing for water. Water allotted to one use often deprived another; and water shortages, though not universal, have been frequent enough and general enough to demand greater and carefully planned Federal water resource developments.

Multipurpose Basin-wide Development 

As public money became available, large multipurpose reservoirs storing enough water for many uses became feasible. 

Dams were built on the lower and middle tributaries of rivers. 

Although the earlier reservoirs succeeded in making large

additional amounts of water available for irrigation, power generation, and other supplies, they did not prevent waterwasting floods.

A river's maximum usefulness was not realized until it was developed and controlled as a whole, from mouth to headwaters, including tributaries down to the smallest creek. Rainfall throughout the entire watershed had to be conserved by means of smaller check dams and ponds.

This watershed conception of water development in the United States has been advocated only within the past couple of decades. 

It still is in the process of development. The country cannot continue to develop without broad and carefully engineered plans for utilizing available water resources. 

Water Pollution 

The pollution of water supplies has accompanied U. S. urbanization and industrialization.

Pollution of water by sewage from private dwellings, public facilities, and by waste from factories, has reached such proportions that it is the No. 1 problem of water supply in the U. s. S.

Water pollution has reduced the amount of available usable water faster than new sources of supply could be developed to meet needs. In addition to endangering health through disease and toxicants, it is killing fish and aquatic life, and making recreation areas unfit for use. It has also increased the cost of industrial production to the point of hampering expansion. 

Industrial water must meet certain standards of quality, sometimes even higher than that for human consumption. Processing polluted water to remove impurities was industry's solution to the pollution problem, but an increasingly expensive one.

Water Purification 

Owing to increased pollution, purification of water for safe, human consumption and other municipal use grew increasingly more elaborate and costly. Water treatment at the point of consumption came into use as a necessary initial protective measure toward safeguarding public water supplies. It offered no substitute for the solution of the over-all pollution problem. 

Purification has limitations that would ultimately be overwhelmed by the amount of waste pouring into rivers and other bodies of water from cities and industry. This realization has emphasized the importance and the necessity for the prevention and reduction of pollution at its source.

Treatment of Sewage and Industrial Wastes 

The reduction of polluting waste at its source is the initial step in water purification. Therefore, more and more cities and industries have begun to treat sewage and factory wastes in order to lessen the amount of polluting substances discharged into water courses.

Regrettably, in the United States, construction of sewage and industrial waste treatment plants has not kept pace with the amount of polluting materials being added to streams, lakes and oceans. New kinds of pollutants being created by new technological development, including increasing use of agricultural pesticides, make the problem even more complex. Research on different and more efficient methods of treating waste water is being intensified to meet the national problem of water pollution.

Water Quality Management 

Pollution control is achieved by managing water quality. 

Water quality management ensures the usability of water. 

In developing water resources to meet continually increasing needs, United States attention was originally centered upon quantity only--the most water possible at lowest cost. Quality was assumed. The assumption was justified at the time. It was not realized how quickly municipal and industrial waste from an expanding economy would degrade water quality, impairing its usability. 

Preservation of water quality--and hence usability--has long been a statutory responsibility of public health authorities of the United States. Water quality management to control pollution and thus ensure fullest use of water resources is also a public health responsibility, executed in cooperation with all other water resource development agencies, State, regional and Federal. 

Water quality management has only recently been instituted in the United States. It is, therefore, still in the developmental and trial stage. But general principles have been worked out, are being applied--and are believed to be the soundest approach to the modern water problem facing the people of the United States. 

General Principles 

The primary goal of water quality management is the provision of water of adequate quality as well as quantity to fill all needs. The methods of achievement are principally by the reduction and prevention of water pollution at its source and by utilization or treatment of all wastes ordinarily discharged into water. The total quantity of water made available for use will be limited, of course, to the natural run-off potential of the watershed under development. This is dependent, however, on the developed storage capacity of the watershed.

Water The total quantity of water in effect can be increased by efficiency of use and reuse through management of quality. quality management programs in the United States are being instituted in watershed or river valley basins. In working out area requirements for water, degree and types of pollution present in the river system are being ascertained. 

Ranges of concentration of polluting material are being controlled by a combination of waste treatment and water dilution. Hydraulic and quality control criteria are also being determined. 

Appropriate operational methods and procedures to meet required standards of quality are recommended to cities and industries for most effective reduction of sewage and industrial wastes. 

Indiscriminate use of sewers to dispose of all waste matters are being discouraged. Also, locations for large waste outlets serving metropolitan and industrial centers are being studied with a view of the least possible damage to the stream, lake or ocean waters.

Quality Controls 

For efficient and effective water quality control, each area is being supplied with automatic sampling devices to monitor both the quality and quantity of water. Electronic telemetering instruments are placed strategically below waste discharge outlets and other critical points for the continuous recording of water conditions. The data goes to a master control center where corrective measures are automatically worked out by computing equipment.

When water fails to meet a pre-set standard, its deficiency in quality or quantity will be met by additional releases of high quality water from upstream reservoirs, as an emergency measure.

Advance planning of needs is expected to help reduce such

emergency measures.

However, continuous releases of high quality water from storage for dilution of water of unacceptable quality is economically undesirable.

Underground Reserve 

The large underground reserves of water in the United States are being included in assessing total water resources for water quality management. These reserves are not separate or distinct from surface water, which in actuality is merely that portion of ground water which appears on the surface, plus precipitation not yet soaked into the earth.

Pollution that contaminates surface water can also contaminate underground resources. Water supply both above and below the surface must be maintained in quantity and quality by controlling pollution while developing new sources of both. 

The over-all quantity of water precipitated in any watershed is relatively constant over a period of years. The underground supply also is stable unless overdrawn. The variable factor in the water resource situation, therefore, is pollution.

Since extent and comprehensiveness of each area management program will be shaped by local financial participation, water quality management for the area is designed for application to parts as well as to the whole. 

Extent of the program is governed in a large degree by local attitudes. A progressive area is usually far-sighted enough to sacrifice immediate returns for larger and more lasting benefits in the future.

Although every water quality management program should have as its aim full use of all water resources in the area, programs are developed in intermediate stages. Readiness of people in the area to share the cost of the program has great influence on its consummation. Their financial participation is an essential ingredient of every program.

Initially the area management plan needs to be flexible and capable of modification to meet changing conditions. But it should be detailed enough to guide the program toward the ultimate objective of enough water of constant good quality for all the demands of area development. 

If demographic development exceeds the limits of sustenance (as determined by water supply or pollution control capabilities), scientific research must produce breakthroughs in quality control techniques such as economic desalinization of sea water in coastal areas. The undesirable alternative is lower quality water, with curtailment of recreation, destruction of fish and wildlife habitat and even other more serious aspects such as a smaller health safety margin.

Maintenance of the quality of water supply is an important factor in determining the country's future standard of living because quality will govern the quantity available for economic progress.

Insect Disease Carriers 

Comprehensive area planning for water resource development and water quality control must not overlook design provisions for environmental health. Aside from the basic bacterial and chemical threat to human life in polluted water, a very important aspect of water development is the construction and operation of water facilities so as to avoid spread of insect-borne diseases. 

Experience in the United States has demonstrated that increased irrigation development has increased certain mosquitoborne diseases when not properly guarded against in program development.

Construction plans for irrigation developments should therefore include measures for prevention and elimination of mosquito breeding sources, and also provide for water level fluctuation to prevent mosquito breeding.

Similar planning and foresight should also be applied to other water resource developments. They, too, are potential mosquito breeders.

Irrigation canals and ditches and flooded land present the most serious insect vector problem, however. Major weaknesses of irrigation systems consist of incomplete project drainage, inadequate disposal of irrigation waste water on individual irrigation farms, and leakage or seepage from canals and laterals.

The basic philosophy of insect-borne disease control rests on preventive or source reduction measures, with insecticides used only as interim, supplementary or emergency measures.

Reservoirs and Weeds

If properly planned and constructed, reservoirs with their clear deep water usually present no mosquito breeding problem. 

However, the possibility that reservoirs may increase mosquitoborne diseases other than malaria should be recognized. 

In addition to clearing reservoir sites, eliminating shallows by deepening or filling, draining ponded and marshy areas in the zone of drawdown, removing drift and flotage, and providing cyclical fluctuation of reservoir water level during breeding season will help keep mosquitoes and other insects in check.

It has been found that reservoirs and ponds kept free of vegetation and flotage ordinarily do not breed mosquitoes. 

Larvicides are recommended only for emergency use, and not as a substitute for sanitary design and operation. 

Summary and Conclusions 

The public health aspects of water resource development consist of "built-in" safeguards and operational methods for the prevention of water-borne and water-bred diseases. Pollution control practices are aimed at the reduction and elimination of contaminants from water courses. Snail and insect control measures in many areas, are equally important. Through water quality management the United States is endeavoring to ensure maximum usability of its water resources. 

Engineers and public administrators charged with water resource planning and management, through neglect can be responsible for spreading illness and death. Such neglect can be very costly. On the other hand, by comprehensive planning and operation designed to prevent disease, they can be instrumental in improving the health of the people.
