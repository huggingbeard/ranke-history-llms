SOME ASPECTS OF PAROLE AS A PROFESSION* 

99 There was a time when one could come into the state of New York and discuss parole with equanimity. Indeed, he might do so with the idea that he knew something about the subject and, if he were very conceited, with the idea of being a teacher. Doubtless there were those who regarded New York as a pupil in matters of parole and perhaps not a very apt pupil at that. You all remember that it was parole in New York that was once described as an "under-financed moral gesture. Times have emphatically changed. Not only have you an entirely new organization and set-up, but you have a little money to spend, and you have placed brains in charge of your parole work. No longer is it possible to come here with the idea of being a teacher. There is only one wise state of mind in which to enter this state today-and that is the healthy and humble condition of the learner. It is hardly too much, I think, to say that, in respect to parole, the eyes of the civilized world are upon New York. Of course we in New Jersey shall continue to do our best to divert the eyes of the world to us --and to allow Trenton to be the capital of the parole world, instead of Albany. But as I inspect the parole system of New York, I am not over-confident of success. Anyhow, so far as I am concerned, I feel very much this afternoon as Hector must have felt when he faced Achilles, or as some of Napoleon's victims must have felt when they faced the little Corsican in action. I shall make my small contribution and go away happy if I have not disgraced myself.

Parole is coming into its own. It is impressive, I think, that here at a state conference of social work such intensive consideration is being given to the subject of parole. I had the same feeling' a few weeks ago at the annual congress of the American Prison Association in Baltimore. It struck me then that if one wished to make a sharp contrast he might point out that there was a meeting of the American Prison Association, and yet that a great deal of time was given to the discussion of probation and parole. Possibly there is deep significance in penology in this. When a person is on probation, he is not in a prison or institution; when he is on parole, he is not in a prison or institution. Is it possible that there is in this country today a tendency toward extra-mural, as contrasted with intra-mural, treatment of the offender? This would be interesting if true.

We know that in Massachusetts the number of persons on probation greatly exceeds the number in institutions, the figures being well over twenty thousand on probation and approximately six thousand in institutions. We know that in 1927 in New York the number on probation was over twenty-three thousand and the number in institutions about 18,000. on the increase. Parole, too, shows no tendency to suffer in public confidence. On the contrary, it has become the commonest form of release from an institution. By that I mean that more people leave institutions by parole, by the process of being paroled, than by any other method. In 1927, according to reports of the United 

States Census Bureau, 49 per cent of all those released from state prisons and reformatories were paroled; substantially one-half. I would not over-emphasize the point, but it is possible that we are face to face in this country today with a silent revolution, with the gradual abandonment-or at any rate with the constantly diminishing importance-of institutional treatment for crime. We know that England is passing through such a revolution, and it may be that the United States is following suit.

What Is the Parole Officer's Work? 

I am going to attempt a characterization of parole just for fun -and then I am going to make some remarks about the nature of parole, about some aspects of it as a profession. Parole is a period of adjustment, under supervision, from the exceptional and artificial life of an institution to normal life in society or the community. It is a period of adjustment or transition. It is not merely a method of release from prison. It is not clemency. It is not shortening an offender's term. It is a continuation of the process of treatment. It is an extension of custodial responsibility, and the offender on parole is still a ward of the state. He is still undergoing treatment. He is still subject to control, and it is the function of the parole officer to substitute the discipline, education and watchfulness available in community life for the discipline and treatment available in an institution. The parolee may be brought back to the institution for violations. Theoretically, at least, parole presents these two aspects: it is supervised adjustment from one thing to another, and it is a continuation of treatment. 

If this be true, what is the nature of this supervision and treatment-and what is the parole officer's work? This afternoon I wish to present to you a conception of the parole officer's work, a conception in which I believe, and which I present to you as a statement of an end, a goal, an ideal if you please, and not primarily as a description of what parole officers are actually doing today. It never hurts, I think, to define our conceptions and to trace, if we can, the direction in which we are going. I wish to discuss with you the conception that the parole officer is, or ought to be, engaged in social case work. 

To those of you who are in any way associated with parole work, I would say that there is nothing novel about that. Case work, as you all know, is simply the careful examination and study of the difficulties of an individual, and then the manipulation, marshalling and use of all the remedial, educative and therapeutic agencies and measures necessary to remove those difficulties. That is all it is. It sounds simple. And yet how many parole officers are really attempting the methods of social case work today!

Let us look at a few parole officers. The first, perhaps, is the policeman, the type of parole officer who is really a policeman. 

He has the policeman's view of his duties. Earlier he was perhaps a prison guard; he may have been a member of the uniformed police force; he may have been a detective. Or he may have been a butcher, a grocer, a miner, a street car conductor, a prize fighter. 

These are all excellent people, but their experiences do not commonly qualify them for the careful duties of a parole officer. 

A second type of parole officer may, I think, accurately be described as the kidder, the officer whose attitude toward his parolee is: 

"Hello, kid! Put her there! How're things going? Been in any trouble lately? Say, you and I are going to see you through this little pickle, aren't we? Don't forget my instructions. Don't forget what I told you! You know I'm your friend. You call me your parole officer, but"-here with a dig in the parolee's ribs. "y know you've left the institution now and y'know on the outside we don't look at you the same way. We want to help you make good—bring your troubles to me; bring all your little troubles to me.

At this point the officer leans forward and perhaps lowers his voice. "I know you were out till one o'clock night before last.

Yes, yes, I know it. Don't ask me how I know it. Say, skirts are bad things for young fellows like you. Better leave skirts alone.

Let's see' -the parole officer glances at a card of information "was it a skirt that got you into trouble the first time? No, it wasn't a skirt. Well, skirts are dangerous; better get home early, then your folks'll be satisfied and I'll know you're living up to the conditions of your parole. You know I can take you back to that institution; any little misstep, boy, and back you go! How'd you like that? Wouldn't like it, eh? No, I know you wouldn't. Well, you know my address. Send me your reports, and-say-here's my card; you probably lost the last one. My telephone number's 

Cadwalader KKKK; if you get into any trouble, let me know.”

This colloquy or monologue-is probably held out of the window of the car, or at the front door-or across the street from where the parolee works. It is repeated again next month, or two months later. It is evidence, I think, of two things: first, that the parole officer is not engaged in anything approaching social case work, and secondly that his case load is probably so heavy that he does not have time to learn much about his job. 

A third type of parole officer, man or woman, might be called the routineer, usually very conscientious. He regulates his official duties by routine. His paper work is excellent. He knows just what his superiors expect of him and he does it. To him a parolee is part of his day's work-not a human being to be adjusted. He is like a bowler, gazing at ten pins at the other end of the alley. 

I would not be too hard on him, but I think he has about as much sense of the differences among his parolees as a bowler has of the

differences among the pins at which he casts his ball. Reports sent in by him are prompt and neat. Fundamentals are outside the scope of his understanding, and he possesses neither inventiveness nor initiative. He does not grasp the importance of causative factors in the difficulties of his parolees. He goes through his job on the theory that it is merely a grind which brings him his pay. 

I could describe other types of parole officers. There is the efficient parole officer, who knows his job and does it very well. 

He, too, is probably handicapped by an excessive case load, but he makes the best of that monumental trouble. He knows that it is important to have a great deal of information about his parolees, and to know what really are the causes of their difficulties. He tries to ascertain these, and if he fails, he goes ahead and establishes the best adjustment for his charge that he can. If the family in which the parolee is living is wholly unsatisfactory, he recommends a change to another family. He has some insight into the personalities of those in his hands, and he tries to make use of this in his treatment of them and his contacts with them. He is conscientious in trying to find jobs for them, or in spurring them to the effort to find work. If philanthropic help is needed, he goes after it, and tries to induce the proper agency to furnish the assistance called for. He makes use of other community agencies, such as mental hygiene clinics, boys' clubs, Y.M.C.A.'s, recreation centers and the like. He maintains as close contact with his parolees as he canand unites in his handling of them the proper mixture of firmness and assistance.

In short he is, if not in name, at least very largely in fact, a case worker.

Parole Is Social Case Work 

To trace the history of social case work before this audience would be, in me, not only needless but impertinent. I am aware that it is customary to go very far back-to seek for origins in remote days. I know that it is quite common to turn to early scriptural writings--to the Koran, to the Talmud, to the Bible, to the writings. of early Christian Fathers, to St. Paul and then, coming down through the centuries, to St. Francis of Assissi, in order to find the origins of a scientific approach to the problems of the individual unfortunate. I doubt if the careful historian can justify such attempts. My guess is that these early forerunners of modern charity did not have the scientific approach. My guess is that they were humanely motivated dispensers of alms and that that was about all. 66 Probably it is impossible to fix upon any date as the beginning of modern social case work. We are told that that intrepid and spirited English minister, Thomas Chalmers, first used the phrase a thorough investigation" in 1823; whether that is as good a date as any I do not know. Mr. Chalmers was talking of the charitable relief of poor people, and he thought it was wise to know something about the dependents, or the charges, or the clients, whom one

sought to assist, in order that one's assistance might be useful and permanent. But, forgetting dates, we all know something of the subsequent development of case work. We know that it consists of a careful examination and study of the needs of a person requiring assistance, the outlining of a plan of treatment based on such study and diagnosis, and the giving effect to that plan. We know that it has been most widely and expertly organized in the assistance of those who come for help to family welfare societies or charity organization societies. We know, however, that it has not stopped there; it has spread to other fields. We know that the hospital and the medical folk have taken it over, and that today medical social case work is a branch of social case work. It has been recently estimated that more than 500 hospitals and clinics in the United States have social service departments, one of the purposes of which is to study home conditions, to report upon their part in causing or prolonging disease, and to modify untoward conditions standing between the patient and recovery. We know that it has spread to the treatment of persons suffering from behavior or mental troubles-and that a branch of social case work today is psychiatric social case work. We know that the methods and pursuits of the social case worker have invaded the child guidance clinic-and that the physician, or psychiatrist, who undertakes to minister to the conduct difficulties of youngsters employs social workers and takes the whole family into consideration. We know that the visiting teacher is a school social case worker-and that problems of maladjustment and discipline in school, formerly solved by the rod and the dunce's cap, are now solved by the ministrations of the social case worker.

One after another, the professions dealing with the trials, the tribulations, the difficulties, the maladjustments, of individuals have yielded to the wisdom and the practice of the social case worker. 

Even the handling of offenders has so yielded. Probation is generally recognized today, where it is recognized at all, to be social case work. Case work, the treatment of the problems of an individual as if he were an individual, is even attempted in some penal institutions. We know, if we are honest and if we study the problem carefully, that the last field to be reached by the social worker, the last stronghold against the obligations of individual study and treatment, the fortified castle against the procedure, the practices and the point of view of competent case work is the field of parole. Now, of course, in a case work conception of parole, it makes no difference whether the person on parole is young or old, whether he is fifteen years of age or thirty, whether he is male or female, whether he is an old offender or a beginner in crime. The fundamental necessity is to know something of his difficulties and to do what you can to restore him to a happy relationship to the world around him and to a life in which there is both achievement and

conformity to law. But because adults on parole received the majoi consideration last year, let us for a moment look at youngsters on parole.

The Adolescent As a Parolee 

By youngsters I mean people in the stage of adolescence. Undoubtedly there are parole officers who are more at home in handling adolescence than in handling maturity. Obviously it is important that they should know something of adolescence-and obviously, as their experience increases, they will become more and more familiar with the peculiar ways and the peculiar psychology of boys and girls who are adolescent. 

There is no time here to discuss adolescence, but I should like to point out one sharp characteristic of adolescence, which I think parole officers, along with all others who deal with people of that age, ought to keep very clearly in mind. That is that adolescence is emphatically the age of non-conformity. It is the age of lawviolation. It is the age when people, almost naturally, it might be said, are on parole. That is, it is the age when they reject usual standards, when they break away from the family, when they throw off the authority of parents, when they resent interference and control-and when they launch out for themselves. Customs are things to be examined and discarded. Authority has lost something of its sacred character-and immaculateness. The world is a rather fresh discovery at adolescence, due both to biological and psychological changes and he is the enemy, and not the friend, of adolescence who does not know this. Adolescent boys and girls are ambitious, curious, full of questions, none too happy and certainly none too stable, hungry for experience and wondering what in the world life is about. To them law-whether the law of legislators or of custom-is a straitjacket and not a means of life. Later we learn to live within the law; adolescents do not know this. They are on a voyage of discovery-and grown-ups are bars and reefs in their path. All this has little to do with the intellectual acumen, or the intelligence quotient, of the particular individual; he cannot escape the inevitable conditions of his particular age or state of life.

Now the significance of this, to the parole officer, is, I suppose, clear. He cannot deal with adolescents as if they were three-yearold children or thirty-year-old men and women. Not only must he know their nature-that goes without saying-but he must be prepared for shocks. He must apply to them-primarily—the standards which they either accept or can be made to understand, and not the standards which, ten years or twenty years hence, they will perhaps be able to put into their lives. 

Frankwood Williams, speaking once to parents, said that if your 17-year-old son spent night after night in the library until 10 o'clock, reading, and showed no interest in girls, you might well look out for trouble. He went on to remark that if the son came home some morning at one o'clock, with the odor of alcohol upon

his breath and a broken collar-bone-all as the result of being on a joy-ride with a girl, you might feel much more comfortable and might conclude that probably he was learning some of the things that boys at that age ought to learn.

Particularly important, perhaps, at the age of adolescence, are questions of recreation, of clothes, a sense of being wanted and of engaging in some constructive activity, having a buddy or confidante, the development of a healthy and active interest in the other sex, and freedom to experiment a little in the extraordinary situations that life puts up to you at that important age. Perhaps there can be further discussion of these at a round table tomorrow. 

Differences Between Parole Case Work and Other Fields of Social Case Work 

Now, after this digression on youthful offenders, I should like to return to the thread of my argument and make a few more remarks about the case work aspects of parole. What are the peculiarities of parole case work? Does it have any peculiarities? Does it have any particular problems? In what ways, if any, is it different from other case work?

Naturally, I am not going to undertake an exhaustive answer to that question-for one excellent reason: I don't think I could if

I were to try. We have all read, I suppose, the job-analyses of case work in three fields recently published by Louise Odencrantzthe fields being family social work, medical social work and psychiatric social work. There is, in process of preparation now, as I understand it, a series of job-analyses of case-workers in the field of penology-the case-work of the probation officer, the case-work of the corrective or penal institution, the case-work of the parole officer, and so forth. If this volume were now on the market, I should have no excuse for the remarks I am about to make. But since it has not yet been published, I am free to exhibit my amateur ideas so long as you will listen. When the volume is finally out, I suppose I shall be silenced.

Some of the peculiarities of parole supervision or case-work are obvious so obvious that we often overlook them. In the first place, a parole officer doesn't pick his charges or parolees; he has them handed to him. It is no more in the power of the parole officer to refuse to accept an offender handed to him for supervision than it is in the power of the earth to refuse to accept the rays of the sun or the rain dropping from the clouds. The institution may detain a man for a longer or a shorter period-or it may hold him until the expiration of his maximum sentence. But once the offender is paroled, the parole officer takes him whether he wishes to or not. And so the first conclusion is that his intake of cases is, for the parole officer, fixed and controlled; he can exercise neither choice nor discretion.

A second obvious characteristic of the parole officer's work is that he is handling very difficult cases. I do not know whether the parole officer may justly claim that, as compared with other workers with individuals, he is handling the most difficult cases in the world

or not. He is handling the end results of institutional treatment; he is handling those whose careers of misconduct have made considerable advance. In this respect he might claim that his cases are more difficult than those of the probation officer. Whether he can claim that his cases present more baffling and intricate problems than the problems presented by persons leaving hospitals for mental diseases I do not know. Certainly he can claim that his cases are among the most difficult in the world. 

A third obvious characteristic of the parole officer is that he is a legal officer. Here he resembles the probation officer but differs from many other case workers. Duties, responsibilities and perhaps even prohibitions are placed upon him that do not rest upon all other workers with individuals. In some instances his duties are defined in the law. In some instances he is not free to take action that other case workers could take. If his charges begin to fail and slip, he sometimes has no alternative but to return them to the institution as parole violators, whereas fellow-practitioners of the art of case work might avoid any one, set measure of that kind and re-double their efforts, finding a variety of measures to meet the emergency that has arisen.

One of the questions agitating case workers in almost all fields is: Who is the client? For whom are we responsible? Are we concerned only with the first person coming to our attention, or does our responsibility go farther? Does it extend to the family?

I do not see how the parole officer can escape asking himself that question. Does his responsibility end with the offender placed on parole, walking out of the institution doors? Is he to confine his attention to the parolee himself, or is he to take into account those whose lives are closely associated with the parolee, who act and react upon him, and who are undoubtedly acted upon by him. 

We know that modern family welfare societies regard the entire family as the client-not merely the member of that family who happens first to come to their attention. The difficulties of that particular individual may be merely a symptom of deep-seated family difficulties, and so long as these deep-seated troubles continue the difficulties of the individual may never end. The family welfare society therefore aims to see the family-and to see it whole. 

Suppose a parole officer has on his hands a parolee whose father is either suspected of insanity or is pretty clearly insane. Suppose he thinks the parolee will never make a satisfactory or law-abiding adjustment until some provision is made for the welfare of that father. The parole officer might easily, of course, reach that conclusion. Must he keep his hands off the situation of the father, or does his responsibility for the parolee, his responsibility to society, require him to undertake some solution of the father's situation? 

Suppose a parolee is a young married man who has two children. 

Suppose one of the children is so feeble-minded as to be almost clearly an imbecile, and that this fact is driving the father and

mother frantic with worry. The child is living in the house and if he were placed in an institution, so that the daily sight of him would be removed from the father, the inexpressible tension of the father's life might be relieved. Is it the job of the parole officer to attend to that situation?

Suppose a parolee has very difficult relations with his wife. Suppose a discussion of separation or divorce would obviously relieve the parolee's mind-and might even be good for the wife. Is it permitted the parole officer to take cognizance of that situation? 

Suppose a whole family is so racked by trouble, disease and unemployment that all of the members are in a bad fix and none of them can function up to the real level of his ability. Suppose the parole officer realizes that he cannot expect decent adjustment by his parolee until the family difficulties are modified, if not removed. 

Is it to be considered as part of his job to call upon the welfare and social agencies of the community to help straighten that family out? Or must he say, No, only the parolee is my responsibility?

These questions, I think, cannot be avoided. For myself I offer the following formulation: The client of the parole officer is the family. In so far as may be necessary to bring about the proper adjustment of the parolee, and to effect a law-abiding life, the duties and obligations of the parole officer extend to the family situation. He is no more justified in overlooking those than he is in overlooking any other factor in the parolee's more personal existence which may be causing difficulty. Indeed, as a conscientious social case worker he is perhaps even bound to take cognizance of removable maladjustments in the family even if the connection of these with the parolee's misconduct is not strikingly clear. 

And now, in conclusion, a word of caution. As I said at the beginning, I have no notion that these ideas are novel in New York. 

In some jurisdictions, however, I think they would be revolutionary. 

Neither would I for a moment suggest that all parole officers can do all of this at the present time. Generally speaking, I am sure, they are too heavily worked to undertake it. I know, too, that not a great many parole officers are adequately trained for so large a task. But I do not know how parole work is to be improved unless we undertake some analysis of what it might be, and unless our notion of what can be done is always just a little ahead of what we really do in practice.
