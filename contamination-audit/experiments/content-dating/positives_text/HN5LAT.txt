"Good morning, Betty!" Silence. "I say, good morning, Betty!" (Sternly.) 

More silence tense silence. "Why don't you speak to me?" demanded an authoritative voice.

Betty lifted swollen eyes to her stepfather's face and spoke, slowly: "I would rather not talk with you this morning." "And why not?" His voice was more gruff and authoritative than ever.

Again Betty was silent. Then, as if seized with a paroxysm of grief, she turned her face away and, shaking with sobs, fled out of the dining-room and up the stairs, where she buried her face in her pillow, already wet with the tears of her sleepless night.

Never before in all her life had Betty Dreanard felt such awful anguish of spirit as that which now seemed to have destroyed forever her happiness. And never before had she had such cause for sorrow. 

Happy childhood days had come and gone, quickly lengthening into weeks, months, and years. High school days had come too, bringing with them all their thrills and friendships. 

Then one day when the sunshine of girlhood happiness seemed brightest of all, there appeared on the horizon a

dark cloud. Before long other clouds appeared, and their lowering countenances bespoke the certainty of an oncoming storm, a storm which threatened to leave in its wake a dark, dreary sky in which that sun might never shine again. 

With anxious eyes, Betty had watched and waited. 

How could her mother, her own precious mother, be so blind as to marry that man! His face was so cruel; the lines about his mouth were so hard and cynical; the eyes were so greedy, speaking such awful things, things which revealed the depravity of the man, of his life, of his thoughts. But this much she did know: she loved him dearly. Her one fond remembrance of him was of the times when he had romped and played with her and then had lifted her high on his strong shoulders and carried her into the house.

But the storm had come. Their new house on Michigan Avenue was perfect. So was the smooth green lawn that rolled down to the boulevard on which cars raced continuously. But it had not been home. Mother had been there, but she was not the same mother. Deep lines of sorrow soon appeared on her sweet face. An hour had passed after her flight up the stairs, before Betty rose from her bed. She turned to the mirror. Yes, it was the same sorrow-torn face, only now it shone with a strange, new determination. Even in those eyes of suffering something showed her capacity for laughter and love.

She must not give up, even if Mother were gone. Dark clouds could not always hide the sun. One finds happiness not by waiting for it. Betty felt sure that by searching she must find it.

Betty bravely wiped away her tears, and as she did so her eyes fell upon the key still on the floor. She stooped to pick it up and with new courage called out to her stepfather: "You know I haven't had any breakfast. If you'll just be patient and wait long enough, perhaps the door will open." Then as an afterthought, "I know where Molly keeps things. I've always liked to get my own breakfast, you know. Don't worry about my running away this morning." With that she began to stir about as if she were preparing a meal. 

Molly looked at her, first with wondering, and then with understanding eyes. How long Jaird might be deceived, they did not know, but it would give Betty ample opportunity to get away at least.

CHAPTER III 

Tim Bentley was as inquisitive as he was polite. No sooner had Betty disappeared into the cab, than he began. to wonder who she was and where she was going in such evident distress of mind. These led naturally to other questions: Why was she in such haste, and where had she come from? He recalled that she had appeared quite suddenly. He had never seen her before, but this was only natural, for he had never sold papers on this street before. In fact, while he knew nearly every section of the city with as much familiarity as a country boy knows every nook and corner of the farm and woods near his home, this was the part with which he was least acquainted.

His experienced eye measured the entire length of the block in either direction, then came to focus on the alleyway directly before him. "Um hm!" he said to himself. "I bet she came from down there, and if she did, I'm going to find out why. A girl wouldn't be running away without a mighty good reason. No sir! Here's a good chance for a detective."

Leaving his papers with a fellow newsboy and chum, he made his way, boy-fashion, down the alley, whistling as he went, keeping his eyes wide open for anything that might look like a "clue." Clues, as he put it, were his "long suit" and some day he was going to be a detective, yes sir! "I knew I'd find something down here," he exulted to himself as he stooped to pick up a white monogrammed handkerchief, evidently feminine. "Now I'll get her

initials. I bet it's hers all right, 'cause a handkerchief wouldn't stay clean long in this alley, no sir!" He took a long whiff of the fragrant perfume with which the dainty little piece of linen was freshly redolent. "And that's another reason why this handkerchief was dropped here only a few minutes ago," he assured himself. "B.D.! Hm! - don't know anybody with those initials, I am sure. But I bet it's hers anyway." Tim carefully tucked the "clue" into his inside coat pocket and went shuffling along, still whistling, as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world for him to be trudging down that alley. Already a plan was beginning to form in his mind. as to how he would find out from Gerald where she had gone, and what would happen when he should walk up to her and say, casual-like: "Pardon me, but did you lose this beautiful handkerchief?" Then she'd give him another of those smiles of approval which she'd given him this morning, just as she was whirled away in Gerald's taxi. And then she'd say: "Oh thank you, Tim, it was so thoughtful of you to bring it to me." Unconsciously, Tim's chest swelled with pride and he shoved his hands down deeper into his pockets and trudged happily on till he came to Michigan Avenue.

He turned to look at the row of great, beautiful houses. 

Selecting one of them, he made his way up the walk to the front steps with his usual nonchalant air. He was looking for a name on the door which began with "D." What to do after that he hadn't quite decided, but he could get the number of the house anyway and well, perhaps he could figure out something later. In less time than it takes to tell it he was standing on the bottom step of residence number 732 and reading the name on the door. "Jaird Barloman! Hm!" Tim grunted. "So 'at guy lives here! He's the kind of a guy she might be running away from."

"732!" He repeated it to himself several times so as to be sure of keeping it in his memory until he could reach a place of seclusion and write it down in his "clue" book. 

He was turning to leave when the postman came up. "Here you are, my boy," he said, pleasantly. "Only one letter this morning." And before Tim could even say, "Thank you, Mister," he had handed him a long, official looking envelope and was on his way down the walk. 

For a minute Tim was nonplussed. He cast a hasty glance at the name and read: "Miss Elizabeth Dreanard." 

The letter was postmarked "New York." "Oh shucks!" he said to himself. "E.D., that don't fit!" Then like a flash there came to him the thought which he almost expressed aloud: "E.D., it's hers! It's hers! Her name is Elizabeth and they call her Betty for short! Well, Betty don't live here any more, I happen to know, I'll see if I can deliver this to her in person."

A moment later he was whistling along cheerfully, with the letter tucked away in the same coat pocket as the other "clue."

He had been gone only a few moments when a boy rode up on a bicycle with a telegram. He rang the bell several times before the door opened. "A telegram for 

Mr. Jaird Barloman," he announced. Jaird had answered the door in person, and the moment he heard his name he growled: "Here, give me that!" "Sign here," the boy said, handing him a pencil.

As soon as the boy was gone, Jaird tore open the envelope and read hastily, a peculiar expression coming over his face:

With his jaw set firmly he entered the hall door and passed into the library where he paused to set himself for the task before him. Jaird Barloman was one who could blend the two in perfect harmony. 

Saturday night at eleven! That gave him thirty-seven hours! Thirty-seven hours in which to play the diplomacy game, get the letter in his possession, and arrive at the

place agreed upon. And that meant - immediate action. 

And immediate action meant an interview with Betty, voluntary or otherwise.

He stepped quickly down the hall to the dining room and paused at the door to the kitchen and listened. "She seems to be quite contented," he mused. The sounds which came to his ears were those which might have been made by anyone familiar with the culinary art. 

Molly with a grim countenance kept stirring about. In order to make it sound as if she were really working she began to do that very thing. Betty had always liked waffles and so she set about to mix the batter and to get the iron ready. When, after a few minutes, their delightful aroma permeated the room, she knew Jaird would be sure to catch their fragrance. 

But Jaird could not wait any longer. "Oh, Betty," he called, forcing a pleasant tone through the cruel lips. "Your waffles smell delicious. Perhaps you wouldn't mind if I breakfasted with you." The words were no sooner uttered than he realized their folly, for Betty knew that he had already eaten. There was no answer: only the continuous stirring about of foot steps which seemed to mock him. "Oh, I say, Betty," he tried once again. "There was a letter for you." That would be a good way to let her know that he was aware of her having received the letter from New York.

But in response to his words there was only the continuous moving about on the other side of the door and the rattle of silverware as though Betty were eating.

This was maddening. Jaird Barloman was accustomed to having men bow to his every request. To think that he should be standing outside the door of his own kitchen and begging entrance! He paused to reflect on the ridiculousness of the situation. Jaird Barloman, the ruler of

men! Jaird Barloman, before whom men of affluence and social position bowed in deference, and into whose coffers many of them poured their cash in order to be kept from being crushed by a single grip of his iron hand! Jaird

Barloman, whose moves weaker men obeyed as if drawn by a magnet!

Jaird was a man of decision. Problems which involved the expenditure of thousands or the crushing of a life were matters of common occurrence, and his decisions were instant and final. But dealing with Betty was different. 

His plan to marry her — and, as he admitted to himself, there was nothing he wanted to do more than that very thing put him in a place where his mighty power was not so mighty. He could never rule by force in the realm of love. This he began to realize with a sense of humiliation that stung him deeply. But he must have that letter. Its contents could demolish his plans with as much ease as a child could knock down a house of blocks. It was plain to him that she would not open the door of her own free will, and that to force his entrance would be utter folly. He stooped down to peer into the keyhole. 

He had heard the key fall to the floor. Perhaps Betty had not again inserted it into the lock. If not, then with his master key he could gain entrance. With Molly gone, nothing would be more pleasant than a few hours of tete-tete with his beautiful stepdaughter - ahem — his future wife! His fingers closed over the skeleton key which he always carried with him. With a malicious grin he inserted it into the lock. It worked! He seized the knob, turned it, and with an expression of triumph on his face, opened the door and stepped inside.

CHAPTER IV 

Molly had heard the door-bell ring, but she had not heard his footsteps as he had returned to his vigil. She was taken completely by surprise, but she never allowed emergencies to sweep her off her feet.

She rose from her chair where she had been seated at a table in the far corner of the room and where she could not have been seen by him when he looked through the keyhole a few moments ago. "And what kin I be doin' fer you?" she asked, assuming a tone of politeness.

Even though she vowed loyalty to Betty, it was not her purpose deliberately to antagonize him, for her pay check was liberal and came regularly. 

Jaird stopped, bewildered. Then because he was in no mood for interference and because he was thoroughly annoyed at her unexpected presence, he growled: "You can do nothing for me except to leave the house at once! When I give you a day off, I expect you to take it! Either that or take the rest of your life off!" His voice had grown more calm as he spoke, yet each word came out with a snap that meant authority and power. 

Molly's reply was characteristic of her nature, outwardly calm with a slight betrayal of inner unrest. She addressed him coolly: "I'm accustomed to being spoken to ez if I was a lady, Mr. Barloman. My name is O'Hagen, Molly O'Hagen, and I am a human being with a sound mind, a woman's soul and-" she raised herself to her full height and rested the back of her hands on her ample hips - "I am not inclined to physical infairmity!"

Jaird scowled. This woman did not fear him and was

likely to annoy him further. She had been in his employ only two months and she had not shown him the deference his wealth demanded nor the veneration his power deserved. Her fondness for Betty and her mother had disturbed him to no little degree and there had been times when he feared she knew too much. She was altogether too keen of intellect to suit his purpose. If he could be sure of her loyalty but that, he was becoming more aware daily, was an improbability. And such improbabilities, to him, were liabilities. Confound this woman! he must send her out at once. His eyes circled the room once more. Betty was nowhere to be seen. "Where's Betty?" he demanded. "Betty?" Molly countered. "And what would she be doin' in the kitchen, I'd like to know?" "Mrs. O'Hagen!" Jaird's voice snapped and his eyes flashed. He stepped toward her. "Tell me where she is; 

I'm in no mood to be trifled with!"

His eyes fell on the closed door of the pantry and he started forward. 

But Molly was there ahead of him. She would play the game for Betty's sake, do anything to gain more time.

She would pretend that Betty was in there. With her back firmly planted against the door, her chin lifted high, and with eyes that never flinched, she stared at him — an unwavering, formidable stare. 

For a moment they stood there, each measuring the other with eyes of scorn and aversion - he, disdaining to be thwarted by a woman; and she, in a mood that made her impregnable to all opposition of either man or beast. 

And just which the creature before her represented, she was not certain!

Two persons cannot face each other with such furious. gaze for long. One or the other must yield, or else nerves and muscles will catch fire from clashing minds and there

will be physical encounter. Jaird's temper was rising higher every moment. But Molly's had already risen; she struggled to control the surging force of her wrath. 

For weeks she had noticed Jaird's cruelty to Betty's mother and the leering, sensuous glances in Betty's direction. And Molly O'Hagen had not learned that "Vengeance is mine, saith the Lord." It might be His, she argued, but some of it was also hers. "Molly O'Hagen!" Jaird spoke more calmly than ever, though his face was a livid white. "Step aside - now! 

I am going to open that door!" 

Molly did not move, but her eyes flashed fire. He had drawn a revolver and she found herself staring into its cold, threatening muzzle. "And what if I don't?" she asked, still standing unwaveringly before him. 

"If you don't," he spoke slowly, icily, and raised the revolver, pointing it directly at her "if you don't open that door, I shall open it myself and walk in. Either past you or over you! I am going to count three—” "Ye are goin' to do nothin' of the sort!" Molly returned, firmly. A flash of ingenuity stirred her to instant action. 

Her hand found the door knob behind her, and before

Jaird could sense her purpose, she had opened the door and stepped to one side. With one powerful sweep of her right arm, she caught him on the jaw and sent him staggering back. There was a roar as the gun went off harmlessly, Molly pounced upon him, lifted him bodily and hurled him through the pantry doorway. "And I hope ye enjoy your stay!" she cried. Her voice was calm although every nerve was trembling. With a thrill of delight she slammed the door, locked it and gleefully piled the table and every chair in the kitchen against it. Then with a grim and satisfied countenance, she picked up the revolver from the floor where it had fallen in the encounter. 

And for no other reason than that people do strange

things in moments of extreme mental strain, she took a piece of white wrapping twine and tied the gun to the pantry door knob. "I reckon he'll stay there an hour or two," she murmured to herself. With that she made her way to the dining-room and up the stairs. "Poor Betty, it's a sin and a shame that she had to leave this beautiful house. But she won't have much to fear from now on, I'm athinkin'." She paused at the door of Betty's room. A glance inside showed her that the bed had been left unmade, an unusual thing for Betty. "The sweet darlin'," Molly breathed as she glanced lovingly about the room. "Molly O'Hagen'll miss ye like iverything and if I don't find out where ye've gone and still be a friend to ye, it won't be because I didn't try." 

She turned to leave, but as an afterthought decided to make up the bed. It would give her a little satisfaction to do that. Then she would quickly pack her own few belongings and leave the house, since she could no longer stay in Jaird's employ.

When Molly was completing the arranging of the bed and was putting the pillow in its place, she drew back her hand. A pillow wet with tears! That meant deep suffering! She knew Betty well enough to know that each tear would come forth against her will. As she stood there the vision of the crumpled pillow faded dimly, while noble tears slipped silently through trembling lids and fell, to make hallowed and blessed the tears that were already there.

The great mother soul of Mrs. O'Hagen reached out with great yearning toward the girl who had lived in this room and whose mother had been so recently swept away.

For one brief moment Molly allowed the vision of her own sorrow to come before her. So many times it had come, swiftly and relentlessly, only to be dismissed and

cast aside until it should come again—for it always came. Its memory had long haunted her, but she had never allowed it to crush her completely. For one brief moment it gripped her and held her in its sway. Then with a shrug of her shoulders she hurled it aside. “Some day I'll understand," she comforted herself. "But I reckon 

I'll have to wait." She took up the pillow tenderly and with a caressing hand laid it in its place. 

Then as she left the room she turned quickly and ran down the stairs. "I reckon I want to be sure that Mr. 

Barloman knows I'm resigning," she said to herself. "So's he won't expect me to help him out of that pantry."

After being graduated from high school, I entered the State University. There I fell into bad company and was robbed of my faith by an atheistic professor. Thus left without an anchor, I threw off all responsibility and restraint. Rejecting my mother's Bible and her Christ,

I became a prodigal in the far country of skepticism. I had received my diploma with high honors, but already drink was beginning to get a grip upon me. And drink, my friends, is the devil's tool. At first I could take it or leave it alone. This I did for years, first leaving it alone. and then taking it, until I discovered that it was drink itself which was taking me. Soon it refused to leave me alone any longer. "A short while after my graduation I was employed by a large corporation and for years made considerable money. But drink dragged me down and dulled my sense of right and wrong. The money left me when my mother died was soon squandered in drink and gambling. At last I found myself completely helpless in the grip of the octopus of appetite. Good resolutions were always broken and I became a wanderer, doing anything for enough money to buy liquor. "Once in Indianapolis, I was thrown in jail for a six months stay. I later spent two years in Bridewell for safe-breaking. In New York I was taken before the chief of police and given two hours to get out of town. 

I then drifted back to Chicago and one stormy night I chanced to be passing by this mission. It was the third of January, 1928. A man stopped me and seeing my

look of despair, fairly pulled me inside, where, in my drunken condition, I staggered into a chair down near the front. "I was hardly sober enough to understand the message or the testimonies. One thing I do remember, however. 

They sang my mother's favorite song, 'What can wash. away my sin? Nothing but the blood of Jesus.' Childhood memories returned and I could see myself once more at my mother's knee. With a sob, I slipped out of my seat and staggered forward where I fell on my face-"

The speaker paused and his voice shook. "It was right down there on my face that I came to the end of myself. 

And like the prodigal son, I came to myself, only when I came to the end of self. I don't know how it happened; 

I can't explain it, I don't try to explain it. Jesus did it; that's all I can say. I rose from my knees with Mr. 

Raynor's arm around my shoulder and with Jesus Christ in my heart. I was a sober man. The power of drink was broken. I have been sober ever since and a new man in Christ Jesus. "Somewhere in this world I have a wife and daughter. 

I have no knowledge of where they are, but I still consider myself unworthy to be near them-I have so disgraced them. Some day God may allow me to see them and be with them once more. He has blessed me with a good business, a good name, and best of all, a sweet fellowship with Himself. "Can Jesus save? Is He God's Son? Can He break the power of sin and shake off the shackles of skepticism? 

He can! I know it, for I have proved Him. What He has done for me, He can do for you. Only you must come to the end of yourself and call upon Him!"

Following this testimony of God's power through His Son, were others that told the same story. Men had wandered from home and the path of right. Several of

those who so testified were men of education and affluence who had gone down under the rule of sin. Some testimonies were given with all the gusto and enthusiasm of a prize fighter. Women too, rose and told of God's grace and goodness in saving them and their husbands. or sons, and of homes made happy through the reuniting power of faith in Jesus Christ. And always, with every testimony it was Jesus, the Son of God, Who had saved. them. His love! His power! His Mercy! All in answer to prayer in His Name!

Once more the truth of Christ's Deity came to Betty with overwhelming conviction, a conviction that moved her to the very depths. Here was no mere theological argument for Christ's divinity. Here was experience! Men knew that He was God because they knew Him, had met Him, talked with Him, experienced Him. The words of her instructor in music seemed trivial, silly, foolish to her now. 

And then with the truth of Jesus' absolute Deity pressing itself home to her heart, came also the alarming truth that she, Betty Dreanard, had never accepted Him! She had merely thought of God as a helper, a great, good spirit. Now she faced the tremendous and staggering certainty that Jesus was God! 

It was He who held the "key to all unknown." That was what Mother had meant when she said that Father had trusted the Saviour. Oh, it was so plain now. She too must open her heart as these had done. 

A great sob came into her throat and she cried out in her soul: "O Christ! I do believe, I do, I do! You are the Son of God and You are my Saviour. I accept 

You now-into my heart and life-and forever!"

The peace of a quiet summer day dispels and drives into oblivion the memories of the raging, blasting fierceness of the storms of yesterday. And so peace, unspeak-

able peace, stole softly through the open door of Betty's lonely heart. Gently it crept to the inner chamber of her soul, to the throne made vacant when skepticism had gone out. And as the rainbow shines out in all its glory when the sun sheds its light upon the dark and receding clouds after the rain, so tonight the Sun of Righteousness shone upon the darkened souls of men and women, and the rainbow of God's grace cast its smile upon them and upon Betty. For a moment her fingers would not follow the keys and she bowed her head.

When the meeting closed, she was rejoicing as never before. Thoughts of Jaird and of her strange plight were far from her mind, and with a singing spirit she left the mission accompanied by her new friends.

As they walked slowly up Logos Street toward Laramie, their attention was attracted by shouting newsboys, calling out, "Extra! Extra! Read all about the big murder!"

Murders were common enough in Chicago, and so were extras. However, Mr. Raynor called out, "Here, boy, I'll take one!" On the street car he said to Betty: "You're a wonder, Miss Dreanard. You handled that piano beautifully, and your singing was excellent. That duet must have touched every heart there. Your experience and talent have truly fitted you for this work. Here's hoping you stay with us a long time." 

But Betty did not hear his words. Her eyes were staring wildly at the flaming headlines of the newspaper which he held in his hand:

JAIRD BARLOMAN MURDERED! 

City-wide Search Being Made For Pretty Stepdaughter 

Suspected of Shooting!

For a moment things went black before her eyes, but instantly she rallied and forced herself to stay calm. What

had happened? Had Molly been compelled to kill Jaird in self defense? But Molly would have no gun! No, that was extremely unlikely.

Newspapers were being read by nearly every person in the car; and almost without thinking, Betty turned her face to the window and shielded her eyes with her hand. The situation was desperate! If Mr. Raynor should read her name he would turn her over to the police! Oh, what should she do? Her first impulse was to flee from the car and trust the crowded streets for protection. But almost instantly came the more sober thought: "Jesus can help-He is all powerful and He is 

God! He alone knows what I should do. I can trust 

Him!" And her cry of despair winged its way heavenward: "And now, O Christ, prove Yourself to me now and help me, please! For I do need You!" 

Mr. Raynor was speaking: "I guess this news will stir the city, all right-one more gangster has been put on the spot and this time by a woman-whew! Look at this, will you? He is supposed to have been shot by his stepdaughter, a girl twenty years old, named Betty Dreanard! I guess that fits you all right, doesn't it?" He laughed pleasantly and turned to her with a smile. But she turned away and he did. not see the frightened expression on her face. He went on: "The shooting occurred evidently about ten o'clock this morning, so that lets you out; for your train was coming in about that time. I suppose there are plenty of Betty 

Dreanards in the world," he finished. 

How she ever managed to keep from betraying her feelings during that long ride home, Betty never knew. 

She wanted to tell them all about it and let them give her their help, but she feared that perhaps they would

not believe her. Then, too, she had asked the Saviour to help, and would it not be showing lack of faith to seek the aid of others? If only she could see that paper and read it for herself! And yet when he had offered it to her, she had declined to accept it. There must be some mistake. Surely Jaird was not dead! With the thought came a sigh of relief which she instantly rebuked herself for permitting. For after tonight she could not wish evil to anyone. Had not Christ saved worse men than he?

At the Raynor residence she excused herself, asking with a smile if she might not take the paper to her room. "I want to read all about the murder I have just committed," she explained. She was surprised at the calmness of her spirit and the restfulness of her entire nature. did not occur to her to attribute it to her new life in 

Christ. She had not yet learned that "perfect love casteth out fear."

CHAPTER IX 

When Gerald Dawnery stepped into his cab after leaving Betty with Mrs. Raynor, he read the meter and at once paid the full amount of her ride. It was not that he felt able to do it, for his funds were never far above the water line of his own personal needs. But the situation seemed to call for chivalry which should manifest itself, not in mere politeness, but in an act of sacrificial gallantry. He could not explain why he felt, as he did, that there was a worthy cause, made more worthy because -well, because for some indefinable reason he found himself holding a profound respect and admiration for the girl. It may have been her frightened manner or the feeling of sympathy which had come to him when she mentioned her mother's death-whatever it was, he felt strangely drawn to her and avowed to himself that today should not be his last time to see her or to offer his assistance if ever opportunity afforded itself. 

With skill and precision born of experience he threaded his way through the crowded traffic back to the block where he had last seen Tim. He must see Tim: not that it was any of his business where the girl had come from or that Tim might have any more knowledge concerning her than himself, but that something within him seemed. to be impelling him to drive back to that place. 

It was four years ago when he had found Tim, homeless, hungry, friendless and exhausted. He had been standing on the corner of North Clark Street and Chicago Avenue with his bundle of papers in his hand, shivering pitifully with the cold.

"Hey there, Mister! Want to buy a paper? . . . Aw please, Mister! Just one!" Tim had begged. "Sorry, kid, but I just bought one a moment ago," 

Gerald had replied as he started his cab and whirled away.

But deep in the heart of Gerald was the memory of his own struggling days as a newsboy, and especially of one cold stormy night when he, too, had stood on that same corner and faced the heartless public as they went hurrying by. Well too did he remember how his little heart had swelled with warmth toward a kind hearted woman who, although she had already bought a paper from a rival newsboy, paused at his stand and gave him a whole dollar for a single copy, refusing kindly but firmly to receive any change. "No," she said, "I reckon as how ye might need it more than I." Wherewith she had hurried on never to be seen by him again. "Fool!" Gerald had chided himself. "Why didn't you buy a paper from that boy?" With that he had circled the block and stopped once more at Tim's corner. “Here, my friend," he had said, "never mind the change," and handed him a dollar bill.

Such an expression of pleasure and appreciation as Gerald had never seen before, swept across the little fellow's face as he said: "Oh, thanks, Mister! Thanks! 

Just wait'll I get a chance to do somep'n fer you sometime and I'll do it quick, yes, sir!"

After that Gerald made it a point to stop every now and then to see his new friend. And one day on his day off they went to the zoo together and became more intimately acquainted. Gerald learned that Tim had no home, but had been sleeping in an alley in an old piano box with another boy.

So it came about that Tim had gone to live with Gerald and his mother over on North La Salle Street. Tim

had paid for his room every week, not much of course, but enough to help keep the rent up on the apartment. 

Gerald had finished his course at a certain out-of-thecity University and was now taking postgraduate work in Northwestern. To earn the necessary funds, he drove his taxi. Although he was able to be employed only part time during the school term, he still retained his position. 

The bond of friendship between the two became more and more firm as the months rolled by. One day Tim said to Gerald: "I wish I had a mother like you have. 'S funny I never had a mother, isn't it?" He sighed wistfully and with a far away look in his eyes. "But maybe if I ever get to heaven, I'll just ask Jesus all about it and I'll bet 

He'll fix it up fer me so's I won't be lonesome all the time I'm in heaven too!"

That peculiar way of saying it had touched Gerald, strangely, and served to tighten and strengthen the cords of love which had wrapped themselves so tenaciously about his heart. Gerald was impressed, too, with Tim's keenness of mind. That he was a precocious lad was more than evident, as he applied himself eagerly to his school studies and came home always with the highest of grades.

It was through Mrs. Dawnery that Gerald had become acquainted with the Raynors. No woman of stronger 

Christian faith ever lived.

Although her work as a writer kept her exceptionally busy, she found time to be at the mission three or four nights of each week. Love such as only an understanding mother and a dutiful son can have was theirs. How happy were their hours together and how happy was she when she received her checks every now and then for her stories. Her one published book had had a large sale and was now attracting nation-wide attention. This

had encouraged her to begin faithfully and enthusiastically on another. More than once the temptation had come to write stories which would find a market in some of the worldly magazines and bring her at once fame and a possibility of larger remuneration. Gerald too was interested in her work and often complimented her warmly on her increasing improvement in technique. His sympathy was keen and welcomed by her on those occasions when her literary efforts returned with the inevitable and heart-breaking rejection slip.

But between the two had arisen, slowly but surely, a darkening wall whose shadow cast its ominous proportions over their years of understanding, and little by little drew them apart-she, anxious and prayerful, and he, impatient. "I know, Mother," he had argued only yesterday. "I used to believe all that stuff, but I can't do it any more. 

It's completely out of date. It belong in the museum of antiquated religious faiths. Over at the University we don't take things for granted any more. In the street he looked about for a cab. 

Gerald had been driving about rather aimlessly for some time, thinking. Just how much was a man responsible for his actions? What about the young chap who had only yesterday taken the life of his mother and then his own? "As a man thinketh in his heart, so is he.”

Jesus had said that. It meant, "Your thoughts are responsible for your actions and your character." The cause produced the effect. The youth who had murdered his mother had done so because of his belief! His beliefs were wrong, else they could not have led him to commit the crime. His act was not that of one overwhelmed by a sudden and violent flood of intoxicating temper, but of premeditation. Gerald stopped his cab and gazed about him, gripped by the conclusion at which he had just arrived: A man was responsible for his thoughts as well as his deeds! If a deed was wrong, then a philosophy of life which permitted or caused such a deed, was also wrong! Make it right to think wrong and one could not prove that it was wrong to do wrong. The peculiar sense of responsibility in regard to right thinking which had gripped him so startlingly a half hour ago was still asserting its presence and Gerald felt himself sending up silent petitions for aid in the situation which he was about to face. Was this prayer, this crying out to Something, or Somebody for help? The naturalness with which he found himself reaching out for outside assistance surprised him. Of course it was cowardly to pray. Had not Emerson said that prayer was a “disease of the soul?" No, he must not pray. Besides he did not believe, really, that there was a God. There might be a

That the man he had just discharged was in some way implicated in the strange mystery, he was certain. What to do was a question he could not answer. 

Someone had said that when one knew not what to do he should "wait for developments." But here it seemed that to wait was unwise. Perhaps Tim's life was in danger.

CHAPTER XIII 

The early morning sun had been pouring its cheering rays in golden splashes into a little room in Fairhaven Hospital. The form under the white covers turned listlessly as though enjoying to the full the last few moments of luxurious sleep. A pair of tired eyes opened to gaze wonderingly about on the quiet surroundings. The song of birds came floating in through the open window. The roar of the racing traffic a block away was only faintly audible, while the patter, patter of flying feet was heard in the corridor outside the room. 

Softly the door opened a crack. The uniformed nurse who peered in, evidently satisfied that her patient was awake, tiptoed softly to her bed, adjusted a thermometer under the patient's tongue and broke the silence with: "A letter for you, Miss Dreanard. It came early this morning. The boy said to tell you his name was Tim, that you would remember him. He seemed so disappointed when I told him he couldn't see you." 

Betty reached for the letter, glancing at the postmark.

The name in the left hand corner brought vague memories of the days before her father's death. The letter had been addressed to her at the house on Michigan Avenue. "You may be released this morning, Miss Dreanard," the nurse said pleasantly. "They have captured the man who is, beyond a doubt, the slayer of your stepfather. It is expected that you will be absolved of all implication in the crime. Of course, you will be questioned and must

be ready at a moment's notice to be called upon by the police. I do hope you are strong enough to be allowed to go home. As soon as the doctor comes we shall know. 

Would you care to see the morning paper?" "Oh, no, I'm afraid not! No, thanks, not just yet,"

Betty refused, recalling her fright of the night before and shuddering as she reviewed in memory the horrors of the last twenty-four hours: the awful man who had appeared suddenly in her room; the gag; the binding of her wrists and ankles; the crash of shattering glass; then the darkness and the sinking-floating experiences of semioblivion; her awakening here in the hospital, and the visit of the Raynors-that was the one bright spot of it all—; the kind nurse who had pricked her with a hypodermic needle; and then forgetfulness once more—until now.

In spite of the severe strain on her nerves and the terror of the night before, she felt strangely rested and calm. A tranquil memory lingered as though in the midst of the darkness there had been a tiny ray of light, as if in the very fury of the raging storm, the gentle winds of peace had blown softly upon her. She remembered now.

It was that service at the mission and the precious memory of a new Presence that had flooded her heart with peace. And as she still mused, floating in a maze of halfconsciousness, the Presence seemed to pervade the little room. She could sense Him, feel Him, almost hear Him. 

The tired eyelids drooped once more and Betty slept.

A tray of food placed beside her by a hurrying orderly remained untouched and Betty slept on; slept till ten o'clock when another white clad figure paused by her side, evidently bent on making up the bed. Seeing the sleeping form, the nurse passed on to other rooms. And still Betty slept on, the envelope unopened at her side. 

Did she dream that she heard a cautious footstep beside her bed? That a greedy hand reached out and took the

letter? Did she really hear those receding footsteps which tiptoed warily out and down the hall? 

Unconsciously Betty's hand reached out to her pillow to clasp the envelope. It closed upon emptiness. She started wide awake in a moment. The letter was gone!

At ten-thirty the doctor came, looked at her quietly and smiled. "You are a very innocent looking woman," he asserted with a twinkle in his eye. "Too innocent looking to be guilty of such a crime as you were suspected of last night." He read her chart, took her pulse and temperature for verification of the nurse's report, and nodded as if satisfied. "The nurse will bring your clothes after lunch," he said. "You have had a pretty bad scare and your nerves are still on edge. I'd advise you to take a few weeks vacation, out in the country somewhere."

No man can glimpse the Rockies for the first time, catching their cloud-like imagery, without feeling a thrill of ecstatic joy, a stab of pain that goes straight to the heart.

It was a vista never to be forgotten, an impression that would leave its beauty forever on the pages of memory. To these who had never seen a mountain, it was like a drink of water to a thirsty traveller; a ray of light in a darkened. room; a garden spot in the desert.

Tomorrow they would delve into the beauties of those giant creations of picturesque loveliness; they would wind in and out along the edges of steep precipices with sheer drops of thousands of feet; they would follow the snakelike lariat trail that leads to the top of Lookout Mountain, pausing at the grave of Buffalo Bill. 

And so tomorrow came.

The trip had been a delightful one, made so by as jolly a group as ever travelled together. To Gerald's mind, however, there was a mingled wonder and doubt as to the reason why he had been allowed to come. Since that morning last week when he had first glimpsed Betty Dreanard, and driven her in his taxi to the Raynors, he had felt strangely certain that he had met the girl who henceforth was to figure a large part in his life. He was too much of a man and too much in the strength of that manhood, to pass by lightly the call that had challenged his soul when he had handed her over to Mrs. Raynor. Youth

cannot lay aside lightly that first appeal to the heart. It is a serious, a sacred thing.

The rapidly moving events of the hours and days that had followed, in which she had played so prominent a part, had but served to enhance his respect and admiration for her; served, too, to make him realize that some day he would come to a choice as to his life partner. All men face that thrilling, destiny-determining hour. He was no longer a mere youth, he was a man with a man's yearnings and aspirations and a man's capacity to love and be loved. Having no clearly defined beliefs in a personal God, or His guiding providences, he did, however, believe that "fate" played some part in the life of every man, and that it had allowed this girl to cross his path. 

If so, he must place himself in the hands of that fate and follow where his heart bid him.

She was a girl that no man could meet without feeling his pulse quicken, and bowing low in reverence Gerald realized that this journey was going to mean more to him than he had dreamed. And so, with youthful but wary anticipation, he looked forward to his first opportunity to meet her alone, when he would draw aside the veil that would lead into a new and entrancing friendship. He did not know that as she had sat behind him on the long trip out, she had looked at him with respect, admiring his clever witticisms, his strong athletic build, and gentlemanly manner.

The party had spent a gay yet worshipful fifteen minutes, standing in reverent adoration at this wondrous unfolding to their hungry eyes. Betty had edged her way northward to a jutting rock which overhung the very edge of the precipice. Her soul thrilled to the scene before her as her eyes followed the downward sweep of that vast expanse of jagged rock and stately fir trees, the latter lifting their heads somberly as if waiting in this glorious out-

door palace to serve the bidding of the mighty Monarch whose presence and bearing they themselves seemed to portray.

Oblivious to the rest of the party, Betty stood enrapt, her whole being permeated with the wonder of it all. She did not know that she was being watched, nor that they had already waited for more than ten minutes for her to turn and come to them. "We'll walk on to Buffalo Bill's grave," Mr. Raynor said to Gerald. "You and Tim can bring Betty in the car." "Aw, let Gerald bring her," Tim had cried, not too loudly. "I want to see where 'at guy was buried, too." 

And so for a few moments the crunch, crunch of feet was heard on the road, and then all was silence. Betty still stood there, silhouetted against the sky. His step behind her was the first intimation she had that she was not alone. Quietly she turned and faced him. For a brief second their eyes met ere they both looked once more at that downward sweep of carved-rock beauty. Her arm gestured slowly, gracefully toward that which had gripped her for the past ten minutes; his eyes followed hers. They were both delightfully conscious of being together. The silence continued. No one with a sense of the fitness of things would have spoken in that moment. Spoken words are the glaring lights which intrude rudely on the trysting places of lovers.

Two forms now were silhouetted against the sky, one the graceful figure of a young woman, wearing a cleverlycut sports suit. A crown of wavy chestnut hair, arranged in a careless but perfect coiffure, with an occasional straying curl blowing lightly in the breeze, harmonized with a pair of hazel eyes. The chin was firm and the lips full and laughing, yet now strangely sober. Beside her stood the tall, athletic form of a young man. Nobility of char-

acter lined his face and strength showed in the square jaw. The student's brow was only what one would expect after seeing into those clear, blue eyes.

For a full moment they stood there. Still neither of them spoke. Their lips were sealed. But in those moments when silence reigned, great and beautiful thoughts were being written across the newly turned pages of two awakening spirits, thoughts that some day would find a voice, and the spoken words, perhaps, a waiting ear. 

Love is like that.

Slowly they turned as if by common consent and walked pensively to the car. In this colloquy of silence, they had told each other much, neither one fully grasping the other's meaning.

Gerald this morning.

Then his eyes roved about the hills, gazing at that gorgeous array of pines, rocks, and mountains spreading before him in never-ending vista. Each new scene seemed more entrancing than the last. His shoulders squared, his muscles tensed, and the thrill of vigorous youth flowed through his veins. Reaching the crest of the hill, she had found a little niche behind a rock and had paused to rest, reading from the little book. 

Now she saw him as he stopped at the spring, felt with him the urge of his soul as it responded to the irresistible challenge of the hill. She watched him as he climbed watched and admired. Who was this young man? Who was any man that could so stir her heart?

She did not know him, had only met him a week ago under circumstances which had made it impossible for her to have thought of him romantically. She must not feel this way. It was silly of her. He of course would

have other interests. And so Betty set herself to fight the call of her heart. After all, it might be but a mere passing fancy, one of the evanescent heart stirrings of youth.

CHAPTER XXI 

The week-end was spent rather quietly, the entire group attending the little Sunday school and Mr. Raynor bringing a brief message to the fifteen or twenty persons assembled there. 

The Tuesday following was the day in which Gerald faced anew the ever-recurring problem of the why of his own existence in a world which offered him no satisfactory philosophy of life. Fontaine's suicide, committed before his very eyes, had unnerved him. Here was another whose vision of life after death was like that of the young chap from the university whose notion that death was a "mere chemical change," had ended in a murder and a suicide. With some who were taking their unbelief seriously, its consequences were devastating in their finalities. 

About ten o'clock in the morning, after he had wandered aimlessly about camp for awhile, written a letter to his mother, and frolicked with Tim, he sauntered over to his cab, slid under the driver's wheel, and drove down the little wagon-road which led to the town. Tim and 

Mr. Franks, who was staying in camp with them a few days, had already gone off with Mr. Raynor to their favorite trout stream. Betty was reading and Mrs. Raynor writing letters.

The sleepy little old county-seat was more alive than usual this morning. Photographers and reporters for the 

Denver and Colorado Springs papers had continued to pour in to get all the up-to-the-minute news on the slaying of Barloman and the capture of Spade Arms Ruby.

In fact scarcely a day had gone by since the arrival of

the little company of vacationists, but that one or more reporters had called at the camp. 

Some of them had paused to take pictures of the courthouse with the old gallows in the background, which had been left as a reminder of the days when culprits were treated with more speed than courtesy. Nation-wide interest was being created in Goldridge. Its buildings stood unobtrusively along rough, weed-grown streets, like tombstones on whose weather beaten surfaces one might read the hieroglyphics of history. The depression that was sweeping America had swept a flood of idle dreamers, adventurers, and some earnest minded, hard-working men into the gold fields. Old mines and shafts had been reopened. New-fangled sluice-boxes were being built, and little colonies of gold seekers dotted the streams and mountain sides, where they were feverishly placering and digging in search of the hidden treasure. 

But Gerald was not interested in gold this morning.

His recent interview with Betty had created within a great hunger, a yearning after something that would satisfy and calm his restless spirit. She had said that he would find, and he had avowed that he would search honestly.

Before leaving camp he had reached into his suit case and dug out his little-used Bible. She had watched him from her tent as he had climbed into his car, and secretly longed and prayed that he might find that for which he sought. Just as he was driving away she stepped quietly outside. The song that had been forming itself on her lips was the same song that had come to her on that morning more than two weeks ago: "God holds the Key of all Unknown, And I am glad —

He heard. The music came floating to his ears like a

melody from the courts of heaven if there were a heaven. She was his heaven now. His unbelief robbed him of the meaning of the soft notes, and they fell, in minor cadences, upon his soul. He could not believe! 

Silently, Betty returned to her tent and slipped to her knees, she waited, her petitions coming forth from moving lips, although her voice was still: "O dear Heavenly Father!" her soul sobbed. "He should not find it hard to find Thee when Thou also art searching for him. O Father, please answer my prayer today! Give him peace and joy, and make Thyself real to him as Thou didst to me. . . . In Jesus' Name. Amen!"

For a long time she continued kneeling. Then she arose, a look of the sanctuary on her face. "The prayer that moves the Hand that moves the world, must first move the heart of him who moves the lips." That thought came to her and gave her faith, even in the midst of a curious paradox of anxiety and certainty for the one who had been the object of her petition. 

A quiet little nook about a mile north of town welcomed Gerald as he drove slowly along. He parked his car some three hundred yards from the road behind a clump of firs. The sun shone down warm and friendly. 

He was far enough from the little-used wagon-road to feel safe from the view of passers-by. 

The hush of the hills is contagious. It begets reverence. 

Quietly he waited, and quietly listened. What was he waiting for? What did he expect? How would he know if 

God did reveal Himself? He must do something. She had said that it would be through the Book. Must he believe it to be His Word before he read? Perhaps it might be like an experiment in chemistry. One need not have absolute assurance that the formula was correct before endeavoring to follow its laid-down laws. One need assume at the beginning only that it might be correct. If it should

work, then it was correct. If not, then it was wrong. 

Simple! He would assume that the Bible might be right, and endeavor to allow the Author of the Book to reveal its truth to him. He would put it to the test now! 

He found the gospel of John and began: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made by Him; and without Him was not anything made that was made. In 

Him was life; and the life was the light of men." It was all Greek to him. Nevertheless he read on till he came to see that by the WORD, Christ was meant. He was startled to note that the Bible explicitly declared that

Christ was God and that He was the Creator! Well, he couldn't believe that!

Then he remembered the words of the lawyer who in the midst of his skepticism had turned to the Bible for light. The Bible was a closed book to him because he had not read it. He remembered, also, Betty's words.

Saw her, too, in his mind's eye, as her face shone with the light of another world as she said: "I do not ask you to believe it, but to read, study and search it."

So he read on. He must not try to make himself believe. 

He must only be honest with the Word and allow Him, the Holy Spirit, to use the written word to produce faith in the LIVING WORD. Simple enough. But would it work? Something must happen soon or he would go mad, it seemed. Perhaps he ought to try prayer, as Tim had said. He had never been able to erase from his memory that quaintly worded philosophy of Tim's. Perhaps it was true that he did not believe in prayer because he had never tried it!

Gerald read on. Then, suddenly, when it seemed that he could go no farther, he paused. The Book made him

tremble made him sick of self and the old philosophies. 

And again, as he had in the park in Chicago some weeks ago, he felt the urge to pray. This time the words were formed, coming earnestly through desperate lips, out of an anguished heart. "O God of my mother of Betty! If you really are 

GOD, and if Jesus Christ is Your Son and my Saviour, oh, make Thyself known to me and I will follow Him wherever He leads. I am willing to be shown. Oh, I do not doubt Thee, wilfully. "In the Name of Him in Whom I do not yet believe, but Whom I will gladly follow if I come to know Him— 

Jesus Christ. ... Amen!"

He kept his eyes closed, face uplifted, waiting, almost believing. 

A look of disappointment formed on his face. His heart was still empty! God had not heard his prayer. There was no God! . . . No God! Then he checked himself, as a question formed itself sharply in his brain: "Will you be willing to tell the boys of your fraternity of your new life, and will you renounce the questionable things you do there in your social life?" This was the hardest test of all for him, and he knew it. 

With a desperate cry, his soul shouted: "Yes, Lord! Yes - gladly! Anything - everything! 

Only let me know Thee!"

As Saul of Tarsus met the Son of God in Whom he had not believed, met Him in a climatic hour and was instantly transformed, so Gerald Dawnery met that same

Lord in the hour of his surrender. He had searched with all his heart, and he had found! "The water that I shall give him shall be in him, a well of water, springing up unto everlasting life." Jesus had said that, and it was true. It did manifest itself in noble tears of joy and gladness which coursed unashamed and unrestrained

down his cheeks. With the ineffable ecstacy of the presence of God in his heart, Gerald cried out softly, fervently: "Thank You, O Christ, thank You. You are real, You are the Son of God. I believe, I KNOW!"

For a long time he sat there quietly, drinking the sweet sacred nectar of the knowledge of God, reading and meditating upon the Word. He looked about in his mind for his doubts. They were gone. Those questions seemed so trivial now. He had met the Son of God; and the Son of God would meet for him the problems of life. 

He looked at his watch. One o'clock! He must hurry back to camp to break the news. He could see their faces now and he rejoiced inwardly because he knew that the happiness he had found would not be all his own. 

They would welcome his return from the far country with the same joy as had the father the prodigal son. 

He was about to step on the starter when his left hand touched the pocket in the upholstering on the door of the car. Why all the bulge in that pocket? A newspaper! He had placed a copy of a Chicago paper there weeks ago and had not removed it. Slowly he pulled it out and unfolded it. Its glaring head lines stood out boldly, shockingly:

JAIRD BARLOMAN MURDERED! 

City-Wide Search Being Made for Pretty 

Stepdaughter Suspected of Shooting!

Below the awful words was a picture of the girl. His eyes focused upon it while he recalled the first time he had met her. Then followed in memory a kaleidoscopic picture of the many events of his life in the weeks that had ensued in which she had played a leading role. Long and steadily he studied the picture before him as if en-

deavoring to fathom the depths of her eyes and through them to understand her soul. It seemed almost sacrilege to gaze so long when he knew she was not here to prevent him. He would not dare indulge so precious a moment were he looking directly into her lovely face. Then his lips moved, reverently: "Betty Dreanard, some day I hope to be worthy to tell you face to face and from the depths of my soul that I love you - you only. I cannot do it now. You would not believe me. We have not known each other long. But by the grace of my newly found Lord I shall prove myself, until I may hope to ask for your love in return."

Meditatively, he turned the pages of the paper. Various captions attracted his attention, without creating any particular interest. A wealthy man had died, leaving several millions to endow a chair in a great university. Another man of means had been separated from his wealth by a crash of a Wall Street market and in his desperation, had committed suicide. "He had no anchor," Gerald thought. An airplane had fallen bursting into flames in its descent, and six passengers had lost their lives. 

Gerald was in the act of folding the paper with a view to driving back to camp, when his eye fell on an article which drew his attention, sharply. Before he had read it half through, he was keenly interested. It ran:

Religion Directly at the Door of the Modern Minister 

It was an account of Mr. Raynor's address at the

'If My people, which are called by My Name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek My face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land.' Slowly Gerald folded the paper, forgetting to cut out 

Betty's picture as had been his intention. "May it be so!" he murmured. His jaw was set. "And may I, too, join the ranks of His army!"

He came into camp and parked his car under a pine. 

Just how to break the news, or whether to wait till later, he had not decided. He did not know that the light of the cross was radiant on his face and that his story would be self-revealing to those who had read a similar story on the faces of others.

He was restrained from an immediate announcement of his Discovery by news that stirred the whole camp into great excitement.

A little old narrow-gauge railroad train puffed and spit its way by fits and starts up into the mountains and to 

Goldridge once every day. Its rasping whistle could be heard reverberating through the hills in screeching, undulating echoes, every afternoon about one o'clock. Its clanging engine wheezed to a grinding stop at the dilapidated depot, and tarried for only a few minutes, before coughing on up the canyon, and winding its dilatory way to the next town some fifty miles distant, with its precious burden of mail and high rate freight and an occasional passenger.

Today it had carried an unusual amount of mail and a large packet of papers from Denver. Tim, waiting at the post office for the mail, had not seen Gerald as he had driven past; and Gerald had been too engrossed in his rapturous experience to think of mere letters. Consequently, when Tim came puffing into camp after a hurried hike from town and burst out hilariously: "Extra!

Extra!" Gerald, who was alighting from his cab, was taken completely by surprise. "Hey, there, Mister!" Tim urged, shoving a paper into his hand, and racing off to the Raynors' tent with another. "Extra! Extra! Big fire in Chicago-whole city's ablaze!"

The whole camp was immediately astir and for a minute there was real concern on the faces of them all. 

Then Mr. Raynor let out a "Hooray!" that started on a bounding ricochet through the mountains. A moment later his face was sobered as he read further. "What is it?" Mrs. Raynor asked, anxiously, as she came quickly to his side. "What's all the excitement about, Tim?" "It's a real fire!" Tim exclaimed, excitedly. "Another 

Chicago fire! One that'll go down in history sure enough as somep'n! Hurrah, the whole city's on fire! Oh boy, oh boy, oh boy, oh —!”

CHAPTER XXII 

The Sunday following the Y.M.C.A. address of Mr.

Raynor to the ministers had seen the first manifestation of the "Chicago Fire." Hundreds of pastors went to their pulpits with the light of Calvary shining on their faces. 

There were no carefully phrased or flowery sermons read from Saturday night manuscripts. The modernist preachers who had renounced their betrayal of Christ, went to their churches to preach the Gospel of the Cross. 

The results were glorious. From Calvary Immanuel 

Church on the south side to St. John's Maranatha on the north, the effect on the congregations was like an electric shock. One modernist pastor of nation-wide fame endeavored to preach the true Gospel with all the enthusiasm of a fervent evangelist, and was himself converted before the sermon was over. 

It was like the days when a member of the Hell Fire club returned from listening to one of Finney's sermons to give a report of the revival. He stood before his society, endeavoring to speak with Finney's vehemence and enthusiasm, and had been compelled to cry out in the midst. of his oration: "Oh, I'm converted myself, I'm converted!"

The mid-week prayer meetings which followed the Sunday sermons were attended by many Christians who had already caught the spirit of revival. They carried the banner of a new vision to the shops, the stores, and the streets. Sins were confessed and forsaken and old scores were settled.

The revival spread like wildfire. The churches hitherto unable to hold Sunday night services because of the

indifference of their members, now held two meetings each Sunday, and the people flocked to hear "the new minister." The faithful few in the ranks of the fundamentalists, who had carried on bravely during the awful days before the "fire," were quickened to an even more zealous ministry.

In those churches where the Sunday lectures on “Ethical Culture," "The Higher Religion," and "The New

Psychology" were still given, the people became dissatisfied. The fervency of their neighbors who attended the churches of the "Fire," made them restless and hungry for something that would meet their great need. In other places they heard the old message of the cross, and began to wonder what was the matter with their own pastor and church. Some of them united with those churches which had the fire in the pulpits; others rose in protest and demanded that the minister either preach the Gospel or cease to draw his salary from the people of God. 

Higher and higher the enthusiasm mounted till it reached the city hall. High officials and business men turned to Christ. The city hall clique was wiped out in the fall elections. Many teachers in the schools found 

Christ. With a Christian majority on the school board, a number of new teachers who knew and loved the Gospel were chosen. The avowed intention was that of wiping out crime by making every child Christian. For a man will be in later life what he is in his youth. 

On and on the fire spread, until youth aflame with the world, became youth aflame for Christ. At this rate it would not be long before the mission fields would be crowded with zealous, spirit-filled young men and women, supported by Christian men who would turn their money into the channel of the cause of Christ. 

Other cities caught the flame until it spread like an epidemic of holy zeal from state to state. Even those

ministers who did not possess a great gift of oratory, delivered their messages in the power of the Spirit, and men and women were converted. 

Gerald and Mr. Raynor were sitting in the latter's office, discussing things of vital interest to themselves and to the country at large. "What are your plans now, Gerald?" Mr. Raynor asked, his voice revealing his keen interest in the young man. "My plans are rather indefinite as to any immediate moves, except that I expect to continue in school," Gerald replied. "But as to what shall be my ultimate work, I am as sure of that as I am of my own existence." "You mean?" "That it didn't take the Chicago fire to give me warmth.

I had a thorough thawing out that eventful day in Colorado. The vision has been before me growing brighter and brighter since then." 

"Well, you've a big job cut out for you, young man.

I almost envy you, with a whole life before you and the churches crying for the gospel. Ten years ago— even last year you would have had to beg for open doors. 

But now from every town and from nearly every church there is an earnest appeal for someone to help draw in the net. I cannot recall any time in the past when the cry for 'evangelism' has been so widespread and earnest." "Yes," Gerald returned with feeling. "And to think that I was blind all through my years at the university, feverishly working for degrees, when what I needed most of all was the B.A. degree — 'born again!' "And you're sure you have that now?" 99 "Sure? I'm so sure of it that I'm going out, by the grace of God, to confer that degree upon every sinner I meet who will only believe!" "God bless you, Gerald! If it means anything to you, I'll be praying for you every day as long as I live. It will

not be an easy task for you, even though you will have the ministry back of you. The whole country has been prejudiced against evangelists. Evangelistic work has been branded as 'emotionalism,' and 'wildfire.' Even in the midst of this present revival you will still have the old wall of prejudice to break down in the older citizens." "What do you think will be the final result of this big sweep to the Gospel? What will the 'Chicago fire' do?

Continue to spread until the whole world will be converted?"

Mr. Raynor's answer was deliberate and yet with conviction: "Would to God that it might be so. But all men will never be converted, as Christ plainly taught in His parables of 'the sower,' the 'drag net,' and the 'leaven.' 

What will really happen remains to be seen. If this revival continues to spread, as it surely will if the ministry continues to be alert and awake and if the great denominations take their false prophets from the throne, in a few years from now, with the growth of the new generation, we shall have a different world. But the people will never turn to God until the ministry returns to God. We have thousands of noble men in the pulpits, and their number is increasing with this revival, but there are still thousands who are not God's men, who do not believe the Bible, and who have no business in the pulpits." "Do you feel that the great crime wave of this country could have been avoided, if we had had a faithful ministry?" Gerald asked wonderingly. "Absolutely! The modernist minister has nothing in his message to make men fear God and be afraid of the consequences of sin. If the pulpits of our land had thundered all these years against sin and continuously preached the Bible as the very Word of God, the American people would not have tolerated infidelity in the uni-

versities and seminaries. It is this let-down in the preaching of the cross which has permitted a public opinion that is responsible for crime. We could never have kept all men from being criminals, but we certainly could have kept them from ruling this country and costing us thirteen billions of dollars annually." "Then it amounts to this," Gerald said reflectively, asking a question at the same time. "While you and others are trying to save men from the depths of sin, the universities, as well as much of modern literature, have been creating the necessity for your labor. It is true that the schools do not teach men to do evil — in fact, they teach a system of ethics that is commendable — yet they rob us of a faith in the true God. And because we are all sons of Adam, having an unregenerate nature that loves sin, we go out and practice the very opposite of their ethical teaching. In other words, they teach us how to tie good fruit on our evil trees, while taking away the very thing that would make of us good trees! Is that not right?" "I'm afraid that is so."

There was another question which Gerald wanted to ask. "Do you think the seminaries will remove the modernistic professors from their positions of trust? Will the fire burn out the dross from them, too?" "There's the rub, my man. Either the Lord will prevail, or Baal. If these institutions continue to turn out a deluge of young semi-atheists to fill the pulpits, then there is only one end for the world. For when a nation. or a world loses its sense of God, it also loses its soul. 

This new god which is being introduced by the modernists may have the same name as our Christ, but he is not the same. There are thousands of men in the world by

the name of John Smith, and there may be false Christs.

But we want only the Christ of the Bible for He alone is God."

Gerald rose to go. He paused to add as he was leaving: "Thank you, Mr. Raynor. You have answered my questions. Well, I must be going. I've another class before lunch." He jumped into his cab, which was parked just outside, and whirled away.

It was two months since he had enrolled as a student in the great Chara Bible Institute. Since the day when he had tried the great experiment, the desire to tell others of the Light of the World had burned with passioned glow in his breast.

Within a fortnight after his wondrous discovery, a letter was on its way to Chicago. The reply was immediate and cordial. The Superintendent would be glad to welcome him to the Chara Bible Institute, but he must make haste with his application blank, for already the enrollment was nearing the one thousand mark.

And so the vacation period ended and the intervening weeks slipped quickly by. The opening day was of unusual interest, although for him there was nothing new in the matter of registration. He was deeply impressed with the spiritual atmosphere of the place. To be one among a thousand young people, all of whom were preparing for some form of definite Christian work, and on whose faces the light of faith shone in peaceful glow, was an experience he could never describe with tongue or pen. 

Great had been his surprise and delight when he discovered that Betty, too, was to enroll as a student. Barloman's estate had been settled and the beautiful mansion on Michigan Avenue had come into her possession. The rules of the Institute required that all students room in the dormitories, so it was no more than natural that Gerald and Betty should see each other almost daily in

the round of classes and trips to the post office. However, things did not seem the same between them as they had out in the west, where there was the freedom of the open spaces and the lure of the hills, together with the whispering of the pines, whose sighings seemed always to inspire romance and love.

Her smile was pleasant and it thrilled him strangely whenever they chanced to meet. But when he stopped to talk he noticed a fleeting cloud come over her face. When the cloud had passed, instead of the joyous expression which he had come to know and love as one of those distinguishing characteristics which made her the woman she was, there was a lurking shadow in her eyes, that made her appear uninterested, as if her thoughts were far away. Her smile, then, was as beautiful as ever, but not so radiant; and he felt himself dismissed-not rudely, but definitely.

At such times he returned to his room with clouded brow and soul, to dig deeper and deeper into his studies, to spend more and more time in secret prayer, until, lost in the wonders of the presence of God and His Word, he forgot. He must not allow the call of a woman's love to make less audible the voice of his soul. The need was appalling and there must be those who would fill the ranks left vacant by those who were passing on. 

And so he set himself to forget and to plod grimly on. 

The world was perishing without Christ and he must be a flaming evangel to carry the Message; and that meant preparation, and a working knowledge of the Bible, first of all.

Now as he drove down Michigan Avenue and swung over toward La Salle, his heart was light. He was filled with a great desire to complete his school work as soon as possible. His course would take two years, although he need not wait so long before beginning to tell the story.

Following the plan of the school for all its students, he had been given at least three assignments weekly, when he was afforded an opportunity to speak for his Christ. 

One was a Sunday School class on the south side where the "fire" was burning grandly. Almost weekly he had the privilege of leading one of his class to the Saviour. 

Today he must prepare for that class. He parked his car near the school post office and stopped to look into box number 624. There were several letters. He had no time to read them now, for the bell was ringing for his class in Church History. He hastened to find a seat in the rear of Rieth Hall and settled himself for the opening song. All classes at Chara were opened with a song or prayer or both. The selection this morning was that old masterpiece whose first stanza begins: "Rise, O church of Christ, arise, O church of Christ, lift up thine head."

The students sang lustily and reverently, their voices ringing out the challenge. Gerald caught the spirit and knew that it was a challenge to him also. 

In the interval following the prayer and before the necessity of taking notes, Gerald glanced through his mail. Most of it could wait. He supposed he should wait till after the class before reading any of it. 

He started. An envelope without a stamp! It was addressed in a smooth, evidently feminine hand, simply: 

Mr. Gerald Dawnery,

Student. 

Wonderingly he tore it open, and read: 

Dear Gerald: 

May I have an interview with you at your earliest convenience, please? . . . Urgent! Sincerely, Betty D.

Gerald's heart leaped with a feeling of mingled anxiety and delighted anticipation. Instinctively he glanced about the classroom. Down near the front he espied a shapely head, crowned with an abundance of wavy, chestnut hair.

Even as he looked she turned, and he glimpsed briefly her profile - the firm chin, the demure nose, and the curve of long, dark lashes. He thought he saw, too, a faint smile as she turned once more to her notes. Was she in trouble? Would she say, "Urgent," unless there were something seriously wrong? 

Professor Grant was speaking now. Gerald opened his notebook and waited for the signal to begin. Even while he wrote he was conscious of a wild beating of his heart as over and over again the words of her brief note recurred to his memory: "May I have an interview with you at your earliest convenience, please? . . . Urgent!"

At the sound of the bell he hastened to his room, where he re-read the letter once more, made his toilet, and betook himself with nerves aquiver to the women's dormitory. She informed him with a smile that the person requested would apear almost as quickly as if he had rubbed Aladdin's lamp.

Even now he could not be sure he was to be privileged to talk to her. Again he read her request, studying the smooth handwriting, fancying the dainty hand that had held the pen, recalling the day several months ago when a pink-nailed finger of that same hand had indicated a verse in the Bible, a verse that had later meant life - eternal life. to him.

The elevator door opened, and an envelope was handed

Inside the room, he locked the door again, took the letter from his pocket, and sat down on the edge of the bed. 

Once more he read the peculiar message without any change in expression. 

A half hour later he was still sitting there, staring ahead of him. The envelope had slipped from his fingers to the floor, but he had not changed his position.

CHAPTER XXIII 

From her window Betty looked out upon the court and across to the main entrance of the Administration building, whose upper stories were used for the men's dormitory. She was attired in a crepe negligee whose gay sleeves conspired to add a touch of charm and youth to the more somber black. She had draped it around herself hastily after her hurried flight to her room. She had not wanted to see him immediately after class, even though her note had said "urgent."

She had seen him as he left his dormitory, and had watched as he strode straight for the side entrance to her building.

A sinking sensation had seized her and she suddenly felt very weak and faint. How could she meet him now? 

She had expected him to wait till after lunch. No, she could not must not see him just yet. He would be sending for her at any moment now. She managed to pen the brief note. What strange thing was this? Why should she be so swayed by love? Was it love? True love surely did not bring such pain! How ridiculous that she should feel thus for one whom she had known only a few months! She must not allow herself to lose her equilibrium. No, no, the true Christian girl must be poised and calm, quiet and controlled. She must not let him know that she cared.

Such had been her determination and such had been the motives that had caused her to seek to hide from him her love. But hide it she could not. Then something had happened and in the intervening weeks she had been torn between an almost mad desire to tell him good-bye

forever, and the more tempting longing to ask him for an explanation.

There was a knock at the door and Gerald's card was presented. Betty handed the envelope to the waiting girl. "Thank you, Nina," she said simply. The door closed and she returned pensively to her vigil at the window. 

She saw a vast sweep of city, terracing southward in jagged irregularity. Below her in the court men and women, students and faculty alike, hurried to and fro with Bibles under their arms. Some stopped and chatted. 

Men moved along in pairs, as if discussing something of vital and tremendous consequence. 

Then she saw him once more, as his footsteps fell heavily upon the grey asphalt of the court. His eyes were downcast and his shoulders drooped, as if he were carrying a heavy burden. She watched until he disappeared up the steps. For a full minute she tarried there, her eyes fastened to the doorway through which he had just passed. Then the door opened and a youth dashed out hurriedly, evidently bent on some errand of importance. 

A boy with a Western Union cap rode up on a bicycle and disappeared within. A moment later he came out and rode away. And so the stream of humanity continued to flow.

Betty waited. It seemed that life was about to lose for her its one great satisfying portion. She sank into the depths of a lounging chair and buried her face in her hands. In that moment the arch enemy of the souls of men opened before her the door to self pity, through which he bade her enter that thus she might find solace from her new sorrow. This door he always opens to those torn by grief. Betty knew that door well. She had discovered that it leads only to despair. And so she turned away, seeking eagerly the open Bible on the window sill. 

The bell rang for lunch but she did not notice it. A

greater need held her. Her soul was nourished and strengthened. At last she closed the book.

From a drawer she drew out a letter, which she opened with trembling fingers. It was dated September 18 and, although the name was written in longhand, the letter itself had been written with a typewriter. How often she had read and reread this letter in the past weeks! It ran: 

Miss Elizabeth Dreanard,

Student, C. B. I. 

Dear Elizabeth:

I regret deeply if I have caused you in any way to believe that I cared for you. I have had no such intentions whatsoever, and feel that I owe it to you in all candor to say that for the good of all concerned, we had better allow our pathways to merge no longer. Sincerely, Gerald Dawnery. 

The word, "Elizabeth," had been enough.

A tear glistened hesitatingly, before it fell unbidden upon the word "Gerald."

The breach had continued to widen between them as the days and weeks flew by. Had Gerald but known the subtleties of the underworld and their persistent annoyance of those who are in possession of great wealth, and had he but been allowed to listen to a conversation which took place two nights prior to the day Betty received the letter purported to be from him, he would have rushed to her at once and explained it all.

One by one the stars came out and added their bit to the glory of the heavens. Night birds called from the shore and fireflies appeared and disappeared in phosphorescent flashes. And still the oars of the boat continued their silent dippings, while the shore line crept by. 

It was a night for love, when youth calls to youth and the answers are beautifully mutual and right. Betty, lounging lazily in the other end of the boat, watched the oarsman dreamily, as his arms moved slowly back and forth. He was using only enough energy to keep the boat in motion.

A sheer, navy blue frock made her very attractive tonight. Her right hand trailed in the passing waters. 

To speak now would be to break the spell, and yet

Gerald knew that the moon, the soft lapping of the waves against the boat and the calling of night birds were conspiring to impel him to say the words which for months he had repressed. Tonight he knew that he must tell her. His eyes had betrayed him a thousand times in the past year, he knew; but had she understood?

The last bit of amber from the train of the departed sun faded slowly away. Betty lifted her eyes to the moon and gazed long and steadily at it. Then she spoke softly, musingly: "A silver moon, a boat, a crystal lake, night birds-a perfect night." "Yes," he said, for a minute allowing the oars to rest, while he clasped his hands about his left knee and leaned back. She was lovely tonight, more so than ever. “A moon, a boat, a crystal lake, night birds and-" he knew he was going to say it, and he could not help himself- "love!"

She did not answer, but with the word it was as if someone had touched the strings of her soul and a chord of exquisite harmony had begun to vibrate there. 

Again he spoke: "Do you recall the time in a far western state when a certain young man, one morning at the crest of a high hill, heard the challenging words 'I dare you'?" 

Briefly she waited. Then she answered, "Yes, I remember." "That young man," he said solemnly, "has authorized me to say that from that very moment he has been using every ounce of ingenuity and persistency he has possessed in an attempt to find the answers." "And did he enjoy the task so recklessly assumed? Did he find the conundrums difficult?"

Their boat had drifted to the shore and its prow was touching the sands. The branches of a willow swooped downward as if to shadow them. The moonbeams poured in, leaving only irregular patches of darkness on the shore and on the water. The boat had found one of those shadowed places.

"You are cold," he said as he became suddenly aware of the breeze from the lake. "Your fault! You promised to go back sooner." "My fault, yes." He had removed his coat and was placing it gently about her shoulders. "Rowing will keep me warm," he defended, anticipating a possible objection. "Thank you, Gerald." She smiled up at him. "And may I sit beside you?" he requested. "It's lonely so far away!"

She made room for him without speaking. In the movements her hand touched his. The touch thrilled her.

And then he reached out, slowly, and gently encased that hand in his own.

Her heart began to flutter furiously, while a beautiful emotion staggered her. Softly her hand lay in his. She wanted it to be there, loved the sense of security it brought to have it there.

Then his words came once more, as steadily as he could command them. She could feel the strength of the man behind each word. His other hand reached out to assist the first one in its newly-permitted privilege. "Betty, my Betty! I have lived for this hour!" His voice was husky. "Ever since our first ride together in the old taxi. Do you remember?"

And then he told her of his love, told her passionately, worshipfully. "Tell me!" he cried. "Do you-can you -love me?"

He felt the precious hand tremble in his own and the form at his side lean toward him ever so slightly. And then he could wait no longer. He drew her to him in a long tender embrace, as she whispered: "Yes, Jerry dear, with all my heart, and forever!"

A moment later she breathed happily: "And we will carry the gospel together, Jerry. 'For whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people and-"

He joined her after the first few words and they quoted the passage to its end-together.
