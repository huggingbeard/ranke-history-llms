Bicentennial Message 

In honor of our nation's bicentennial anniversary, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit has commissioned three projects which collectively reflect this court's enduring interest in its past and future contributions to the legal system and the judicial branch of the 

Government. 

The first project commissioned by this court was the design and fabrication of an Eighth Circuit seal in the form of a hooked wall hanging. The seal, which is presently located in the office of the Clerk of the United States Court of Appeals, is symbolic of the rich traditions and future institutional role of this court. 

As a second bicentennial project, the court heard oral arguments in pending appeals at the University of South Dakota, the University of 

North Dakota, Drake University and the University of Iowa. This project allowed law students and others not directly involved with appellate practice to observe this court in operation and to become more familiar with our function and procedures.

Finally, this court commissioned the research and preparation of an Eighth Circuit History, which traces the evolutionary development and traditions of the Eighth Circuit. By looking to the past, we can place in proper perspective the contemporary problems confronting this court. 

By looking to the future, we can gain a greater understanding of and appreciation for the institutional and adjudicatory problems that judges will encounter throughout the third century of our nation's existance. It is hoped that we and future judges of the Eighth Circuit will continue to reflect an abiding adherence to existing principles of law coupled with a responsiveness to inevitable change, a sensitivity to societal problems and the resiliency to cope with novel and sensitive legal issues. If so, the cause of justice will continue to be served responsibly and effectively in the Eighth Circuit.

We appreciate the assistance of Dr. Theodore J. Fetter, our historian; 

Muriel Nezhnie Helfman, the designer of our seal; the faculty and students at the aforementioned universities; and the Bicentennial Committee of the United States Judicial Conference.

CHAPTER I THE CIRCUIT COURT 

Since the passage of the Judiciary Act of 1789, the judicial system of the United States has been remarkably stable. Compared to the changes in the executive branch, the judiciary (as well as the Congress) has continued to function along the broad outlines prepared by the founding fathers. Its goal is unchanged, the formal method of judicial selection is the same, and its procedures, while greatly altered in detail, follow from the same principles and guidelines. The major exception to this stability has been the circuit courts, the middle position in the federal court structure. 

The Early Federal Court System. 

Article III of the Constitution establishes a Supreme Court “and such inferior courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish." It tersely sets forth the jurisdiction of the federal courts and the manner of judicial selection, but so clearly that only one constitutional amendment has modified it in 189 years. 

The first Congress established the original federal court system by the Judiciary Act of 1789. That Act established the district court as the principal federal trial court. The district courts were independent of the state courts, but they reflected state interests and prejudices since each state formed one or more federal districts. (Virginia and Massachusetts has two districts each.) The Supreme Court was the appellate court in the system.

In addition, the Act established the circuit court, but it provided for no circuit judges. The Act divided the states into three circuits. Two 

Supreme Court justices and a district judge sat twice a year in each district of the circuit. The circuit court heard most of the cases arising out of diversity of citizenship, presumably because it would reflect individual state interests somewhat less than the state-based district courts. While the circuit court also had some appellate jurisdiction over the district courts, it functioned primarily as a trial court. 

Congress thus established a three-tier court system staffed by two tiers of judges. Supreme Court justices rode the circuit several months each year. From the start the assignment as Circuit Justice was an onerous duty generally disliked by the justices. Observers doubted the capacity of the justices to carry out their duties both in Washington and on the circuit. As the country expanded, of course, circuit riding became more and more questionable. "More territory implied more 1

circuits. More circuits meant either more circuit riding for the justices or more justices for circuit riding.”¹

The first attorney general noted the effect of the circuit riding requirements on the justices in his first annual report. Edmund 

Randolph argued that the justices needed all their energy and talent to discharge their first responsibility to "pronounce the law of the land without appeal." Randolph continued: "But what leisure remains from their itinerant dispensation of justice? Sum up all the fragments of their time, hold their fatigue at naught, and let them bid adieu to all domestic concerns, still the average term of a life, already advanced, will be too short for any important proficiency."2

Randolph also questioned how the justices who served on the circuit. court in a case would act if the case came to the Supreme Court on appeal.

Gouverneur Morris displayed some humor when discussing circuit riding in the Senate in 1802: "I am not quite convinced that riding rapidly from one end of this country to the other is the best way to study law. I am inclined to believe that knowledge may be more conveniently acquired in the closet than in the high road.”³

There remained, of course, several arguments in favor of the system. 

Requiring the appellate judges to serve in trial courts regularly might give them a wider experience with the operation of the courts. The justices closely observed the states in which they traveled. When state cases came before the Supreme Court, this familiarity should have helped them. Also, the Supreme Court justices could not become cloistered. They worked with the bar throughout the country, and they dealt with the citizens who came before them.

Circuit riding continued to be part of the Supreme Court justices' routine until 1869. Throughout the period, however, district judges sat more and more frequently in the circuit courts, especially in the new states added in the West. Congress created a seventh circuit in 1807 to include Kentucky, Tennessee, and Ohio, and the corollary to this decision was the appointment of a seventh member of the Supreme Court. The pressure for more circuits and thus more justices continued. 

Citizens of states in the larger Western circuits complained that the circuit justice never visited them, and the justices complained of the rising demands of circuit court work. Throughout these years Congress considered many bills to change the system, but the legislators could not agree on any significant measures. In 1838 the nine justices reported to the Senate the miles each of them traveled that year on circuit duty. They ranged from Chief Justice Taney's 458 miles to serve the Fourth Circuit (Maryland and Deleware) to Justice John McKinley's reported 10,000 miles to attend to the Ninth Circuit (Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Arkansas). Even with this total, Justice 

McKinley had not been able to get to Little Rock because of travel difficulties. Six of the justices had traveled over 2,000 miles in the year.

During the last half of the nineteenth century the workload of the federal courts increased substantially. In the 1860's the Supreme Court's caseload doubled, and in the 1870's it doubled again. Congress enlarged the jurisdiction of the federal courts in 1875, allowing suits asserting a right recognized in federal law to begin in federal courts. The number of suits arising from diversity of citizenship increased dramatically as the pace of commercial activity grew. Also, litigation grew from new federal laws, such as the Fourteenth Amendment and the bank acts, from war claims, and from increased economic activity at the state level.8

Lyman Trumbull of Illinois, the chairman of the Senate Judiciary Committee, led the congressional forces for judicial reform in the 1860's. He sought to reduce the demands on the Supreme Court by establishing intermediate courts of appeals. The various proposals that followed foundered on the rocks of the major political issue of the day, the conflict between the radical Republicans and the moderate Reconstructionists and the resulting institutional strife between 

President and Congress. 

With Grant in the White House in 1869, judicial reorganization advanced. Senator Trumbull proposed a measure that would ease the pressure of circuit riding without establishing intermediate appellate courts. He proposed to establish nine circuit judgeships which would bear most of the circuit court work. The circuit judge, the circuit justice, or a district judge could hold court, or any two of them could sit together as a panel. The bill became law on April 10, 1869.9 It was by no means a comprehensive approach to federal court problems. The 

Supreme Court still had an intolerable appellate burden, and the justices still had a statutory requirement to attend circuit court in each district of the circuit at least once every two years. The district courts also received no major relief from the burdens of higher volume. The egislation did, however, establish on a national basis a middle tier of judges to work in the middle tier of courts which had always existed. 

From 1869 to 1911 circuit judges, as well as circuit justices and district judges, operated the circuit courts, but few observers of the court system were satisfied. The circuit court was still a nisi prius court like the district court and the district judges held circuit court much of the time. In 1876 the House Judiciary Committee investigated district and circuit court operations and reported the condition in various districts throughout the nation for 1875. In Arkansas the circuit judge was in attendance only one week per year, although the circuit court

The Eighth Circuit. 

During the first half of the nineteenth century, Congress frequently realigned the circuits as new states came into the Union. The Act of 

July 15, 1862 described a circuit which begins to resemble the later Eighth Circuit, although at the time it was called the Ninth. The Ninth 

Circuit in 1862 included Missouri, Iowa, Kansas, and Minnesota, to which Congress added Wisconsin in 1863. The Act of March 25, 1867 aligned the circuits in a form very close to the later circuits of the courts of appeals. The Eighth Circuit then included Minnesota, Iowa,

Missouri, Kansas, Arkansas, and Nebraska. Subsequently, states in the 

Great Plains and Mountains admitted to the Union joined the Eighth 

Circuit: Colorado in 1876, North and South Dakota in 1889, Wyoming in 1890, Utah in 1896, and the Territories of New Mexico and Oklahoma by Supreme Court order in 1900. From his appointment to the Court in 1862 until his death in 1890, Justice Samuel F. Miller of 

Iowa served as circuit justice for these states. 

With the addition of these states and territories, the Eighth was by far the largest circuit. It included approximately one-third of the continental United States. It covered states which led the nation in agriculture, mining, railroad construction, and other fields of economic growth. By the end of the period, it had both the highest population and largest caseload.

The Circuit Court from 1891 to 1911. 

With the establishment of the Circuit Court of Appeals in 1891 (see next chapter) the old circuit court became again a court without a full-time judge. The court continued to function, however, until 1911. 

Generally a district judge would sit in circuit court, but the judges of the court of appeals sometimes held circuit court. Some court of appeals judges sat in the trial court more than others, and Judge Van Devanter wrote in 1910 that he had presided over several dozen major circuit court cases covering antitrust prosecutions, railroad rate cases, and felony charges against two senators. 19 The circuit court after 1891 had no appellate jurisdiction, but it continued to share original jurisdiction with the district courts. Where the jurisdiction overlapped, confusion ensued, since many statutes granted one court or the other jurisdiction in a particular type of action. 

Individuals and groups made many attempts to abolish the circuit court: 

The American Bar Association recommended it in 1894, a Commission on the Comprehensive Revision of Laws joined in the recommendation in 1899, and a Joint Congressional Committee on the Revision of Laws followed suit in 1907. The joint committee drafted a major revision of statutes in 1910 which culminated in the enactment of the Judicial Code of 1911. The new code abolished the circuit courts and established the district courts for the first time as the dominant court of original jurisdiction in the federal system.20

CHAPTER II THE ESTABLISHMENT OF THE CIRCUIT COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT The Circuit Courts of Appeals Act. 

By 1890 the conditions which had plagued the federal court system for decades had come to a climax. The Supreme Court was inundated. 

Its appellate jurisdiction had been modified slightly in previous statutes, but it still had very little control over its docket. In 1884 the Court had 

1,315 cases on the docket, in 1888 there were 1,563, and in 1890 the Court convened with 1,800 cases before it. Circuit riding had generally ceased in spite of the statutory requirement. The circuit courts had likewise become a pale imitation of what the statutes intended. The Act of 1869 favored a panel of two judges in circuit court, one of whom would be either the circuit justice or the circuit judge. In the 1880's eight-ninths of the circuit court cases were heard by single judges, usually district judges. The Eighth and Ninth Circuits were the two busiest (since the Second received an additional circuit judge in 1887), and they also covered the greatest geographical area. 

The old circuit court always had some appellate jurisdiction. By the late 1880's it was common to see a single district judge sitting in circuit court on an appeal from his own decision in the district court. An ABA report in 1890 characterized the situation well: "Such an appeal is not from Philip drunk to Philip sober, but from Philip sober to Philip intoxicated with the vanity of a matured opinion and doubtless also a published decision."¹

Also, circuit court decisions were not appealable at all in cases involving less than $5,000 (the result of an unsuccessful attempt to lighten the Supreme Court's work load), so dissatisfied litigants in these cases could not seek further relief.

Congress had long recognized these problems. The legislators considered many bills which addressed the issues. Some created an intermediate appellate court. Others curtailed the opportunity to pursue litigation in federal court. Still others reduced the right of appeal to the Supreme Court. The proponents of the various proposals 'Walter B. Hill, “The Federal Judicial System," 12 ABA Rep. 289 at 307, quoted in Frankfurter and Landis, p. 87. 11

clashed strongly in Congress without producing major legislation.2 The late nineteenth century was an extremely partisan era, and federal judicial appointments were among the most prized rewards the government could bestow. In addition, the major political issues of the time frequently came to the federal courts for resolution. Issues involving regulation of commerce and industry, state rights, and banking and currency dominated, and legislators addressed the structural organization of the courts with a view to its effect on the particular issue they emphasized. 

Both Grover Cleveland's first attorney general, August H. Garland, and his successor under Benjamin Harrison, William H. H. Miller, recommended an intermediate appellate court. President Harrison concurred in his first annual message to the Congress in December, 1889. Throughout this time over 20 bills for an intermediate appellate court were introduced in Congress. 

In April, 1890, the House passed a bill by Rep. John H. Rogers of 

Arkansas. His bill eliminated the circuit courts and the circuit justices and created nine appellate courts with three judges each, which would have final decision in diversity of citizenship cases, subject to certiorari by the Supreme Court. In the Senate William M. Evarts prepared a substitute. The Evarts bill also established new appellate courts, but it provided for direct access to the Supreme Court in particular types of cases from the district and circuit courts. The bill provided one additional judge in each circuit, and the new appellate courts would include the two circuit judges and a district judge. Evarts received powerful support since his bill made as few changes as possible in the traditional structure and still would relieve the Supreme Court of a substantial number of cases. The Supreme Court generally favored the 

Evarts bill and Chief Justice Melville W. Fuller worked with Evarts in the drafting. The Senate passed the Evarts bill by a wide margin in 

September, 1890. The Evarts bill prevailed in the conference committee, and the Circuit Court of Appeals Act became law on March 3, 1891.3 

The new courts quickly began operation in 1891. They relieved the burden on the Supreme Court and provided additional judges to attend to federal litigation throughout the country. Generally President 

Harrison's appointments to the new courts (as well as most of his judicial nominations) were excellent, largely because of the extensive canvassing and investigation carried on by the Justice Department under Attorney General Miller.

Henry Clay Caldwell. Henry C. Caldwell was perhaps the ideal judge to preside over the sprawling Eighth Circuit. His background bridged the sectional antagonisms inherent in a circuit that included the Plains, the Rocky Mountains, and part of the Confederacy. Also, both 

Republicans and Democrats respected him greatly and supported him for high office.

Caldwell was born in 1832 in Marshall County, Virginia, and moved with his family to Davis County, Iowa, in 1836. He worked in a law office in Keosauqua, Van Buren County, Iowa, after his secondary school education, and was admitted to the Iowa bar in 1852. In 1856 he was elected the county prosecutor, and in 1858 he went to the state legislature where he was chairman of the judiciary committee. In 1860 he was a delegate to the Republican convention in Chicago and supported Lincoln. At the outbreak of the Civil War he joined the 

Third Iowa Cavalry and later became a colonel, engaged in much fighting in the lower Mississippi Valley.

In early 1864, with the Union Army in control of almost all of Arkansas, Col. Caldwell applied for the district judgeship vacant there. 

He had the support of the leaders of the Iowa bar. Justice Samuel F. 

Miller recommended him to Lincoln. The Iowa congressional delegation remarked that “his connection with the regiment has given him great opportunity to mingle with the people in Arkansas, and win the confidence of the loyal and well-disposed portion of that people.” 

Judge Caldwell came to Arkansas as an Iowan and a Union soldier, but he gradually earned the respect of the state's citizens. He was cautious in federal cases during Reconstruction, and he actively supported the efforts of Arkansans to regain home rule. He also worked for several major reforms in the legal procedures of Arkansas. 

When Judge Brewer resigned the Eighth Circuit position to go on the Supreme Court, President Harrison selected Caldwell for the circuit court. Judge Caldwell apparently did not seek the job. The 

Department of Justice screened at least 27 possible nominees, however, including Sanborn, Thayer, and Frank B. Kellogg, later Secretary of

Walter Henry Sanborn. Walter H. Sanborn sat on the Eighth 

Circuit court for over 35 years. He had doubtless one of the best legal minds of his generation, and his many opinions are thorough, clear, and forceful.

Sanborn received wide support for the additional circuit judgeship in 1891 from lawyers and public figures in Minnesota, New Hampshire, Nebraska, North Dakota, and South Dakota. Amos Madden Thayer. Amos M. Thayer of St. Louis was the last of the original three appellate circuit judges to be appointed. Congress authorized an additional seat on the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals in 1894,¹¹ and Thayer got the seat. He had frequently sat on the court as a district judge of the Eastern District of Missouri. 

Thayer was born in 1841 in Chautauqua County, New York. His father was a farmer. Thayer graduated from Hamilton College in 1862 and joined the Union Army. At the end of the war he was a major and served as chief signal officer of the Department of the Susquehanna. 

He moved to St. Louis in 1866 and studied law in his uncle's law office. He practiced law from 1868 to 1876, when he was elected judge of the state circuit court in St. Louis on the Democratic ticket. In 1887 he sought and secured the U. S. District judgeship. He told Senator F. 

M. Cockrell, “Judicial work accords with my taste and disposition better than work at the Bar, and I am naturally desirous of a broader field of action." A St. Louis attorney supported Thayer, characterizing him as “a model judge and a sterling Democrat."12

In 1890 Thayer also served on the faculty of the law school of Washington University in St. Louis. He used this position to advocate legal education as part of an academic system rather than as an apprenticeship.

These three judges launched the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals. 

They came from different political backgrounds and from different states in the circuit, but taken together they demonstrated what legal opinion of the day most sought: soundness of mind, extensive legal, political and judicial experience, and a familiarity with the legal issues which developed from the dominant agricultural and commercial interests of the region.

CHAPTER III THE GROWTH OF THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT COURT: 

THE SANBORN YEARS 

From 1902 to 1929 the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals grew in size, volume of cases, and number of citizens it served. It sat in St. Louis, St. 

Paul, and Denver, forcing judges and lawyers to travel hundreds of miles and correspond by letter when not in session. The Eighth Circuit received a fourth judge in 1903, a judge from the Commerce Court from 1911 to 1922, and two additional judges in 1925. The number of cases docketed went from 173 in 1902 to 405 in 1929. It was generally the second busiest of the circuit courts, following the Second Circuit. It had its share of headline making cases, such as the Northern Securities case in 1903, the Standard Oil Trust case in 1911, and the Teapot 

Dome case in 1926.

At the same time it maintained a high reputation for quality. Walter 

H. Sanborn, chief judge after 1903, was greatly admired by the bench and bar. Willis Van Devanter served on the court before going to the 

Supreme Court. William S. Kenyon joined the court following a distinguished career in the U.S. Senate. Other judges won wide respect, including Kimbrough Stone and Robert E. Lewis. These judges gave the Eighth Circuit a tradition of respect, continuity, and hard work. 19

Not only did the court's volume increase, but the type of case changed during this period. The Circuit Court of Appeals for the 

Eighth Circuit did not docket its first criminal case until 1895. By 1908 the caseload was about 10% criminal, a percentage maintained until the 1920's. Then, doubtless because of the Prohibition legislation, the work became 20% criminal in 1922 and 30% criminal from 1924 through the rest of the decade.

Biographical Sketches. 

In this period eleven new judges served on the court. All but one had periods of service of seven years or more, so that the sense of continuity and tradition governed the court. 

Willis Van Devanter. Willis Van Devanter of Wyoming was the first member of the Circuit Court of Appeals from the western part of the circuit. That region had argued since 1891 for a representative on the court who would reflect its interest in mining, ranching, public lands, and irrigation.

Van Devanter was born in Marion, Indiana, in 1859. His father, Isaac 

Van Devanter, was a lawyer and an outspoken abolitionist. Van 

Devanter attended Indiana Asbury University (later De Pauw) and received his law degree from the University of Cincinnati Law School in 1881. He practiced law with his father for three years, also serving as the assistant prosecutor in Marion. When his father retired in July, 1884, Van Devanter and another associate, John W. Lacey, moved to 

Cheyenne, Wyoming, where Lacey had been appointed territorial chief justice.

Van Devanter enjoyed his life in Wyoming. Cheyenne was a boom town because of the cattle drives, and Van Devanter soon became a prominent lawyer there representing railroad, land, and cattle interests and occasionally some horse thieves and cattle rustlers. He became very active in Republican politics in Wyoming as an ally and advisor of territorial governor (later senator) Francis E. Warren. Van Devanter served as commissioner to revise Wyoming statutes in 1886, city attorney in Cheyenne in 1887 and 1888, and a member of the territorial legislature in 1888. After Wyoming became a state Van 

Devanter was the Republican state chairman and the party's national committeeman for the state.

In 1889, though just 31 years old, Van Devanter became chief justice of the territorial supreme court. President Harrison appointed him on the advice of territorial governor Francis E. Warren.¹ Van

Van Devanter served for twenty-seven years on the Supreme Court. 

His opinions reflect a concern for the right to property and an opposition to increased federal government economic controls. These characteristics made him a major figure in Franklin Roosevelt's clash with the "Nine Old Men" in the 1930's.

Van Devanter did not appear to the public as a leader of the Supreme Court. He wrote few of the famous opinions, but he worked long and hard on areas of law which some of his colleagues avoided, such as water rights, land issues, and corporation law. Within the Court he was the most skillful negotiator, a role that led Taft as Chief Justice to call him “far and away the most valuable man in our Court. Van

William C. Hook. Theodore Roosevelt appointed William C. Hook, of federal district judge in Kansas, to the Eighth Circuit Court upon the retirement of Henry C. Caldwell in 1903. William C. Hook was born in Pennsylvania in 1857, but during his boyhood his father, a merchant, moved to Julesburg, Colorado, and Leavenworth, Kansas. Hook studied law in a Leavenworth law office and received an L.L.B. degree from Washington University in St. Louis in 1878. He practiced law in 

Leavenworth for over twenty years. During this time he became a prominent Kansas attorney. In one of his interesting cases he served as counsel for Fred Harvey when the Santa Fe Railroad tried to nullify a contract for Harvey to operate the railroad's dining car service. Hook won the suit for Harvey, who continued to spread his restaurant chain throughout the southwest.

Hook entered the judiciary in 1899. President McKinley, whom 

Hook had supported in 1896 as chairman of the Kansas delegation to the Republican convention, appointed him to a district judgeship. 

Hook joined the circuit court four years later, where he had a lively career as a circuit judge. His opinions in several major antitrust cases put him in the public eye. His well-written opinions in the Standard Oil case and the Union Pacific-Southern Pacific merger case (see below) attracted much comment, both positive and negative. Hook served on the court until his death in August, 1921. 

Elmer B. Adams. In 1905, following the death of Amos M. Thayer, the second Missourian joined the Eighth Circuit, establishing Missouri's hold on the seat which has never been broken. Elmer B. Adams was born in Pomfret, Vermont, in 1842. He graduated from Yale in 1865 and then joined the American Union Commission, with which he traveled to Georgia to establish free schools for white children. He returned to Vermont in 1866, studied law there and at Harvard, and was admitted to the Vermont bar in 1868. 

Adams moved to St. Louis the same year and began his practice. He was a judge of the state circuit court from 1879 to 1885, but he declined to run for reelection and returned to private practice. In 1895 

President Cleveland appointed him district judge for the Eastern District of Missouri and ten years later Theodore Roosevelt elevated him to the circuit bench. Judge Adams served there for eleven years until his death in 1916. 

John Emmett Carland. John E. Carland came to the Eighth Circuit as a judge of the U.S. Commerce Court. President Taft requested a specialized court to handle interstate commerce issues in 1910, and Congress established the court over the opposition of progressives of both parties who feared the court would too often favor the railroads 23

against the ICC. Taft appointed five judges to the court, including 

Carland, but the criticism of the court continued. Of the 22 Commerce 

Court cases appealed, the Supreme Court reversed thirteen. In 1912 the Congress abolished the court but Taft vetoed the bill. In 1913, with 

Democrats in control of Congress and Wilson in the White House, legislation to abolish the court succeeded, but the five judges were assigned to serve on the circuit courts. Carland came to the Eighth 

Circuit under the provision that upon his death or resignation his position would not be filled.

Carland was born in Oswego County, New York, in 1853. He attended the University of Michigan and was admitted to the Michigan bar in 1875, after which he moved to Bismarck, Dakota Territory, to practice law. President Cleveland appointed him U.S. Attorney in the 

Dakota Territory in 1885 and associate justice of the territorial supreme court in 1888. In 1889 he was a member of the North Dakota 

Constitutional Convention, in which the Democratic Carland was chairman of the judiciary committee in spite of a two-to-one Republican majority among the delegates. He then moved to Sioux 

Falls, South Dakota, where he practiced law and was active in Democratic politics. In 1896 he was chairman of the South Dakota delegation to the Democratic national convention. That convention marked the rise of William Jennings Bryan on the tide of silver coinage, and Carland described his role as leading "the only delegation west of the Mississippi which voted solidly in support of the principles of the present Administration."7

In the summer of 1896, with the Democratic party split between the gold-standard Cleveland Democrats and the Bryan forces, the administration had to fill the vacant district judgeship in South Dakota. It was natural for Cleveland to reward his supporters, and Carland received the appointment in August. In addition to his political connections, however, Carland clearly had a high reputation. Two circuit judges from both parties, Sanborn and Thayer, recommended him. The Pennington County, South Dakota bar association, along with other county bars, gave him their endorsement with a petition signed by several lawyers, including the rising young attorney Archibald K.

Walter I. Smith. When Willis Van Devanter went to the Supreme 

Court, President Taft appointed Iowa congressman Walter I. Smith to the Eighth Circuit vacancy. Except for a nine month interval, an Iowan has ever since held that seat.

Walter Smith was the first Eighth Circuit judge who did not emigrate from the state of his birth. He was born in Council Bluffs, Iowa, in 

1862. He studied law in a local law office and was admitted to the bar at the age of 20. In 1890 he was elected a state district judge in Council 

Bluffs. He served for ten years and was twice reelected. Smith was elected to Congress to fill an unexpired term in 1900 as a Republican and served until his appointment to the circuit court of appeals in 1911. 

As a congressman he was a leader of the Appropriations Committee. 

He also played a prominent role in the reform of the House of Representatives in 1910 which reduced the power of the speaker and enhanced the position of the committee chairmen. 

In early 1911 Smith was in contention for two offices. In addition to the Eighth Circuit judgeship, he was being promoted as a compromise candidate for the vacancy in the United States Senate to succeed 

Jonathan P. Dolliver. The Iowa Legislature was deadlocked over the

Senate seat, but after Smith went on the court it chose William S. 

Kenyon for the position. Kenyon later resigned from the Senate to succeed to Smith's court seat.

Smith served on the Eighth Circuit court until his death in 1922. For a time before his death a lingering illness prevented him from contributing fully to the court's work. 

Kimbrough Stone. Kimbrough Stone was one of the dominant figures on the Eighth Circuit court. He served for 30 years and was senior judge for 19; he was one of the key members of the Conference of Senior Circuit Judges, the forerunner of the U. S. Judicial 

Conference.

Stone was born in Nevada, Missouri, in 1875 to a family that had long distinguished itself in American public affairs. One ancestor, 25

William Stone, came to Virginia in 1632 and became governor of Maryland in 1648. This promising career came to an end in the furor between Catholics and Protestants in the colony. Stone was removed from office in 1650 and his attempted return in 1652 led to an unsuccessful insurrection against the party which had ousted him. 

Thomas Stone of the Revolutionary period was a signer of the Declaration of Independence and a draftsman of the Articles of Confederation. Kimbrough Stone was the son of William J. Stone, who moved to Missouri after yeoman service in the Confederate Army. 

William Stone became a lawyer, a Democratic congressman, governor, and U.S. Senator from 1902 until his death in 1918.9 

Kimbrough Stone grew up in Nevada, Missouri, except for a short period during his father's congressional tenure when he worked as a page in the House of Representatives. He graduated from the 

University of Missouri in 1895 and from Harvard Law School in 1898. 

He spent his final year of law school in Europe, studying primarily in Hanover.

Stone was admitted to the St. Louis bar and joined his father in his law practice there. He also served as clerk of the legislative committee that revised Missouri's statutes from 1899 to 1900. Starting in 1900 he took time out from his law practice to court Lucy May Cockrill, whom he married in 1906. Stone's strong devotion to "Miss Lucy" was a well-known part of the rest of his life. 

When his father went to the Senate, Kimbrough Stone moved to Kansas City and resumed his law practice. He enjoyed trial work and did most of the trial duties for the large firm with which he became associated. Stone began his judicial career as a court commissioner appointed by the Missouri Supreme Court in a case involving the city limits of Kansas City. In 1912 he was elected to the Jackson County 

Circuit Court, expecting to use the judicial experience in a law practice he would resume later. He served for four years, but he did not have the opportunity to return to private practice. 

When Elmer B. Adams died in 1916, Missouri Democrats suggested 

Kimbrough Stone for the position on the Eighth Circuit. In order to protect itself against a charge of nepotism, the Department of Justice investigated the career of the senator's son, only to find that not a single protest was lodged. The Senate confirmed the younger Stone unanimously, and he was sworn in on January 15, 1917, his fortysecond birthday. "This biographical sketch is taken largely from Elmo B. Hunter, "Judge 

Kimbrough Stone,” 26 Univ. of Kansas City L. Rev. 2-16 (December-February 1948). 26

Judge Stone's service on the Eighth Circuit court was remarkable. He sat in over 1,800 cases and wrote the court's opinion in almost 700. He thoroughly studied the law, compiling throughout his career a personal analysis of every Supreme Court opinion on particular fields of law. His work as senior judge (later called chief judge) for the circuit is described later, but it included a leadership role on the Conference of Senior Circuit Judges and the organization of the first circuit conferences in the United States.

Judge Stone was always patient and understanding with the lawyers who practiced in his court. Whenever possible, attorneys had access to his office, and they "never saw an unpleasant thing happen around them" while discussing their business with the judge.10 In oral argument he was always courteous. On one occasion a lawyer who was arguing a case before the court of appeals had used forty-five minutes of his allotted hour without contributing to the issues involved. One judge on the panel had tried to clarify matters with several questions without success. He finally gave Judge Stone a note saying, “Do you understand what this case is all about?" Stone replied, "Not yet, but he still has fifteen minutes in which to tell us."11

A few statistics measure the degree of Judge Stone's influence on the court. In the 1800 cases in which he sat, he wrote only 51 dissents and five concurring opinions. Of his almost 700 majority opinions, the

Supreme Court reversed only twelve and modified two. The Supreme 

Court also affirmed sixteen and denied certiorari on 125.12

One of Judge Stone's great interests in his later years concerned Communism. He greatly feared the threat that the ideology posed to the United States, both internal and external. While the sentiment was not unusual during the 1940's, it is remarkable to find that Stone's comments on the issue were always the result of thorough study instead of quick conclusions. His opinion in Dunne v. United States (183 F.2d 

137), which upheld a conviction of Smith Act conspiracy violations for 

18 Communists, demonstrates this careful approach.13

In 1906, Moses Hallett resigned from his federal district judgeship. 

Hallett had been a prominent judge for many years and had been prominently mentioned for the Eighth Circuit judgeships of the 1890's. 

While a lawyer named K. R. Babbitt seemed to be in line for the district court position, criticism of Babbitt developed. The chairman of the Republican State Central Committee recommended Lewis, charging that Babbitt had worked mainly for mining companies and could be "classed among the trusts." In 1921 Judge Lewis was promoted to the circuit bench, becoming the third person from the western part of the circuit (after Van 

Devanter of Wyoming and Hook of Kansas) to sit on the Circuit Court of Appeals. He was a member of the court until 1929, when Congress created the Tenth Circuit from the western half of the Eighth. At that time, as the senior circuit judge from the region, Lewis became the first Senior Judge of the new circuit. He died in 1941. 

William Squire Kenyon. The Eighth Circuit has had among its judges several men who have been extremely successful politicians. It has also had judges who have clearly spoken their mind on a wide range of public issues and thus added controversy to their careers on and off the bench. But it has seldom had a judge who combined these two characteristics as much as William S. Kenyon. 

Kenyon, born in Elyria, Ohio in 1869, was the son of the Reverend Fergus L. Kenyon, a Presbyterian pastor. The family moved to St. 

Joseph, Missouri in 1870 and to Iowa City, Iowa in 1878. His father had hoped Kenyon would become a preacher, but Kenyon decided early on a legal career. He attended Iowa College (later Grinnell) and received his law degree from the State University of Iowa in 1890. He then moved to Fort Dodge and began his practice.

Kenyon advanced rapidly in his legal career, always relishing trial work. From 1893 to 1897 he was the Webster County prosecuting attorney. In 1900, at the age of 31 he became a district judge in Iowa, but he resigned two years later, apparently because the salary was inadequate.15 His practice then included the Illinois Central Railroad, and he became its general counsel in 1906. In 1910 he resigned to accept President Taft's offer to be Assistant to the Attorney General.

Illustrative Cases. 

The Eighth Circuit was among the busiest of the circuit courts, and it saw its share of cases which made headlines and those which marked a new development in the law. During the early twentieth century the major area of constitutional development involved the government's regulation and control of corporations. Several of the largest antitrust cases came to the Supreme Court through the Eighth Circuit, including the Northern Securities case (193 U.S. 197), the Standard Oil case (221 U.S. 1), and the case involving the merger of the Union Pacific and the Southern Pacific railroads (226 U.S. 61). Also of historic importance was U.S. v. Mammoth Oil (14 F.2d 705), the Teapot Dome case. 

United States v. Northern Securities Co. et al (120 F. 721) was heard before the Circuit Court for the District of Minnesota in 1903. Judges 

Caldwell, Sanborn, Thayer and Van Devanter sat as a court of original jurisdiction. The four judges heard the case en banc following a 1903 statute20 requiring at least three circuit judges to hear a case certified by the Attorney General to be one of general public importance. 

The Department of Justice had filed suit to enjoin the merger of the 

Northern Pacific Railway, the Great Northern Railway, and the

Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railway. The government maintained that such a merger violated the Sherman Antitrust Act as an unlawful restraint upon interstate commerce. Until the Supreme Court heard the

Administering the Court.

As the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals grew larger in caseload and enlarged its clerical staff, its operation was becoming more complicated. 

The federal courts were organized on the premises of independence and local interests, and there was no centralized management or administrative oversight.34 "Congress created a hierarchical system of courts, not of judges."35 One of the tenets of governmental reform in the early twentieth century was the responsiveness of the government to the will of the citizenry as expressed through political action, and one of the frequent goals of political action was the orderly operation of government. 

Progressives discovered "efficiency," citing frequently the studies of 

Frederick Winslow Taylor on time and motion. Schools of business management flourished.

The movement toward efficiency was slow to affect the courts, traditionally the branch least subject to popular pressures. Roscoe Pound in his 1906 address on the causes of popular dissatisfaction with the courts may have started a movement, but it took years to spread. In the meantime the federal courts continued to operate much as they always had, in spite of the growing dockets.

CHAPTER IV THE DIVISION OF THE EIGHTH CIRCUIT 

The Eighth Circuit as drawn in 1866 was large. As new states entered the Union in the succeeding decades the demands on the circuit grew. 

The circuit stretched 1,000 miles from Canada to Louisiana and 1,000 miles from the Mississippi River to the salt flats of Utah. It also had the largest population of any circuit: 11,166,000 in 1890, and 18,259,000 in 1920, almost 4 million higher than the next most populous circuit. 

Congressmen from this region had long been concerned about the ability of the circuit court of appeals to serve their constituents. In addition, some of them doubtless welcomed the chance to secure more federal judgeships. Members of Congress introduced at least sixteen bills to establish a Tenth Circuit between 1891 and 1906, and most of these proposed to divide the Eighth Circuit.¹

In 1910 Judge Van Devanter, then under consideration for the Supreme Court, described the Eighth Circuit to Charles Nagel, Secretary of Commerce and Labor. Van Devanter faced criticism because he had written fewer court of appeals opinions than his colleagues. Van 

Devanter cited the size of the circuit among other factors. He wrote that the court met regularly in St. Louis, St. Paul and Denver. In St. 

Paul and Denver, Van Devanter insisted, he was doing as much work as anyone. St. Louis, he admitted, had more cases than the other cities, and he attended court there only in alternate years. He maintained that such a routine was not unusual since none of the judges attended all the terms. In Denver, where Van Devanter was always present, one of his associates had attended court only once and another only twice. He concluded: "The truth is, this circuit is too large. It embraces twelve states and one territory, has a population of five millions in excess of any other circuit and has a still greater relative proportion of railroad mileage."2

By the mid-1920's, a subcommittee of the American Bar Association agreed with Judge Van Devanter. In 1927 the committee recommended a realignment of several of the most congested circuits, the Second, the Eighth, and the Ninth. Promised more manpower, the judges supported the Newton bill. Newton's proposal passed the House unanimously in early 1929.

CHAPTER V THE KIMBROUGH STONE ERA 

Kimbrough Stone was the Senior Judge of the Eighth Circuit from the death of Walter Sanborn in 1928 until his own retirement in 1947. 

It was a time of continuity and established routine; most of the judges on the court had long periods of service, they met in St. Louis, Kansas 

City, and St. Paul at regular times, they corresponded with each other when writing opinions, and they were able to work well as a group without significant animosity. Yet at the same time the Eighth Circuit was changing, and Kimbrough Stone led the change. The Conference of 

Senior Circuit Judges, in which Judge Stone had a prominent role, became an important force in the federal court system. Each circuit reported to the Conference, so that the leading judges of the system became more aware of the developments in the whole system. The 

Conference made recommendations to the courts for administrative improvement and to the Congress for legislative action to help the courts. In addition, Stone developed circuit-wide management. He started the first circuit councils. He prodded judges who fell behind and assigned judges to help out where necessary. The theme of the 

Kimbrough Stone years may be that the court's administrative methods developed smoothly so that the benefits of continuity and good personal relationships could be preserved. 

The caseload of the circuit court of appeals fluctuated in this period. 

With the establishment of the Tenth Circuit, the backlog in the Eighth declined, and with the repeal of the Prohibition legislation, the number of criminal cases and total cases decreased. In the mid-1930's, however, cases related to the Depression and the New Deal regulations grew. In the 1940's the number of cases again declined, both in the Eighth Circuit and in all circuits combined. 47

Biographical Sketches. 

In 1929 Judge Stone had served on the court for twelve years. The other Eighth Circuit judges, Kenyon, Booth, and Van Valkenburgh, would die or retire in the early 1930's. Their departure led to a new group of judges in the Eighth Circuit who would lead the court over the next several decades Archibald K. Gardner, John B. Sanborn, Joseph W. Woodrough, and Seth Thomas. 

Archibald K. Gardner. A. K. Gardner of South Dakota filled the new seat on the Eighth Circuit authorized by the legislation that created the Tenth Circuit. Although he was 61 when he came to the court, he served for 31 years, the second longest period of service the Eighth 

Circuit has seen.

Gardner's father, William Gardner, immigrated to Pennsylvania from Scotland in 1851 and became a naturalized citizen. In the 1860's he lived for a time in Canada, and there Archibald K. Gardner was born in 

1867. The family moved to Newton, Iowa in 1868, where young 

Gardner grew up. Gardner attended Grinnel College and, after teaching school for two years, the University of Iowa. He received his 

A.B. degree from that school in 1892 and his law degree in 1893. 

Gardner was admitted to practice in Iowa but soon moved to Greenfield, Missouri, where he practiced law for two years. 

In 1895 Gardner moved to Rapid City, South Dakota. It was a boom time in the Black Hills, and Rapid City was growing tremendously. He practiced law there until 1907. He was elected State's Attorney in 1901 and served for four years in the only elective public office he ever held. 

In 1907 Gardner became the general attorney for one of the two railroads in South Dakota, the Chicago and Northwestern. He moved to Huron, South Dakota, for the job and formed a new partnership there in addition to his duties for the railroad. He was an indefatigable worker during his years of practice and came to be regarded as one of the leaders of the South Dakota bar.

He entered the circuit judgeship in 1929 without prior judicial experience, but he soon proved his ability in his new role. In 1947 he became the chief judge although, at 79, he was seven years older than his predecessor. He retired from that position in 1959 and from the court in 1961. He died in 1962 at the age of 94. 

Judge Gardner was a constant and methodical worker. He worked quickly, penetrating at once to the essential points of a case. In his career he wrote 837 majority opinions. That he preferred consensus to dissent is shown by his total of only 14 dissenting opinions and two 50

concurring opinions. As chief judge, Gardner discouraged dissents as much as possible. He preferred to do as much of his legal work as possible by himself, and he seldom used a law clerk's assistance. 

His memory was remarkable. In 1957 he was on a panel which heard a case involving search and seizure. As the lawyer was laboriously trying to establish a point, Judge Gardner, his voice then thin with age, interrupted the lawyer, saying, "I think we decided that point in an 

Eighth Circuit case. Why don't you cite our court? Didn't you look up 

53 Federal Second?" The lawyer, embarrassed, mumbled that he was sorry if he missed a case on the issue. Meanwhile, one of the other judges on the panel motioned to his law clerk to get the volume. The clerk found the case as he returned to the courtroom and handed it to the lawyer. The attorney glanced over the opinion written by Judge 

Gardner in 1931 and exclaimed, "Why, it's even on my side of the question!"'1

One of Judge Gardner's outside interests was hunting. He organized 

Gardner's Guerrillas in Huron in his later years, a group of about twenty-five prominent citizens who hunted together and enjoyed each other's company. Gardner also hunted with his colleagues on the bench. In 1934, and probably in many other years, he shared his pheasant catch with his fellow judges.2 In later years he lost his eyesight but he still liked to hunt. In the 1950's his much younger colleague on the Eighth Circuit, Judge Charles E. Whittaker, struck up a friendly conversation: 

"Judge, I understand you've bought a new gun." "That's right." "Did you go hunting?" "Yes, of course I went hunting." "Judge, did you hit anything?” "Young man," Gardner said indignantly, "you've gone too far!”³

Judge Gardner was a methodical, precise man who enjoyed his years on the bench. He gave up his responsibilities with reluctance when, his eyesight gone but his mind still keen, his wife or secretary had to read to him all his research. South Dakota has relatively little litigation before the court of appeals, and it has given only one regularly appointed judge to the court, but that one representative was a major influence. John B. Sanborn. The judge who came to the Eighth Circuit following Judge Gardner was John B. Sanborn of Minnesota. Sanborn served for 27 years, a period which matched very closely Gardner's term on the court. The two were both leaders with great talent and drive, and for many years they were the co-leaders of the court. 

John Sanborn was the cousin of Walter H. Sanborn, one of the original members of the circuit court of appeals. He was the son of 

General John B. Sanborn who had practiced law in St. Paul with his nephew Walter after an illustrious Civil War career. General Sanborn commanded the Fourth Minnesota Regiment which was very active in the operations along the Mississippi River and which was the first unit of the Union Army to enter Vicksburg when that city fell in 1863. 

John B. Sanborn was born in St. Paul in 1883 and lived there all his life. He graduated from the University of Minnesota in 1905 and from the St. Paul College of Law in 1907. Judge Sanborn practiced law in St. 

Paul from 1907 until 1922. For part of that time he was associated with the firm of Butler and Mitchell, which included Pierce Butler, later an 

Associate Justice of the Supreme Court. 

During these years Sanborn occupied several public offices. He was elected to the Minnesota House of Representatives as a Republican in 1913. In 1917-18 and again in 1919-20 he was the state commissioner of insurance. Between these periods of service, he enlisted as a private in the infantry. In 1920 he became a member of the Minnesota Tax 

Commission. In 1922 he was appointed a judge of Minnesota's second judicial district. After three years as a state judge, President Coolidge appointed him to the federal district court where he occupied the seat vacated by Judge Booth. 

John Sanborn was widely recommended to the circuit court. In 1928 when Walter Sanborn died, many in Minnesota recommended the late judge's cousin. Again in 1929, when Coolidge and then Hoover considered the appointment to the added seat on the Eighth Circuit bench, many, including Judge Booth, urged that Sanborn be named.* 

All of these letters testified to Sanborn's large capacity for work and his high ability. From 1925 to 1932 Sanborn, as a district judge, frequently sat with the circuit court of appeals. In 1932 when Judge Booth retired, the bench and bar greeted President Hoover's nomination of Sanborn with acclaim.

Judge Sanborn exerted a very great influence as a circuit judge. He wrote over 900 opinions, but like Gardner he wrote very few dissents. 

He was an intellectual leader of the court with an impeccable knowledge of the law; yet he had an even firmer sense of the people behind the legal issues. Both on and off the bench he enjoyed working with people. He enjoyed his ten years on state and federal trial courts because that experience kept him in constant touch with lawyers, litigants, witnesses, and jurors. He cometimes compared an appellate judge to a “gnome in the library," but he performed the library duties conscientiously and devotedly. Mr. Justice Blackmun, referring to both 

Sanborns, has said that while he and his Supreme Court colleagues are frequently reversing or modifying the opinions of the illustrious Hands of the Second Circuit, they almost always affirm the opinions of the Sanborns.6

Judge Sanborn spent his leisure on the banks of the St. Croix River north of St. Paul. There he had a summer home, and he could canoe, hunt, and plant trees and flowers. He enjoyed bringing his colleagues to this retreat whenever the court was in St. Paul. He also built a library adjacent to the house and put many of his law books, his father's books, and his own extensive collection in the library. Unfortunately, the library burned to the ground in the 1950's. 

Judge Sanborn was active in several areas of service to the law. He worked on several committees of the Judicial Conference. In 1944 he was the chairman of the committee on post-war building plans. From 

1945 to 1948 he served on the advisory committee that helped revise the Judicial Code. From the mid-1940's until 1962 he served on the bankruptcy administration and bankruptcy rules committees. In St. Paul he worked with his former law school. He was vice president of the St.

Joseph William Woodrough. Judges Stone, Gardner, and Sanborn led the Eighth Circuit for almost three decades. Judge Woodrough served during the same period; he may not have led the court, but he undoubtedly charmed it. He was a fine judge, but everyone recollects first his personality, humor, friendliness, courtesy, and walking.

Woodrough was born in Cincinnati in 1873. As a young man he studied in Germany and returned to the United States in 1893. He lived for a time in west Texas where his father and some associates were organizing Ward County. Woodrough was admitted to the Texas bar and was elected to the county judgeship in 1894. After two years on the bench Woodrough became county attorney. In 1897 he moved to Omaha, Nebraska, where he practiced law.

Woodrough quickly became a prominent member of the bar, and in 1916 President Wilson appointed him to the federal district court. He was 42 years old, the youngest member of the federal judiciary at the time. Among his district court assignments was the trial and sentencing of the Birdman of Alcatraz. He was an effective and hard-working federal judge, frequently accepting assignments throughout the Eighth Circuit. Judge Woodrough once recalled the first time that Senior 

Circuit Judge Walter Sanborn sent him to Arkansas in the 1920's. The judge whom Woodrough was assisting welcomed him to Little Rock and took him to the top story of the hotel to enjoy a cocktail. “Liquor was taboo in that country then," Woodrough recalled, but they always enforced Prohibition on the other fellow."8

With the retirement of Judge Van Valkenburgh, Franklin D. Roos evelt had an opportunity to make an appointment to the Eighth Circuit in his first days in the White House. President Hoover nominated 

Assistant Attorney General Seth W. Richardson of North Dakota in the last days of his term, but the Senate refused to confirm any judicial appointments by the outgoing President. The new President offered the post to Arthur F. Mullen of Nebraska, a major Roosevelt supporter in the 1932 campaign. Mullen declined the offer but urged the appointment of Woodrough. Within a month of his inauguration, Roosevelt nominated Woodrough. Woodrough recalled being escorted into the Oval Office by Mullen. "Here comes Woody!" shouted

Charles Breckinridge Faris. When Judge Kenyon died in 1933, President Roosevelt appointed Charles B. Faris, a United States district judge, to the seat. The appointment broke several of the customary guidelines for judicial appointments. He was a Missourian who took a seat normally allotted to Iowa, and he was 71 years old when he took his circuit court seat.

Faris was born in 1864 in Mississippi County, Missouri. After studying at the University of Missouri and finishing his legal education with private study, he was admitted to the bar in 1891. He practiced law in Caruthersville, Missouri until 1910. During that period he was also the city attorney from 1892 to 1893 and Pemiscot County prosecutor from 1893 to 1899. He served in the state legislature from 1899 to 1910. He was also the president of the Bank of Caruthersville and the Caruthersville Ice and Light Company. In addition, Faris served on the Board of Curators of the University of Missouri from 1903 to 1909, occupying the presidency of the board in the last two years. 

In 1910 Faris turned this prodigious energy to the judiciary. He was elected Missouri circuit judge and served two years before he was chosen for the state supreme court. When a federal district court vacancy occurred in the Eastern District of Missouri in 1919, President Wilson nominated Faris, in spite of the protest from the state Democratic Party whose leaders, while granting Faris's ability, 13Ibid. 56

complained that removing him from the state court would give the Republicans an advantage in that arena. 14 Judge Faris served on the district court for fifteen years. On the district bench Judge Faris made the first federal court ruling on the gold clause in contracts following Roosevelt's monetary policies of 1933-34. He upheld the government's power to set monetary policy.

When the vacancy occurred on the court of appeals, Senator Bennett Champ Clark of Missouri urged the appointment of Faris, as he had the year before when the previous opening occurred. As a favor to Senator

Seth Thomas. When Judge Faris retired, Roosevelt appointed Seth

He was an excellent trial lawyer, anticipating the reaction of judge and jury to his particular presentations. In 1914 he became Assistant United States Attorney. In

Harvey M. Johnsen. In 1940 Congress added two seats to the court of appeals. President Roosevelt selected Harvey M. Johnsen of 

Nebraska for the first of those seats. Judge Johnsen quickly became a leader of the court and he remained an active judge for 25 years. 

Johnsen was born in Hastings, Nebraska, in 1895 and grew up in 

Fremont, Nebraska. His father was a foreman on the Chicago and

Northwestern Railroad. After his high school graduation he taught school for a year and then proceeded to the University of Nebraska. 

While in law school there, he worked for three years as the legal secretary to Chief Justice Andrew M. Morrisey of Nebraska. The job qualifications included typing and shorthand, so Johnsen mastered the skills in one month.17 Johnsen graduated from law school in 1919. 

Johnsen practiced law in Omaha from 1920 to 1938. He was one of the leaders of the state bar and occupied several bar association offices. 

In 1938 he became the first president of the state bar after the state supreme court established the unified bar. In 1938 the governor appointed Johnsen to a vacancy on the Nebraska Supreme Court, and he went from there to the court of appeals in 1940.

Judge Johnsen was devoted to the Eighth Circuit. When he first came on the court, he offered to establish his chambers at whatever place he could help the court most. Kimbrough Stone, then the senior judge, responded that if Judge Johnsen would join him in Kansas City, he would have available a quorum of two judges at the court's administrative seat. Judge Johnsen therefore resided at Kansas City for about nine years until after Judge Stone retired. 

Judge Johnsen possessed a keen mind. He penetrated legal questions quickly and got to the crux of the important issues. During oral argument he grasped the essential points of a case and asked questions that went to the heart of the issue. He frequently worked without a law clerk and for a while went without a secretary, but his research and typing skills and his quick mind allowed him to work without a staff. 

With these qualities he was often impatient with trivia. He neglected the necessary routine of life, such as cashing his checks. At times he was behind in his production of opinions because he spent considerable time recasting the drafts. In conference he was anxious to resolve points quickly and proceed; but in his opinions he wrote long, labored sentences. 18

Judge Johnsen was stiff and formal to the outsider or the newcomer, but upon better acquaintance he displayed warmth and wit. These qualities endeared him to his colleagues and to the leaders of the bar.

The rumors squelched, Roosevelt nominated Riddick in December, 1941, and he was confirmed without a struggle. Judge Riddick served on the court of appeals for eleven years until his death in 1953. 

Illustrative Cases. 

It fell to the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals in 1943 to consider the first appeal of a violation of the Smith Act. Passed in 1940, the Smith 

Act represented the first major attempt since 1918 to legislate against members of the Communist Party or other so-called subversives. The 

Act made it a crime to advocate the overthrow of the government by force or to advocate insubordination in the armed forces. In the district court for the District of Minnesota, the Justice Department brought 28 persons to trial for violating the Act. Eighteen persons were convicted. 

They appealed to the court of appeals in 1943. Judges Stone, Johnsen, and Riddick exhaustively studied the entire record and unanimously affirmed their convictions in Dunne v. United States.23

Judge Stone wrote the opinion. In a logical and methodical manner he dealt in turn with the validity of the Smith Act, the sufficiency of the indictment, and the sufficiency of the evidence. The Smith Act was not an invalid restriction of free speech, Stone wrote, because it did not limit expressions of opinion or criticism of the government unless the expressions were made with the intent to bring about the unlawful results, in this case insubordination in the military. Judge Stone reasoned carefully that the indictment and the evidence were both sufficient. Since all the defendants acknowledged their membership in the Socialist Workers Party, Judge Stone examined the documents issued by the Socialist Workers to show that they did conspire to cause insubordination in the military. The Operation of the Court. 

The Court of Appeals operated according to a set routine in the 1930's and 1940's. The members of the court worked together smoothly. They looked forward to the terms of court when they would associate closely, and they conducted their business by correspondence when they were in their separate home chambers. They remained a close "family."

The decision-making process seldom varied. While the time allowed for oral argument gradually diminished as the dockets became more crowded, almost all cases received an oral argument. Following the arguments each day, the judges would meet in a preliminary conference and discuss each case in general terms. Then each judge on the panel would prepare a memorandum on each case, and differences would be reconciled at a memorandum conference. After the judges had returned to their cities of residence, they would correspond by letter on the draft opinions. Judge Stone, Judge Gardner, and the other court leaders believed that the letter-writing forced each member to think through his questions and present his points concisely.20 26 The circuit judges made a harmonious group. When the judges met for a term of court they would generally stay in the same hotels, eat lunch and dinner with each other, and socialize as a group. They even walked together at noon. When the wives traveled with the judges they would, of course, accompany the group, but it was frequently a gathering of males (no woman has yet sat as a regular Eighth Circuit

CHAPTER VI THE MODERN COURT 

In the last twenty-nine years the Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit has changed dramatically. It has grown and charted new directions. The most obvious change is the volume; the court received 

189 new cases on its docket in 1948 and 1009 in 1975. The court also became considerably younger and had fewer long periods of service. 

The average age and years of service among the seven active judges in early 1948 was almost 70 years and over 15 years. In 1975, the averages for the eight active judges were 55 years and 5½ years. The changes in criminal law in the 1960's greatly affected the character of the work of the court of appeals. Finally, all of these pressures had an effect on the concept of the court family that was so cherished by 

Kimbrough Stone.

The Judicial Code of 1948 and subsequent legislative changes affected all federal courts, including the Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. First, some titles changed. The senior circuit judge became the chief judge, and retired judges became senior judges. A judge over 70 years of age could not serve as chief judge. These changes meant that the personnel of the court would see a faster turnover. A. K. Gardner served as chief judge from 1948 to 1959, and 

Harvey M. Johnsen held the post from 1959 to 1965. Since then, five circuit judges have been chief judge. The judges now regularly take senior status at 70 years of age.

Sixteen judges have joined the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals since

Kimbrough Stone retired in 1947. About half were United States district judges before they joined the Court of Appeals. Six have been from Missouri, and every other state in the circuit (except South Dakota) has had two appointments. They have been evenly divided by party affiliation, and they have constituted an evenly balanced court.

Harry Andrew Blackmun. Harry A. Blackmun, the last of 

President Eisenhower's five appointments to the Eighth Circuit, succeeded John B. Sanborn upon his retirement in 1959. The choice was very fitting; not only did both come from Minnesota, but the younger man worked as a law clerk for the older. 

Harry A. Blackmun was born in Nashville, Illinois, in 1908. His family moved to Minnesota in 1910, and he grew up there. He graduated from Harvard University with an A.B. degree in 1929 and a law degree in 1932. Even though he had a job available in Boston, he returned to Minnesota following his graduation because his father was ill. That circumstance led to his first association with the court of appeals.

He sought a job as a law clerk, and Judge John B. Sanborn granted him an interview. Judge Sanborn began by saying he probably would not hire a clerk. He had not had one before as a district judge, and because of the Depression the federal government was trying to save money. The young lawyer persisted, and Sanborn finally gave him the job. Within a short time after becoming a federal government employee, however, the Economy Act passed and all government workers took a 15% reduction in salary, except judges whose salary is constitutionally protected. In spite of the inauspicious start, Justice 

Blackmun enjoyed his service with Judge Sanborn, and the term as clerk began a close relationship that ended only with Sanborn's death in 1964.

From 1934 to 1950 Justice Blackmun was in the private practice of law in Minneapolis, specializing in tax law. He also taught law, first at the St. Paul College of Law and later at the University of Minnesota. 

From 1950 to 1959 he was the general counsel of the Mayo Clinic and the Mayo Association in Rochester, Minnesota.

Illustrative Cases. 

In the last twenty years, the Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit has frequently been in the forefront of the developments in constitutional law and public policy. It has decided major cases concerning civil rights, due process in criminal cases, and freedom of speech. It has played a role in major political issues such as the power of the executive branch over appropriated funds and the regulation of the environment. And it has not avoided cases which might reflect on the United States judiciary itself. It has not sought these headlinemaking cases, but it has never avoided them once they arise. 80

School integration has been a complex and emotional issue for over twenty years in the United States, and the federal courts have generally been called upon to deal with it. Perhaps the most dramatic confrontation in the long series of confrontations occurred in Little Rock in 1958. The Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit considered the issue several times as individual cases were appealed, but perhaps the key case came in August, 1958, titled Aaron v. Cooper. 14 

District Judge Harry J. Lemley had granted the Little Rock School 

District a two-year delay in implementing the integration plan. He reasoned that emotions were too high for the schools to operate peacefully. Education, he thought, could not take place in the presence of violence or the threat of violence. 

The court of appeals convened an extraordinary en banc session to hear the appeal. The case was argued in August, only a few weeks before the schools would open, so the circuit judges acted under great pressure of time. After hearing the argument, they voted 6-1 to reverse the district judge and deny the extension of time (Judge Gardner dissenting). Judge Matthes, then the most junior judge on the court, wrote the opinion, and the court announced the decision on August 18, 1958.

Judge Matthes' opinion reviewed the events leading up to the decision. He gave examples of the high degree of public emotions on the issue. He described the events during the previous school year, when several acts of violence occurred as federal troops guarded Central High School in Little Rock. He then explained why the court could not uphold a delay. He cited several Supreme Court and court of appeals cases to show that a constitutional principle should not be withdrawn because of disagreement or difficulties in implementation.

The court insisted that the violence was not the direct result of the presence of the black students but of popular opposition to their presence. The black students were responsible for none of the incidents. Judge Matthes concluded by insisting upon the rights of the black students and the adherence to the law: "We say the time has not yet come in these United States when an order of a Federal Court must be whittled away, watered down, or shamefully withdrawn in the face of violent and unlawful acts of individual citizens in opposition thereto." The decade of the 1960's witnessed a number of strongly-supported causes which led to a wider and sometimes more symbolic expression of political opinion. Picketing, sit-in demonstrations, and other similiar activities took place throughout the country. If the demonstrators were arrested or punished for their action, they claimed that their activities were protected under a right to freedom of speech, and courts had to decide what activities could be considered "speech." The Court of 

Appeals for the Eighth Circuit wrestled with this problem in 1967 in the case of Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District. 20 

Three students in Des Moines wore black armbands to protest the Vietnam War in spite of a school regulation prohibiting the wearing of the armbands. The school authorities asked the students to remove the armbands and, when the students refused, suspended them from school. The students then requested the federal district court to enjoin the school district from enforcing the regulation. 

Judge Roy L. Stephenson, then the Chief Judge for the Southern 

District of Iowa, heard the case. Judge Stephenson found that wearing armbands did not constitute speech and that the protection of free speech was not absolute. He reasoned that the school officials must maintain a "scholarly, disciplined atmosphere within the classroom.” In an emotional issue like the Vietnam War, such an expression of opinion might lead to disturbance in the classroom. Since the students could still wear their armbands everywhere except on school property, and since they could still express their opinions in an orderly discussion in school, Judge Stephenson concluded that the regulation was a reasonable infringement on their political expression.21

The court of appeals heard the case en banc in 1967. After considering the issues, the eight circuit judges divided 4-4, and the decision of the district court stood. Because the court of appeals did not come to an independent conclusion, they did not issue an opinion. 

This case marked the first time that an equal division occurred on the 

Eighth Circuit court.22

Operation of the Court. 

The changes in the operation of the court of appeals during the last 25 years reflect the increased volume of cases and the heightened philosophical differences that have developed in most courts. The 

Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit still adheres to many of the traditions that developed during the first half of this century, but it has been forced in recent years to accommodate its routine to outside pressure.

In the 1940's Judge Stone called the court of appeals a family. Its judges stayed at the same hotel, they walked to work according to rigid routine, they ate lunch and dinner together, and they met informally in the evening. 30 Today the judges fly in and out of a city where the court is in session. They hold court much more often and hear many more cases. The cases seem more complex, and the decisions more often receive public attention. They have two law clerks each, a central legal research staff, and a larger administrative staff. The judges remain colleagues. They can put aside differences that arise over particular cases. But they no longer have the opportunity to establish the exceptional camaraderie that characterized the Kimbrough Stone era. 

The size of the court's administrative office has more than doubled since 1958. In that year Robert C. Tucker became the clerk and supervised a staff of six. Today he has thirteen. In addition, since 1972 the court of appeals has employed a circuit executive and his secretary. 

The clerk's office handles the paper work for almost five times as many cases as it did in 1958. The circuit executive has a wide variety of management duties that help the court of appeals find more efficient ways to operate and deal with its expanded responsibilities.
