FOREWORD 

Personal efficiency is attained through the develop ment of those personal qualities which underlie individuaj effectiveness. The study of these fundamentals, which have in the past been left almost wholly to the accident of their occurrence in the individuals placed in subordinate, in supervisory, and in higher executive positions, may be pursued just as definitely as any of the studies of the technical fundamentals that underlie industry. 

The principles outlined in this brief course are those which have successfully been applied in industrial and business fields. They will be found to be equally applicable to the individual in his search for increasing his value "on the job" and in the furtherance of his personal ambitions there and elsewhere. After a stimulating exposition of the method of analyzing the personality of the individual to determine just where he stands, the mechanics of efficiency is studied and the principles applied to the development of personal efficiency.

No purely "question and answer" method, designed to test the ability to give back, parrot-fashion, a list of facts, rules and principles, is offered here. 

Efficiency is not attained through the following of imposed rules; there can come from the outside only stimulation which will lead the individual to appreciate the desirability of correct attitudes on fundamentals.

The efficient individual is essentially a

V self-made product.

The value of the work presented here lies in its clear indication of the "one best way". The individual makes the application to his individual problems. 

The self-analysis charts and tests presented will be found suggestive. They are by no means exhaustive the student will add to these his own personal tests which are the more valuable as he better grasps the fundamental notion of analysis, and constructive effort based on the findings of the analysis. 

A few suggestions as to the best method of studying this subject are to be found following this introduction. They should be carefully read and faithfully followed as they represent the experience of the many who have already pursued this course

The material of this course represents the accumulated experience of a number of years spent in presenting the Principles of Personal Efficiency mainly by lecture and conference to groups of business and industrial employees and executive groups. The addition of the Personality Rating Charts is an expansion of the supplementary material furnished such groups, which it is believed will render the course particularly applicable to home-study by the correspondence method.

PERSONAL EFFICIENCY Chapter I THE FUNDAMENTALS UNDERLYING EFFECTIVE PERFORMANCE 

Everything that is done in industry and business as elsewhere in science, the professions, the arts, is done primarily through human beings. Although industry has depended at many points upon machines and mechanical aids, at some point along the line man becomes an important part of the process, the key to it. A man puts into effect his knowledge, training, what we call technique, by means of certain human qualities and abilities. It is these human qualities and abilities that are the determining factors in success or failure.

THE HUMAN ELEMENT FUNDAMENTAL. 

Studies made a few years ago under the auspices of the Carnegie Foundation showed that success, even in technical lines like engineering, was due approximately 15% to the technical knowledge of the particular field and about 85% to the human qualities which are necessary to successful performance. 

By this is meant those qualities which have to do with dealing successfully with people. Schools and colleges were training their students in only about 15% of the essentials of the job knowledge and facts 

that is, in leaving practically untouched the trainit ing in the larger part of the basis of success. The feeling was that, having been given the facts the technique was entirely up to the students to make good. The personal element was ignored 

that was the student's affair.

EDUCATION CLOSES THE GAP. To close up this obvious gap between training and performance on the job, educational institutions have begun to alter their study program courses so that they will train in this 85% formerly ignored. The student will understand how to deal with people personal qualities are to be developed and likewise the ability to make use of the knowledge he acquires. Until 

THE GREAT PROBLEM IN BUSINESS. Business, like the school, has come to realize that the greatest part of its problem, whether from the point of view of the individual or that of the employer, is to develop the personal qualities of the individual. It is not at all difficult to get men who can learn all the facts pertaining to a given job, methods to be used and the technical details of a given job. The difficult thing to do is to develop in them the personal qualities necessary to make the fullest, the most successful use of the facts and methods they have been taught. very recently the employer contributed only the facts and the methods when training a new man; it was entirely up to him to "make good". The employer would hardly admit it, but he really did not know how to teach the man to "make good". But now progressive business knows that personal development is a matter of interest to both employer and employee; and the intelligent employer is looking for methods which will help him to develop the personal qualities which make up the 85% they once ignored.

QUALIFICATIONS VARY. In industry these proportions

or percentages will vary. There might be arranged for any industry, any department, a scale of the various jobs, showing the relatively greater amount of the human qualities needed as the scale is ascended. The man who stands by a machine, presses a foot lever, or manipulates a clutch handle, will have need for but a small percentage of the human qualities on such a job. The man handling an excavating machine on a highway needs but a small proportion; the driver of a bus over the same road needs decidedly more, it will be agreed. 

The filing clerk has less opportunity to exercise these human qualities than the girl at the information desk, the bookkeeper less than the salesman, or the man at the paying teller's window in a bank. 

Coming into the job of the supervisor, foreman or other executive, we find required a high percentage of the human qualities. While a man in these positions may first have attracted attention to himself for his technical, manual or mental abilities, he now rarely uses machines, hands or even his mind directly upon any job. He deals with jobs through other men's hands, minds and technical abilities. 

His human qualities and abilities are primarily important. 

INDIVIDUAL EFFECTIVENESS MAY BE DEVELOPED. 

Individual effectiveness may be developed through study. This is not done by sitting down quiescent and having a lot of wise sayings poured over us by some gifted individual in a painless lecture course it is done by self-study with a little guidance

A DEFINITION OF EFFICIENCY. 

As a preliminary to the study of Personal Efficiency, it will be useful to cite a few examples of efficiency in the industrial world. A definition of efficiency in its mechanical sense might be useful. When we speak of the efficiency of a machine or device of any sort we mean the ratio or proportion of its actual output, or capacity, to its possible output. In an electrical device we mean the relation of output to input. A machine producing only half its possible output is said to have an efficiency of 50%. As the actual output approaches its possible total, we say its efficiency is increasing. The province of the engineer is to increase efficiency in the industrial world. The aim of the individual who would achieve effectiveness is to increase his personal efficiency.

EFFICIENCY IN THE INDUSTRIAL WORLD 

The progress of engineering, of industry, of management has been marked by successive increases in efficiency. 

The first boiler was a wasteful hollow sphere, heated in the open air by flames applied externally; the reciprocating engine it supplied was open at one end and the steam that lifted its piston was condensed by shooting cold water into the cylinder a terrific waste of heat. Now we have internally-fired boilers or vaporizers in which water practically flashes into steam generated at 1500 or 1800 even 2500 pounds per square inch, feeding large turbines, or a series of them, which have all the refinements of high-vacuum condensers and highly efficient heat insulation. We now have

a boiler using mercury (quicksilver) whose vapor runs a turbine, then generates steam by the heat of its exhaust and drives a new series of turbines. Electricity, once generated by costly chemical cells, is produced by dynamos of marvelous electrical efficiency and its transformation effected with extremely low losses, less than two per cent being wasted in the latter process.

INVENTION AND RESEARCH. 

By the application of invention and improved processes to industry, production is secured with less and less loss; by means of labor-saving machinery, elimination of waste and the utilization of by-products, whole industries have sprung up, auxiliary to the primary operations which owe their existence to hitherto wasted by-products. 

This is particularly true in the metallurgical and chemical industries. You all recall that years ago President Hoover, then an engineer, said "The march of civilization is marked by its progress in the elimination of waste". This waste was not only in the use of raw material and by-products, but managerial wastes.

MANAGEMENT FALLS IN LINE. More recently within the last two decades management has in turn begun to apply efficiency principles to its functions, analyzing methods of routing, progress, scheduling, and the like to reduce delays and eliminate lost motion. It has applied similar principles to its personnel problems to the same end. Only now it is beginning to analyze its sales in distribution problems.

MODERN SCIENTIFIC MANAGEMENT 

Some detailed account of modern scientific management methods may be of interest in reference to the particular subject of Personal Efficiency, which will engage our attention in this course.

During his early management methods was Frederick W. Taylor. years as a workman he had observed that many of his fellow workmen did not accomplish more than one-third of a fair day's work. When he was appointed foreman of the machine shop at the Midvale plant of the Midvale Steel Company, he determined to work out some system of management in which the interests of the management and that of the workers should be the same as far as possible.

Many of his immediate followers made noteworthy additions and modifications to his theories and a few of them achieved real success as industrial counsellors.

THE PRINCIPLES OF SCIENTIFIC MANAGEMENT. 

Taylor developed the following duties of management as outlined in his book "The Principles of Scientific Management": 

1. The development of a science for each element of a man's work, thereby replacing the old rule-of-thumb method. 

2. The selection of the best worker for each particular task and then training, teaching and developing the

workman, in place of the former method of allowing the worker to choose his own task and then develop himself as best he could. 

3. The development of hearty cooperation between the management and the men in carrying on the activities in accordance with the principles of the developed science. 

4. The division of the work in almost equal shares between the management and the workers, each department taking over the work for which it is better fitted, instead of the former condition in which most of the work and the greater part of the responsibility was thrown on the men. Never in the old days did management say to its machinists, for instance, "Set up the work thus and so; take such cuts on your lathe at such speeds

Nor did it take thought on deliv- 11 ering material to the workman or removing finished products. 

The workman not only did the work but developed the process. 

Today in the representative industries the story is greatly different.

Taylor's "System" was applied by its author to the work of the Link-Belt Company, the Tabor Manufacturing Company of Philadelphia, and the United States Arsenal At Watertown, Massachusetts. Others took up this work. At 

Bethlehem interesting studies in pig-iron handling and shovelling were made which have become classic. The experiments with high-speed steels were the first outgrowths of his attempts to find "the right way to do jobs".

A CLASSICAL EXPERIMENT. The best known of Taylor's achievements is his classical experiment with pig-iron handlers. Selecting men who were strong, tractable and of the phlegmatic type from a group of seventy-five laborers who had hitherto been doing the work, Taylor trained those selected to work at a certain gait, resting for such and such intervals and resuming the work when told. Without any more fatigue than before, apparently, the trained men carried the pigs of iron weighing about 92 pounds each from a pile, up an incline, into a car, handling 47 tons per day in place of the 12 tons handled hitherto this latter rate being that considered as satisfactory all over the country at that time (about 1898). The working wage for this type of labor at Bethlehem then was $1.15 per day. The men trained by Taylor were paid by the piece and received 60% more than the day rate for handling the 47 tons as compared with the 12 tons under the day rate. They averaged a little more than $1.85 per day during the three years Taylor was connected with the work.

So startling were these results of the application of scientific study of operations which took into account all the factors, including fatigue, that general attention to similar problems was the order of the day henceforth in a growing number of industries,

Taylor laid down a general method which has been widely applied to a large variety of manufacturing and assembling

operations.

The general steps are as follows: 

1. 

Find, say, 10 or 15 different men (preferably in as many separate establishments and different parts of the country) who are especially skillful in doing the particular work to be analyzed. 

2. Study the exact series of elementary operations or motions which each of these men uses in doing the work which is being investigated, as well as the implements each man uses. 

3. Study with a stop-watch the time required to make each of these elementary movements and then select the quickest way of doing each element of the work. 4. 

Eliminate all false movements, slow movements, and useless movements 5. After doing away with all unnecessary movements, collect into one series the quickest and best movements as well as the best implements.

This one new method, involving that series of motions which can be made quickest and best, is then substituted in place of the ten or fifteen inferior series which were formerly in use. This best method becomes standard, and remains standard, to be taught first to the teachers (or functional foremen) and by them to every workman in the establishment until it is superseded by a quicker and better series of movements. In this simple way one element after another of the science is developed. 

FRANK B. GILBRETH. In 1911, Mr. Frank B. Gilbreth

first aroused general interest in the application of efficiency methods to the individual task by the publication of his time studies of the brick layer. 

He made a time study of the different motions used by the brick mason in the placing of a brick and of the methods of bringing the brick and the mortar to the mason. For example, he found that the bricks would be dumped in a pile somewhere near, by the hod carrier. The mason would then step over to the pile, pick up a brick, go back again to the wall upon which it was to be placed, turn the brick over several times in order to determine the proper side to place upward and finally put it into place. He also found that there were a large number of similar unnecessary operations in connection with the placing of mortar. Using the results of his time studies and analysis of the different motions required to lay a brick properly, he was able to design certain standard equipment.

This included a container for holding the bricks at proper level, into which the bricks were placed right side up and a scaffold which could be adjusted to the proper height as the wall was built up. He then made studies to determine the proper methods of the use of this equipment. By the use of the methods the number of bricks laid in a day could be greatly increased. Dr.

PERSONAL EFFICIENCY 

But all this improvement has been in terms of business and factory efficiency. All of our investigations hitherto have been on the mechanical plane and devoted to the physical products of industry. Why should we not discover the principles that underlie individual effectiveness? Why not apply efficiency methods to the man himself in all his relations? 

Let us lay down the laws of Personal Efficiency. 

A DEFINITION. Personal Efficiency may be defined as the science of self-management. That the vast majority of men have neglected the study of this science and the practice of the art is shown by their condition today. 

SOME EXAMPLES. It has been estimated that out of every ten men, seven are in the wrong job; that the majority of men use only about one-third of their natural ability; that what is wasted by the average American family would actually support them; that in this country there are always over 3,000,000 people on the sick list; that the number of preventable deaths each year is over 700,000. 

The difference between a man who digs a ditch and the chief executive of one of our greatest corporations is that the one works with his muscles and the other works with his head. The ditch digger has trained his muscles to perform one task, and to that task he is bound, but the leader has trained not only his muscles but also his mind, his nerves, the organs of his body, his thoughts, actions and

emotions, his instincts, habits, aims, and ambitions. The one has gone through life without any self-analysis, simply doing whatever work came to hand while the other trained himself to do his most and best, in the shortest time and easiest way, and as nearly as possible to the satisfaction of all concerned. 

He has increased his personal efficiency to the highest possible degree

EFFECTIVE LIVING A PRIMARY CONSIDERATION. 

inary to any program for improvement in one's ability to meet one's social and business obligations effectively, is a plan. 

It is a fundamental weakness that most of us approach our human relations in total reliance upon instinct, intuition, chance. We do not plan. Then these principles must be made the basis of personal activities. 

THE PHYSICAL FACTOR 

Effective living requires, in the first place, the normal functioning of the human body. The fullest success can only be assured by the healthy mind in a sound body.

men · Though we often read of the wonderful achievements of sickly witness Herbert Spencer and Charles Darwin, chronic invalids, we regret that, given these wonderful minds, we could not profit by tremendously greater tasks performed by those same minds, sustained by strong bodies. We marvel at the 

English Chancellor, Philip Snowden, with his crippled body, swaying the whole Reparations Council at Geneva, and bending them to his will, upsetting the German indemnity payments with his bold attacks.

We read of the crippled Steinmetz, the mathematical genius of the General Electric Company, and wonder what the same mind might have done in a normal body. 

Napoleon the Third was a sufferer from an unrecognized complaint that made his journeying astride his horse an agony. 

The physical torture reacted on his mental ability to meet the military situations. His whole army and he were looked up in Sedan and forced to surrender to Frederick William of 

Prussia. Historians might insist that Germany would have beaten him anyhow, but lack of leadership hastened the end. 

CONSERVING OUR ENDOWMENTS. 

Although we are endowed by heredity with a wide variety of bodily characteristics, some with superabundant natural health, others with poorer physique more susceptible to disease, it is obvious that attention to hygiene will conserve that natural health or better the frail body. Health is comparative; it is not a question of Jones being as healthy as Smith but of Jones maintaining a standard of health for his body, bolstering a poor physical equipment,

or bettering a fair one.

Thus we have the well-known example of the statistician Babson who, suffering from tuberculosis, gave up his bank-clerk's job but made a fortune while he gained comparatively good health by living and working out of doors at a new profession.

Regular hours of sleep, adequate food in correct quantity, such exercise as is suited to one's physique, go a long way towards maintaining the standard. 

THE SENSIBLE ATTITUDE. No man should be fanatic on diet, or exercise; such an attitude bespeaks a degree of mental unbalance. The body is a machine, entitled to proper care and consideration there are times when it must be 

speeded up, stressed to full capacity, but the wise mechanic observes when it needs repair, renovation. It is wrong to devote the mechanic's mind to the machine entirely when his main interest should be the work it turns out, just as it is wrong for a man to be concerned solely with the physique, as such, unless that be one's vocation in life, as in the case of a Gene Tunney or a Jack Dempsey. On the other hand it is wisdom to care for this wonderful mechanism that must stay with us for perhaps the next forty, fifty, or sixty years of life devoted to useful pursuits. 

THE HEALTH PROBLEM

GOODNESS TO OTHERS 

Teaching a child the difference between good and bad is moral training.

And moral training continues throughout life. When such training is neglected we deteriorate, we become immoral. 

TWO KINDS OF IMMORALITY. 

Immorality is of two kinds: the harm we do to others, called crime; and the harm we do to ourselves, called vice. It is well to remember that the laws against crime have never been repealed, while the laws against

vice have never been continuously enforced. 

Others can prevent us from burning down our neighbor's house, from stealing his property, from maiming him or killing him. But others cannot prevent us from being vicious, for that is a matter which is controlled only by ourselves. 

And it is our vices far more than our crimes, which will give us trouble in life.

THE NEED FOR LAW. Nothing can grow, no technique can be built up where anarchy prevails. It is because elementary morality is at the bottom of all civilization that the English paid far more attention to it than to the other moralities. The English lawmakers considered the element of honesty, for example, to be fundamental. A man could lack coordination, could be filthy and diseased, could be discontented and unhappy, but (no matter how much he might be pitied) if he stole however little, he was imprisoned, tortured, punished even with death. 

THE IRON HAND. During the reign of Henry VIII of 

England over 80,000 men, women and children were hanged children of only twelve years for crimes as small as stealing a sheep.

It was not the amount of the theft that determined these extreme penalties; it was the violation of the law that was held to be unpardonable. 

It was severity of that sort that was brought by the early English settlers to America and practiced during the early colonial period. And even up to the latter part of the

nineteenth century horse stealing was a hanging offense on the Western frontier. Severity still characterized legal penalties. 

A HIGHER STANDARD THAN LAW 

DUTIES TOWARD OTHERS. It is well for every man and woman definitely to determine what his duties are toward others, He can save himself much trouble and will not go very far wrong if he resolves to obey the laws of his state and country. On the other hand, he may set up for himself a higher standard than he can find in the statutes. 

THE LEGAL AND THE RIGHT. Many atrocious actions may be legal, but this is no excuse for them, nor does it condone them. And much that is fundamentally right is still illegal. 

Legality does not always mean that which is right. It was not so long ago that it was legal to separate husband and wife by selling one of them; children could be sold away from their parents; and it was illegal to keep or harbor a fleeing slave. 

It required a frightful civil war to abolish these iniquities. 

A few years ago it was legal in England for a husband to rob his wife of all her property. It is still legal in this country for a father to refuse to provide for his illegitimate children.

These matters are mentioned because the time is coming, and coming very fast, when we shall have to revise our theories of right and wrong, and progress to a higher morality.

STATING THE PERSONALITY PROBLEM. For successful performance there is needed both selection and development 

· selection to find the human material which has possibilities for development, and development to bring out these possibilities.

Successful performance may be looked for, once we have solved the problems of personality. Experience in selecting, training and developing students, foremen, executives and sales people show this.

Personality has, like the weather, been widely discussed but as in the case of the weather "nothing is done about it".

The employer in looking for a man for an important position, after stating the desired technical qualifications, usually ends by saying: "I want a man with a good personality".

While unable to define "good personality", he is positive that there are qualities which he desires in addition to knowledge or "brains". There is a growing need for a definite and practical method of rating "personality".

THE PROBLEM OF PERSONALITY 

Stated practically, the problem of business personality concerns itself with two questions: 1. 

What is meant by personality?

2. What definite steps may be taken toward developing personality and increasing business ability so as to increase the individual's effectiveness in business?

These questions are vitally important to the in- dividual and to the institution, industry and activity of which he is a part not only for promoting his own personal development, but also for the purpose of developing and obtaining their cooperation. This course is concerned with the effective development of the personal qualities and their application, in business and industry particularly, although they may be applied universally.

ELIMINATING VAGUENESS. The study of personal quali ties has rarely been conducted along definite, tangible lines in all of their details. To deal in generalities will only add to the vagueness which has always clouded the "personality problem",

It is necessary to fix the attention upon the tangible, the ascertainable. The common attitude that success after all is primarily dependent upon the intangible something called personality: this or that man has it and is lucky, this or that one hasn't it and is unlucky" is far from helpful to those who would achieve. There is in it no suggestion that is of value for those who would develop either themselves or others. "Personality" has been quite properly emphasized, especially in business. Granting a reasonable amount of in-

telligence in the individual, the imparting of knowledge, facts, methods, even in the most technical lines, is not so very difficult. The difficult thing is to develop the personal qualities of the individual so that he can make effective use of that knowledge. This is the major problem of business as it is of the school. then the job of the manager, the sales executive, the teacher If knowledge were the principal factor, to "knowing" must be would be easy. EVERY ONE HAS PERSONALITY. 

The fact is every one has personality. It may be strong or weak, positive or negative, purely a matter of degree. The assumption that every one has personality is the only one which gives a practical approach to the development of personality. By studying qualities which are found in all individuals, we determine the degree to which these qualities are present. 

4. The greatest misconception has been that personality has been considered a thing in itself, which if one had it, would make for the effectiveness of the individual. Personality is not a trait by itself.

AND PERMANENT RECORDS. Any program which does not make use of all available incidental information lacks so much of capitalizing all of our present equipment, past experience and curent accumulations of experience. It is for this reason that the value of records is emphasized. 

TYPES OF RECORDS HISTORY. The written histories of nations, insti-

tutions, organizations, individuals have many reasons for existence. The perpetuation of the memories of outstanding events of the past appeal to the pride of the present, create fraternal and nationalistic spirit, and arouse sentiments of ambition and a determination to carry on traditions of excellence and accomplishments.

This sentimental value is not without importance, but when history is studied to learn the lessons of the past, stimulate reform, arouse emulation, the desire to equal others in accomplishment, it reaches the height of its usefulness. 

BOOKS. The books which record the advancement of science, industry, business, in all their minutiae have a definite and specific value in their wealth of detail. They are the more important as they are accurate and, for present purposes, up to date.

The chemistry textbook of 1860 which gives the formula for water as HO instead of H20 would be interesting, perhaps amusing, but hardly useful to the modern chemist. A code of civil laws the record of legislation affecting business extending only to 1900 would be of little value to corporation counsel drafting a charter for a new concern. The records of a weather bureau, rainfall, temperature have a usefulness beyond mere geographical data they may determine business development and promote industrial advancement. 

The records of a geologist's investigation of a country old or new may determine the economic future of a whole people. The familiar devices of the office, the index, the

alphabetical file, blank forms and special memoranda, all have their place in establishing records. 

THE VALUE OF RECORDS. 

By the study of past performance in their minutest detail, we can readily check deviations from correct procedures as evidenced by a lack of success in certain ventures. Mistakes made in the heat of the task l001 up as we look back over the written record of it. Valuable methods dictated under the inspiration of concentrated thought are recognized at their full value, and become part of the accepted routine in a similar case. 

RECORDS MUST BE COMPREHENSIVE. 

The valuable record in a given case must be comprehensive; it must give all the information needed. An encyclopedia with half a dozen volumes missing, a letter file with several sections lacking, is for all practical purposes useless. Therefore, records of all types must be accurate, must be complete, not fragmentary, must be up to date this last of highest importance for those used in connection with current projects. 

USING RECORDS 

But records must be used the mere filling out of a form is not enough. It may have a certain temporary value as a step in mechanical accounting or the transmission of data but its efficiency-promoting value lies in its study, its analysis for the underlying principle involved and the conscious application of the conclusions arrived at through this study. 

PERSONAL EFFICIENCY APPLICATION. So much for

generalities: the personal application of record-keeping to the tabulation of one's own time can be made intensely interesting if honestly kept and highly profitable if sincerely studied. 

Industry and business are right in emphasizing the keeping of time records. A sizable staff in a modern concern is kept constantly busy with the accounting of that precious commodity time. Where the older order used the time office largely as an adjunct to the paymaster's department, the modern business revolves about the data supplied for the cost accounting fundamental to all the activities of a concern which is to remain solvent. 

The individual who is to begin the path to personal efficiency must account for his personal time, and conscientiousiy keep in some form a time record and use it. A personal time record is proposed, not that it be a permanent institution necessarily kept up day after day for weeks, but for a period sufficient to serve the purpose of this study. 

THE PERSONAL TIME RECORD 

Such a record, setting aside the eight hours to be devoted to sleep, the time for meals, for bathing, shaving, dressing, which presumably would total twelve hours, would leave for the purpose of record twelve hours a day. A tabulation showing five-minute periods has been suggested, neatly ruled. Such a form honestly kept, showing the hours in which one is profitably employed either for oneself or another, would enable one to determine his efficiency. If our record of 12 x 7 hours in the week, 84, be divided into the hours profitably employed, the percentage obtained is our efficiency.

WHAT THE RECORD REVEALS. 

Such a record could be used for keeping either the hours of a man's stated employment or the hours when he is not so employed, or both; in either case, the entries, if honest, would give in most cases a revelation of wasted time. How many five-minute periods of actual waste would a truthful record show? The tabulation itself need not, of course, be made every five minutes; perhaps a few minutes at the end of the day would suffice if the retrospective glance can show how we have spent the day and our memories serve us to recall each appreciable idle period.

A record kept for a week, a month, accurate in all Joints would be as we have said above, a revelation and in itself a spur to more efficient living. Try it.

VALUE OF THE PRINCIPLE OF PLANNING 

Of all the thirteen principles of efficiency, there is none more important, especially to Americans, than the principle of planning. Americans, as a people, are remarkable for their progressiveness and efficiency. In planning, however, there is a pathetic deficiency. It is characteristic of Americans to plunge recklessly ahead, intent upon todays

immediate advantage, forgetful of tomorrow's needs.

Americans are inclined to trust to luck or ingenuity, rather than to planning. We settled upon a continent surpassingly rich in natural resources. Because there was no planning for the future, the wealth of the forests has been wasted, the soil, in many cases, has been depleted, and wasteful mining methods have rendered inaccessible millions of dollars worth of coal and other minerals, while incalculable values in petroleum and natural gas have been wastefully burned up. 

For 600 years the Black Forest of Germany has been harvested. There is today more timber there than when the forest was first attacked. 

DEFICIENCIES IN INDUSTRIAL PLANNING. The same defi ciency in planning has characterized our railroad building, canal construction, location and erection of factories, ag well as commercial buildings and homes. American cities have sprung up without plans, and we are now forced to reconstruct them along saner lines, with consequent enormous expenditures of money.

CITY PLANNING INCOMPLETE. Because the American people do not plan, we have unsightly railroad yards at the entrance to nearly every large city; we have water fronts made ugly by private commercial operations, when those water fronts should have belonged to the whole people for their enjoyment. 

Throughout America you will find tremendously expensive reconstruction operations made necessary because the original work was not intelligently planned. If you are observant and

thoughtful, you will also see unspeakable wastes of time, effort, money, and material that must go on indefinitely because there was no plan in the beginning and it is now forever too late to improve.

When radio was first broached for police use, a large city spent $60,000 for receiving sets to be placed in the various stations. There was no definite plan for police broadcasting developed to go with it. Ten years later, $100,000 was asked to equip police automobiles with receiving sets.

LIFE PLANNING. This national trait of not planning enters our very homes. Our boys and girls are often brought up in badly planned homes; they are insufficiently nourished by badly planned meals; undisciplined because there is no definite plan for training them; sent to schools where they are put through an inefficient routine instead of being educated according to plans founded on their future needs. Men and women take up occupations for which they are unfitted because they drift into the first work that offers instead of planning for the future. Even those who make more than mediocre progress fall far short of their possibilities. 

Through lack of planning they have wasted a large percentage of their time.

EFFICIENCY CALLS FOR PLANS. 

Efficiency can only be accomplished by most careful planning of every detail of machinery, stores, equipment, service, and the other necessary

items requisite for the work in hand. A railroad time table illustrates the necessity of having a plan a time table is both a plan and a record. A school course is another example of planning. Every hour, or even every half-hour, of every day for four years is planned in advance for the college man. 

The entering freshman, should he so desire, could foretell exactly his various student activities in the class rooms by consulting the school calendar so far ahead as his senior year. That is the reason why he is able to cover so much ground during his school year.

PLANNING FOR PRODUCTION. 

There are some manufacturers who have planned operations to such an extent that they are able to tell exactly what any man in their organization will be doing on any day for months in advance. 

A well-conducted farm is also a good example of efficient planning. The soil must be prepared, seed must be on hand, machinery must be ready, horses and hands must be in their places, food for man and beast must be provided, and every operation must dovetail into every other operation if the crops are to be planted, cultivated, and harvested at the right time and in the right way.

SEQUENCE OF RAILROAD DESPATCHING. 

We are all familiar with the importance of despatching in the case of a railroad train. Despatching means more than the starting of the train on the minute it is scheduled to leave, which is merely a mechanical detail of setting it in motion at a predetermined time; that motion is but the culmination of a whole series of plans and schedules which each have been despatching. 

For the locomotive to be repaired by a given hour, to have its tank and tender filled at a specified time, and its steam raised to a given pressure by the time it is due at the passenger station, necessitates despatching at the round house. Supplies must be aboard the dining and sleeping cars. The crew must be ready by an appointed time. Each man's personal schedule must be in operation in other words, every detail must be despatched.

The safety of this and other trains requires that all others on the road must each have been informed as to its own activities; this one must make a certain point by a cer-

tain time, that one must remain in a siding between such periods. Not only must the plan and schedule be made in advance, but they must be carried out. So much depends on despatching.

AN EXAMPLE OF DESPATCHING. 

Harrington Emerson gives a very interesting example, albeit a common one, of despatching. He writes: "In a baseball game there are several forms of effi- 

The umpire's cry, 'play ball', which cient despatching. starts the game is an example of despatching. The captain sends his men to bat one after the other according to a definite, prearranged, but flexible, schedule. The pitcher is, to a certain extent, a despatcher. 

When he throws the ball across the plate he issues a tacit but none the less imperative order to the catcher to be ready for action; also to the batter. When the batter hits the ball, he becomes the despatcher, issuing orders for instant action on the part of fielders and basemen. He is, also, by the very same act, despatched on his way towards first base. An instant's delay on his part puts him out, and he may lose the game, or even the pennant".

DESPATCHING A UNIVERSAL NEED. The efficient operation of any business calls for despatching; equally do the smallest human needs. The routine of the housekeeper in a family that has members who must get to work regularly and on time calls for close attention to this principle of despatching. The

preparation of meals, the forehanded ordering of necessary supplies, all must be considered. Any detail omitted causes delay with consequent friction in the home and danger to the welfare of the family abroad. No less so is the conduct of a great hotel, an amusement enterprise, every business that caters to a public need or demand, subject to this principle.

REVIEW. In brief retrospect: 

We have, after an introduction, made clear the need and use of records for the individual's great effectiveness; shown the necessity for conscious planning and the value of scheduling, preferably in writing of all his activities. PERSONAL EFFICIENCY PRINCIPLE NUMBER FIVE YOU MUST STANDARDIZE CONDITIONS STANDARDIZATION. 

It will be apparent to the most unobservant that conditions under which work is done will greatly affect output in both quality and quantity. The most ordinary mechanic will not expect a stamping machine, say, to turn out satisfactory work if bearings are loose, if dies are badly adjusted, if power is insufficient or inadequately supplied through a loose slipping belt, if the machine is not easily accessible or accumulations of waste and finished parts are not promptly removed.

Yet how often do we see the human machine, whether situated in office, storeroom or shop, trying to operate under unfavorable conditions and its inefficiency unnoticed. 

Each individual so far as lies in his power must study the conditions under which he works.

ONE BEST WAY TO DO THINGS. Of a hundred ways in which Mathematical a thing may be done, there is but one best way. ly, then, there is but one chance in 100 of lighting on that best way accidentally. The American people as a nation are deficient in planning, many of their operations being dictated apparently by chance or accident. HISTORICAL EXAMPLES OF INCREASED EFFICIENCY. 

From the beginning of the use of guns in naval warfare to the Battle of Santiago was 482 years. Yet the victorious American fleet sent only three per cent of its big projectiles into the ships of the enemy. The average distance at which the shots were fired was less than a mile and a half. Each gun was discharged at a maximum rapidity of less than once in five minutes. That was in 1898. Since that time gunnery practice in America has broken away from traditions and its operations have been standardized

It is interesting to note that only in 1931 has the "holystoning" of the wooden decks been abandoned in war

ships.

From the earliest times when ships were wood throughout, the common-sense method of applying soap or plain water with a brush was never used to clean the decks, but a species of stone was used by hand or at the end of a stick to grind away the dirt, tar, etc., with a great deal of the wood! 

For 50 years, perhaps, iron and steel ships have had wooden decks applied above the steel in certain sections and the grinding of these continued. Their renewal was a contin ual expense but tradition adhered to the grinding method. 

Economy measures seem to have forced a tardy change. 

MODERN HISTORY. 

According to Harrington Emerson whose railroad work was epochal, American railroad shops have been repairing locomotives for over ninety years, and yet it requires from fifteen to sixty days to complete general repairs on a locomotive. By breaking away from tradition, planning, writing schedules, despatching, and standardizing conditions and operations, it would be possible to complete all necessary repairs on a locomotive in a very much shorter time.

The 1931 Yearbook of Railroad In formation shows some interesting increases in inefficiency: "In 1920, 18.8 pounds of coal were required in the passenger service to move a passenger-train car for the distance of one mile; in 1930, this figure had been reduced to 14.7 pounds, a reduction of 22 per cent.

"In 1920, 198 pounds of coal were required in the freight service to move 1,000 tons of freight and cars for the distance of one mile; in 1930, this figure had been reduced to 138 pounds, a reduction of 30 per cent. *In 1920, each freight train performed each hour, on the average, a service equal to carrying 7,303 tons of freight for the distance of one mile; in 1930, this figure had been increased to 10,839 ton-miles, an increase of 48 per cent. "In 1920, the payments made by the railways for loss and damage to freight amounted to $2.66 for every car loaded with revenue freight; in 1930, this figure had been reduced to 76 cents, a reduction of 71 per cent. If by any misfortune you have gathered the impression that an important question can be brought up to a high degree of efficiency by a few minutes' time and motion study, now is your best opportunity to correct the error.

Suppose you spend the aggregate of two years' time, standardizing the principal operations of your life and work, and thereby increase your efficiency only 100 per cent. In exactly two years you make up this time and reap your doubled profits on every year you work thereafter.

DRILL AND PRACTICE. Having determined to break away from tradition, and having assured yourself that the task of standardizing your operations is worth your most conscientious and unwavering persistence, you have next to learn that an operation is not standardized until by drill and practice you have made yourself its master. The great value of drill and practice in the mastery of standardized operations is that you thus pass from great effort and slight results to slight effort and great results.

EXAMPLE OF STANDARDIZATION. 

The value of standardization is immediately obvious in our highly organized modern industry, particularly those industries in which machine processes are supplemented at certain stages by manual operations.

In manufacturing the component parts of a complicated mechanism like a dollar watch, the highly efficient mechanical production of the interchangeable parts can conceivably be negatived, and its cost-reducing features entirely overcome, by inefficient assembling. 

CHANCE ELIMINATED. Given the elementary parts that

make up certain assembly units, the worker, uninstructed, would choose at random the order in which he took up the separate units, and his assembly would, while correct, take a great deal more time than had the proper order been observed. Continuing to make this assembly in the same order, familiarity with its details would gradually enable him to achieve a relatively high speed, which would, however, fall short of ideal performance THE BEST METHOD. There is one best method, and that method standardized and observed by all working on that operation would achieve the maximum output. The same speeding up which comes from long familiarity, the habit learned, in other words, would have its effect here as everywhere else and the final efficiency would be reached. 

DEVELOPING EFFICIENT ROUTINE. As pointed out before, analysis, study, is necessary to develop the most efficient routine. This study involves determination of the order or sequence in which operations may best be performed. Some sequences are necessary, dictated by the relative position of the component parts; fastening three members a, b, c, together they must be picked up in the order a, b, c, or c, b, a, in each case the middle member second. Which of the two sequences will serve best is determined by trial. 

Once having settled upon the sequence, the motions are studied:

1. 

To eliminate those not needed. 2. 

To speed up those retained.

Following this process comes constant study to check up on those sequences adopted in search for better. To this end investigators have used motion pictures with time clocks in the field of view and ruled screens in the background, with other devices suitable to the operation and the mechanism involved, with the manual operation under study. 

It is possible to apply methods, the same in principle although not always identical, to a study of the more intimate personal operations. The executive who must apportion his time to correspondence, interviews, close supervision of a given department, together with a more remote supervision of a larger field can by trial and observation speed up his own performance and at the same time render it more efficient. 

PERSONAL APPLICATION OF STANDARDIZED OPERATIONS. 

Harrington Emerson points out at considerable length the value of detailed time and motion-study applied to one's rising and dressing, not alone in the details of that operation, but in the familiarity it gives with the fundamental theory involved. 

He says: "More valuable than anything else will be the knowledge and training you acquire in the use of time and motion studies for standardization of operations. The same method you would use in standardizing your operations of rising and dressing is now in use in many large manufacturing and other

concerns for standardizing operations. A saving of many thousands of dollars may result for every minute saved". 

IMPROVED EFFICIENCY PROCEEDS IRREGULARLY. It is in connection with the personal applications of standardizations that the great exponent of personal efficiency strikes a warning note. "The greatest gains in efficiency are ordinarily made when the student first begins to apply the principles. It is unwise, perhaps, to be too enthusiastic when entering on a systematic effort to increase individual efficiency. It is better to make a gradual approach to higher efficiency thereby training the mind slowly to achieve greater ability. A gradual improvement allows standardized habits to be formed; a very rapid gain does not give sufficient time for the development of such habits and is apt to lead to an actual decline."

Harrington Emerson, "in Efficiency, as in mountain climbing, does not necessarily mean easy work. It sometimes means exceedingly hard work, but whether you make the work easy or make it hard, the effect of the standardized work is to increase the desirable result far beyond the amount of effort. 

To pass from great effort and little result to little effort and great result sometimes requires a supreme effort until the transition is made. For example, it is much harder to learn how to ride a bicycle than to walk. But once you have learned to ride, you travel a mile with less effort than it took to walk a quarter of the distance."

FRANKLIN IN ACCORD WITH MODERN PSYCHOLOGY. 

The written instructions of Franklin and those who have followed his precepts, have time and again proved their value. While these emphasized the "old-fashioned" virtues, they still are very modern in that they are positive; that is, they put the stress on the definite practice of the virtue instead of forbidding the practice of its opposite vice.

In accordance with the best principles of pedagogy and the practical psychology of today Franklin's practice eliminated bad habits not by prohibiting them but by substituting good ones for them. 

Personal efficiency results then from following, after writing them down, certain definite practice instructions. A definite goal is set instead of the indefinite "I am going to improve myself". The written instructions give purpose, direction, and focus to one's efforts. The enthusiasm stimulated by a definite aim and the setting down of achievements as they are attained, gives zest to the pursuit of the outlined course.

CHAPTER X DISCIPLINE 

The Verb, discipline, means to train to obedience, drill, educate

The Noun, discipline, means (1) systematic training, or subjection to authority, (2) punishment for the sake of training.

Personal Efficiency Principle Number Eleven YOU MUST DISCIPLINE YOURSELF INTO ACTIVE HARMONY WITH THE PRINCIPLES, LAWS, AND RULES GOVERNING THE DESIRABLE THINGS OF LIFE 

To most people the word discipline brings up at once the thought of punishment and, as we see in the dictionary definitions, the word has such a meaning or connotation; but only secondarily

WHAT DOES THE WORD REALLY MEAN? The primary meaning of the word derived from the Latin disco, I learn, and more directly from discipulus, learner is training, drill, education. This is by far the most important meaning. Oddly, how ever, when the usual teacher, or parent, speaks of disciplining a child, we have visions of the rod, the deprivation of a privi-. lege, temporary banishment from the table and all the catalogue of punishments that ingenuity suggests to the fertile brain of the annoyed pedagogue or the irritated parent. Where the feeling of revenge or retaliation dominates the motive of punishment, the disciplinarian has completely lost sight of the purpose of even this meaning of discipline which is "to punish

for the sake of training".

DISCIPLINE 

PUNISHMENT. 

Let us first consider, then, this punishment meaning of discipline. In the world of nature, and in that carried on under man-made government, certain rules or laws are laid down. For the infraction of each one a penalty is imposed. It may come swiftly; it may be deferred. In nature it is inexorable; under perfectly just government it is equally sure. 

NATURE'S WAY. When the small child touches a hot stove, or the larger boy picks up a hot horse shoe in the now infrequent blacksmith shop, punishment is sudden and severe and its cause vividly obvious. The lesson is never forgotten and if the child is not an idiot he will never again break. nature's law that hot things burn. The training value of this punishment is high; the transgressor is effectually disciplined for his infraction of the law. 

The simple justice of the court that sits at the roadside, to fine automobiles for breaking traffic laws, is striking in its sudden application of punishment for misdemeanor.

Sometimes Nature is not sudden and sometimes not severe. But she is always inexorable. Her punishments are sure. The penalties for errors of diet, over-eating for example, for late hours, for vicious living, may be long deferred but they are certain.

THE PRINCIPLE OF DISCIPLINE 

PUNISHMENT 

Following the sudden application of Nature's penalties it is easy to deduce the principle which applies to Discipline. Punishment to carry training value must be sudden, severe and easily identified with the reason for punishment. 

MAN-MADE DISCIPLINE. The child who abuses the privi lege of playing with others by annoying acts, is best punished by banishment from the game. The best authorities agree that the discipline of his classmates or playmates is usually much more effective than that of his parents. We employ this principle effectively in the formal games at school and college and among adults. 

Where this punishment would be lightly regarded, as often among the professional players, a fine is added appealing to the professional's most easily touched sensibility his pocketbook nerve. The banishment from the game, even in this case, strikes at his manager for his neglect to control the player. Its effect is to train the former so to handle his group that repetition of the offense will not occur. These are man-made disciplines.

DISCIPLINE FROM THE OUTSIDE. The examples cited show 

The principle is that discipline applied by outside agencies. 

of Elimination, Extirpation. The transgressor of Nature's laws sees in the burning of his hand a threat of total elimination, that is, death, if the sudden warning is disregarded by retaining his hold on the iron, or contact with a still larger heated

mass. The pains of nipping cold warn that death by freezing is possible. The pain in some organ, the stomach for instance, warns that some error of living may result in death if not corrected. The training value lies in picturing by a relatively slight punishment the dreadful outcome of continued or repeated infractions - death. So it is in Nature. 

MAN IMITATES NATURE'S DISCIPLINE. The foreman who lays off a lazy workman, the railroad superintendent who suspends a careless engine driver, the military board which demotes a recalcitrant officer, are simply picturing for them the extreme penalty of elimination from the service they are engaged in. 

It is this discipline, administered as soon as may be after the event, that is most effective. If it is not immediate and distinctly connected with the offense there is little, if any, training connected with it. This principle is most frequently forgotten by parents and teachers, that punishment should follow as closely as possible the commission of the "crime". 

This is particularly true in the case of the disciplining of the young and those who lack strong sense of responsibility. ing "until father comes home is bad.

THE BROADER MEANING OF DISCIPLINE 

Discipline has another much broader meaning than this one of punishment. It implies systematic training to act in accordance with established rules. This training feature must be kept clearly in mind. It is the very essence of good discipline. If everyone is thoroughly instructed in what is expected from him, there will be few infractions of rules. 

PENALTY IN THE BACKGROUND. 

Of course the possibility of penalty is always in the background. If rules are disregarded or broken in spite of sound training, corrective measures must be taken. However, if the ground work for discipline has been well laid, the need for such measures should be of secondary importance. "If the little jobs are done well, there are no big jobs" applies here as elsewhere. 

MILITARY DISCIPLINE. 

As a general rule, the larger the group of men involved and the more vital the enterprise upon which they are engaged, the greater is the need for discipline and the more rigid must be the code by which it is maintained. 

Until the advent of our modern industries and great transportation systems, the largest organized groups of men that the world had ever seen were the military forces of the various nations. Their purpose, to increase the scope and power of the nation and often to preserve its very life, was of vital consequence. Hence, military discipline, from the early days, has been developed in elaborate form and along highly scientific lines. It remains today as the most stringent and rigid form

of discipline in the world.

INDUSTRIAL DISCIPLINE. 

The great business enterprises which have grown up during the last two or three generations now rival many armies in the number of their employees. The Pennsylvania Railroad, for example, has a working force of nearly 200,000 men. Many of the armies that have made history, even in comparatively recent times, were much smaller than that. In our own Civil War no armed force on either side, in any single battle, approached that number of men. In so far as numbers along are concerned, the need for discipline of the highest type is as great in many organizations as it is in an army. 

THE VITAL PURPOSE OF DISCIPLINE. The need for indus trial discipline also exists from the viewpoint of vital purpose. 

Practically every industrial organization is established to render some form of service to the public. In many cases, the lives of thousands of persons, and the safety of millions of dollars in property value, are dependent upon the prompt and faithful performance of duty. So, in this respect also, the need for industrial discipline is as obvious as in a military force. 

Industrial discipline is not as rigid or severe as military discipline, the penalties for infraction of rules are not as definitely prescribed. An industrial supervisor must depend largely upon his own judgment in administering disciplinary measures. While this increases his responsibility, it also has certain advantages.

It is possible for the supervisor to vary his treatment of different cases in accordance with the temperament and demeanor of the offender.

It should be understood, however, that the superior must not exercise too much discrimination in dealing with his men as he is very likely to subject himself to the charge of favoritism and thus lose the benefits to be derived by establishing the feeling among the men that they are being treated fairly and with impartiality. 

PURPOSE OF DISCIPLINE. 

Discipline should not be considered as a theory but from a practical point of view. Discipline is never an end in itself. It is merely a necessary means to an end, and must always be viewed in that light. 

THE SOLDIER. 

Soldiers are not instructed and drilled for hours on end, day in and day out, and constantly taught team work and implicit obedience to orders merely so that they will look well on parade. The object of such discipline is to weld them into a fighting unit that can be counted on not to fail in any emergency arising in the stress of battle. 

THE THINKER. Industrial employees, on the other hand, are organized for the accomplishment of work. The object is to provide for the advancement of production and commerce so that the people of our country may not only live but live better. Industrial discipline is maintained and disciplinary penalties administered simply and solely to aid in accomplishing the purpose for which industry is organized.

"WORK" IN THE NAVY. One of the wastes of human energy is exhibited in the "polishing of bright work", as it is called, in the Navy. Under the necessity of keeping a large personnel in training for the emergency of wars which may occur at intervals of 20, 30, or 50 years, the enlisted men of that service are often put at "work" whose real unnecessity is realized by both officers and men.

This type of work is defensible, of course. Idleness is more demoralizing than the most useless of activities and hence large numbers are put at the work of polishing bright surfaces which conceivably might as well be painted. Not only the showy brass railings and mountings of various devices are daily rubbed to a glittering brightness, but even the steel hand-rails which become tarnished over night in the steam of the engine room are laboriously rubbed to a mirror-like surface, 1

I have seen an enlisted man sit on the engine room gratings rubbing a section of hand-rail and from 8 o'clock until noon polish perhaps 10 or 15 feet of the steel tubing. Anytime one cast an eye in his direction he seemed to be engaged upon his task. He had learned the art of appearing busy. Perhaps it was all that was expected of him he was "busy" and out of mischief. 

ANOTHER PICTURE. But contrast this busyness with that of the same man, occupied with work engaging his intense interest, spurred by emulation, desirous of the praise of his officers, in war time stirred by patriotic motives. The same man would work in a cramped space in some emergency repair upon whose speedy completion depends, it may be, the lives of his shipmates. Such men have labored until they dropped and had to be carried from their task.

What was back of this tremendous burst of energy? Was it the supervision of a hard boiled "chief" or stern officer?

Was it the hope of early "liberty" to go ashore, or even possible promotion. None of these. It was the self-imposed discipline of a man aware of the importance of his task. 

SELF-DISCIPLINE IN INDUSTRY. 

Industry and business every day offer numerous instances of the self-imposed discipline. 

Who has not seen the feverish energy exhibited in an emergency repair in a great mill? Or the energy of an office staff when some unexpected call comes in? Or the tireless efforts of a mercantile establishment when an order must be filled in record time to sustain the reputation of the firm?

And how often is observed the sacrifice of sleep and the taking of great personal risk in a railway or industrial catastrophe; or even a tie-up of traffic facilities, a public utility, light, power, water, communication, which has only an interruption of service as its cause of extra effort. 

In the days when laws and regulatory measures permitted, men have worked for 24, 48, and more hours with hardly an intermission save for food. The extra pay they might earn had little to do with their willingness to make the extra sacrifice; the authority of the foreman or supervisor no more. One who has worked among such groups, or even observed them, easily senses the personal pride in the performance. 

STRONG SELF-DISCIPLINE. 

Yet, that is discipline, but it comes from within. Some men have a strong sense of honor which will not permit them to violate a principle at any cost; their discipline is the dread of losing their self-respect.

ANALYSIS OF CHARM. "Charm of manner and efficient practice of the amenities spring, in their best form, from four sources; first, justice; second, desire to serve; third, kindliness; fourth, self-discipline. The fundamental element in all peaceful and efficient relationships between man and his fellowmen is justice the principle of equivalency or the fair deal. 

Justice involves a keen recognition of the rights of others. couthness, roughness, brutality, selfishness, and inconsiderateness all trample upon the rights of others, as do many of the more aggressive and violent faults. And yet we may be perfectly just in our treatment of others in a cold, dispassionate, exacting manner a manner which repels, chills, and antagonizes rather than one which draws, warms, and awakens friendliness. Something more than rigid justice is required. "A true desire to serve is coming to be recognized in the business world as the very heart of business building.

Similarly, in human relationships it is a true desire to serve that gives genuine charm and leads on to the highest success. 

Kindliness is the sunshine which warms and beautifies the rugged and stern rocks of justice. Justice administered harshly or coldly antagonizes, while justice vitalized with a true desire to serve, and warmed by kindliness, makes friends of even those who must be punished. I once knew a man whose kindliness and courtly dignity were so efficient that when he discharged an employee, as he was sometimes compelled to do, the man left his office feeling the warm glow of one who had received a favor. 

DISCIPLINE BACK OF ALL. "That you may be just; that you may express in your manner a true desire to serve, and that you may be kindly, you must discipline yourself. It would be impossible for me to set down in this lesson complete, detailed, and specific directions as to how to practice the amenities. 

This is not a dissertation on etiquette. There are plenty of books on the subject which cover the ground telling you what not to do and what to do in order to be conventionally polite. 

CONVENTIONALITY. 

"But while it is important that in general you should practice the conventionalities of polite society, the things you thus do are relatively unimportant compared with your manner of doing them. The man with a warm, genial, friendly smile and the kind of handclasp that goes with it makes a far better impression upon most people, although he may sometimes shake hands and smile when, according to strict conventionalities, he should do neither, than a man with a stiff,

icy grin, and cold, limp, clammy handshake, who walks according to the strictest sect of the Pharisees of fashion." 

WE MUST PLEASE. In a world where we come in such close contact with others that they see us, hear us, smell us, feel us, and almost taste us, these others have the right, in all justice, to demand that our effect upon their senses, as far as possible, shall be pleasing. If we have a true desire to serve, we recognize the justice of this claim. We strive not only to meet it, but to give good measure. We refrain from offending the eyes, ears, nostrils, and physical feelings of others as well as their more sensitive physical feelings, and, in every way possible, give pleasure instead. This brings us to the very core of the whole subject.

TRUE COURTESY. 

The crude, uncouth, and tactless man, while violating none of the externals of courtesy, can, by his word or manner, wound and outrage the tenderest feelings and sentiments of others. The truly courteous, charming man, though he may be ignorant of local conventionalities, by the fineness, keenness, and sensitiveness of his own feelings, by his profound knowledge of human nature, adopts just that beautiful deference of manner, voice, facial expression, bearing, and dignity, and that delicate turn of phraseology which give pleasure to the feelings of others. No man attains this keen responsiveness to the feelings of others except through the four factors desire to serve, kindliness, and self-discipline.

SELF-DISCIPLINE AGAIN. Now what are you to discipline

yourself to do? First of all, to be just in your dealings with others; to let justice be a ruling factor. In justice there is first of all a sense of justice, which is a part of common sense since it has in it sound practical judgment, strong right feeling, and keen and true intuitions. Kindliness is also a feeling, but like justice, like the desire to serve, its value as a mere feeling is unimportant compared with its value as a feeling in action. "As the basis of any art becomes more scientifically developed, the art becomes less and less dependent on personality; for scientific investigation consists in part, of separating facts from personal opinion. Scientific management, so called, is not a code of rules as much as it is an attitude of mind that aims to replace 'I think' with 'I know'; and the extent to which this can be accomplished will depend on how far the principles involved can be developed quantitatively.

Strange as it may seem, there are many people who have done things well, but who dwell upon their failures rather than on their achievements. THIRD, DISCOUNTING OF FAILURES. The obvious deduction

from the plan of paying attention to victories is the rule of discounting defeats. The failure is a significant thing, because there are many aspects of it which can be turned to account in a constructive way. One executive says: "Remember the reasons and forget the mistake." This attitude will help to prevent a recurrence of the failure and will have no harmful effect on the individuals' morale. A wise executive of many years' experience adds an interesting bit of advice: "If I were to fall down completely, probably I should not be able to tell the difference for ten years. FOURTH, COMPARISON WITH OTHERS. One of the conditions of self-confidence is a reasonable satisfaction with oneself. 

This assists in blowing away the clouds of illusion. But how can the executive reach that "reasonable" satisfaction? Is it reasonable to be satisfied with anything less than perfection? 

A prominent man was confronted with exactly this question at the age of about thirty-five. He was continually despondent because he was unable to do anything as well as some other men. 

As a boy he had expected to go down in history as one of the immortals. But he found one famous character who seemed to have a better memory, another who was more wealthy, a third with greater skill, others with other qualities which he prized but possessed in a less degree than they. With so high an ambition, and so keen

a realization of the impossibility of excelling, he became embittered. But an end came to his difficulties when he began to observe the average man around him rather than the best in history. 

He was in many respects superior to the run of men around him, and as soon as he recognized this his despondency disappeared. The lesson from his career is, of course, that a reasonable satisfaction is to be had from comparison with the average rather than with the best. Such a conclusion does not agree with the mottoes of the copybooks of a couple of generations ago, the so-called "magazines of success". But it does not need to agree with them, for it has behind it the authority of successful psychiatrists who have attacked the problems of this generation in a disinterested way.

FIFTH, POSITIVE SUGGESTION. 

There is the method of positive suggestion popularized by Coue, the French Apothecary, who, by the way, did not originate the idea but did give it vogue. 

It is as old as man. It turns on the influence of mind over matter, long a problem in technical philosophy, recently a problem of those who are specializing in some branches of psychology, but recognized everywhere in common sense as an important influence. 

The world becomes roseate when friends give praise. Worry brings nervous dyspepsia.

USING THE SUBCONSCIOUS MIND. If a man can educate his subconscious mind to believe that things are "getting better and better," not only will his self-confidence be increased, but also his actual powers, physical and mental. There are limits to the improvement that can be so effected, but in the worst cases the limits are far off. The practice of this scheme involves repeating to oneself that all is well. If, before retiring, upon awaking, during the day whenever the scheme is recalled, one affirms

that this or that job is easy, Coue claims that faith in oneself will result. The secret of success by this method is to continue to reiterate the sentiment; constant quiet repetition brings conviction, and conviction brings the desired result. It seems quite simple.

Positive suggestion is not a substitute for hard work, nor for the other more solid methods which have been outlined. 

It is a supplementary measure which can be used in simplifying the task of developing self-confidence, and constitutes an excellent plan for an attack on the whole problem at once. 

SUMMING UP. Thus there are five methods which can be used in connection with our abilities to secure success: (1) setting of reasonable goals; (2) the reviewing of past successes; (3) the discounting of mistakes; (4) comparison with average associates; and, (5) the method of make-believe. These may be employed together or separately. But the man who tries these methods should not expect to find them an overnight method.
