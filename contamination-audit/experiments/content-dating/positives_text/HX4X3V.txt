SIDNEY LANIER 

SIDNEY LANIER was born at Macon, Georgia, 3 February 1842, and died in North Carolina, 7 September 1881. 

The Genius of Music possessed him as a child; he seemed to be able to play any instrument without instruction; if left alone with it, the two came together like steel and magnet. He organized a boys' orchestra; and the language of music was as natural to him as to a bird. He simply could not strike a false note. Over the violin he fell into a veritable trance; and that instrument, with the flute, were his favourites; later he was to be a professional flautist. 

At the age of fourteen, he entered Oglethorpe

University, a Presbyterian institution that perhaps by its rigidity of creed strengthened his later antipathy to narrow theologies while it did not deprive him of religious passion. 

There happened to be in this somewhat primitive institution a Professor Woodrow, who had returned from two years of study in Germany; this remarkable scholar fired Lanier's soul with a zeal for learning, so that he hoped to spend some years of study in Germany. He was graduated at the head of his class; but all thoughts of advanced research in scholarship were annihilated by the artillery of April 1861. An ardent Southerner, he

believed with all his heart in the cause of the Confederates, served in the army and was distinguished by remaining a private throughout the four years of the war!

He was captured and the months in prison were disastrous; for his health, never good, was permanently injured.

Then for the rest of his life he was engaged in a war more terrible for him than a national struggle. He fought against chronic sickness and chronic poverty. His only source of happiness lay in his ideally happy marriage.

Here was a fight with no spectators, no cheering or hostile crowds, no glory, and no rewards; but he fought day after day, night after night, like the gallant gentleman he was. It is possible that in the lives of some poets terrible obstacles minister to their genius; that if it were not for their struggles, they would not have been inspired. But in this case, Lanier did not need privation, hardship, or poverty, or sickness; it seems to me certain that if he had enjoyed comparative health and leisure, he would have accomplished much more. It is all but miraculous what he actually achieved. 

The passion for poetry, the passion for music, the passion for scholarship, the passion for religion devoured him. Here is a letter (in the Aldis Collection of American Literature, Yale University 

Library) that he wrote to Edmund Clarence Stedman.

Westminster Hotel, [New York] 

Tuesday, Oct. 5th, 1875.

DEAR MR. STEDMAN: 

Won't you sometime or other-if only for your sins-read my "Symphony" in the magazine sent herewith?

Although written in a mere lawless gust-a sort of little whirlwind originating at a point where the passion of music and the passion of pity, blowing different ways, happened to converge yet, since it has cooled a little, and I have called myself to account for it, so many and great extensions of the artistic principles underlying it occur to me that I feel a strong desire, in beginning to exemplify them in new works, to do so with at least the hope that better embodiments of them than this very crude one might find favor in the eyes of so keen a critical faculty as that which reveals itself in your "Victorian Poets."

When we meet again, which I hope may be soon, you shall abuse or praise my poem just as much as you like, knowing that in either case I'm going to remain 

Your friend,

SIDNEY LANIER. 

And a year before this, on 15 November 1874 he had written, "My head and my heart are both so full of poems which the dreadful struggle for bread does not give me time to put on paper, that 

I am often driven to headache and heartache, purely for want of an hour or two to hold a pen. 99

Lanier was a scholar; he taught in the Johns 

Hopkins University and the results of his researches are seen in his books on the relation of music to poetry and on the English novel; he was a musician and was a professional flute-player in the Peabody Symphony Orchestra; he was a poet, and wrote some poems that will never be forgotten; he was a teacher and fought two foes-intolerance in religion, and the spirit of philistine commercialism in public life.

As a literary critic, Lanier was both subtle and profound, especially when free from certain prejudices; his "lasso" criticism of Browning's powers as shown in The Ring and the Book is one of the most brilliant interpretations ever written on that epic.

Whether Lanier's professional skill in music was good or otherwise for his creative powers in verse is impossible to determine; all we can say is that if the power of transfiguration be the essential and unmistakable power of the true poet, Lanier had that power to such a degree that if he cannot be regarded as a luminary of the first magnitude he will ever shine as one of the fixed stars. He transfigured by his imagination and by his powers of expression the natural scenery he knew and loved; familiar sights and sounds; and out of the clash of creeds and the ever-increasing roar of machinery, he reduced discords to harmonies; future generations will hear the melodies of his verse, even as after the tempest we hear the singing of a solitary bird.
