Of the many hundreds of decisions rendered by the National Labor Relations Board since its establishment in 1935,¹ only a small minority has been reviewed by the courts.2 In the earlier cases, the issues most frequently pressed were the constitutionality of the act and the power of the federal district courts to restrain administrative actions of the Board, including hearings. Since the Jones & Laughlin decision, however, the cases arising in the circuit courts of appeal have for the most part raised problems concerning the substantive aspects of the statute. 5 A general body of law regarding unfair labor practices is rapidly being crystallized by the decisions of the Board and the courts. The Board's contributions on questions of law, while immensely important, must stand as tentative conclusions until upheld by the courts. The reported decisions do not run the gamut of the unfair practices condemned by the Board. Nevertheless, it may be useful at this formative stage to take stock of the existing concepts of unfair labor practices enunciated by the courts." 

Most of the cases have been concerned with discriminatory discharges, which have been treated as violations of both section 8(1), forbidding interference with the right of self-organization, and section 8(3), prohibiting discrimination with regard to conditions of employment. In no decision has there been a violation of subdivision 1 unaccompanied by such discharges. However, it is unquestioned that independent infractions of section 8(1) are possible, such as espionage, to cite but one instance.

The evidence in such cases is typically in sharp conflict. It is clear that the courts may not substitute their judgment of the weight and sufficiency of the evidence and the credibility of the witnesses for that of the Board. Although the statute provides that the Board's findings, if supported by evidence, shall be conclusive, it is not enough that there be but a scintilla of proof. On the other hand, the mere conflict of testimony does not preclude the finding of discrimination.10 

There appears to be a tendency to require substantial ¹¹ evidence of a violation, one court having gone so far as to suggest that the test is the same as that on an appeal from a refusal to direct a verdict in favor of the defendant.12 A criterion couched in less technical language and which affords greater latitude to the Board was formulated by Learned Hand, J.: . it was probably possible to reach a different result from the Board; yet there was more than a speculative basis for its findings "18 The test of substantiality opens the door to a full review of the evidence by the courts and may lead some tribunals to substitute their own appraisal of the testimony for that of the Board.14 The statute contemplates that the Board's findings will be respected by the courts and only overturned when so manifestly and demonstrably lacking in evidential support as to result in a miscarriage of justice. MISCONDUCT OF UNION MEMBERS UNDER THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT 

1 Although the National Labor Relations Act does not expressly penalize the misconduct of unions and their members, recent judicial utterances ¹ indicate that such misfeasance may have important legal consequences in the enforcement of the statute. Conflicting tendencies have been manifested in the few court decisions dealing with the problem. While the differences between the Board and some of the circuit courts of appeals are quite fundamental, the apparent inconsistency in the court rulings, it is believed, is capable of resolution. The absence of any direct sanction obviously precludes the issuance of any affirmative order against the union or worker guilty of wrongdoing. Only by a denial of the benefits of the statute, in whole or in part, can such misconduct be punished by the Board. The statute does not in express terms authorize the visitation of such penalty upon the delinquent employee or bargaining agency. On the contrary, its language is both mandatory and unqualified in directing the issuance of a cease and desist order against an employer who has engaged or is engaging in an unfair labor practice. The Board, however, has conceived its functions to be equitable in nature,5 at least so far as its remedial orders are concerned, and this conception permits it to import into some of its determinations a doctrine of "unclean hands." 

The statute protects the workers' rights of self-organization and collective bargaining against five species of unfair labor practices. What significance can be attached to the misconduct of union members, no matter how gross, where an employer has been guilty of espionage or other overt interferences with or restraints upon the self-organization of his workers, or where he has been instrumental in the formation of a company union, or where he has discharged an employee for filing charges or giving testimony under the statute? 

The "you too" finger of scorn pointed by the employer at his accusers can scarcely be deemed a justification for his own derelictions. A doctrine which clothed such clear infractions of law with immunity by reason of his employee's transgressions would be of dubious morality. Wrongdoing by whomsoever committed should be curbed, not condoned.

AFFIRMATIVE ORDERS OF THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD 

The enforcement orders of the National Labor Relations Board are of two types: (1) negative restraints, in the form of cease and desist orders which are designed to prevent the commission and recurrence of unfair labor practices, and (2) affirmative edicts, remedial in nature, which seek to redress the injury wrought by the employer's infraction of the law. The Board's authority to issue remedial orders is derived from section 10 (c) which empowers it "to take such affirmative action, including reinstatement of employees with or without back pay, as will effectuate the policies of this Act." The mandatory orders promulgated under this broad charter cover a wide range,¹ but only the more controversial and important decrees will be examined and appraised in this paper.

The statute, although expressly authorizing the reinstatement of employees, does not specify the circumstances under which, and the persons to whom such relief may be accorded. These issues were left to the Board for determination with merely the limited guidance to be found in the general declaration of policy in section one. Only by reinstatement can conditions consonant with the requirements of the statute be established where there has been a discriminatory discharge or where discrimination has been practised in the rehiring of strikers. A negative order by itself would be entirely ineffectual. 

That the inherent logic of the statute requires affirmative relief in such situations was early apprehended by the Board and by the courts.³ 

Somewhat more debatable is the question of the necessity and propriety of reinstating strikers in the case of a strike induced or prolonged by an unfair labor practice. The situation briefly is this: Following the denial of their statutory rights by the employer, the workers strike. The strike is unsuccessful and the positions vacated by the strikers are filled by a new crew. The 

Board, after hearing, finds that various unfair labor practices have been committed prior to or during the pendency of the strike. An order forbidding indulgence in any of the five practices prohibited by the statute will be of little benefit to strikers whose statutory rights were vetoed by the employer unless there is an accompanying order of reinstatement. Until the status quo is restored, an order in the terms of the statute, such as a command to bargain collectively, can have little meaning. WRITTEN CONTRACTS UNDER THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT 

A problem which bulks large in contemporary labor relations is whether the National Labor Relations Act requires an employer to execute and sign a written memorial of such accords as may be reached in treating with the representatives of his employees. That he is obligated to meet, confer, and negotiate in good faith with such representatives is settled by numerous administrative ¹ and judicial 2 rulings. In one of the first decisions of the old Board the employer's obligation was defined as follows: 3 ... the employer is obligated by the statute to negotiate in good faith with his employees' representatives; to match their proposals, if unacceptable, with counter-proposals, and to make every reasonable effort to reach an agreement.' 

Good faith negotiations necessarily import a sincere effort to reach an agreement. Without a contracting state of mind there can be no bargaining. 

As the noun "bargain" connotes a compact, the verb necessarily relates to the process by which an accord is reached the higgling and haggling of a dickera treaty over the terms of a contract-in short, the steps preceding and leading up to the making of an agreement. One who initiates negotiations with the determination not to agree is but observing the amenities while denying the substance of bargaining. "An assertion that collective bargaining connotes no more than discussions designed to clarify employer policy and does not include negotiation looking toward the adoption of a binding agreement between employer and employees is contrary to any realistic view of labor relations. 

The development of these relations had progressed too far when the Act was adopted to permit the conclusion that the Congress intended to safeguard only the barren right of discussion." In this respect there is no difference between individual and collective bargaining, or between the bargaining of seller and buyer and that of employer and employee. While the purpose and end of bargaining is agreement, not all negotiations can be or are successful. When there is an impasse, the parties can do no more than agree to disagree until either or both are prepared to yield on the points in dispute. It is in this sense that it can be said that the National Labor 

Relations Act does not compel agreements; the employer is not required to agree where there are honest differences nor to continue negotiations where

This principle has been expressed by the Board in terms which admit of no misunderstanding: "We must not be considered as holding that an employer is obligated by the Act to accede without more to the terms of a contract presented to him by the representatives of his employees. The Act does not so provide and we make no such interpretation. The Senate Report is clear on this point: ""The committee wishes to dispel any possible false impression that this bill is designed to compel the making of agreements or to permit governmental supervision of their terms. It must be stressed that the duty to bargain collectively does not carry with it the duty to reach an agreement, because the essence of collective bargaining is that either party shall be free to decide whether proposals made to it are satisfactory'. "Thus an employer is not required to sign the specific agreement presented to him by representatives of his employees. Nor is he obligated to agree to any of their demands solely for the sake of reaching some agreement when genuine accord is impossible although both sides are acting in good faith.” 

Not whether the statute requires the making of agreements, but whether it requires the signing of a written contract when negotiations have ripened into an accord is the issue which is still the subject of controversy. 

The Board in the Inland Steel case states the issue in the following terms: "... the precise question . . . is whether a refusal to embody, in a signed agreement, any understandings that may be reached, constitutes a failure to bargain collectively, within the meaning of the Act. In essence, the question is whether a willingness to embody understandings in a signed agreement is a concession which the employer may legitimately withhold." 

On this issue the statute furnishes no direct guidance. In none of the court cases has the question been even remotely involved. Elaborate consideration was given the problem in the Inland Steel case, when the Board came to the conclusion that a written contract was demanded by the statute. 

The steps by which this result was reached merit careful examination. 

The Board first relies upon arguments of policy and convenience. Collective agreements cover a wide range of topics and are complicated affairs; human memory is fallible and the absence of a writing provokes contention and discord; a writing, by adding solemnity and dignity to the compact, is more likely to command the obedience of both signatories and to stabilize employment relations.

Were the issue one of enforceability alone, it could be pointed out that numerous business arrangements of great complexity are valid though oral, and that the desirability of a written integration is no more compelling in the field of labor relations than it is in other fields. But the Board does not contend that an oral labor contract is unenforceable. It premises its argument on the fact that the oral is less effective than the written agreement, and then assumes that Congress, in imposing a statutory obligation upon employers, intended this obligation to be performed in an effective manner. Where the workers are satisfied with oral assurance, the statute demands no more; it is only where the workers insist upon a writing that a denial constitutes a refusal to bargain collectively.

The fundamental weakness of the Board's position is exemplified in the distinction which it draws between oral and written agreements. Its argument results in the following paradox: the oral agreement may be a valid collective bargain, but its consummation is not collective bargaining by the employer within the contemplation of the Act. In other words, the process of collective bargaining implies the making of an effective as well as a valid bargain. • St. Joseph Stock Yards Co., supra, note 4 at 54-5.

Doubtless it is within the legislative province to use ordinary language in a special sense, but some proof, more persuasive than that contained in the preamble to the statute, should be forthcoming that Congress intended the broader meaning contended for by the Board. No lawyer will dispute the vast superiority of the written over the oral agreement. Were the issue one of policy exclusively, there could be but a single answer. The issue, however, is primarily one of statutory construction. 

The Board has no authority to add to or subtract from the statutory requirements. Policy arguments may be of inestimable value in giving direction to the processes of construction; they will not, however, justify an interpretation which the words themselves cannot carry.

We turn, therefore, to the problem of construction. The Board's position hinges upon the proper meaning to be given to the term "bargain collectively" in the statute.10 It holds that the statutory obligation includes all the usages, conventions, and traditions of collective bargaining as practiced before the enactment of the law. Accordingly, evidence was offered to the effect that "written, signed agreements have become the established practice in collective bargaining relationships." 11 The Board, in addition, took judicial notice of the fact that written and signed agreements are the fruits of collective bargaining in the overwhelming majority of cases.12 From this evidence, it concludes that the statutory expression automatically includes the requirement of a written contract and that in its absence there can be no collective bargaining. 

The strength of the Board's position obviously depends upon the validity of its assumptions. Even though the written contract may be the accepted form of collective agreement in the majority of cases, it does not follow that the oral agreement is not in itself a species of statutory collective bargaining, or that it was not regarded as collective bargaining prior to the National Labor Relations Act. The teachings of experience are that collective agreements are generally embodied in signed, written instruments. But there was evidence in the Inland case that the oral agreement is not unknown to the history of collective bargaining. Something more than proof of the general practice before the enactment of the statute is necessary to sustain a construction which expels the oral agreement from the concept of collective bargaining. 

To justify its stand, the Board would have to show affirmatively that at the time the statute was enacted, an oral exchange of assurances was repugnant to the concept of collective bargaining; in other words, that a written contract was a necessary element as a matter of practice or law, or was so considered in the legislative history of the statute. 

The most that can be said for the Board's proof is that it affords a basis for reconstructing the general purpose of Congress; but on this vital issue it is not enough to prove that the general legislative purpose was that the statutory obligation should conform to the traditional pattern; there must in addition be specific proof that one species of collective bargaining which was both valid and in limited use before the statute was definitely rejected. 

Nor is it to be overlooked that at the time of enactment, a collective agreement was not an enforceable contract, in some jurisdictions, whether written or not. In England, such a compact is today but a gentleman's agreement, devoid of legal sanction, and depending for its enforcement on strikes and 49 Stat. 449 (1935), 29 U. S. C. § 151 et seq. The Board adverted to no portion of the statute which might be thought to support its position.

It is undoubtedly the tendency today to treat a collective agreement as a legally enforceable contract, but the authorities are too sparse for it to be said that collective bargaining had, by 1935, become a term of art connoting the more effective of two types of enforceable obligations. 

Another approach to the problem is possible. As previously stated, the statute demands good faith on the part of the employer in his negotiations. 

We have seen the merit in the ruling that he is lacking in good faith where he has no intention of making any agreement, oral or written, with the representative of his employees. Is he acting in good faith where he announces at the outset of negotiations that he will not, under any circumstances, execute a writing? The Board's experience has taught that the unwillingness to sign a union contract is not infrequently part of a deliberate program to weaken and discredit the union.15 In many cases the employer is desirous of reducing the process of collective bargaining to the level of meaningless ritual, while sapping it of all vitality. Certainly the refusal to embody an accord in a written, signed agreement is probative of the faith, good or bad, of the employer. What reason can be advanced for the employer's refusal? An antiunion bias may indicate that his ostensible reasons are but sham, and that his objection to the writing is a desperate last line expedient to circumvent the statute.16 On the other hand, the refusal may conceivably be consistent with a desire, in good faith, to live up to the statute. The word "conceivably" is designedly used because it is not easy to outline circumstances consistent with the allegation of good faith.

The Board in the Inland Steel case invoked the "good-faith" analysis,¹7 but it was not content to rest its decision on the bad faith of the respondent. 

Although the results in particular cases may be the same, there is vital difference between an analysis based on good faith and one based on statutory construction. Under the former view, where there is good faith no matter on what facts predicated, there is no violation of the statute despite the refusal to sign a written contract.

Does the concept of good faith demand merely a subjective honesty of intention, or must the grounds of the refusal be objectively reasonable? The 

Board veers toward the objective construction in the Inland case, and its position appears to be sound; otherwise, the administration of the law could be crippled by the insistence of the employer that bizarre objections to the continuance of bargaining were sincerely and honestly entertained. Here again is a corner of the law still requiring judicial examination and development.

THE MORGAN CASE AND THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD 

In its three opinions in the Morgan case,¹ the Supreme Court has attempted to define the rudimentary requirements of a fair hearing by an administrative agency. Though the case related merely to the procedure of the Secretary of Agriculture under the Packers and Stockyards Act 2 the Court used the occasion to discuss the problem in its broader aspects and in terms applicable to all administrative agencies. Litigants have not been slow to perceive the potentialities of the Morgan rulings in their contests with the National Labor Relations Board, and the validity of its methods of hearing and determination has already been challenged. Whether the Board's rules conform to the requirements of the Morgan case is a question meriting careful consideration. 

The Morgan case had its inception in a proceeding initiated by the Secretary of Agriculture in April, 1930, by an order of inquiry and notice of hearing. The purpose of the inquiry was to investigate the reasonableness of the rates of fifty commission agents doing business in the Kansas City stockyards. After evidence had been taken by an examiner at an extended hearing, an oral argument was had before the Acting Secretary of Agriculture. In 1932 the Secretary issued his findings and an order prescribing maximum rates. Subsequently the order was vacated and a new hearing granted as a result of changes in economic conditions. The evidence received at the first hearing was resubmitted and supplemented by additional testimony and exhibits. No preliminary report was prepared by the examiner despite the request for such report by the respondents. Oral argument of a "general and sketchy" nature was then had before an Assistant Secretary of Agriculture sitting as the Acting Secretary.

A brief was submitted by the respondents but not by the government. Findings of fact were prepared by the government representatives who had conducted the proceedings, and were submitted to the Secretary, who signed them with a few changes. The Secretary found that respondents' rates were unreasonable and unjustly discriminatory, and he prescribed a new schedule of charges. Thereupon respondents brought suit to restrain the enforcement of the Secretary's order, claiming that they had been denied the fair hearing demanded by the statute, and alleging, on information and belief, that the Secretary had not heard or read the evidence or considered the arguments, and that his sole information was derived from ex parte consultation with the employees of the Department. In its first opinion the Supreme Court held that the trial court had erred in striking these allegations and required the Secretary to answer them. Following this decision, interrogatories were submitted to the Secretary, the answers to which disclosed that the evidence in the case had

The Board's use of the review section has had serious consequences. On the theory that "the Court ought not to enforce pretended findings of the Board which are not really such," 23 a circuit court of appeals compelled the Board to answer the employer's interrogatories regarding the manner in which the findings of fact were arrived at. The Morgan decision is distinguishable on this point, since there was reason to believe that the final decision had been made by one who had not sufficiently addressed himself to the evidence and the argument. Moreover, the Morgan case related to the somewhat indefensible administrative procedure under which important judicial functions are reposed in a busy executive official and cabinet officer. The 

Labor Board consists of three members, whose time and attention are concentrated upon the administration of a single statute and who are in a position to supervise the work of subordinates and effectively to utilize such assistance in the discharge of their own duties. The efficiency and dignity of the Board's work will be materially impaired if litigants in each case are to be permitted to interrogate Board members concerning the intimate details of their labors. 

The principal doubt cast on the Board's procedure by the Morgan ruling concerns the transfer of proceedings and the promulgation of orders without an intermediate examiner's report or oral argument. The dubious validity of this method of decision is apparent and the Board itself has abandoned the practice and has caused its orders in such cases to be vacated, and the causes reopened for further proceedings.24 Under the revised rules either a trial examiner's report or proposed findings of fact and conclusions of law are served in every case and the attention of the respondents directed to the fact that oral argument may be had on request.25 It would appear preferable to have an examiner's report in every case, since one who has seen and heard the witnesses is clearly in the best position to pass upon issues of veracity. It is perfectly possible, however, that in some cases the examiner's report may not fully disclose all the claims and contentions of the government. Where this occurs the necessary disclosure should be made either in brief or in an argument.

A danger lurks in the preparation of proposed findings by the Board in advance of oral argument, since one who has studied the record and made findings is apt to be less receptive to the contentions of the party against whom the findings were made. The danger could be avoided if the review section, and not the Board, prepared the proposed findings, and if the ultimate findings were made by the Board after argument. 

Serious attention should be given to the Court's strictures on ex parte discussions between those representatives of the government who have tried the case and those who are preparing the findings and making the ultimate decision. Whether or not it is the practice of the Board to permit such discussions does not appear in any of the Board's publications. Public confidence in the integrity of the Board's methods would be immeasurably increased were it to be known that the Board does not countenance such communications. 

While the magnitude of the Board's labors and the limitations of personnel and funds preclude a degree of personal attention to details by Board members which might be desirable, and make inevitable some compromise with the ideals of administrative procedure, its methods of hearing and deciding do not appear to offend any constitutional requirements of fair hearing.

LABOR BOARD CASES PENDING BEFORE THE SUPREME COURT 

The Supreme Court has recently issued writs of certiorari in three cases involving further construction of the National Labor Relations Act.¹ Another such case was placed on the Court's docket near the close of its last term.2 

The decisions in the four cases will doubtless add considerably to the principles governing the meaning, scope and application of the Act which have been enunciated by the Court in the twelve kindred cases already decided by it.³ 

The Consolidated Edison case, the first-and, in many respects, the most important of the pending causes, presents a number of interesting problems. 

Foremost among these is the question of the applicability of the Act to this type of employer. The company is a public utility holding and operating concern, subject to the jurisdiction of the New York Public Service Commission. 

It sells gas and electrical energy to local residential and commercial consumers, some of which, such as railroads, ferries, telephone and radio companies, are engaged in interstate or foreign commerce. It uses in its business large quantities of coal and oil brought into New York from other states. Do the company's labor disputes "affect commerce" within the meaning of the Act? Under the constitutional doctrines in vogue before 1936, there would not have been the slightest doubt that the labor relations of a local public utility were beyond the ambit of federal regulation, despite its purchase of supplies in interstate commerce and sales to consumers engaged in such commerce. With the concept of interstate commerce in its present state of flux, it is difficult to predict what the Court's decision will be on this issue. The application of the statute to the facts of the case carries the law to the extreme limits of constitutional power, if indeed it does not transcend them. The Board's procedure is claimed by the Consolidated Edison Company to have been so defective as to constitute a denial of due process. After the hearing before the trial examiner and before an intermediate report could be rendered, the proceedings were transferred to the Board for decision. At no stage of the case was the company informed that it might present oral argument to the Board. No such oral argument was had. A written brief which had been submitted to the examiner was considered by the Board. The Board's rules were not clear as to the right of a respondent to demand oral argument."

The company insists that its request for a hearing before the Board was rejected, but this is denied by the Board in its brief. 99 10 Administrative due process has never been held to require that private parties be allowed to present oral argument at a hearing. The requirement, rather, is that the party be afforded complete opportunity "fully and fairly to meet the charges and claims by the presentation of evidence and argument. "Argument may be oral or written. The requirements are not technical." 11 

The absence of oral argument, thus, is not by itself a fatal omission. But the case is made more difficult by the added omission of an examiner's report. It is to be observed that in the Mackay case,12 where the Supreme Court upheld the Board's procedure despite the lack of any intermediate report, the employer had been permitted to present oral argument before the Board. Whether the conjunction of the two omissions is sufficient to constitute irremediable error when neither deficiency by itself has that effect, is presented for the first time. 

It will no doubt be argued that grave dangers inhere in a system of procedure which allows important decisions to be rendered, largely on the basis of a written record, by an administrative agency which has neither seen nor heard the witnesses, and which has not availed itself of the reactions and impressions of the trial examiner. The Circuit Court of Appeals frowned on the procedure, but did not consider that it violated any rights guaranteed by the Fifth Amendment.12 If, in fact, the record irrefutably supports the Board's findings, and the company's written brief in the proceeding reveals that it was fully apprised of the government's claims and had ample opportunity to state its position, the Supreme Court may find that while there may have been some deprivation of purely formal rights, the company was denied none of the substantial aspects of a fair hearing. In the Morgan case 13 the procedural defects consisted in lack of notice to the respondent of the government's claims, insufficient acquaintance with the record of the hearing by the Secretary of Agriculture and ex parte discussions by the Secretary with the departmental subordinates who had tried the case and prepared the findings.14 In the Edison case no argument is made that the Board failed to master the record, or that it conferred with the staff members in charge of the case. And although lack of apprisal is alleged, this contention goes only to a portion of the relief ordered by the Board-which will be considered below-and not to the entire proceedings. The likelihood of insufficient apprisal is much more remote where the complaint details the specific statutory violations charged, than in an amorphous rate proceeding. The Morgan case is thus technically distinguishable on its facts, but the question will remain whether the Board's procedure conformed to the spirit of that decision.

The facts were that after the company had been granted a continuance to locate two witnesses who were out of town, the Board refused to hear any other witnesses on the resumption of the hearing. On other occasions, the Board has been upheld in its edicts ordering the disestablishment of company unions and the withdrawal of recognition by the employer.17 The Consolidated Edison case brings before the Court for the first time the problem of deciding what steps the Board may take against a bona fide labor organization which has been improperly favored by the employer. The statute interdicts all employer-interference with the rights guaranteed by section 7; its terms are equally applicable whether the beneficiary of the violation is a bona fide or illegitimate union. Assuming that the record contains sufficient evidence that employees were coerced to join the Brotherhood before its collective agreements were signed, only by an order nullifying the agreement insofar as it affects the statutory rights of employees can the status quo be restored. Anything short of such an order would fail to undo the effects of the coercion and would allow the employer to retain the fruits of his violation.

The Edison Company claims it was never apprised that the validity of the contracts was in question, and both the company and the Brotherhood complain of the failure to make the latter a party to the proceedings.18 In the complaint, the company was charged with coercing its employees to join the Brotherhood; a finding in support of such a charge would clearly make the legality or continued life of the contracts the subject of a ruling by the Board. Moreover, the record indicates that the contracts were discussed at the hearing in considerable detail. The Morgan case leaves it unclear whether there must be a full disclosure of the precise relief sought by the government. Unless the standard is pitched on so high a level, it would not appear that there are sufficient grounds for reversing this phase of the Board's order, despite the apparent looseness of the Board's procedure in this respect. The argument based on the non-joinder of the Brotherhood 19 appears to be refuted by the decision in the Pennsylvania Greyhound case.20 However, it would seem to be a judicious practice to join bona fide unions as parties defendant in such cases, even though there be no constitutional requirement of this nature.

The issues for determination are whether such discretion exists and if so, whether it was properly exercised in this case.

The Sands 23 and Columbian 24 cases raise questions of fact and law which are of vital importance in the administration of the Act. Do employees who strike in violation of a binding contract lose their status under the Act? Does a prior breach of contract by employees exculpate an employer's violation of the statute? To what extent may the courts substitute their findings of fact for those of the Board? In both decisions the lower courts held that a breach of contract by employees resulted in a forfeiture of their statutory rights. The arguments in favor of such a result are that the union does not come into court with clean hands, and further, that although hardship may be caused in individual cases, any indirect sanction which compels either party to a collective labor agreement scrupulously to honor its obligations is a salutary means of securing an abiding industrial peace. On the other hand, other circuit courts of appeal 25 which have considered the same or closely allied issues have pointed out that the clean hands argument is an equitable doctrine, inapplicable to the proceedings under the Act. Moreover, the suit is by the Board, a public body, and not by the offending employees. The effect of union misconduct has been considered in a previous Comment. Certainly, employees who violate a collective agreement should not lose their status of employees under the statute. Whether, if the victims of discrimination, they should be reinstated should be discretionary with the Board. Whether an employer should be compelled to bargain collectively with a union which has broken its engagement raises a difficult question on which little enlightenment is to be found in the statute.

In both cases the lower courts ignored or overruled important findings of the Board, including the finding that the employees' conduct did not amount to a breach of contract. A careful examination of the record by the appellate court is highly desirable, but there is no warrant for the court's substituting its own inferences from the testimony for those of the Board.26 If the Supreme Court finds that there was no breach of contract in either case, it may find it unnecessary to pass on the interesting questions of law otherwise raised by the records. 

The four cases constitute the most important group of Labor Board cases to come before the Supreme Court since its initial decisions.
