THE FEOPLE'S PEACE 

Plans for a new post-war world are multiplying from day to day. The vision of the age ahead as a "century of the common man, # a world in which all men will be free and all will be prosperous, is held before us. Why are this vision and the plans to realize it meeting with less than the enthusiastic response that might be expected from the common man who is to come into the enjoyment of such a future?

There may be other answers, but it is safe to say that one of them is this:

The common man knows, perhaps intuitively, that a change which reaches far deeper than any of the economic and political readjustments proposed however sincere and determined those who propose them is needed to create a world so profoundly different from the pre-war world or from this warring world of today. In the most democratic and prosperous countries before the war, there were millions of men and women and even children who did not have the "quart of milk a day" which Vice President Wallace uses as the symbol of the abundant future of all peoples everywhere. They didn't have it because we thought other things were better worth doing than providing food for everyone. Obviously a fundamental change in values is necessary if we are to create the kind of world we now look forward to. 

What Kind of World 

Do We Live In?

Within modern times, men's conception of the universe was completely altered by the 560632

-20 revelations of modern science. It is time for a second alteration, for the picture based on early scientific findings to be replaced by another more nearly in line with later knowledge.

It was natural enough that as science developed there was increasing emphasis on tangible values as the only real values, on mechanical, man-controlled power as the only power. It followed easily, from announcement of Darwin's evolutionary theory of the universe and life as it was interpreted, that human progress should be thought to depend upon struggle between com-. peting groups in which survival depended upon strength and the weak were not meant to prosper. 

Today science has progressed far enough to recognize its own limitations, to sense powers other than mechanical and human, to acknowledge values that cannot be weighed and measured, even to question the reliability of the human senses and their reports. The theory of evolution has been modified and now traces the line of survival and progress through creatures sensitive, aware, "open-minded," adaptable, cooperative, beyond the average. Above all, discoveries in separate fields, which once tended to divide and pigeon-hole, have converged toward recognition of an underlying unity throughout all creation. The unity of mankind, which once only seers and prophets attested, is now proclaimed by science and is demonstrated in forms within the grasp of everyone.

Because, in acting on the old idea of a universe of struggle and of a humanity divided against itself, men were acting on a false

assumption, such disaster as is now upon us was bound to result. Only as we correct our underlying conceptions and alter our behavior accordingly can we hope to have more success in arriving at the goals we intend.

Need for "Total" Change Recognized

Wherever there is profound thinking today, along either philosophical, econonic or religious lines, there is insistence on the need for this kind of deep-reaching, "total" change. their book on "The Problems of Peace, !! Hoover and Gibson suggest the intellectual and spiritual qualities (not conspicuous in the old world) which will be needed if civilization is to be saved at the peace table. "Never," they point out, "will humanity need more objectivity, more tolerance and more vision, more open and more prayerful minds."

Huch more explicit on this point are such books as "Ele Crisis of Our Age," by Professor Sorokin, head of the Department of Sociology at Harvard. o few sentences can properly state the theme of such a book, but, very inadequately stated, it is this: The crisis we are passing through is one of the few really great crises of history, which have marked changes in the underlying values on which cultures are based. The period which is now coming to an end in catastrophe is one in which material values have pre… vailed to the exclusion of all others.

Acceptance of material value as the highest value led inevitably to concentration on selfish immediate goals, to contractual rather than natural relationships, and to the ultimate breakdown

even of those contractual relations when they interfered with individual ambitions. It led to reliance on force, to consideration of man as a material organism, a behavioristic mechanism, therefore as merely a means to be used, not an end in himself.

This materialistic culture, divisive as everything material is bound to be, is disintegrating because of inherent faults and lacks not that its values are wholly untrue, but that they do not represent the whole truth. Escape from the chaos in which a materialistic age has culminated can come not through its restoration in some other form, but through acceptance of the values which it denied and discarded. Those neglected values were spiritual values. It is through cultivation of spiritual qualities that man is able to rise above what limits and separates, that he is able to see life whole.

Escape From Chaos 

Preparation for the change that we must make to escape self-destruction, to quote from the book itself, "implies the deepest re-examination of the main premises and values of sensate (material) culture, rejection of its super-annuated pseudo-values and re-enthronement of the real values it has discarded." 

There must be recognition that "Man is not only an organism but is also a bearer of absolute value. As such, he is sacred and, regardless of sex, age, race and social status, cannot be used as a mere means for anything or anybody... there must be a change of the whole mentality and attitude in the direction of the norms prescribed in the Sermon on the Mount. Basic Economic Change 

In political-economic terms, extent of the change required is made clear in "Conditions of 

Peace" by E. H. Carr, of the University of Wales.

Democracy itself must be reinterpreted. The ideals of "equality" and "liberty" must be given economic content. A sense of responsibility must be injected into mass democracy--for only those who accept responsibility can be independent. In the economic field, activity must be directed toward increasing the human welfare of the consumer instead of the wealth of the producer. "The period in which the first essential was to stimulate production has ended." "We are working toward a substitution for money values of social values... this by itself means a profound revolution in practice and thought." "The economic crisis is in essence a moral crisis."

The extent of the change is indicated also in the historical summary set forth in a post-war proposal adopted by a Town Hall panel of economists and students of foreign affairs in November, 1941.

A Call From The Churches 

The churches on their part are issuing clear calls to a change of heart and setting an example which emphasizes values other than material and reliance on a power above human powers. Consider the bishops of occupied countries who are refus ing to bow to the authority of the state and thereby subordinate spiritual to political values. 

Consider the commandments and whether they do not represent a new beginning which have been issued to his churches by the Nazi-resistant Cardinal von Faulhaber of Germany, as quoted by 

Carl Sandburg in the Washington Post: "Do not unleash anger, but conquer your vexations so that your conscience shell not be cone deadened by quiescence before injustice, or your spiritual powers consume themselves in discontent and discord. ! "Rejuvenate your soul in keeping with the best examples of our culture so that peace may possess you more thoroughly than war has done.

Do not dissociate yourself from the sufferings of mankind, but instead love men with a warm heart and adjudge your enemy in justice. Praise light even in darkness, for light ever remains light."

More hopeful than all of this is what H. G. 

Wells has cited in an article in the Washington 

Star as "a rising tide of human common sense and consciousness" amounting to "a realization of the common brotherhood of man!! not, be it noted, to an aspiration toward an ideal of brotherhood, but to recognition of the fact of brotherhood. --> Even the briefest consideration of but a few of the political and economic problems which it is recognized must be solved to build a free and prosperous world makes it clear that none can be solved by itself, that all must be solved if any are to be. Here also is indication that there must be a change underlying all of them. 

It must be such a change as is represented in acceptance of the fact, the truth, of the unity of all men. Intellectual recognition of economic interdependence is not enough. Absorp tion into the subconscious of a directing sense of your neighbor "as yourself" will be required if the sacrifices involved in achieving the four freedoms for all men are to seem worth while. 

Today, "nothing but war seems sufficiently worth while to command sacrifice." "Our civilization is in danger of perishing for lack of something with which we have dispensed for 200 years, but with which we can dispense no longer: a deliberate and avowed moral purpose, involving the call for common sacrifice for a recognized common good." Opening The Way To Change 

Is the factor which most seriously blocks the way to change the need it implies for itpentance on the part of all of us? But it is through repentance that the present generation can have its part in the better future. The evils from which we must escape are evils that have found their most extreme expression among the forces we are opposing in this war, but they are evils which have been present throughout the whole of civilization and among all peoples. If our present determination not to tolerate them in their extreme form carries over into the will to eliminate them in whatever form and wherever they appear, this war may be more than the end of a period and of one form of culture; it may mark the beginning of a new. One of the faults of the years following the last war was that, rather than admit and eradicate our own errors, we excused those errors wherever we found them rather than condemn and change our own conduct, wè tended to justify the conduct of our enemies. There is greater hope today because the world's sins and errors and failures have caught up with the whole world. A disaster com mon to us all has opened the way for a "rising tide of brotherhood." The common suffering that will encompass all men when the war ends may call forth impulses of mutual help native to men because they are one.

Out of a world effort at reconstruction, may come the determination and the wisdom to devise plans and policies that will guide the world aright. It was Jane Addams who urged at the end of the last var that the first

undertaking of the League of Nations might be to feed the starving everywhere. Such a begiming of its work, she maintained, would head it in the right direction and would put back of it the eager and understanding faith and support of all peoples.

Along this line of thought lies the wisdom of present proposals for a prolonged period of rehabilitation before a final settlement of the questions which gave rise to this war and to hich the war has given rise If indeed we think in terms of the future and not in terms of the past, there can be no such thing as a #final settlement." There can be instead only provision for continuing change, for growth.

The basic changes that will be needed cannot come quickly. The way must be left open for them to find expression as they do come. 

A Peace Of, For, and By The People 

So long as we see the future world in terms of government policies and economic sys- .tems, the average man must feel that there is very little he can do to assure its coming.

If instead it is seen as a "rejuvenation of the soul" then indeed we know that it can be achieved only through the "incessant, active efforts of every individual."

Statements of the churches of England and of the United States point to the new spirit which the individual must supply as an animating force back of political and economic changes.

In their words, "The sense of divine vocation must be restored to man's daily work"--which implies the compelling reason for "full employment.

Back of "equal access to raw materials" lies the fact that the earth's resources are "God's gifts_ to the whole human race." Back of material disarmament must lie disarmament "in the spirit." 

Back of international organization must exist the will to "loyal and faithful" fulfilment of agreements. It is out of mutual confidence" that adjustments must proceed. It is according to "the unshakable rules of divine lav" that human statutes and policies must be weighed. It is as an expression of "the dignity of man" that self-government must be granted all peoples. 

Is it beyond possibility that not only all branches of the Christian church should unite, as to a new extent they are doing, but that all religions may unite in leadership toward a world spiritual revival? Is it beyond possibility that leaders of science may join in a new statement of its findings on the nature of the universe and man?

No legislative plans for material well-being, for security, can produce a century of the common man or of any kind of man. It is reassertion of man as more than a material organism that is needed, and it is the individual alone, though he craves leadership, who can reassert the human spirit to it and for its service those things which can be blue-printed and legislated will be added.
