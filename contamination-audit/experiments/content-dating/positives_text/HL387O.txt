The Court of Common Pleas is the highest trial court in Pennsylvania. It has general civil and equity jurisdiction. Its judges are also the judges of the 

Criminal Courts. The Court of Common Pleas is an ancient court. The first session of the Court of 

Common Pleas of Allegheny County was held on March 17, 1789 only six months after the formation of the

County of Allegheny by an Act of the General Assembly dated September 24, 1788.

THE COURT MOVES AHEAD 

This is a statistical report of the operations of the Court of Common Pleas of Allegheny County for the 

12-month period from July 1, 1964, to . Some commendable areas of progress deserve comment. 

The Court reduced the number of pending cases at issue from 5,520 to 4,713, the lowest total of pending cases in more than 10 years. This 14.6% reduction in the docket of pending cases was accomplished in the same 12month period that saw a record 3,176 cases placed at issue. (Cases are placed at issue after all pleadings have been filed and preliminary motions, if any, pertaining to the pleadings have been disposed of by the Court). 

Cases filed after July 1, 1963, but not placed at issue as of June 30, 1965, totaled 4,787. The Data Processing System of the Court became operative on July 1, 1963. 

Prior to that date records for cases filed and not placed at issue are not available.

To place a case at issue means that the pleadings have been closed and "issue" joined on disputed matters of fact. Cases are not suitable for trial, or even conciliation, until the issues are joined. Therefore, it has been the custom in Allegheny County, and in most other jurisdictions, to exclude cases not at issue in counting the backlog. This practice is further justified by the fact that a large percentage of cases is filed merely to make a record and is not intended to ever reach the trial stage. It is certain that only a few of the cases filed prior to July 1, 1963, and not placed at issue by June 30, 1965 all more than two years will ever be placed at issue. old 

---

A real criterion of the success of the Court's persistent efforts to reduce the backlog is the continued decrease in the average age of pending cases at issue. On 

, the average age stood at 12.7 months lowest such mark in more than 20 years. On January 1, 1963, the average of the backlog was more than 36 months. 

the A total of 5,172 cases were disposed of within the report period. A major contributing factor to this achievement was the success of the Calendar Control program. 

Instituted late in 1963, it bore full fruit in 1964 and 1965. 

In this program the Calendar Control Judge and at times one or two additional Judges conducted conciliation sessions immediately prior to jury trial. No case is assigned for jury trial until this is done. These conciliation efforts have been successful in bringing about settlements in many cases which otherwise would have required the time and cost of a jury trial.

The work of the Court is not limited to the trial of trespass, contract, equity and miscellaneous cases. A series of landmark decisions by the Supreme Court of the United States, establishing basic changes in the field of criminal law, has resulted in a heavy flow of petitions from long-term prisoners seeking their freedom through Writs of 

Habeas Corpus.

A measure of this flow is seen in the fact that 203 prisoner matters were active before the President Judge in the 12-month period ending December 31, 1964. In the following 6-month period ending June 30, 1965, 295 additional prisoner matters were active before the President Judge. There is no indication of any early lessening in the number of such matters involving the Court. 

The President Judge hears all Habeas Corpus cases involving Judges deceased or no longer in commission, most cases where the prisoner was sentenced by visiting Judges, and cases where he was the sentencing Judge. The other 

Judges of the Court hear Habeas Corpus cases where they had been the sentencing Judge.

Additional business of the Court during the year included entering 2,657 divorce decrees and granting 12 annulments. Most of these cases were heard by Masters. All were reviewed by the Judges and the decrees were entered by the Judges. 

The Judges conducted 1,080 miscellaneous hearings and 95 hearings on preliminary injunctions in labor and other cases, some of them requiring many days of continuous sessions. 

We might add that statistics, though accurately compiled, never tell the entire story. They fail to measure and record the many hours, frequently days, the Judges spend in resolving the often complex questions that arise in many cases. 

To the litigant, the real measure of the efficiency of the Court's operation is the length of time his case is pending before disposition. This is because important financial stakes are involved. Using this yardstick the length of waiting time as a measure of the Court's work, the Judges and attaches of the Court can take pride in the fact that they are steadily reducing the number of months that cases are pending until disposition. This is particularly gratifying because it is counter to the current trend in many metropolitan centers whose courts report increased delays in the disposition of civil cases.

The Court of Common Pleas of Allegheny County, one of the ten largest metropolitan courts in the land, also is proving an exception to the general rule that court calendar congestion closely parallels population. 

It may be noted that the Court has improved many of its procedures. It has also introduced Electronic Data 

Processing equipment as an aid to the administration of the Court. Information recorded and tabulated by the Court's 

Electronic Data Processing Division provides an accurate basis for the statistical studies contained in this report.
