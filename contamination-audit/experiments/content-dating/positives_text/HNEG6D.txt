ECONOMIC HISTORY OF THE UNITED STATES EXPLORATION AND COLONIZATION I. EARLY TRADE ROUTES 

A. Close of fifteenth century showed awakened commercial activity. 

1. Navigation had been greatly improved. 

2. The standard of living had been raised. 

3. Foreign products were more in demand. 

B. Trade increased with the Orient especially to secure spices, precious stones, and pharmaceutical products.

C. Precious metals exerted an influence. 

1. Drainage of gold and silver to pay for goods. 

2. Search for new sources of metals, especially in New World.

D. Dissatisfaction arose with existing trade routes. 

1. Distances were long and fraught with danger. 

2. The time and money consumed in transportation of goods emphasized the need for safer and cheaper facilities. 

E. Period of exploration followed. 

1. Search for shorter trade route to India led to accidental discovery of America by Columbus. 

2. Voyages following that of Columbus redefined trade routes. Rather than to follow inland seas and edge of 

Atlantic they began to cross open oceans.

II. MOTIVES FOR EXPLORATION AND COLONIZATION 

A. Economic motives predominated. 

1. There was a continued search for a route to India, especially for a northwest passage. 

2. Following discovery of gold and silver in Mexico, many early French and English expeditions were in search of gold and silver. 

3. Discovery of fishing grounds led to fishing expeditions to supply European demand. [ 1 ]

EXPLORATION AND COLONIZATION 

4. A lucrative fur trade developed with the Indians of the St. Lawrence region. 

5. A reciprocal trade was established to supply home land with raw materials and to provide a market for finished products in colonies. 

6. There was a growing dissatisfaction with European conditions accompanied with a desire to emigrate. a. A feeling had developed, especially in England, that there was a surplus population. b. The influx of gold and silver had caused a rapid rise in prices which was seriously reducing the standard of living and creating dissatisfaction. 

B. Political influences played a part. 

1. Rivalry among England, Spain, France and Holland was evident in early explorations. England sought to establish colonies in south to check Spain and in north to anticipate France. 

2. Many individuals sought escape in the New World from political oppression found in European countries. 

C. Religion also played a part. 

1. In some cases the only apparent motive was to Christianize the Indians. a. Priests made long and arduous journeys preaching to the Indians. b. Missions were established among the Indians both in 

Mexico and in the St. Lawrence region. 

2. Many colonists came to secure freedom to worship according to the dictates of their own conscience. a. Massachusetts was settled by the Puritans. b. Maryland was a refuge for English Catholics. c. Pennsylvania was settled by the Quakers.

III. NATURAL CONDITIONS WERE ADVANTAGEOUS 

A. Trade facilities were abundant and excellent. 

1. The coast line was irregular and indented with many excellent and protected harbors such as Boston, New 

York, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Galveston and New Orleans. 

2. Rivers furnished routes inland. Many could not be navigated beyond the fall line but some, such as the Mississippi and St. Lawrence, gave access to far inland areas. Upon these rivers and harbors were established the trade centers. [ 2 ]

EXPLORATION AND COLONIZATION 

B. Climate was similar to that of the home land. 

1. The colonists were relieved of the hardship of adapting themselves to new climatic conditions. 

2. The rainfall and temperature were conducive to the success of the agricultural pursuits which predominated among the early settlers.

C. The soil of the lowlands was extremely fertile. 

1. Many of the colonists were inexperienced in agricultural pursuits and the fertility compensated for this. 

2. In spite of the crude methods available for tillage, satisfactory crops were obtained. 

D. Native products supplied food and warmth. 

1. Meat could be had in abundance from deer, fowls, rabbits and other edible animals. 

2. Both the salt and fresh water abounded in fish. 

3. Fruits, nuts and other products came from the trees of the forest. 

4. Maize, because of its food value, was one of the most important native products. Tobacco became an important article of commerce. 

5. Wood for houses and shelter came in abundance from the forests. Often it came from trees cleared from the area to be tilled.

IV. MOTIVES FOR COLONIZATION WERE NOT ALWAYS THE SAME 

A. Several features characterize Spanish colonies. 

1. The natives in the region colonized by Spain were politically and physically weak and easily subdued. 

2. Colonial overlords subjugated the Indians to a state of serfdom. 

3. In earlier years trade was restricted entirely to the parent country. 

4. Chief aim was to enrich privileged class in Spain. Low price was paid for colonial goods, yet high price charged colonists for Spanish goods. 

B. Natural environment of Netherlands made inhabitants a commercial people. Her motive for colonization centered around trade as seen in activities of 

Dutch East India Company and Dutch West India 

Company.

C. French activity was influenced by region occupied. 

1. Because of severity of climate in St. Lawrence region the activity of French centered around fur trade. [ 3 ]

I. ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT DEPENDS UPON MANY FACTORS 

A. Colonial population doubled itself about every twenty-three years. 

1. At first immigrants were few and the severity of conditions resulted in a high mortality. As conditions improved, immigration increased. 

2. Economic conditions favored a natural increase in population. Subsistence was cheap and a large family was an asset. 

3. Extreme distinctions of wealth, poverty, and social standing were not found in the colonies. a. The population of New England was remarkably homogeneous. Conditions were essentially equal even though there was some degree of aristocracy attached to the professions. b. Several nationalities made up the population of the middle colonies, but no especial distinctions developed. c. Introduction of slavery in southern colonies separated population into clearly marked social classes ranging from plantation owners to slaves. Population was widely scattered with little social intercourse.

B. Occupations were carried on by many types of laborers. 

1. The purely cooperative plan of labor and supplies soon failed. It became necessary to say, "he who will not work shall not eat." Well-defined classes of laborers were soon established. 

2. The independent proprietor was found in the New 

England and middle colonies. The proprietor and his family performed the labor and became largely a selfsupporting group. Such was conducive to rapid development. a. The proprietor and his family reaped the benefits of their own efforts. b. The better the proprietor organized land, labor and capital into a productive unit, the larger was his reward. 

3. With the growth of population and towns free artisans such as carpenters, tilers, masons, wheelwrights, and thatchers began to appear. At first most of these came [ 5 ]

from the home land, but later the trades were recruited through apprentices. They either located in a particular place or became journeyman tradesmen. They flourished most in New England because of the diversity of occupations and because slavery played such a small part. 

4. A part of the demand for labor was supplied through indentured servants. a. Under voluntary indenture the individual presumably made a voluntary contract with a company or individual to give his labor for a certain number of years in return for passage and "keep" for the period of the contract. At the expiration of the contract 

Thus immigrants who desired to come could in this way finance their passage. he would be free. b. Involuntary indentures were forced to come to 

America and work a certain number of years for passage. (1) A part of these were convicts and sentenced to a period of indenture. While some were undesirable many had committed crimes no more heinous than indebtedness or vagrancy. (2) Many individuals were seized in the streets and elsewhere by those who could make a profit from transporting them to the colonies and indenturing them. c. The treatment of indentures varied greatly, and, as numbers increased, was regulated by law. It can scarcely be said that their lot was an easy one. 

5. Slaves were used from the beginning. a. In some quarters the Indians were forced into slavery, but their temperament made them ill-adapted to such a state. b. The pressure for labor resulted in the introduction of 

Negro slavery in the colonies and the slave trade eventually became one of the most important forms of colonial commerce. (1) First slaves were landed at Jamestown, Virginia, in 1619. The rice and indigo plantations in the South, together with the hot climate, made slavery especially adaptable here. (2) Slavery spread to all the colonies but it proved to be uneconomical in the North. [ 6 ]

II. MANY TYPES OF ECONOMIC ACTIVITY DEVELOPED IN THE COLONIES 

A. Agriculture was always important among colonial industries. 

1. Agriculture is a basic industry and must be practiced in any new country which looks towards self-sufficiency. a. A dependable food supply is essential and the most dependable source for such is domestic agriculture. b. The raw materials for many industries and for much commerce depends upon agriculture. 

2. Colonial agriculture was influenced by English methods. Those colonists who were familiar with such methods were influenced by them in their new agricultural enterprise. a. Small fields were cultivated with rotation of a few crops. b. Crude plows and harrows were in use, while the hand implements were the hoe, rake, sickle and spade. c. No attention had yet been given to breeding cattle or other live stock. 

3. Many suggestions were taken from the Indians. These were more applicable in the solution of the agricultural problem than were the methods with which the colonist may have been familiar. a. The Indians taught them how to clear the land and how to cultivate it by hand. b. The extreme fertility of the cleared areas produced an abundance in proportion to the energy and skill used. c. When fertility began to wane, they needed but to clear another area and to start the process over again. 

4. During the first one hundred and fifty years there was much experimentation in the selection of plants. a. The colonist could hardly be expected to forget the products to which he had been accustomed. The native product, maize, was enthusiastically received from the beginning. It was easily raised, possessed great food value and could be stored for winter use. 

Tobacco became of great economic importance. 

Squash, sweet potatoes, beans, pumpkins, sunflowers and many other products were native to the new country. [ 7 ]

b. Sailors brought seeds and plants from many parts of the world to be tested in the soil and climate of the colonies. Many of our 

Many of our now common fibers, vegetables and fruits were thus introduced. 

5. Agriculture was not the same throughout the area of the colonies. It is sensitive to climatic variations; consequently, since climatic conditions varied, agricultural activities were also dissimilar. a. New England agriculture was influenced by the glaciated soil and short growing season. (1) Grains and vegetables of quick maturity were grown. (2) Farms were small, and intensively cultivated. (3) Fruit trees of many varieties were everywhere to be found. (4) Horses, sheep and cattle were introduced from England. (5) Implements were crude and usually home made. b. In the middle group the climate was less severe and the soil more fertile than in the northern colonies.

Agriculture assumed the rôle of major importance. 

The types of activities were much the same as those in New England, but were carried on more extensively, with less difficulty and with greater success. c. Agriculture of the South was largely of a commercial nature. The products of the land were exchanged for goods not produced on the farms. (1) Maize was at first the important crop but with the increase in demand for tobacco it became the leading crop. At times indigo, wheat, rice and cotton were important. (2) The abundance of land caused its fertility to be exploited. (3) Cattle were early brought from England. Hogs roamed the forests where they matured and furnished meat, both for home consumption and export.

B. With agriculture established, trade again assumed an important place. 

1. The basic character of agriculture gave it its early dominance in colonial activity. As time passed, however, its relative importance waned in comparison with trade. a. Settlers became acclimated, with activities centering around individual occupations. b. Experience showed that different regions were es- [ 8 ]

pecially adapted to the production of particular commodities. c. Demand for colonial products increased abroad. 

2. Trade can be hindered or stimulated by the facilities available to those desiring to engage in it. Both favorable and unfavorable conditions affected colonial trade. a. Water facilities were abundant. (1) Many navigable rivers existed, although some of them could be traversed but to the fall line. (2) The broken coast line furnished many fine harbors and here developed many important commercial centers. b. Land travel was slow, difficult and dangerous. Indian trails were utilized. Local roads were constructed, but because of obstacles in building and lack of engineering knowledge, they were far from satisfactory. c. The absence of any generally accepted medium of exchange hindered the development of colonial commerce. (1) Some coins came in from abroad but were never generally accepted. (2) Many colonies attempted to establish their own monetary units, but such attempts were invariably unsuccessful. (3) The greatest success was in carrying on transactions in terms of some staple crop, such as tobacco in Maryland and Virginia. 

3. Domestic trade varied in character. a. Fur trading with the Indians occupied a place of great importance. Colonists acted as intermediaries; they traded articles received from Europe for furs to be sent to Europe. Protection of the fur trade resulted in conflicts among countries with interests here. b. Local trade was transacted through general stores, fairs and peddlers. c. Most intercolonial trade was coastwise. It consisted in interexchange of products from different parts of the colonies and in the movement of products from different areas to more advantageous points for export. 

4. The Atlantic Ocean was the trade route for colonial foreign commerce. a. Colonists had to depend upon Europe for their supply of manufactured products, while Europeans were [ 9 ]

anxious to get furs, fish, lumber, grains and tobacco from colonies. b. Northern colonies bought much more from England than they sold there, but carried on an extensive export trade with the West Indies. c. Exports of middle and southern colonies more nearly balanced imports; existing disparities were frequently balanced by paying of shipping charges. 

5. The volume of illicit trade was relatively large. The extent cannot be measured but it was undoubtedly a disturbing factor in normal foreign trade and invalidates any statistics of such which may be available. a. The seas were poorly policed, and with the passage of many navigation acts, considered by the colonists as placing unwarranted restrictions upon them, smuggling flourished. b. Piracy was common and profitable and encouraged by the eagerness of the colonists to exchange provisions and naval stores for the cargo of the pirates. c. Frequently during a war England would authorize a private vessel to prey upon the commerce of her enemy. 

C.

To establish industry was an important motive for colonization. 

1. England was anxious to secure from the colonies such products as she could not produce at home. a. Many of the first men sent over were miners, craftsmen and artisans. b. One of the early expectations was to establish factories of many sorts, with little consideration as to the adaptability of the country to such activity. 

2. Many industries were based upon forest products. 

Timber existed in abundance and England viewed with alarm her disappearing supply because of its importance for fuel, for the manufacture of iron, and for ship building. a. Lumber products occupied a place of considerable importance. (1) Iron was scarce and expensive; consequently the abundant and cheap supply of wood was used in making articles of all sorts. (2) Most agricultural implements and household utensils, as well as buildings, were made of wood. [ 10 ]

(3) The demand for barrels and casks in which to transport crude sugar, rum, wine, fish, meat and naval stores caused many staves, hoops and heads to be made in the colonies. (4) Saw mills were established in many localities and commanded the services of a large number of men. b. Shipbuilding early became an important colonial industry. (1) Timbers of proper dimensions for construction of ships had become scarce in England and to supply this consequent need was a profitable business. (2) The increasing importance of colonial commerce was an impetus to the construction of colonial ships and these were soon recognized as among the best in the world. c. The predominance of wooden vessels constituted a demand for naval stores. Turpentine, tar, resin and pitch were used in practically every part of the ship. 

England had relied upon northern European countries for such products but was glad to turn her purchases to the colonies. Domestic shipbuilding likewise consumed large quantities of such products. d. Potash was made from the ashes of hard woods and was used for bleaching, in the manufacture of soap and glass, and as a fertilizer. The industry was incidental to clearing the land. 

3. The fishing industry was confined almost entirely to the New England colonies but it was of great importance here. a. The fishing grounds along the submerged coastal plain were the most productive yet discovered. The cod and mackerel, especially, entered extensively into the export trade with Europe and the West Indies. b. Later in colonial history the whaling industry became important. The various products obtained from the whale were in great demand both at home and abroad. 

4. The wide distribution of mineral resources fostered the manufacture of mineral products. a. The need for iron products and the high cost of their importation early stimulated the colonists to utilize the deposits of iron ore. It was cheaply smelted into pig iron because of the abundance of fuel and this was then worked into articles in numerous small [ 11 ]

workshops. Nothing which resembled a factory system was in existence. b. Clay products were used for building purposes and as household utensils. Fine quality of clay was widely distributed, processes of production were inexpensive and non-technical, so that brick, pottery and glass were turned out in considerable quantities. 

5. The products of agriculture were the basis for industries of different types. a. The processing of many agricultural products really assumed the nature of an industry, and many men not directly engaged in agriculture were busy in such processing pursuits. (1) Mills for grinding grain into flour were widely scattered. (2) Tobacco had to be dried, packed and converted into its final form for consumption. (3) Meats had to be cured and packed. (4) Wheat, barley and rice were fermented into beer and ale while rum was distilled from molasses. b. To secure an adequate and suitable supply of clothing was a serious problem. (1) English regulations made the price of imported cloth almost prohibitive. (2) Home manufacture was tedious and expensive. Most homes, however, had a spinning wheel to make woolen yarn from which "homespun" was woven. Considerable use was also made of linen both for clothing and for articles of household use. c. The supply of hides which came with the livestock industry, together with the abundance of tan bark, led to the establishment of the leather industry both in homes and in separate establishments. Leather was used to make harness, saddles, hinges, springs, belts, boots, shoes and many articles of clothing. 

6. The development of industry in the colonies was both stimulated and retarded by legislative regulations. a. Most of the legislation enacted by England was motivated by selfish interests. (1) Production of iron and shipbuilding were encouraged because she needed iron and ships. 

(2) The Navigation Acts attempted to regulate trade so that England would be the beneficiary. (3) She was especially solicitous of her woolen industry and placed many restrictions upon production of woolens in the colonies. [ 12 ]

(4) The growth of some industries caused alarm. This is shown in her later attempt to prohibit the manufacture of iron and in the refusal to allow skilled artisans to migrate from England. b. Most colonial regulation was of the stimulative type. (1) If especial need was felt for certain products, individuals were compelled to produce them as in the case of flax and hemp in some colonies. (2) The most usual device to stimulate production was the bounty. Special inducements were offered for the production of those articles which the officials thought to be most in need. [ 13 ]

COLONIAL PRIVATE AND PUBLIC FINANCE I. MANY PROBLEMS OF PRIVATE FINANCE DEVELOPED IN THE COLONIES 

A. Private finance deals with exchange activities among individuals. 

1. In a simple society, as in early colonies, the direct exchange of one good for another, or barter, was the rule. 

2. With the development of extensive and complex trade need for money and credit facilities arose. 

B. Metallic money was of minor importance. 

1. No precious metals were mined in the English colonies and what were found had to be imported. Most of the coins came in exchange for goods sold to Spain, Portugal, and the West Indies. 

2. Specie was of little service in local trade and quickly went abroad in exchange for products for which colonists were in such great need. 

3. The weight and fineness of the minted coins often varied considerably, while they were not infrequently clipped after leaving the mint. 

4. Frequent attempts were made to establish colonial mints but the small amount of specie thus provided soon found its way abroad for much needed commodities. 

C. As exchange became more complex barter became cumbersome and some form of commodity money was substituted. 

1. For many years the currency of local transactions was wampum, a name applied to beads made from a particular type of shell. a. Wampum was peculiarly adapted for use as money since it was already so used among the Indians. b. The value of wampum fluctuated closely with that of beaver skins until the colonists "inflated" the amount by developing efficient methods of manufacture. c. Inflation, together with the practice of counterfeiting, practically removed wampum from the realm of money. 

2. The use of agricultural products as money was found in all the colonies, but the same product was not used as money in every colony, nor in the same colony from [ 14 ]

time to time. That product was chosen which was in most general use and whose value was best known. a. In Massachusetts corn was at one time ordered to be money, and at other times wheat and cattle. b. In some of the southern colonies tobacco made a very acceptable money, especially when the certificates of deposit, indicating a particular grade, began to pass freely among the people. c. 3. A system of money built around such a heterogeneous mass of commodities was unsatisfactory. a. The lack of uniformity in the different units of some of the commodities caused difficulty in making exchanges. b. Many losses were suffered because of deterioration in the commodity or because of a fall in its value. c. Many of the objects could not be divided to accommodate small transactions, nor could they be transported from place to place with any degree of ease.

D. The search for a satisfactory money resulted in extensive provisions for paper money. 

1. The colonial governments carried out many experiments in the issue of paper money. a. In 1690 Massachusetts issued paper money to pay soldiers. This issue was successful because the amount was kept small. Later, however, as other issues were put forth, the value of the money fell. b. The history in other colonies was similar to that in 

Massachusetts. Small, successful issues came at first, followed by others with depreciation as a result. c. Considerable dispute often arose between the governors, in their attempt to limit the amount of issue, and the people, especially the debtors, who clamored for more.

It was always impossible so to control the issue as to provide for the acute need for money and to avoid disastrous inflation. 

2. Many banks were organized with the purpose of issuing bills of credit. a. The first bank to issue bills of credit was the Land [ 15 ]

II. THE PROBLEMS OF PUBLIC COMPARATIVELY SIMPLE FINANCE 

WERE A. Little revenue was needed during the earlier years. 

1. No payments were exacted by the mother country. 

2. Military campaigns were few and of small moment. 

3. Voluntary cooperation provided most of the roads, churches and schools. 

4. Most early revenues came from fees, fines and from land rentals.

B. Definite and different systems of taxation began to appear as the increasing population began to make more demands upon government. 

1. In the northern colonies there was great economic equality in that ownership of property was rather general. A tax levied upon property gave substantial justice, especially when supplemented with a poll tax graduated to some extent in accordance with income. [ 16 ]

2. The southern colonies were marked by an aristocracy and unequal ownership of property. Much reliance for revenue, therefore, was placed upon the poll tax together with an export duty on tobacco and an import duty on slaves and liquor. 

3. Conditions in the middle colonies were a hybrid of those in the northern and southern groups. Here a system of customs and excises produced a considerable amount of the revenue, although this was supplemented to a great extent by taxes upon property. [ 17 ]

COLONIAL POLICY AS  I. COLONIAL POLICIES BEFORE 1763 

A. Early regulations were less intense with lax enforcement. 

1. Gain to Great Britain motivated most of the regulations placed upon the colonies. Following the French and Indian war these became more intense and pointed, with an attempt at strict enforcement.

B. Mercantilism was the dominant economic doctrine. 

1. The mercantilist doctrine emphasized nationalism and, in its flower, was responsible for a great deal of the legislation designed to strengthen all phases of national life. 

2. To a great extent mercantilism dictated the policies which England pursued in her colonial relations to the end that the activities of the colonists would build a stronger nationalism for herself. The Revolution was the result. 

C. Commerce was extensively regulated. 

1. Foreign commerce was important in building up a strong nation. Through such activity, with a favorable balance of trade, wealth was expected to flow into the country. Much English legislation was directed to the regulation of colonial commerce. 

2. Colonial shipping was subjected to much regulation by England. a. During the early years of development much of the colonial shipping had been carried on in Dutch vessels. b. Through a series of navigation acts it was required that all colonial exports to England must be in ships owned and operated by Englishmen. Since this was not interpreted to exclude colonists, it proved a great boon to the colonial shipbuilding industry. c. A board of royal governors was eventually established to enforce the navigation acts, and all colonial laws in conflict with the English trade laws were nullified. The board, in fact, acted as general overseer of colonial affairs. 

3. The solicitation for the merchants and traders of England is seen in the laws regulating exports and imports. a. In 1660 certain articles were enumerated which could not be sent to a continental European port without first being landed at a British port. This gave British manufacturers first choice of raw mate- [ 18 ]

rials while merchants retained a handling charge on re-exported goods. The list was changed from time to time but the principle was retained. b. In 1663 a law prohibited importation to the colonies of any European goods unless from British ports and in British built and manned ships. Thus a colonial ship, having taken a cargo to Spain, could have a return cargo only from England. Such regulations insured a market for British goods and a profit for British traders, but were at times vexatious to the shippers. c. The colonies had developed a profitable trade with the French West Indies in sugar and molasses. Protests from British planters brought a prohibitive tax upon these products which would have destroyed the trade had the legislation not been ignored. 

4. When it was discovered that intercolonial trade was being used to frustrate some of the navigation acts, legislation was enacted to require every vessel carrying exports to give bond that they would be landed in England or an export duty would be charged. 

D. Industry did not escape regulatory measures. 

1. Alarm arose over the development of colonial industry and resulted in restrictive legislation. a. In 1699 the sale of wool, yarn, or woolen cloth under any conditions was prohibited. b. In 1732 the sale of hats abroad or to any other colony was prohibited and apprentices to hatmakers were limited. c. In 1750 a law allowed the exportation of pig iron to England without duty but the erection of rolling mills, forges or steel furnaces was prohibited.

England without duty. Such were the products greatly needed by British industry.

II. COLONIAL POLICIES AFTER 1763 

A. Problem was different than before the French and 

Indian war. 

1. England now had a greater expanse of territory to utilize for her benefit. 

2. The war had been costly and she needed revenue with which to pay for it.

B. Lack of gain because of non-enforcement of previous regulations caused British merchants to demand more aid. 

1. Westward expansion was forbidden. a. A large Indian reservation was established west of the Appalachian mountains from which settlers were required to withdraw. b. Settlement was restricted to the sea coast to keep settlers accessible to English commerce. 

2. The trade acts were extended and rigorously enforced. a. The list of articles which could be exported only to 

England and Ireland was gradually extended until by 1776 it included nearly all colonial products. b. Only a few products were still permitted to be sent to countries south of Cape Finisterre. 

3. In 1764 the Currency Act prohibited the use of paper money in all the colonies. Previous acts had prohibited it in certain colonies. It was felt that the use of such money was detrimental to English traders.

C. Taxes placed upon the colonists were increased. 

1. Taxes were no longer levied primarily for regulation with little attention to their collection. To pay the war costs required revenue and it was determined that the colonists should help supply it. 

2. Grenville became prime minister in 1763 and proceeded to attempt to get revenue through revised and enforced customs duties. Such attempts are seen in the Grenville, Townsend and North Acts. [ 20 ]

3. The Sugar Act sought especially to regulate trade with the West Indies. a. A lower rate of tax than previously was levied upon molasses but was enforced. b. The duty upon sugar from the English West Indies was increased and was required to be paid in specie. c. By other acts the colonists were forbidden to import rum from foreign countries or to trade with the 

French West Indies. 

d. Many additional articles were added to the taxable list while severe penalties were enacted against nonpayment and against smuggling. 

4. The Stamp Act was the most objectionable of all because of its general application. a. The levy was upon all legal documents, licenses to enter many occupations, and on newspapers and other publications. b. The tax was heavy and penalties for evasion were severe. c. Many protesting groups, who had not been affected by the other taxes, now arose, and the telling slogan, "no taxation without representation," was coined. 

5. By an act of 1765 troops necessary to enforce the laws were sent to the colonies and the colonists were required to provide food and shelter for them.

D. New regulations and restrictions met with opposition from colonists. 

1. Earlier regulations had been distasteful to colonists; these more stringent and enforced ones made them resentful even to point of rebellion. Such may be seen in non-importation agreements. 

2. There was general refusal to use stamped paper as required under the Stamp Act and administration of the Act was impossible. 

3. Several colonies entered into an agreement to have no further intercourse with England unless the Stamp Act were repealed. British merchants brought such pressure on parliament that the Act was repealed. 

4. A similar agreement against taxes levied under the 

Townsend Act bore fruit except that taxes were continued on tea. [ 21 ]

E. England continued to tax tea, partially to assert her right to tax and partially to aid the British East 

India Company. 

1. Protest took form in Boston Tea Party when a group of colonists threw a cargo of tea into the harbor. 

2. Boston Tea Party resulted in British retaliation. a. The port of Boston was closed. b. The charter of Massachusetts was annulled. c. A more distasteful quartering act was passed. d. The province of Quebec was extended to include lands claimed by the colonies. 

3. Retaliatory acts of Great Britain further antagonized colonists. a. Colonists, driven by a common resentment and fear, rallied in sympathy and aid for Boston and Massachusetts. b. Delegates from all colonies except Georgia met in 

Philadelphia in 1774 in the First Continental Con gress. (1) A bill of American rights was cited. (2) The right of Parliament to impose internal taxes was denied. (3) Protest was made against the maintenance of a standing army in the colonies. (4) Resolutions were adopted to cease intercourse with 

Great Britain until wrongs were redressed. c. Appeals of English merchants and manufacturers to Parliament availed nothing and additional troops were ordered to America. arrived.

I. THE REVOLUTIONARY WAR 

A. Independence was declared July 4, 1776. 

1. By the Declaration of Independence the colonists de clared themselves a nation independent from the rule of England. 

2. The Declaration was a challenge to the colonists and an appeal for aid from others to establish this independence.

B. Many colonists remained loyal to England. 

1. The loyalists, or tories, composed about one-third the population of the colonies. a. Loyalists were made up of the followers and friends of the governors, many merchants of the North and planters of the South, as well as many of the professional and cultural classes. b. Many joined the British army while others banded together for expeditions of their own. 

2. To be a whig, or patriot, now meant to cut loose from 

England and to join the new movement. a. The strength of this group came from the middle and lower classes. b. The lower classes had felt the English restrictions most keenly and were the most easily excited to revolt. c. Conflicts between the loyalists and patriots were often marked by hatred and cruelty. 

C. Financial problems were troublesome. 

1. The Continental Congress, the governing body during the War, had little power but many responsibilities. 

To secure funds to finance the military operations was one such responsibility. 

2. One of the first devices to secure funds was to issue paper money. a. First issue of paper money came with the beginning of hostilities; other issues quickly followed until the amount was greatly in excess of needs. A disastrous depreciation was the result. b. Issues by the individual states aggravated the situation. "Not worth a Continental" had its origin in these worthless issues. 

3. Some funds were secured through taxation and requisitions. [ 23 ]

a. The Congress had no power to compel the payment of taxes and could only make requests for funds. b. Little success attended requests for funds since few were available and because of the adverse attitude towards taxation. c. At times supplies such as flour, corn, meat, etc., were requisitioned from the colonies and such were partially paid. 

4. Efforts at borrowing met with some success. a. Immediately after the Declaration of Independence agents were sent to France, Spain and Holland to obtain loans. France responded most liberally although some funds were loaned by other countries. b. Domestic loans were placed through loan offices in each state; lenders were given certificates resembling modern coupon bonds. About $11,585,000 (specie value) was thus obtained. 

5. Methods of finance have been severely criticized but colonists were victims of circumstance in adopting them. a. Borrowing from abroad was necessary because there were no accumulations of free capital in America. b. The first loans from France and Spain were in revenge against a former enemy rather than as an investment. c. Little could be expected from taxes. (1) Power to tax was never granted to the Congress. (2) Large part of the movable wealth was taken from country by loyalists. (3) Small farmers and artisans had little specie with which to pay taxes. (4) The use of paper money was dictated because it met with popular approval. (5) Half the cost of the War was paid as it proceeded; the other half remained as a national debt. 

D. Land areas and policies changed during War. 

1. The Ohio valley was won from the British and resident Indians. a. Although settlement west of the Appalachians had been forbidden, many unauthorized squatters had occupied parts of this domain. b. At opening of Revolution attempts were made to drive out these settlers by inciting Indians to take warpath. [ 24 ]

c. Expedition led by George Rogers Clark secured for

Congress control of the entire Ohio valley and in treaty of peace all the territory between the Great Lakes and Florida was ceded to the United States. 

2. An internal movement was directed against the feudal land system and privilege in other forms. a. The removal of large land-holding loyalists gave a chance to break up feudal estates which had been established in some colonies. (1) New Hampshire confiscated twenty-eight estates. (2) Massachusetts took property of all who fought against the United States. (3) Fifty-nine estates were confiscated in New York. (4) Pennsylvania took the estate of the Penn family, valued at nearly a million pounds, while Maryland sold confiscated land to the value of a half million pounds. b. More democratic land holding found expression in abolition of quitrents. c. Social change also found expression in removal or reduction of property qualifications for voting.

E. Industrial and commercial conditions were affected by the Revolution. 

1. Agriculture, to the extent that it was self-sufficing, was little affected by the War. a. No early campaigns took place in the South and 

British armies were inactive in middle colonies much of the time. They even aided farmers by providing market for goods. b. Secession of bounties on indigo and naval stores caused their decline but this was offset by increased demand for tobacco and rice. 

2. The War acted as a direct stimulus to manufacturing in the states. a. English supplies were cut off and Americans thrown on own resources. b. Need for clothing caused great increase in produc- ⚫tion of woolen and linen cloth. c. The demand for munitions and war supplies gave great stimulus to iron industry. 

3. The fishing industry was practically destroyed by sinking of fishing vessels by British ships. This prevented exchange of fish with West Indies for sugar and molasses and resulted in the substitution of whiskey for rum. [ 25 ]

4. Commerce and shipping were affected in many and diverse ways. a. To the extent possible American ports were thrown open to European trade without duties or restrictions. This was not intended as a temporary policy but it was hoped to establish a future freedom of trade with other nations. b. The sale of ships to the British market collapsed and many American ships were captured. The latter loss was partially offset by the capture of British vessels. c. American and European vessels eluded the British blockade and carried on trade with Spain, Holland, France and even with the British West Indies. Some 

American products even found their way to Great 

Britain through neutral ports.

II. ECONOMIC DIFFICULTIES AFTER THE WAR 

A. Much attention was given to trade and commercial activities. 

1. At the end of the War the efforts of American ministers to secure commercial treaties with no restraint on trade met with failure. a. European statesmen still clung to mercantalism and were willing to sell to us but not so willing to buy from us or to ship in our vessels. b. One group in England proposed the reestablishment of commercial relations on a basis of equality. Strenuous objection by another group to any form of .commercial treaty exercised a profound influence upon British actions and the defeat of any commercial treaty. 

2. In spite of antagonistic attitude of British, American trade quickly returned to old channels. a. Great Britain was the best place in which to buy manufactured products and in which to sell American goods. (1) British goods were superior and cheaper and Americans had been accustomed to them. (2) Better credit accommodations could be obtained from 

English merchants than from others. (3) England provided a better market for American products than was found elsewhere. (4) The commerce was mutually beneficial and could not be destroyed by mercantilistic regulations. [ 26 ]

3. France made a commercial treaty but refused absolute freedom of trade. Prussia and Sweden granted reciprocal privileges but Spain and Portugal refused to accede to our overtures. 

4. A series of trade restrictions were enacted by England designed to destroy the West India trade. a. Salted beef and pork and dried fish were excluded from British West Indies while tobacco, provisions and naval stores could enter only in British ships. b. France and Spain excluded American ships from

West India commerce while France forbade the importation of many products. Only Danish and 

Dutch islands were freely open to American trade. c. Had evasion not occurred, restrictions would have been disastrous, especially to northern and middle American exports of fish, meat, flour and lumber, and to West India exports of sugar and molasses. 

5. In direct trade with Europe Americans found themselves at a disadvantage since Great Britain had been transformed by the War into a powerful commercial rival. a. British shippers were forbidden to purchase vessels built in America. b. England treated the states as separate entities and permitted American ships only to carry goods produced in states of which owners were citizens. This was especially detrimental to southern section. 

6. Trade with Mediterranean countries remained open to 

American shippers. The former protection of the

British navy had now disappeared and piracy made it impossible to regain this former profitable trade. In 

1784 a vessel sailed from Boston to China. Later, furs from the northwest coast were taken to China and exchanged for Chinese goods to be sold in Europe or the United States. A profitable trade was also developed with eastern Mediterranean ports, Egypt and other countries.

B. Economic and political conditions were far from stable. 

1. The anticipated post-war prosperity gave way to an economic depression. a. Importers had expected to pay for goods with exports to West Indies and through profitable sales to [ 27 ]

countrymen. Trade regulations prevented the former and lack of purchasing media the latter. b. Enormous debts were contracted which the debtors found themselves unable to pay. c. The production of exportable commodities had been greatly curtailed, which aggravated conditions. 

2. With the prohibition of West India trade, specie obtained from it or bills of exchange based upon it could no longer be used in payment for British imports. a. Specie from other sources was used to pay for imports but its inadequacy resulted in piling up of debts. b. State legislatures passed moratoria laws suspending the right of creditors to collect debts for a certain period. c. Seven states issued paper money to take the place of vanishing specie. 

3. The struggling manufactures which had sprung up during the Revolution were ruined. England could manufacture at less cost and importation and sale of these goods forced domestic producers out of business. 

Considerable loss and suffering resulted. 

4. Lack of political solidarity had a disrupting influence and was leading to chaos. a. However unjustly, the government was largely blamed for the economic adversity. b. The central government possessed no power to enforce uniform regulations of any kind among the states. c. Different states were passing tariff regulations against foreign imports either for revenue or to retaliate for wrongs to particular states. Such tariffs varied greatly and goods entered the country at most favorable ports. d. States began to make commercial war upon each other and to enact tariff laws to exclude products from other states. Complete disintegration threatened. was 

III. THE CONSTITUTION AND THE NEW NATION 

A. Conditions were such that disintegration or centralization must result. 

1. The commercial chaos emphasized the need for central control over foreign relations. [ 28 ]

2. General desire was arising for certain types of centralized action, especially for inauguration of protective tariff. To lessen our dependence upon England, a demand to stimulate home industries was arising. 

3. In 1787, after two previous commercial conventions, delegates from all states except Rhode Island met at Philadelphia. Here was framed the Constitution to replace the Articles of Confederation. 

B. The conservative party dominated the convention and framed a document to conserve property rights. 

1. The house representing the people was restrained by checks and balances. 

2. Congress was given power to raise revenue, to levy tariffs and to regulate commerce. 

3. States could not issue paper money, make anything but gold or silver legal tender, or impair obligation of contracts. 

4. Existing debt was made obligation of new government. 

5. Congress was given power to provide protection against foreign and domestic enemies. 

C. Struggle over adoption of Constitution was long and bitter. 

1. Difficulty arose in that diverse interests of different states were not equally considered. 

2. The friends of the Constitution, the strong and intelligent class, succeeded in securing its adoption. 

3. Economic conditions had so improved that a spirit of hopefulness prevailed. [ 29 ]

I. BACKGROUND FOR THE WESTWARD MOVEMENT 

A. Movement depended upon existence of pioneer spirit. 

1. The people of the colonies possessed the characteristics necessary to induce them to force their way westward. a. The pioneer spirit had been the motive which induced many of the colonists to seek the new world and this same spirit induced them to move onward. b. Peoples from various races were thrown together in the colonies; it was therefore difficult to retain old diverse customs and traditions, and a new composite type of American developed. c. The struggle against nature, against the Indians and wild beasts, developed a self-reliant, aggressive type of individual, characterized by antipathy to control and not to be restrained by mere mountains, forests or Indians.

B. Routes of early migration limited by barriers encountered. 

1. Rivers marked the first routes of westward migration.

Boats were pushed up the rivers in search of gold and silver and later to establish trading posts. These early explorers but blazed trails for later settlers. Portages, already known to the Indians, furnished the best routes between the basins of the Great Lakes and the Mississippi. 

2. The Appalachian Mountains presented a formidable barrier to westward migration. After much exploration routes to the west were established. a. The northern and best route was by way of the 

Hudson and Mohawk to the Lakes. b. Another route led from the headwaters of the Mohawk to the upper Allegheny. c. Southern Pennsylvania to the Monongahela to the 

Ohio furnished a third route. d. The most important route for early settlers was that through the Cumberland Gap. 

3. Indians influenced the early movement westward. a. Indian trails marked routes of travel, their clearings made home sites, their contribution to the fur trade resulted in permanent settlements. b. To dispose of the Indian and get possession of his land was a continued difficulty. [ 30 ]

c. Indians controlled many of the strategic passes over the mountains which retarded and made dangerous western migration. d. The alliance of the Indians with the French harassed the western frontiers of the English colonies. 

4. The French and Spanish formed a barrier to western migration by the English. The movement by the 

Spanish from the south and the French from the north resulted in many skirmishes and wars before the supremacy of the English was established. 

5. The antagonism of the Indian to English colonization led England, as a peace move, to declare the area west of the Appalachians as an Indian reservation and not open to settlement. Those who wished to migrate paid little attention to the declaration.

II. INCENTIVES FOR A CONCENTRATED WESTWARD MI- GRATION 

A. Land cessions and ordinances stabilized conditions. 

1. By the treaty of 1783 Great Britain ceded the western crown lands to the United States. a. Difficulty arose when those states which had had previous claims to these lands by charter, conquest or treaty, continued to urge the claim. The states with western claims urged that the United States be the successor to British crown lands. b. The final, but reluctant, cession of claims to western. lands to the United States did much to promote harmony in the new government. (1) It prevented unhappy controversies among the states which would have arisen over boundaries, administration, etc. (2) It removed what would have been dissension between the landed and unlanded states. (3). This large public domain of 488,248 square miles did much to give a national as well as material basis to the new government. 

2. The administration of the public domain was provided through ordinances. a. The ordinance of 1784 provided for the division of the territory into states and for treatment of Indian claims. b. Rectangular surveys were provided by ordinance of 

1785, which land was to be divided into townships and sections. [ 31 ]

c. The ordinance of 1787 provided for the organization and government of the Northwest Territory. 

B. Methods of land disposal fostered westward migration. 

1. It was provided in 1785 that public land be sold only in large quantities and at a low price. Minimum sale was to be 640 acres and price one dollar per acre. 

2. After 1800 laws permitted the sale of small tracts as well as large on a credit basis. Much speculation in land resulted, many payments could not be made, and much land reverted to the government. 

3. By act of 1820 sale for credit was abandoned, price was reduced to $1.25 an acre, and the minimum tract to be sold was reduced to eighty acres. 

4. In 1830 a general preemption law was enacted which gave people who had settled unsurveyed land prior claim to its purchase. 

5. Public lands were employed to encourage internal improvements, for educational purposes, and in direct gifts to individuals and states. (a) By ordinance of 1785 one thirty-sixth of public lands were to be reserved for support of common schools. (b) Under successive laws much land was recklessly given to individuals and states. 

6. Because of the low price of land, the inflated currency and loose banking practices, the early thirties witnessed an outburst of speculation in land which has seldom been equalled in the United States.

C. Additions were made to the continental area. 

1. In 1803 France sold the Louisiana territory to the

United States for $15,000,000. This was not only of great economic consequence but probably prevented political difficulties as many Americans had already settled west of the Mississippi. 

2. Texas was added by annexation in 1845. This area was not public land. Treaty with Mexico and Gadsden purchase settled southern boundary. 

3. After many immigrants had come to the far west, the northwest boundary came into dispute. The American demand of "Fifty-four forty or fight" was not attained but by compromise the northern boundary was placed at the forty-ninth parallel. [ 32 ]

D. Many economic factors were instrumental in inducing individuals to go west. 

1. During the depression which followed the Revolution many people moved westward in an attempt to better their condition. 

2. Debtors sought to escape debt payment or imprisonment for debt. 

3. Many Revolutionary soldiers converted their land script into western lands. 

4. Reduction in price and area sold made it possible for men of small means to buy. 

5. Extinction of Indian titles and withdrawal of Indians made land more available. 

6. Improvement in transportation facilitated the movement.

III. APPRAISAL of WESTERN MOVEMENT 

A. Westward movement went in a series of waves, hastened or retarded by economic conditions. 

1. Movement was rapid during post-Revolutionary depression but slowed during good times of Napoleonic Wars. 

2. Before 1811 the movement had little economic significance and was not a dominating influence in the life of the nation. a. There was little communication with older regions and people lived isolated and self-supporting lives. b. The only outlet for products was the dangerous one down the Mississippi. 

3. A third wave of immigration followed the panic of 

1819. Panic of 1837 and subsequent depression sent still another wave.

B. Settlement shows distinct stages in development. 

1. The early pioneer came without means of transportation or roads and became an isolated unit with no capital or markets. 

2. With the development of roads migration was easier and others came, either to buy the property of the earlier pioneer, who moved further west, or to go ther west themselves. 

3. The men with capital followed. These were permanent settlers who invested in homes and factories and developed the resources of the country. [ 33 ]

4. With last stage came a shift of wealth from seaboard states to the West. It absorbed the surplus of the East and hastened the development of the West. 

C. Economic, political and social effects may be noted. 

1. Many economic problems came with the shift of population westward. a. Drainage of population from East kept wages at a high level. b. Demand increased for better transportation between the sections. c. Development of territorial division of labor resulted in growth of sectionalism. 

2. Difficult governmental problems commanded the attention of the authorities. a. Local self-government was provided in the territorial government to be superseded by states with acquirement of sufficient population. b. Surveyal and disposition of new lands by federal government avoided boundary disputes. c. Conflicts with Indians were held to a minimum through policy of quieting title to lands. d. Disposal of land to actual settlers removed land question from politics and assured its development. e. The guarantee of free navigation of all navigable waters prevented many disputes and aided commercial development. 

3. The movement left its impress on the character and ambitions of the people. . a. The experiences of migration and settlement created in the people individualism, passion for equality, initiative and love of freedom. Such has been their contribution to our economic, political and social life. [ 34 ]

I. PROBLEMS IN DEVELOPMENT OF FOREIGN COMMERCE 

A. First Congress attempted to stimulate American foreign trade. 

1. In spite of pressure to the contrary, retaliation measures were not adopted against Great Britain. 

2. A rebate of ten per cent was allowed on all imports in 

American vessels. 

3. Duties on tea brought direct from the Orient in American ships were about one-half those on tea brought in foreign vessels or in American vessels if brought from London. 

4. Discriminating tonnage duties were levied in favor of 

American built and owned vessels. 

5. Shipbuilding industry was encouraged by confining registry under American flag to vessels built in the United States.

B. European events greatly affected American foreign trade. 

1. War between France and England, beginning 1793, disturbed commercial relations. a. The decline of French fleet left French merchant vessels unprotected which resulted in their disap- 

Vessels of Spain, ally of France, remained pearance. in port. b. Conversion of ships to war purposes and attacks of 

French privateers reduced effectiveness of British merchant marine. c. Carrying trade between France and her allies and their colonies was thrown to American vessels. 

2. America proclaimed a state of neutrality, but belligerents had little regard for rights of neutrals. a. Both France and England sought to prevent neutral ships from carrying supplies to the enemy. b. Rule was adopted which prohibited neutrals from engaging in trade in time of peace. c. Provisions were claimed to be contraband of war and 

French and British government ordered capture of vessels carrying grain and other foodstuffs. d. Protests to Great Britain brought grant of right to carry foodstuffs but not to engage in trade between 

French West Indies and France. Such restriction was evaded by American vessels landing goods at American ports for re-export under fresh papers. [ 35 ]

e. Jay's treaty, in 1794, secured recognition of some neutral rights but did not end impressment of our seamen nor search of vessels, or concede right of enemy property on neutral vessels. 

3. American commerce was severely affected by definite orders and decrees. a. In 1804 England declared all French ports from 

Ostend to the Seine to be in state of blockade and in 1806 extended the blockade to the entire coast from the river Elbe to Brest. b. In 1806 France decreed the British islands in a state of blockade and forbade trade with them and permitted no vessel which had touched at a British port to enter a French port. c. England retaliated by declaring all ports belonging to France, her colonies or allies to be in a state of blockade. d. A French decree in 1807 declared every ship sailing to or from Great Britain or her colonies to be a good prize and liable to seizure. e. The "continuous voyage" regulation of England illegalized the landing of goods from the West Indies in American ports for reshipment, and made them liable to seizure. 

4. Rather than attempt to proceed as a neutral or to declare war America decided upon the Embargo Act of 1807. a. American vessels could not leave ports of the United 

States for those of a foreign power. b. Foreign vessels might depart from the United States only in ballast or with cargo on board when law was passed. c. Under strict surveillance American vessels might engage in coasting trade. d. Because of disastrous results embargo was modified in 1809 to policy of non-intercourse with Great 

Britain and France. 

5. Embargo and non-intercourse did not stop objectionable British practices and War of 1812 was result. 

Many of the troublesome problems thereby came to an end. a. England ceased to impress American seamen. [ 36 ]

b. Close of Napoleonic wars rendered unnecessary a decision as to rights of neutrals and the definition of blockade. c. Our navy secured recognition and we had successfully asserted our commercial independence. 

C. Legislation of a reciprocal nature occurred during developmental years. 

1. From feeling that tonnage and tariff duties were detrimental, law was passed in 1815 to repeal all discriminating duties in the case of the direct trade with any foreign nation that would abolish retaliatory duties against us. a. Gainers were producers for export because of lower freights. b. Treaty was made with Great Britain abolishing discriminating duties, yet West India ports were still kept closed to American vessels which resulted in our retaliation with more duties. c. Because of evasion of restrictions, England opened 

West India ports in 1830 and we removed many restrictions on British commerce. 

2. To retaliate against countries that closed their ports to us, Congress, in 1817, increased the severity of our navigation laws. a. Prohibition of coasting trade to other nations was continued. b. Ships engaged in foreign trade, unless two-thirds manned by American sailors, were taxed fifty cents a ton. 

3. In 1828 the President was authorized to make reciprocal agreements and by 1830 freedom of commerce with other nations on a basis of reciprocity had been obtained. 

D. Foreign trade fluctuated greatly. 

1. In spite of all the embarrassments during the early years, the carrying trade of American shipowners expanded enormously. a. The commerce between Great Britain and the 

United States came to be carried more and more in 

American vessels. b. The carriage of supplies for belligerent nations was responsible for a large part of the trade. c. The demand for American agricultural products increased. [ 37 ]

(1) European wars drew men from production. (2) Crop failures abroad increased dependence upon foreign supply. d. In 1807 the American merchant marine exceeded that of any country except Great Britain. e. The decrease in merchant ships of belligerent countries gave Americans a chance to develop new avenues of commerce as with Argentina and Brazil. f. The increased trade meant a growing American prosperity. 

2. The war with England reduced our trade to a low level which somewhat revived after the treaty of peace. a. The decrees, orders and embargoes practically destroyed trade. b. Accumulated European goods were dumped on 

American markets at end of war. c. Export trade declined as European vessels again entered commercial pursuits. d. Corn law of 1815 practically kept American grain products from England, but their importance as an export was taken by cotton. e. Foreign commerce remained low until 1830. 

3. Commercial activities were widely distributed. a. Northern and middle groups exported agricultural foodstuffs and raw materials for which England was the best customer. b. About one-half of all exports was cotton, having left rice and tobacco far behind as southern export. c. Most imports were consumable goods from Europe although some came from North America, South 

America and the Orient. d. New York surpassed other ports because of rich hinterland for exports and because of center of great consuming area for imports. e. New Orleans was important export center ranking next to New York in importance. 

4. American shipping reflected the conditions of foreign commerce. a. From 1815 to 1840 tonnage engaged in foreign trade remained stationary, which meant a per capita decrease. b. Internal investment offered bigger returns and took capital from shipping. [ 38 ]

c. High tariffs increased cost of ship construction. d. Although tonnage did not increase, sailings became regular and speed was increased. e. After 1840 American shipbuilding was greatly stimulated. (1) British-China war diverted trade to American ships. (2) Discovery of gold in California increased passenger traffic. (3) Lowering of tariff decreased cost of shipbuilding. f. Introduction of steam for power and iron for hulls was to revolutionize shipping. In their development Great Britain took the lead.

E. Regulations upon foreign trade stimulated coastwise shipping. 

1. Since 1820 the tonnage of vessels in domestic trade has equalled that in foreign, and after 1860 greatly exceeded it. 

2. Northern ports sent fish, manufactures of boots, shoes, dry goods, etc., to southern ports in return for cotton, tobacco, hay, foodstuffs, etc.

II. DOMESTIC COMMERCE CONTINUED TO GROW IN IMPORTANCE 

A. Westward movement gave great impetus to domestic commerce. 

1. Attention was forced upon domestic trade by restrictions upon foreign. 

2. Movement of population westward settled new regions whose products and wants were different from those of eastern regions. 

3. Markets were somewhat localized until improved transportation facilities lowered shipping costs sufficiently to make profitable a distant flow of goods.

B. The East-West trade was by canal boat and steamer, then by highway and railroad. 

1. At first surplus products were agricultural and were exchanged for imported manufactures; later products from further west were exchanged for manufactures of eastern regions. 

2. Commerce was held to minimum because of transportation costs. a. Articles of high value in small bulk, as ginseng and furs, were sent east. b. Livestock could be driven to eastern markets. [ 39 ]

c. Light and valuable manufactures were sent west by wagon. 

3. Opening of Erie Canal was great stimulus to commerce. a. Induced much more rapid settlement of western lands. b. Gave new outlet to Atlantic for western products.

Products which formerly went down the Mississippi now came to New York. c. Commercial cities, as Cleveland and Buffalo, sprang up at strategic points along the new trade routes. 

4. Greatest stimulation of trade between East and West came from railroads.

C. The South-West trade was one carried by rivers. 

1. Early trade of developing West was with the South. 

Produce was shipped by flatboat to St. Louis and New Orleans. 

2. Until the development of the steamboat there was little up-river trade. 

3. Development of railroads changed the nature and lessened the importance of South-West river trade. 

D. Coastwise trade among northern, middle and southern groups important. 

1. Northern merchants carried the export products of the 

South-cotton, tobacco and rice and supplied the 

South with manufactured products. 

2. Direct trade existed between northern and southern colonies in exchange of products peculiar to these regions. 

É. An overland trade to the Far West commanded attention. 

1. Most of the trade to California after the discovery of gold was in ocean-going vessels. 

2. With the development of cities such as Salt Lake City and Kansas City land trade routes developed. 

3. All interstate trade was stimulated by guarantee of continued freedom of such trade.

F. Primitive methods of selling were displaced by special types of marketing. 

1. The wholesaler purchased goods from scattered manufacturers, stored them, and resold them to scattered dealers. [ 40 ]

2. A large number of peddlers were found in all parts of the country. Along the rivers such selling was frequently conducted from river boats. 

3. Frequent auctions were held, especially in eastern cities, to dispose of American manufactures and imported goods. 

4. Some eastern merchants established branches of their business in southern and western territory. 

5. The most important avenue for retail sales was the general store. At first it was but an intermediary in a barter economy, but gradually sold more and more for cash. [ 41 ]

BEFORE 1860 

I. AGRICULTURAL CONDITIONS AFFECTED BY LOCATION, STAGE OF DEVELOPMENT AND INVENTIONS 

A. Western agriculture was of primitive type. 

1. Early western immigrants settled along streams and engaged in same type of woodland agriculture as practiced by early colonists. 

2. Early frontiersman had little capital, no communication to markets, few, if any, neighbors and consequently had to develop self-sufficiency. 

3. Building of canals gave access to interior sections and new type of prairie farming began. a. Instead of clearing tillable areas, the problem here was to break the tough sod. b. Cost of fencing led to many experiments with the result that hedges of osage orange were extensively used. c. Western farming was son exploitation. Land was cheap and labor and capital dear, which meant a one-crop system without fertilization or rotation.

B. Demand for cotton changed nature of southern agriculture. 

1. Early southern agriculture was developed around tobacco in Virginia and Maryland, naval stores and live stock in North Carolina, and rice and indigo in South Carolina. 

2. Application of machinery to spinning and weaving in

England reduced costs and increased output. A demand for new textile materials resulted, of which cotton proved most suitable. 

3. Supplies of cotton fiber from Egypt, India and other producing areas proved inadequate and long staple cotton was introduced into the lowlands of Georgia and 

South Carolina. 

4. Short staple cotton was grown in uplands but difficulty of separating fiber from seed greatly limited production. 

5. The invention of the cotton gin by Eli Whitney greatly stimulated the growth of cotton. a. Cotton soon became the leading crop and the most valuable single export of the United States. b. Cotton production spread from Georgia and South [ 42 ]

Carolina to the gulf states and the lower Mississippi valley. c. Commercial cities built up on the cotton trade, such as Memphis and Mobile, flourished in the new cotton country. 

6. The abundance of land led to its exploitation; when yields decreased old areas were abandoned for new. 

7. While cotton became the leading southern crop, other products maintained an important place. a. Tobacco continued to be grown in the old areas of 

Virginia, Maryland and North Carolina, while new areas were developed in Kentucky, Tennessee, Connecticut and Massachusetts. b. South Carolina and Georgia still produced considerable quantities of rice. c. Large quantities of live stock were produced in scattered areas.

C. Character of eastern agriculture changed about the beginning of nineteenth century. 

1. Little progress had been made in eastern agriculture before the beginning of the nineteenth century. a. Distinguishing characteristic of eastern farm was its self-sufficiency. b. Crops were being raised which were unsuited to the region. c. Lack of progress was attributed to ignorance of principles of scientific agriculture, to conservatism of the farmers, and to cheapness of land which led to its wasteful use. 

2. Real hindrance to development was in lack of a market for surplus but this was supplied with development of manufacturing. a. The growth of urban centers made possible commercial farming rather than production for home consumption. b. Around the towns market gardening and dairying flourished while a market for other agricultural products stimulated their production. c. Movement of persons from farms to cities absorbed the surplus of agricultural laborers. 

3. Improvements in transportation facilities greatly affected eastern agriculture. Western competition in some lines drove the eastern farmers to concentrate on [ 43 ]

the types in which they had advantage, such as market-gardening, fruit raising and dairying.

D. Raising of live stock developed into a profitable industry. 

1. After 1830 many breeds of cattle were imported and great improvement in size and quality could be noted. a. Before the railroads, western cattle were fattened in 

Ohio during the winter and driven to eastern markets in the spring. With the development of railroads cattle were shipped from grazing lands to markets. b. Improvement in breed of dairy cattle received attention especially with the production of butter and cheese in centralized plants rather than on the farm. 

2. In early period oxen were used as work animals while horses were used for riding and driving. As machinery became lighter horses began to be used as draft animals. Attention was given to breeding horses for different purposes. Mules were largely used as draft animals in southern sections. 

3. Sheep were raised both for mutton and wool, the latter being of coarse and poor quality until breed of sheep was improved by importations. With development of the West sheep raising lost its importance in eastern sections. 

4. Hogs were generally raised; in the East they were raised in connection with dairies and distilleries; in the 

West they fed upon the products of the forests and later were fattened upon corn. 

E. Labor saving machinery advanced agricultural methods. 

1. Before 1830 all farm activities except plowing, harrowing and drawing loads, were carried on with tools directed and moved by human muscles. Before 1860 most processes had been revolutionized. 

2. One of the early important inventions was the iron plow. With this came implements for cultivating and sowing the fields. 

3. The substitution of the cradle for the sickle in reaping grain was a great advance. The gradual development of the reaper and mower greatly facilitated the harvesting of grains. 

4. The development of threshing machinery facilitated greatly the preparation of grain for the market. [ 44 ]

F. The period was marked by progress and improvement. 

1. The lightness, simplicity and cheapness of the farm machinery resulted in there being no practical limit to production, either through inability to plant or to harvest. 

2. Transition was in progress from a self-sufficient farm life to commercial agriculture. Transportation facilities were largely responsible for this. 

3. Wasteful methods of soil utilization were still found in western areas. Plentiful and fertile soil caused continuous cropping without fertilization or rotation of crops. In eastern sections, however, both rotation of crops and fertilization were extensively developed. 

4. Many factors contributed to the great advance of agriculture before 1860. a. The great expanse of land permitted only the most productive areas to be used. b. The men who engaged in agriculture approached the occupation with a professional attitude. c. Improvements in agricultural machinery and in methods of transportation increased the facilities for production and marketing. d. The formation of agricultural societies awakened inquiry and disseminated knowledge. e. Agricultural fairs spread ideas and promoted rivalry, especially in showing new machinery, in plowing and reaping matches, etc. f. Agricultural journals did much to educate the farmers in best agricultural practices. 

5. The economic position of the farmer was one of increasing prosperity with only an occasional interruption from panics, banking troubles and crop failures. [ 45 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE, PUBLIC FINANCE AND TARIFFS I. CURRENCY POLICIES 

A. Constitution gave Congress power to coin money and regulate the value thereof and forbade states to emit bills of credit.

B. Establishment of national currency was desirable. 

1. Continental currency had ceased to circulate and that issued by states greatly depreciated. 

2. Metallic money of English, French, Spanish and Portuguese coins was insufficient. 

3. Mint proposed by Hamilton was established in Philadelphia in 1792 at which silver and gold were to be coined at a ratio of 15 to 1. 

4. Gold was worth more as bullion than as money and practically none was coined. 

5. The exportation of American dollars to the West Indies and consequent scarcity of coins resulted in coinage reforms. a. No American dollars were coined from 1806 to 1836 because of executive order and gold did not go to mint. Currency consisted mostly of Spanish coins. b. In 1834 and in 1837 the mint ratio between gold and silver was changed to favor gold and as a result gold coins came into circulation while silver coins began to disappear. c. Scarcity of small silver coins was a serious disadvantage to trade and many makeshifts were used. d. By act of 1853 Congress reduced the amount of silver in subsidiary coins and made them token money so they were worth more as money than as bullion.

Such could not be freely coined, but bullion was bought by the government and minted. e. The discovery of gold in California and Australia added greatly to world's supply of specie.

C. Partly to supply credit money and partly as a fiscal agency of the government the First United States bank was established. 

1. The charter was for twenty years and the capital was $10,000,000, of which the government took one-fifth. 

2. The bank was of service to the government in lending, collecting, paying, transferring and keeping funds. [ 53 ]

By refusing to accept notes of non-specie-paying local banks, such were driven out of circulation.

A. Tariff legislation was influenced by the desire for free trade in the South and for protection in the North.

I. INDUSTRY IN PRIMITIVE STAGES 

A. The Revolution stimulated specific industries but they did not survive. 

1. Stimulation was from demand for war supplies, from interruption of foreign commerce, and from high prices caused by paper money. 

2. This was noted especially in iron and steel industry, in textiles and in other articles of necessity such as paper, powder, glass and pottery.

B. Manufacturing was confined to village crafts and household industries and does not imply a factory system. 

1. Most important household manufactures were linen, hosiery, lace, and other knit goods. 

2. Household industries were undertaken on the initiative of the workers and with their capital. 

C. Second type of household industry developed when home-workers received material to work up for employer. 

1. Merchant capitalists gave out work to housewives and thus gave employment to many families. 

2. Even early factories used household labor for many of the operations not possible to accomplish with machinery. 

3. A gradual transition was seen from household industry to workshop, and from workshop to factory. Such development was not uniform in different sections and all stages could be found in existence at the same time. 

4. Great Britain supplied so much of our goods that to stop intercourse with her seemed impossible.

3. Most inventions before the Civil War consisted of labor-saving devices, application of machinery to industry, and simplification of production by new processes. 

4. Gradually appliances were being perfected to make possible large scale production. 

D. The device of the protective tariff stimulated

1. Early attempt at non-interference with trade was given up and tariff of 1789 contained mildly protective features. 

2. Hamilton, in 1791, strongly urged protection, but because major interests were in agriculture and commerce, Congress gave no heed.

4. Foreign legislation curtailed greatly the demand for our agricultural products and resulted in the agitation for establishment of home market for such goods.

7. The higher rates in 1824 did not satisfy and through political intrigue extremely high rates were enacted in the "Tariff of Abominations" in 1828. 

8. Tariff reductions began in 1832 and no excessively high rates were again enacted before the Civil War. 

Rates continued to be sufficiently high, however, to render considerable protection. [ 62 ]

POPULATION, LABOR AND SOCIAL INSTITUTIONS I. POPULATION BEFORE 1816 

A. Population showed a marked increase in numbers. 

1. The first enumeration of the people, for the purpose of levying direct taxes and apportioning representatives, was taken in 1790. a. This census showed a population of 3,930,000, about evenly divided between North and South. b. Most of the people were native whites of English descent, for there had been little immigration since the Revolution. c. About one-fifth of the population was Negroes, most of whom were found in the South. d. Most of the people lived along the Atlantic seaboard.

B. During next seventy years population grew rapidly. 

1. Each decade showed an increase of about thirty-five per cent, which was a doubling every twenty-five years. 

2. Most of the increase was from excess of births over deaths as immigration did not become important until the end of the period.

C. Immigration played a part in the growth of population. 

1. During early years immigration was small. a. The Revolution interrupted normal relations. b. Economic conditions following the Revolution were unattractive. c. European wars provided employment at home. d. Ocean voyages were expensive and hazardous. 

2. Immigration increased after 1820. a. Economic conditions improved in the United States. b. Industrial depression in England in the thirties stimulated immigration. c. The potato famine in Ireland in 1846 was responsible for great Irish immigration. d. Political disturbances sent large numbers of Germans and others. e. In later years the United States was attractive because of high wages, availability of cheap land, discovery of gold in California and cheaper and quicker ocean transportation. [63]

f. Two-thirds of immigrants were between the ages of 

15 and 40 and about two-thirds were males, thus adding greatly to the labor force.

D. The westward migration resulted in a continuous redistribution of the population. 

1. Census of 1850 showed about one-half of the population west of the Alleghenies. 

2. After 1850 states west of the Mississippi gained in number of inhabitants. 

3. The fact that population was spreading more rapidly than it increased was considered by some as undesirable. a. From a lack of social intercourse among the pioneers it was feared they might sink to semi-barbaric state. b. Capital accumulation would thereby be retarded. 

E. Urbanization was marked in latter part of period. 

1. Urban population grew five times as fast as that of the country as a whole. 

2. Improvements in transportation and increasing use of machinery caused urban concentration. 

3. Urbanization was accompanied with the problems of housing, overcrowding, unsanitary conditions and high rents.

II. LABOR PROBLEMS 

A. The distinction between employer and employee developed gradually and by stages. 

1. Labor difficulties did not develop in earlier period. a. In household industries there was no possibility of price or wage struggles. b. When handicraftsman set up his shop he made products on order from his neighbors. 

2. Position of worker became less secure when master workmen began to employ journeymen and to make goods in advance of orders. 

3. Competition among producers for business, which followed extension of market, placed labor in different relationship. a. The desire to sell goods at a low price in order to meet competition led to wage reductions to cut costs. b. Competition also led producers to attempt to cut costs by increased use of machinery. c. Result was beginning of a conflict between labor and capital. [ 64 ]

B. Laborers sought to better their condition through organization. 

1. The first continuous organization for the purpose of maintaining or advancing wages was that of shoemakers in Philadelphia in 1792. 

2. The attempts to reduce wages to lower costs met with opposition and strikes were frequent between 1820 and 1840. 

3. The first National Trades' Union in the United States was organized in 1834 and represented a union of different trades. a. Early labor movement did not spring from factory system since this was confined to textiles employing mostly women and children. b. Movement was a protest against the merchant-capitalist system. c. Abolishment of property qualifications for voting gave unpropertied workers new method through which to accomplish ends. 4. Many demands were made to elevate the position of labor. a. Much stress was given to the need for free schools. b. Abolishment of imprisonment for debt was urged. c. Other demands were equal taxation, freedom of religion, direct election of all officers, abolition of monopolies and abolition of compulsory militia system. d. Because of high prices labor favored specie payments and opposed bank note inflation. e. Public lands should be taken from speculation and secured to the people as an outlet to the oppressed wage-earner. f. Relief against competition of prison labor and that of women and children was sought. 

5. Collective agreements were made with employers as to wages and hours and conditions of employment and by 1854 this method had largely supplanted the system of dealing with employers as individuals. 

6. American labor occupied better position than did labor in English industrial revolution. a. New inventions did not displace labor to the same extent. b. Labor was in constant demand and quickly absorbed. [ 65 ]

c. Access to cheap land kept wages at a higher level. d. There was no permanent class of dependent wage earners. e. Conditions as to child and women labor, hours, wages, etc., were better.

C. The factory system influenced labor conditions. 

1. Its scarcity led to efforts to attract labor. a. Early need was met by employment of women, children and immigrants. b. Boarding houses were established to control the conditions of life as well as of work. c. Rules were established as to temperance, attendance at religious services, neatness, punctuality, etc. 

2. In the forties and fifties several states legislated to protect child labor, but it was little more than a gesture for inadequate provision was made for enforcement. 

3. The competition of the first wave of immigrants in the forties was keenly felt by domestic workers. 

D. Hours of work were long and wages comparatively high. 

1. In 1840 average working day in northern factories was about 12 hours and in southern factories somewhat longer. 

2. Wages varied somewhat for different classes. a. Unskilled labor was greatly in demand and wages were much higher than for similar work in England. 

Reason was access to cheap land, for wages had to be high enough to overcome the lure of independent agriculture. b. Expert artisans and mechanics were scarce because large returns could be had in commerce and agriculture. Many skilled operatives were brought from abroad and wages were high. c. The women and children were temporary workers with low wages. Rapidly expanding production kept them higher than wages for similar work in England. d. Fluctuating prices influenced the real value of the money wages. In times of low prices, wage would purchase much; in times of inflation with high prices it would purchase little. [ 66 ]

III. ECONOMIC CONDITIONS AND SOCIAL INSTITUTIONS 

A. After many struggles, by 1830 the battle for democracy had been largely won. 

1. Old state constitutions were liberalized while those of the new western states reflected the democratic ideals of the frontier. 

2. The early attitude of the Federalists in the federal government had been tempered by the influence of Jeffersonian principles.

B. Period witnessed development of educational and religious institutions. 

1. Early years were marked by educational decline. a. Wars had a demoralizing influence. b. Westward movement broke up old communities. c. Rigorous life of pioneers gave little time or opportunity for educational pursuits. 

2. Influences were at work which laid foundations for system of general education. a. Growth of cities and efforts to combat increase in crime and pauperism indicated need of education. b. Universal franchise gave common men a weapon through which to secure educational facilities. c. Education could be endowed from public lands. d. Feeling was growing that education was necessary for successful outcome of a democracy. 

3. Interest in religion waned in early years but later revived. a. Revolutionary War disrupted churches by severing relations with home bodies. b. Disintegrating influences of frontier life weakened religious activity. c. Later many new sects were organized such as Mormons, some of which had important economic influence. d. Many denominations supplied religious needs of pioneers by sending home missionaries or "circuit riders" to minister to them.

C. Communistic organizations attempted to solve economic problems. 

1. Reformers of all sorts thought America an ideal place in which to try their experiments and a great many were inaugurated. [ 67 ] SOS-ECHUS IO

a. Many groups were religious cooperative commonwealths. b. Organizations motivated by secular motives were of three sorts. (1) Some traced their origin to Robert Owen and his visit to the United States in 1824. (2) Some attempted to practice the principles taught by 

Charles Fourier. (3) Others followed the teachings of Cabet. 

2. The communistic attempts met with failure. a. There was a desire among the people for personal ownership. b. Economic opportunities were too great outside the group. c. Members were as quick to differ with each other as they had been to differ with the old order, [ 68. ]

CAUSES AND CONDUCT OF CIVIL WAR I. SLAVERY AND THE CIVIL WAR 

A. Growth of cotton on a large scale cemented slavery to the South. 

1. After the Revolutionary War slavery declined in all parts of the country. a. Many states provided for eventual abolition of slavery. b. Before end of eighteenth century every state but 

Georgia had forbidden importation of slaves. c. Slave labor, even in the South, was generally recognized as uneconomical. 

2. The invention of the cotton gin in 1793 revolutionized southern agriculture. a. This made profitable the production of upland cotton which could be grown in all parts of the South. b. The textile mills of Great Britain and New England had apparently an insatiable demand. c. The result was an enormous increase in the production of cotton and slavery became profitable as a commercial institution. d. The growth of cotton, and with it slavery, gradually spread westward until by 1860 it extended as far as Texas. e. With increase in cotton production the number of slaves also grew. In 1790 there were 677,897 and in 1860 the number was 3,953,760. f. The white yeoman farmers sought possession of the best cotton lands but they could not compete with the economies of organized large scale production. 

B. Slavery possessed both advantages and disadvantages. 

1. The planter had some advantages in slaves over hired labor. a. He assured himself of a permanent and controllable supply of labor by buying it. b. He could determine in advance the size and character of his labor force. c. He had nothing to fear from strikes or labor troubles. 

2. Disadvantages of slavery more than offset advantages. a. Since the slave did not reap the reward of his efforts, he was not interested in results. b. Slave had no motive to improve methods of pro- [ 69 ]

duction, to increase output, to preserve soil fertility, or to economize in use of equipment. c. Planter had little control of his labor force he had to be content with such slaves as the trader brought or as were born on his plantation. d. Young man had difficulty in embarking in business. 

Land was cheap but the cost of the necessary labor force was often prohibitive. e. System of slavery retarded the accumulation of capital to such an extent that the South was compelled to borrow from the North for commercial and industrial purposes. f. Slavery involved an economic loss both to the nation and to the South. 3. Social effects of slavery were insidious and harmful. a. Slavery stratified society into three broad groups, the slave-owners, the non-slave-owners and the slaves. b. The concentration of ownership in the hands of a few developed an aristocracy with a powerful influence in politics and society. c. Individuals who owned a few slaves were in a different class, worked side by side with their slaves, and were less strict in regard to race and social distinctions. d. Non-slave-owners were of three types. (1) The professional class, identified economically and socially with the planter class, shared their attitude towards slavery. (2) The yeoman farmers owned and cultivated their own farms. (3) There was a small number of poor and degenerate whites who eked out a miserable existence among the plantations.

C. The plantation system was successful. 

1. Products were demanded in which slave labor could be profitably used. 

2. A continued abundance of new land made slavery possible and profitable. 

3. Slavery fitted well into the development of the onecrop system of agriculture. 

4. Concentration of slave labor could be made upon cotton through the purchase of food supplies from the West and manufactured goods from the East or from abroad. [ 70 ]

5. The demand for slaves reopened the slave trade and an adequate supply was furnished. 

6. The plantation proved more profitable than the small farm and largely supplanted it. (1) It was better managed. (2) Specialized and massed labor could be used to better advantage. (3) Work could be carefully planned and executed.

D. Struggle over the extension of slavery precipitated the Civil War. 

1. First real struggle over slave or free territory came with the attempt to recognize Missouri as a slave state. a. Many slaveholders had settled in Missouri and wanted to be admitted as a slave state. b. Compromise admitted Missouri as slave state but no other territory north of 36° 30' should be slave. 

2. The dissension over the admission of California as a free state was settled by compromise. a. California was admitted as free state. b. Utah and New Mexico were organized as territories to be admitted as slave or free as their constitutions might provide. 

3. The Kansas-Nebraska Act, in 1854, repealed the Missouri Compromise and threw open to slavery the vast area formerly closed to it. Settlers rushed from both slave and free states in an attempt to gain advantage. 

4. This economic clash, with the fervor for abolition in the North, resulted in a condition in which each section viewed the other with hostility and distrust. The 

Civil War was the result.

II. THE CIVIL WAR 

A. Civil War was a struggle between two economic systems, each of which had used its political power to promote its own ends. 

1. The planting and commercial states had maintained a fairly even political balance but latter was gaining ascendancy because of development of industry. 

2. The doctrine of states' rights was invoked both by the 

North and South, but latter found more advantage in it. a. Planting economy was based upon territorial expansion and exchange with other regions. b. Economic interest of South demanded freedom of trade, consequently protection was opposed. [ 71 ]

3. South thought that cotton was so valuable in world's commerce that if its exportation were hindered England would come to support of South, recognize its independence, and demand that its ports be kept open. 

Such did not happen. a. England was well supplied with cotton at the beginning of the war, having imported heavily from the record crop of the previous year. b. England needed northern wheat fully as much as she needed cotton. c. Later, as supply was exhausted and blockade became effective, English workmen stood against recognition of the South and slavery. 

4. Other economic factors aided the North. a. During first three years of war crops were poor in 

Europe and abundant in America which afforded relief to British consumers. b. South depended upon trade abroad and with North to supply her with a myriad of commodities; break with North and blockade of her ports demoralized her economic life. c. Gain in South from specialization in one crop was lost since markets in which to exchange products had been cut off. d. North was a country of diversified industry and its economic life was little affected by War. e. Outcome of war is determined as much by economic strength as by military and naval power, and in this 

North had advantage. f. South made unsatisfactory attempt to divert land, capital and labor from cotton production to diversified industry, while in North agriculture and industry were thriving.

B. Problems were encountered in financing the Civil War. 

1. Belief was current in North that War would be of short duration and slight resort was had to taxation. a. New taxes were levied only to meet interest and sinking fund requirements of the new loans. b. Main reliance for revenue was placed upon borrowing. c. Specie payments were suspended before 1862 and resort was had to issue of irredeemable paper money. [ 72 ]

d. Before end of the War increased taxes were invoked through income taxes and extensive excise taxes. 

2. Confederacy experienced difficulty in financing War. a. Taxation was unpopular, population was sparse, and central government had little authority. b. Loans were made at home and abroad, but the market for bonds of the rebel government was limited. c. Specie payments were suspended and resort had to paper money. Not only central treasury issued notes but states, cities, banks and railroads did likewise. 

C. Economic condition in North at end of War was very different from that in South. 

1. Industrial development in North was accelerated by the War. a. Manufactures which supplied war needs and which were protected by the tariff flourished greatly. b. The spread of protective tariffs expanded the operations of manufacturers. c. Chance to make profits under rising prices stimulated expansion of industry. d. To supply war materials caused great industrial activity. 

2. War was responsible for important changes in financial system. a. The protective tariff was greatly extended. b. The national banking system was established. 

3. The Homestead Act was passed which granted title to a maximum of 160 acres, without charge, to actual settlers after five years' residence. 

4. While North was prospering and expanding, South was suffering loss and destruction. a. The investment in slaves was wiped out, but of course the productive capacity of the slave remained as a freedman. b. The former slave was free, yet he was severed from the soil with no conditions of self-preservation or protection. c. It was difficult to introduce wage system for neither planter nor freedman was accustomed to reckon in terms of money. d. Congress forbade payment of southern war debts which placed heavy burden upon security holders. e. South faced problem of arising from demoralized economic condition. [ 73 ]

AGRICULTURAL EXPANSION AFTER THE CIVIL WAR I. AGRICULTURAL EXPANSION, ADJUSTMENT AND ECO- NOMIC EXPERIMENTATION 

A. The passage of the Homestead Act greatly influenced agricultural expansion. 

1. The grant of a free farm to each homesteader brought large amounts of land under cultivation. a. Railroads made western migration comparatively easy. b. In decade 1870-1880 more than 190,000,000 acres were added to the cultivated area of the United States. c. New process of milling stimulated raising of spring wheat and led to settlement of Minnesota and Dakotas. d. Increased settlement in grain belts resulted in increased production of grain which was facilitated by introduction of agricultural machinery. e. From 1880 to 1900 more than 303,000,000 acres were added to the farm area. 

2. Men who settled western farms were largely native Americans. a. Disbanding of army set free many young men who were attracted to free lands of west. b. Germans came first settling Wisconsin and middle west. c. Those of Scandinavian extraction settled in Minnesota and Dakotas. d. This combination of land and labor needed only capital to make it productive. Capital took two forms. (1) That used by the farmer to stock and implement his farm. (2) That invested by society in railroads by which the products of the farmer were carried to market in exchange of goods. 

B. Machinery hastened the advance of agriculture. 

1. Scarcity of labor compelled farmers to use machinery. a. Most of the new inventions had to do with cereal production. (1) Machinery for preparation of soil was perfected. (2) The early reaper merely cut the grain. By successive stages there was added the self-rake, the binder and the combine. [ 74 ]

(3) Other machines were various types of drills, planters and cultivators. 

b. In South the cotton seed planter, fertilizer spreader, cotton stock cutter and many specialized cultivators were introduced. Efforts to perfect cotton picking machinery were unsuccessful. 

2. Use of machinery required motive power so that the number of horses and mules increased. In later years tractor has somewhat displaced animals as motive power. 

3. Methods of transporting and handling grain improved. a. System of grading and classification was introduced. b. Grain was marketed by description instead of by delivery of specific lot. c. Use of elevators facilitated storing and loading of grains.

C. Overproduction of farm products led to discontent. 

1. Efficient utilization of newly settled areas resulted in great expansion in production. a. Production of grain showed most spectacular increase. b. Cereals represented more than one-half the value of total agricultural crops and used about one-half of the improved farm land. c. The increase in cereal production was much more rapid than the growth in population. 

2. Demand for agricultural products did not keep pace with production. a. Farmers were not self-supporting but grew most crops for market and were affected by price fluctuations. b. Farm development proceeded more rapidly than demand for products justified. c. Prices were greatly depressed and crops were grown at a loss. This was offset by rise in value of land. d. That domestic demand did not keep up with production is indicated by the fact that population increased much less rapidly than production. e. Fluctuations in foreign demand adversely affected export crops. (1) Agricultural exports increased greatly during Civil War. (2) Exports of wheat remained stationary after 1885. (3) European countries developed own agriculture. [ 75 ]

(4) Opening of Suez Canal brought Indian cotton into competition. 

3. Agricultural discontent is seen in demands of farmers. a. The Granger movement sought relief through cooperative buying and selling, and especially through reduction of railroad rates. b. The Greenback Party demanded repeal of the resumption act, the abolition of bank notes, and the use of paper money issued directly by the government. c. The Peoples' Party demanded a national currency of greenbacks, free coinage of silver, income tax, postal savings bank and government ownership of monopolies. d. Increased prices and higher standard of living after 

1896 quieted the agitation.

D. Regional specialization marked agricultural development. 

1. Rural population declined in the eastern section. a. Agricultural activities were replaced by industrial. b. Areas could not compete with West in production of grain and livestock. c. Agricultural activity centered in dairying, vegetables and fruits. d. Much of the poorer land reverted to forest and pasture. 

2. The large plantation system of the South was changed by the Civil War. a. The War deprived the planter of his slaves and in many cases of buildings, tools and other capital. b. Increase in price of cotton led many to borrow and produce. c. Attempt to use freedman was unsatisfactory. d. Era of small farms followed failure of large plantation system. e. Since Negroes would not work for wages and were unable to buy land, system of farming cotton lands on the shares came into use. f. The extensive advance of credit by merchants to cotton growers had undesirable effects. (1) Debtor was kept almost in state of serfdom. (2) All supplies must be purchased through the creditor and crops sold through him which gave chance to take unfair advantage of debtor. [ 76 ]

(3) Since cotton was most marketable crop, creditor insisted that farmer concentrate on this and purchase supplies through him. g. Increased prices and improved banking in twentieth century practically eliminated the crop lien and store credit system. 

3. The middle western region specialized in grain and livestock. a. Wheat is an important crop because easily transported and suited to extensive production. The introduction of more suitable varieties has also increased its acreage. b. Corn is the most important cereal crop, representing more than one-half of all cereals grown in the United States. (1) Little of it enters directly into human consumption but is used to fatten livestock. (2) Little corn enters export trade. (3) It is more generally grown than most grains although the production is concentrated in the corn belt. c. The raising of beef cattle, hogs and poultry has developed extensively in this section. d. The conversion of livestock into food products has developed into an important industry especially after the invention of the refrigerator car. e. Dairying had a rapid growth. (1) Urban concentration of population demanded milk and other products. (2) Introduction of factory methods in making butter and cheese revolutionized the industry. (3) After perfection of Babcock test farmers were paid for milk on basis of fat content which stimulated breeding of better herds. (4) The preparation of condensed and evaporated milk have become important industries. 

4. Scanty rainfall determines type of agriculture in Far West. a. Grazing has always been important in western plains. b. Dry farming is practiced in semi-arid region. c. Much agriculture is carried on in arid regions through irrigation projects.

E. Farm tenancy in the United States has not been dangerous. 

1. It has not meant a development of peasant tenantry. [ 77 ]

2. In many cases tenancy has been but a step to farm ownership.

F. Federal government has rendered assistance to agriculture. 

1. The Department of Agriculture was "to acquire and diffuse among the people useful information on subjects connected with agriculture.” 

2. The Morril Act established in each state a land grant college to teach "such branches of learning as are related to agriculture and the mechanic arts."

A. The War marked an increased demand for agricultural products. 

1. European labor and capital was diverted from normal productive pursuits to those of war. 

2. The United States was the only neutral nation capable of supplying the needs. 

3. Exports of most agricultural products increased rapidly and profits were large with the rising prices. 

4. Much unused and pasture land was brought into cultivation and machinery used on a larger scale. 

S. 77,000,000 acres were added to farm lands between 

1910 and 1920. 

6. Much wild speculation occurred in farm lands. 

B. Agricultural depression followed the War. 

1. By 1920 the abnormal need for European imports decreased. a. With the decrease in demand prices of agricultural products fell rapidly. b. Farmers suffered because prices of other commodities did not fall as rapidly, and they were at a disadvantage both as debtors and consumers. c. Domestic conditions accentuated the situation. (1) Substitution of tractors for horses and mules decreased the consumption of feed crops. (2) Consumption of cereals and meats decreased. (3) Reclamation projects continued to bring into use additional land. 

2. Conditions led to search for new uses for agricultural products. a. Corn is being turned into many more products than formerly. [ 78 ]

b.

Many valuable products are now made from skim milk. c.

Cotton seed products have shown a remarkable growth. 

C. Recent years have shown a marked mechanization in agriculture. 

1. Agriculture is now characterized by use of power machinery. a. Gasoline tractors became popular early in the twentieth century. b. Withdrawal of men from farms during War resulted in demand for labor-saving machinery. c. The tractor has made possible the utilization of much more efficient plows, cultivators, reapers and threshers. 

2. Mechanization has important economic results. a. Millions of horses and mules have been eliminated. (1) This is a saving in land and labor formerly required to grow feed. (2) It has added to the surplus of agricultural products. b. The productivity of labor is greatly increased. (1) Costs have been cut from one-third to two-thirds. (2) Acreage which can be cultivated by one man is trebled. c. Unit costs of production have been greatly reduced. d. The size of the profitable farm has been greatly enlarged. e. More emphasis is placed upon the use of relatively level western lands of low rainfall. 

3. Recent years have shown some tendency to large scale farming. a. In 1928 there were 9000 corporation farms in the 

United States. b. In Montana consolidation of wheat farms reduced the number from 35,000 in 1917 to 14,000 in 1928. 

4. Definite social consequences followed farm mechanization. a. Much labor was released to seek employment in the developing manufactures. b. A steady movement of population from country to city was taking place. c. Much of the drudgery has been eliminated so that the farmer and his family may enjoy more social activities. [79]

D. Proportion of tenancy has increased since the War. 

1. Price of land was high and large unit needed for profitable operation. 

2. Cost of equipment required large investment of capital. 

3. Rents were low in comparison with interest rates. 

4. Uncertainty as to future trends in land values discouraged purchase.

E. Continued overproduction led to demands for relief.

Under the McNary-Haugen bill, which was twice vetoed, a corporation backed financially by the federal government, was to buy agricultural products and sell them abroad. b. F. Organizations have been formed to benefit farmers. 

1. The Grange stresses cooperation and education. 

2. The Farm Bureau Federation centers its activities around county agents and other specialists to demonstrate improved methods to farmers. 

3. The Department of Agriculture provides many services of an experimental and educational nature. [ 80 ]

EXPLOITATION OF NATURAL RESOURCES I. EXPLOITATION OF WESTERN SETTLEMENT 

A. Many acts were passed to dispose of the public domain quickly. 

1. Military service of Civil War veterans was to count against five years' residence required of homesteaders. 

2. Pre-emption Act allowed homesteader to buy additional 

160 acres at $1.25 an acre. 

3. Citizens might buy timber and mineral lands at $2.50 an acre. 

4. Grants to railroads were continued on a more lavish scale.. 

5. Many claims under the acts were fraudulent and much of the land passed into hands of land companies. 

B. Extermination of Indians and bison was early problem. 

1. Indians resented the white man invading their terri tory and breaking up hunting grounds. a. Some had been moved to Indian Territory by Jackson. b. Treaties were negotiated with most tribes guaranteeing them large tracts of land. c. Military campaign was begun against Indians in 1862. d. Indians were placed on reservations where they are wards of the federal government. 

2. The bison were practically exterminated between 1830 and 1878. a. The hunter sought the buffalo robes and hides. b. The rancher coveted the ranges for his cattle. c. The homesteader had no place in his economy for the buffalo.

C. Natural resources were exploited. 

1. No attempt was made to conserve the qualities of the soil. a. Policy of giving away the land perpetuated the method of earth butchery. b. Much land has been ruined through the process of erosion. c. Elements of fertility have been exhausted by overcropping and have not been replaced. [ 81 ]

EXPLOITATION OF RESOURCES 

2. No economy was exercised in use of forest products. a. One-third of standing timber was wasted in cutting and logging. b. Milling processes were uneconomic and wasteful. c. Many preventable forest fires took a large toll. d. Census of forest resources was made in 1870 and interest developed in conserving timber supply. e. Forest agency was established in 1876. 

3. Extensive use of mineral products came after 1860. a. Industrial advance created a demand for minerals. b. Little coal was used before the Civil War. (1) Coal burning locomotive had not been developed. (2) Little was yet used for domestic heating. (3) Since 1899 the United States has held first rank among the countries of the world in coal production. (4) Unneeded mines have been prematurely opened, and wasteful mining processes used. (5) The utilization of the heat energy has been wasteful and inefficient. c. Discovery of petroleum had great industrial and social importance. (1) Transportation was provided by tank cars and pipe lines. (2) First important uses were as an illuminant and as a lubricant. (3) Wide use as a source of power came with the general adoption of internal combustion engine. (4) A long list of valuable by-products come from the industry. (5) Production has been highly speculative and the waste enormous. (6) Natural gas is closely associated with petroleum and its production has been under wasteful methods. d. Vast quantities of iron ore are still found in scattered fields. e. Copper, gold, silver, lead, zinc and other metals have been found and mined in considerable quantities. f. The discovery of a commercial process for reducing aluminum has made it the center of a large industry. g. The production of sulphur, salt, lime, potash, clay products, cement and building stone in quantities began after 1860.

D. Water power has had fluctuating importance. 

1. In colonial days it was chief source of power and determined the location of many cities. [82]

OF MANUFACTURES AND INDUSTRY AFTER 1860 I. INCREASE IN MANUFACTURES 

A. Growth of manufactures can be traced to many causes. 

1. Market for manufactured products was extensive and insatiable. a. Growth of population required larger quantities of goods. b. A continuous rise in general standard of living demanded more products. c. New methods of advertising and salesmanship created new demands. d. Industrial demand of construction and other industries was important. (1) Growing urban population had to be housed and supplied with water and other utilities. (2) Rails and rolling stock had to be supplied to spreading railroad system. (3) Factories were being built and equipped with machinery. 

2. Abundance and richness of natural resources supplied raw materials. a. Basic raw materials for practically every branch of industry were found within the country. b. Agricultural resources have been rich and varied. 

3. Quantity and quality of labor contributed much to success. a. Growing supply was furnished by natural increase in population. b. Immigrants of an age suitable for immediate work were coming in increasing numbers. c. The native-born laborers had the qualities of energy, initiative, adaptability, inventiveness and mechanical knack. d.

Many of the immigrants were skilled artisans and mechanics. 

4. Large supply of capital was available for new industries or expansion of old ones. a. Large amounts came as loans or direct investments from European citizens. b. Most capital came from domestic savings and from the earnings of business. [ 84 ]

5. Willingness to adopt efficient machine processes increased production. a. Labor was relatively scarce in comparison with tasks to be accomplished. b. American people possess a native mechanical skill and inventive genius. c. Manufacturers were willing to try new devices. d. Development of system of interchangeable parts stimulated production. e. Standardization of machinery and parts simplified and cheapened production. f. Patent system was a definite stimulus to inventive genius. 

6. Abundance of power in water and coal encouraged growth and distribution of industries. 

7. The system of transportation furnished by lakes, rivers and railroads furnished abundance of raw materials and gave an extensive market for finished products. 

8. Freedom of interstate commerce and absence of hampering traditions fostered favorable domestic industrial relations. 

9. Continuation of the protective tariff kept out foreign competition. 

10. The great organizing and administrative ability of such men as Carnegie, Rockefeller, Armour, Westinghouse, Morgan and Vanderbilt must not be overlooked. 

B. World War influenced American manufactures. 

1. Urgent demand from European belligerents for supplies led to revival and expansion of American industry. 

2. With diversion of labor and capital into war industries other enterprises suffered. 

3. Industry was disturbed to a greater degree after the 

United States entered the War. a. War Industries Board was to obtain materials for military purposes with minimum dislocation of industries, to restrict non-war production, and to fix maximum prices. b. Through Priorities Board fuel, transportation facilities, labor and credit were assigned first to war industries while producers of luxuries were forced to curtail or suspend operations. [ 85 ]

c. Government contracts were let on the basis of cost plus, an extravagant but effective method of speeding up production. d. Profiteering resulted and whole system of war production was wasteful since normal safeguards on cost were disregarded.

C. Resumption of peace time activities compelled a readjustment of industry along many lines. 

1. War industries faced the problem of readjustment. a. Activities which were starved during the War, such as buildings, roads and public works, were revived. b. New industries, such as production of dyes and chemicals, were started. c. Certain natural shifts were taking place. (1) Development of automobiles was cutting into construction of wagons, saddles, harness and railroads. (2) Radio lessened demand for pianos and rayon was being substituted for cotton textiles. d. Scarcity of labor during War led to concentrated use of machinery which, after the War, meant considerable unemployment. e. Greater demand for luxuries led to large investments in factories and machinery to produce them. (1) Demand for luxuries was relatively elastic and therefore unstable. (2) Too large a proportion of labor and capital was being sunk in fixed capital equipment and plant expansion. 

2. New developments caused productive units to increase in size while small establishments became fewer. a. The motor has become generally used for driving machinery. b. The electric motor and gasoline engine have revolutionized economic and social life. (1) They can be furnished at any point desired, in any quantity, and for any length of time. (2) Location and technique of factories has changed as has the equipment in the home. c. The modern worker in a few hours is able to turn out vastly more than could his predecessor in days, while he is no longer a manual laborer but a machine operator. d. Machinery and workers have been combined under "scientific management" to obtain maximum production with least expenditure of energy. e. Research in industrial methods and processes has [86]

grown until practically every great manufacturing plant has a research department in which experiments are being carried on at an expense far beyond the means of an individual inventor. 

3. Progress and changes in manufacturing can be seen in particular industries. a. Iron and steel industries reflect fluctuations of the period. (1) To realize economies in production, iron and steel must be produced in large quantities. Result has been that twenty companies produce 87 per cent of the iron and steel of the country. (2) Integration is seen in the bringing together under one control of the various stages from mining the ore to turning out finished product. (3) Many new processes have been developed, such as the numerous alloys, to extend the market for steel products. b. Changes may be noted in the textile industry. (1) Emphasis upon rayon marks important change in nature of product. (2) Recent years have seen a considerable shift of the industry from New England to South. Advantage of

South lies in cheap labor supply and abundance of raw material. c. Motor industry has had spectacular development. (1) Industry is highly integrated and concentrated. (2) Industry has a host of smaller industries dependent upon it. 

(3) Horizontal combination is seen in General Motors. 

A number of formerly competing units are brought together under one general control. 

D. Localization of manufacturing has become less marked. 

1. Manufacturing is no longer concentrated in New

England and Middle Atlantic states, but gradual dissemination over the country has resulted. 

2. A tendency of factories to move from larger to smaller cities and from cities to the country may be noted.

II. COMBINATION AND REGULATION 

A. Individualism led to industrial combination. 

1. Early period was one of individual initiative and reward. [ 87 ]

a. The manufacturer, railroad magnate and business man strove, not merely to make money, but to gain power. b. Such men often used ruthless and fraudulent methods to crush rivals, and to control wages and prices. c. Individual businesses developed into large units with the result of a ruthless competition between them. 

2. Industrial combinations were effected to secure economies in management. a. Economic efficiency enabled the large plant to drive out the small concern. b. After economies in production had been attained, wastes and losses remained in management. c. Such losses could be eliminated through unified control over production, prices and marketing, which meant monopoly. d. Business had had no interference from legislation. (1) Railroad land grants, protective tariffs, liberal interpretation of constitution concerning restraint of trade and staunch protection of property rights were advantages eagerly seized by business leaders. (2) Business had been dominating politics. (3) Uninformed and apathetic public offered no protest. 

3. Industrial combinations culminated in the trust movement. a. Mechanization of industry made control of production difficult and profits were disappearing. b. Agreements among producers to fix prices or to limit output were attempted, but they were generally unsuccessful. c. Pools were formed in different industries with varying degrees of success. d. The first "trust" was organized in 1879 as the 

Standard Oil Company and became a model for future organizations. (1) Scheme consisted of board of nine trustees to whom stockholders surrendered their stock in return for certificates. (2) Trustees operated all plants and divided profits among certificate holders. e. Between 1897 and 1904 the prevailing form of combination was the holding company. f. Some states modified corporation laws to facilitate combination through holding companies. [ 88 ]

g. Wild orgy of speculative investment followed which reached its climax in 1903. h. Legal and economic difficulties led to other types of combination. (1) One organization frequently purchased properties of related and competing businesses and operated them as a single unit. (2) Control was frequently gained through community of interests and interlocking directorates while associations were organized for the purpose of exchanging trade information. i. From the economic view combinations were either horizontal or vertical. (1) Horizontal combination unites competing organizations in the same trade. (2) Vertical combination unites successive stages of an industry from the beginning to the end. 

B. Combinations possessed many advantages but were guilty of many unfair and reprehensible practices. 

1. Advantages were of various types. a. Only most efficiently equipped plants were operated and obsolete machinery was soon scrapped. b. Best ideas of operation were exchanged. c. Risks are distributed by manufacturing several different articles. d. By-products can be better utilized. e. Best managerial and organizing talent can be obtained. f. Greater division of labor and finer specialization can be effected. g. Raw materials can be bought in large quantities and at lower cost. h. Capital and credit facilities are more easily secured. i. Costs of selling goods, such as those of advertising and salesmen, are less. j. Saving in cross freights is effected when plants are located in different parts of the country. 

2. Against the advantages must be weighed questionable practices. a. Economies effected were not passed to consumer in lower prices but retained as monopoly profits. b. Smaller competitors were crushed by local price cutting and sales at a loss. [ 89 ]

c. Dealers were often required to handle only the product of the monopolist. d. Rebates and discriminating favors were often received from the railroads. e. Valuable rights and privileges were obtained through legislative corruption.

C. An aroused public demanded justice through regulation of business practices. 

1. In 1887 the Interstate Commerce Act was passed which prohibited pools among railways. 

2. The Sherman Anti-trust law was passed in 1890 which provided that "every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy in restraint of trade or commerce among the several states, or with foreign nations, is hereby declared illegal." 

3. Thirty-two states and two territories passed severe anti-trust legislation, but it accomplished nothing because of the laxness of the laws of New Jersey, Delaware and Maine to which trusts went for incorporation. 

4. The Elkins Law of 1903 facilitated prosecution under the Interstate Commerce Act. 

5. The Bureau of Corporations was organized in 1903 to investigate the organization and management of corporations engaged in interstate commerce. 

6. Federal Trade Commission was created in 1914 to administer the anti-trust laws and to prevent unfair methods of competition. a. It was to determine if unfair practices were being used and if so to order their cessation; final enforcement rested in federal courts. b. Commission has taken action against misbranding goods, false advertising, price discriminations and discrediting competitors. c. Many conferences of business men were called in an effort to formulate codes of fair trade. 

7. The Clayton Anti-trust Act of 1914 attempted drastic regulation. a. It enumerated many practices as unfair. b. Acquisition of stock in one corporation by another, whereby competition would be substantially lessened, was prohibited. c. Interlocking directorates were restricted. [ 90 ]

8. The Transportation Act of 1920 favored the consolidation of railroads under certain conditions. 

9. The Webb Export Act of 1918 allowed organization of associations to conduct export trade. 

10. The Cooperative Marketing Act of 1922 allowed agricultural producers to combine for more efficient distribution of products. 

11. The National Industrial Recovery Act was an effort to relieve unemployment and to cure the depression through regulation of the activities of industry. [91]

SINCE THE CIVIL WAR I. INCREASE IN POPULATION 

A. Changes occurred in composition of population. 

1. While there was a large increase in absolute numbers from decade to decade, the rate of growth became less. a. Economic limits of production were being reached · in some sections. b. Free land was disappearing. c. Living costs were mounting. d. Independence of women had grown. e. While birth rate had fallen, death rate fell even faster. 

2. Rate of growth of the native white population steadily declined until it fell below that of the foreign born. a. Immigration replaced native stock rather than increased the population. b. Increase in Negro population was not as rapid as that of white. 

3. Movements and regroupings were taking place in the population. a. Shift of population from East to West continued. b. Population continued to concentrate in industrial centers. c. There was a drift of Negroes to northern mills and factories. d. The growth of urban population was much more rapid than rural. e. Declines in rural population was found in many sections. f. Since 1930 there has been a "back to the country" movement. (1) Depression has sent many country-raised men and women back to the farm. (2) Many of the urban unemployed have gone to live with relatives in rural districts. (3) Social program of the "New Deal" has emphasized a decentralization of industry.

B. Increased immigration brought problem of assimilation. 

1. Great need for labor after Civil War led to actions to encourage immigration. a. Congress legislated to encourage immigration. b. Western states established immigration bureaus to aid foreigners to come and settle with them. [ 92 ]

c. Agents of steamship companies competed for immigrant trade. d. Advertisements were inserted in foreign papers showing advantages to be had here. 

2. Because of great need for labor immigration population was easily absorbed. a. Settlement of West, construction of railroads and development of iron and steel industries demanded a large supply of labor. b. Development of machinery led to possibility of using more unskilled labor in factories. 

3. Concentration and composition of immigrants created problems. a. Concentration in distinct quarters in large cities led to congestion, overcrowding and a lowered standard of living. b. Until 1880 most immigrants were from northern

European countries and were easily assimilated. c. With immigrants in large numbers from southern 

Europe, many problems developed. (1) They were unfamiliar with democratic government. (2) They were unskilled workers and unfamiliar with machine methods. (3) Many of them expected to return to native country with their savings. (4) Assimilation of this group was difficult and led to a racial stratification.

C. Attempts have been made to solve problems of immigration through restrictive legislation. 

1. Regulatory laws were passed by New York, Massachusetts and California but were declared unconstitutional in 1876. 

2. In 1882 federal legislation limited Chinese immigration for ten years and two years later it was made absolute. 

3. In 1882 a law was passed forbidding landing of convicts, idiots, and others who were liable to become a public charge. 

4. In 1885 the importation of contract labor was forbidden. 

5. To protect American labor from influx of cheap foreign labor, in 1917 the head tax was raised to $8.00 and a literacy test imposed. 

6. In 1921 an absolute limitation upon immigration was imposed; yearly immigration of any nationality was [ 93 ]

limited to 3 per cent of the number of such persons in the United States in 1910. Several reasons prompted this legislation. a. Fear of flood of immigration to escape heavy taxes and hard living conditions following war. b. Fear on part of organized labor that our standard of wages would be lowered. c. War disclosed 10,000,000 unnaturalized aliens whose 

Americanization is necessary before further additions are desirable. 

7. In 1924 annual quota was changed to 2 per cent of any nationality residing here in 1890 while Japanese immigration was forbidden.

II. ORGANIZATION OF LABOR 

A. Changes in conditions of labor created problems. 

1. Wage-earning class grew rapidly. a. The early ideal of having every worker an ultimate owner had passed. b. The development of a class that would always work for wages became recognized. c. Relative number of women in factories, offices and retail selling was increasing. d. Number of children employed in industry increased rapidly. 

2. Change in structure of industrial society affected labor. a. Improvements in transportation nationalized the labor market. b. Workers were now exposed to sharper competition and their bargaining power was lessened. c. Competition of products from widely scattered places exerted a depressing influence on wages. d. As labor became more mobile out-of-town journeymen competed with local mechanics. e. Shift from handicraft to machine production meant greater division of labor and breaking up of old trades. f. Large scale production concentrated workers under one plant and management. (1) Substitution of impersonal relation of capital and labor for personal contact of master and man resulted in lack of understanding and interest. (2) Worker was now dependent upon the owner of capital for employment and new lines of social cleavage were introduced. [ 94 ]

g. Massed laborers exchanged ideas, found common needs and grievances, and sought means to better their condition.

B. Problems of labor resulted in unionization. 

1. From 1863 to 1873 twenty-six national unions were organized. 

2. Trades were brought together in 1866 under the National Labor Union. a. This was a federation of local, state and national organizations. b. First convention devoted its attention to unemployment and urged the eight-hour day. c. Attention was directed to cooperation as a means of self-help. d. Demands were made for reduction in interest rate on government bonds and for issue of legal tender currency to aid laborers. 

3. The Knights of Labor attempted to unite skilled and unskilled workers into one labor organization. a. They looked towards abolishment of wage system through cooperation and political action. b. Among their demands were the referendum, reserving of public lands for actual settlers, a weekly payday, abolition of contract system on public works, substitution of arbitration for strikes, prohibition of employment of children under fourteen years of age and the eight-hour day. c. Organization had lost its influence by 1894. (1) Many of the cooperative undertakings had been unsuccessful. (2) There was not sufficient solidarity among the different types of members. 

4. The American Federation of Labor was organized in 1881. a. Under this each trade was organized separately while the unions alone were represented in the national organization. b. Local autonomy of member unions was left intact, only matters of general concern coming before the Federation. c. The purposes of the Federation were to unite labor unions for mutual assistance and to obtain legislation to improve the conditions of the working classes. [ 95 ]

(1) It maintained that the strike, the boycott and the unfair list were justifiable and necessary. (2) It attempted to raise the standard of living by shorter hours, higher wages and better working conditions. 

5. Trade union movement has fluctuated with the business cycle. a. Strength wanes during depressions and increases in periods of recovery. b. At best only about one-fifth of American wage earners are organized into trade unions. c. Strength of unions varies in industries, being relatively strong among miners and railway employees and weak in building and manufacturing trades. 

6. The attempt to place under one control all workers of various trades within the same industry was unsuccessful.

C. Labor has resorted to the strike to accomplish its ends. 

1. Changes in industrial organization during latter part of nineteenth century resulted in a struggle of organized labor with capital. 

2. While many small strikes had previously occurred, the first exhibition of the growing power of labor was in the railroad strikes of 1877. 

3. Strikes became more numerous as the capitalistic system developed and reached their climax in 1886 with marked violence and destruction of property. 

4. Important strikes since 1886 have been the Homestead strike in 1892, the Pullman strike in 1894 and the coal miners' strike in 1902. 

5. With better leaders and more strength unions have become more conservative in the use of the strike. 

D. Employers' associations have developed with the organization of laborers. 

1. National employers' associations were formed to cope with national labor organizations. 

2. After the formation of many smaller organizations the 

National Association of Manufacturers was organized in 1895 with a policy distinctly hostile to unionism and labor. 

3. Employers' associations contributed greatly to maintenance of industrial peace through collective bargaining in framing wage agreements with labor organizations. 

4. With the growth in size and power of labor organiza- [96]

tions, employers' associations were formed explicitly to oppose and crush labor unions.

E. Labor felt the effects of the World War. 

1. Position of labor was greatly strengthened during War. a. Immigration was reduced to a minimum. b. Many aliens returned to their native countries. c. Many American workers entered the military or naval service. d. The scarcity of labor was only partially filled by women entering industry on an unprecedented scale. e. Government recognized right of organized labor to participate in political and industrial affairs. f. Trade unions grew rapidly and attained greater strength. 

2. Post-war period has shown fluctuations. a. Employers reduced number of workers and wages and movement was inaugurated to abandon trade agreements. b. The unwillingness of labor to surrender its wargained power resulted in serious strikes. c. Strikes emphasized radicalism of labor unions and unwillingness of employers to recognize gains made by labor. d. With period of prosperity following 1922 position of labor was strengthened. (1) Employers greatly extended welfare work in the form of hospitals, club houses, recreational facilities and sanitary conveniences. (2) Identity of interest between capital and labor was established in many businesses by making the workers stockholders in the corporation. (3) Labor banks were established to save to labor the profits on its savings, many of which, however, were unsuccessful. e. The intensified mechanization of industry soon resulted in increased unemployment. f. With the economic crash in 1929 unemployment increased amazingly until by 1933 about 12,000,000 persons were unemployed. g. Under such conditions the position of labor is very weak.

III. LABOR LEGISLATION AND THE COURTS 

A. Early labor legislation afforded protection to weaker groups. [ 97 ]

1. Massachusetts took the lead in this type of legislation. a. In 1866 an eight-hour law for children under fourteen years of age was enacted. b. In 1869 a bureau of statistics of labor was established. c. Other laws fixed the hours of labor for women, provided for factory inspection and for safeguarding machinery. 

2. Other states followed Massachusetts in legislation but administration of laws was often weak. 

3. In 1903 Illinois passed a child-labor law prohibiting children under fourteen from work in factories and requiring that they remain in school until that age. 

Similar legislation was enacted in many other states. 

4. Protective legislation for women workers paralleled that for children. 

B. Most legislation affecting adult males has come in the present century. 

1. Massachusetts passed the first minimum wage law in 

1912 and has since been followed by many other states. 

2. Legislation to control accidents, occupational diseases, and to provide compulsory accident insurance is general. 

3. Most states provide public employment offices and restrict the abuses of private employment agencies. 

4. Many states have permanent boards of conciliation and arbitration. 

5. Federal legislation provides for settlement of industrial disputes which involve common carriers. 

C. World War and consequences gave impetus to labor legislation. 

1. Federal Department of Labor was created in 1913 and during War trade union standards were adopted for all government work. 

2. Under Wilson's administration War Labor Board was set up to settle disputes between employers and employees. 

3. Under Clayton Act of 1914 labor organizations were made exempt from the provisions of the Sherman Anti- Trust Act and labor injunctions were abolished. 

4. By law of 1915 federal protection was extended to sailors on American vessels. 

5. In 1916 railway workers were granted an eight-hour day and were extended further protection in 1920. [ 98 ]

6. Two attempts to abolish child labor by federal legislation have been declared unconstitutional. 

7. The National Recovery Act was expected to aid labor. a. It was proposed to raise wages, shorten hours and improve working conditions. b. More jobs were to be created. c. Collective bargaining was to be encouraged. d. A public works program was inaugurated to provide jobs. 

8. Labor legislation by the states has been extended. a. Extension of women workers into more occupations has necessitated additional legislation. b. The shortened day has gained general recognition. c. Extension of minimum wage laws has been checked by courts. d. Social insurance legislation has become general. (1) Practically all states have workmen's compensation laws. (2) Old age pensions are being granted in several states. (3) A start is being made towards unemployment insurance. (4) The federal government is extending aid to the states for social welfare expenditures. 

D. Attitude of courts has affected status of labor. 

1. Courts have declared much labor legislation unconstitutional. a. Constitutional guarantee of freedom of contract and that no one should be deprived of life, liberty or property without due process of law was often invoked. b. Early laws fixing hours of labor were held unconstitutional as abridging freedom of contract. 

2. In present century courts have upheld much labor legislation under the police power. This power allows state to legislate in the interest of the public welfare. 

3. Decision of 1842 held trade unions to be legal organizations. 

4. In eighties and nineties the doctrine of conspiracy was applied to labor organizations and injunctions were granted as means of restraining their activities. 

5. In 1908 Supreme Court held that boycotts were illegal under the Sherman Anti-Trust law and that individual members of a union were liable for damages inflicted by a union. [ 99 ]

2. Investment of capital in advance of economic need led to numerous crimes. a. Competition was ruthless to divert the small amount of existing traffic from rivals. b. It was an age of individualism and practices went unchecked. c. Some of the worst frauds were practiced by means of construction companies. d. Many methods were used in granting discriminating rates. (1) Secret rates and rebates were often granted. (2) Exorbitant rentals were paid for private cars. (3) Under-billing and under-classification were often used. (4) Excessive allowances were made for use of terminals owned by shippers. (5) Localities where there was water or rail competition were given lower rates than non-competitive points. 

3. Ruinous competition led railroads to seek to lessen it. a. Many small competing lines were combined into a few systems. b. Intense competition among the systems led to formation of pools according to which traffic or earnings were divided among former competitors. c. After pools and rate agreements were prohibited combinations were formed of hitherto independent and competing lines while smaller roads were absorbed by large systems. d. Attempts to combine parallel and competing systems were frustrated by the courts.

B. Early regulation was attempted by the states. 

1. Under the Granger movement Illinois, in 1870, enacted legislation establishing maximum rates for passengers, forbidding discriminations in freight rates, and creating a railroad and warehouse commission with large powers. 

2. Other states in the South and West enacted similar legislation. 

3. Such was first effective demand that the railroads be treated as public service corporations and not as mere private enterprises for enriching promoters or owners. 

C. Federal regulation of railroads has had extensive growth. [ 105 ]

COMMUNICATION 

1. Under the federal Constitution states have right to control intrastate commerce while power to regulate interstate commerce is vested in Congress. 

2. Right of the people to regulate railroads was sustained by the Supreme Court in the Granger cases in 1876. 

3. Because of public demand and inability of states to regulate interstate traffic, the Interstate Commerce Act was passed in 1887. a. Act provided that all charges must be reasonable and just and prohibited discrimination. b. A greater charge for a short haul than for a long haul was prohibited. c. Adverse court decisions shaved the act of some of the intended power. 

4. The Elkins Act of 1903 defined more clearly unfair discrimination and rebating and expedited the trial of roads against which charges were brought. 

5. The Hepburn Act of 1906 enlarged the powers of the

7. Legislation in 1913 ordered the Commission to value all property of common carriers as a basis for rate making and in 1916 an eight-hour law for railroad employees engaged in interstate traffic was passed. 

8. For a period of twenty-six months during the War railroads were under federal administration. a. Thus it enabled government to effect economies and to utilize transportation facilities to a better advantage. b. Economies effected were such as short routing of traffic, unification of facilities, expeditious move- [ 106 ]

DEVELOPMENTS IN FOREIGN AND DOMESTIC COMMERCE I. UNITED STATES AS A COMMERCIAL NATION 

A. Exports and imports show growth of commerce. 

1. Growth of foreign trade was slow until end of nineteenth century. a. Civil War interrupted growth of foreign trade. b. After Civil War interest was diverted from foreign trade. 

(1) Homestead Act emphasized settlement of West. 

(2) Energies were expended on railroad construction and development of domestic industries. (3) High protective tariff discouraged trade. 

2. Twentieth century witnessed great expansion in foreign trade. a. Success in Spanish-American War stirred the pride of the American people and broke down provincial barriers. b. Expansion of industry furnished a surplus of products which could not be absorbed by domestic market and industrialists sought an outlet in foreign markets. 

3. Nature of exports changed after beginning of twentieth century. a. Exports of latter part of nineteenth century were chiefly agricultural products. (1) Cotton, tobacco and naval stores comprised exports from southern areas. (2) Wheat, flour and provisions went from northern ports. 

4. In twentieth century a growing volume of manufactures characterized exports. a. By 1914 six leading exports were cotton, machinery, petroleum and its products, copper and copper products, wheat and flour and iron and steel products. b. Abundance of cheap raw materials and mass production gave producers in the United States an advantage. c. Many articles of world demand were produced only in the United States. d. In recent years automobiles have entered extensively into export trade. 

5. Imports consist almost entirely of raw materials and foodstuffs. [ 108 ]

a. Biggest shift has been away from manufactured products. b. In 1914 leading imports were hides and skins, copper, sugar, raw silk, crude rubber and cotton manufactures. c. Most of the important imports are on the free list. 

B. The balance of trade has shifted. 

1. Before 1876 balance of trade was unfavorable, that is value of imports was greater than value of exports. a. People of United States were purchasing more than they sold. b. Much capital came from European countries to build railroads and develop western lands. 

2. After 1876 balance of trade was favorable in most years. a. Exports exceeded imports by small amount. b. Excess was offset by "invisible" items of trade such as interest on foreign capital invested here, expenditures of American travelers abroad, immigrants' remittances, and freight and insurance charges. 

3. The World War made the United States a creditor nation. a. At beginning of war, people of the United States were indebted to people of Europe about $6,000,- 000,000. b. In 1933 the net indebtedness of the citizens of other countries to the people of the United States was in excess of $20,000,000,000. c. Americans are still continuing to invest abroad and our creditor position is becoming more marked. d. The creditor position means that eventually, to balance payments, imports will normally exceed exports. 

C. Importance of trade with different countries has changed. 

1. Until present century Europe was best customer for our exports and source of most of our imports. 

2. In more recent years relative importance of trade with 

South America and Asia has increased. a. The raw materials and tropical foodstuffs were little affected by protective tariffs. b. Development of higher standard of living increased the demand for coffee, sugar, silks, and other items. [109]

D. World War stimulated shipment in American vessels. 

1. Civil War greatly decreased number of American ships. a. About one-third of our vessels was sold to foreigners. b. Some were destroyed by Confederate cruisers and others sold to the government. 

2. War losses were not regained. a. Congress refused to readmit to American registry vessels sold abroad. b. Costs of shipbuilding here were higher than in Great Britain. c. Decline in wooden vessels gave the carriage to foreign vessels until in 1900 less than 10 per cent of our foreign trade was in American vessels. d. Larger profits could be obtained in railroad construction, in exploration of natural resources, and in development of manufactures than in shipbuilding or shipping. 

3. World War changed the status of the merchant marine. a. Congress eliminated the five-year age limit on vessels seeking American registry and in other ways modified the former exclusive policy. b. War greatly reduced the number of German, British and French merchant vessels while demand for our goods was greatly increasing. c. In 1916 Congress created the Shipping Board to regulate shipping to acquire merchant vessels, and to sell or charter them to citizens of the United States. d. Upon our entry into the War the Emergency Fleet

Corporation was organized to build new ships. e. In 1920 the United States was second only to Great 

Britain as a shipping nation. f. The Merchant Marine Act of 1920 provided for lease or sale of government ships to private shippers and gave American shipping a monopoly of trade between the United States and its possessions. 

E. Foreign trade has been influenced by commercial policies. 

1. Tariffs influence trade. a. American tariffs have usually been concerned with effect upon home industry rather than with their effect upon trade. [ 110 ]

b. The almost continuous emphasis upon high protective tariffs reduced imports and consequently had an adverse effect upon exports. 

2. Reciprocity and retaliation have trade influences. a. Policy of the United States has been largely one of equal treatment of all nations without discrimination and in general has been able to obtain equal treatment from other countries. b. In 1889 a Pan-American Congress consisting of seventeen Central and South American countries met in Washington and recommended reciprocal treaties. c. Under provision of McKinley tariff reciprocal agreements were made with many countries, but tariff of 1894 practically destroyed reciprocal agreements. 

Other attempts at reciprocity have met with little. success. Act of 1934 gives the President power to make tariff trades with foreign countries, but as yet little has been done. d. Following high rates in tariff of 1930 many countries have passed retaliatory legislation. 

II. INCREASE IN DOMESTIC TRADE 

A. Domestic commerce influenced by development and shifts in trade routes. 

1. Increased transportation facilities as exemplified in railroad, pipe line and motorized truck have stimulated trade. 

2. Good transportation has permitted regional specialization with inter-regional trade. 

3. As sources of supply changed and new centers of manufacturing developed, the importance of trade routes shifted. a. Mississippi River lost its importance to railroads. b. Importance of lake routes centered much trade in lake cities.

B. Changes in trade organization have been introduced looking to the elimination of the middleman. 

1. A tendency has developed to dispense with middlemen who functioned between manufacturers and wholesalers. 

2. In many cases the manufacturer sells directly to the wholesaler. 

3. In the case of some large companies manufacturers sell directly to retailers. [ III ] 

SOS-ECHUS 15

4. Manufacturer sells directly to consumer by mail or through agents. 

5. The development of large specialty stores, the rapid changes in style, the change in method of buying by retailers and the placement of much of risk of storage on manufacturer have accounted for great decline in number of wholesale establishments.

C. Organization in retail distribution has undergone changes. 

1. Problems of retailer changed after Civil War. a. Long period of falling prices made it impossible to keep goods for any length of time and not sell at a loss. b. Styles changed more rapidly and new inventions were rapidly placing new products on the market. c. Many things formerly made in the home were now made in the factory and were handled for sale in retail stores. 

2. Under pressure of changing conditions the old general store gave way to more economical and efficient methods of retailing. a. The specialty store split off from the old general store and gave better service because of the special management of the business. b. The department store, under the impetus of Wanamaker, introduced the one-price system, gave a money back guarantee and installed conveniences for shoppers. c. The mail-order house buys directly from the manufacturer, and sells directly to the consumer. It substitutes a catalog for salesmen. d. The chain store is an effort to simplify the distribution of goods and thereby to reduce costs. [112]

PRIVATE FINANCE, PUBLIC FINANCE AND THE TARIFF I. MONETARY PROBLEMS 

A. The issues of United States notes caused difficulties. 

1. To help finance the Civil War the government issued legal tender paper money, or "greenbacks" to an amount of $450,000,000. 

2. Both the banks and the government suspended specie payment and as a result gold and silver coins disappeared from circulation. 

3. To meet the requirements of trade, notes were issued in small denominations. 

4. Since the notes were made legal tender all prices and wages soon came to be quoted in terms of the depreciated currency. 

5. Financial effects of the greenbacks were quickly noticed. a. As issues increased they began to fall in value, resulting in a rise in the price of commodities and a premium on gold. b. Depreciation was increased by expansion of bank issues and was influenced by the success or failure of the 

Union army. 

6. Issuance of greenbacks greatly increased the cost of the

Civil War. 

a. Government was compelled to pay for commodities and labor in the inflated prices. b. Returns from the sale of bonds, measured in gold, steadily fell off. 

7. Economic effects of greenbacks were marked. a. Inflated prices imposed burdens upon the people in their business relations. b. Rise in prices affected different articles differently, consequently the burden was not uniform. (1) Prices of agricultural prices did not advance as rapidly as those for manufactured goods, consequently the farmer suffered. (2) Money wages lagged far behind prices which adversely affected labor. (3) Creditors suffered because debtors could pay in depreciated currency. 

8. Agitation for resumption of specie payments came after the war. a. In 1866 policy of contraction was begun and was continued until falling prices brought a plea for their continuance. [ 113 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE 

b. Congress provided that after January 1, 1879, greenbacks would be redeemed in gold. c. About $346,000,000 of greenbacks have been kept in circulation and were redeemable in gold until the suspension of gold payments in 1933. 

9. Depreciation of Confederate paper money was much greater than that of the greenbacks and after the war became worthless. 

B. Panic of 1873 marked by demand for inflation. 

1. Panic was caused by overexpansion with borrowed funds, much of which was not only in excess of needs but was attended with fraudulent practices. a. Immediate cause of crash was failure of Jay Cooke and

Company which precipitated a panic in Wall Street. b. Prices fell, banks failed and the Stock Exchange closed for ten days. c. The result was the failure and bankruptcy of banks, railroads and businesses all over the country. 

2. The fall in prices caused much currency agitation, especially between contractionists and inflationists. a. The contractionists passed the resumption act in 1875 which provided for the accumulation of a gold reserve to redeem the greenbacks after January 1, 1879. b. The opposition was so strong that a Greenback Party, and later the Greenback Labor Party, was formed to sponsor several types of monetary reform. (1) The Resumption Act was to be repealed. (2) Issue of legal paper money by the government convertible into bonds was advocated. (3) Bank notes were to be suppressed. (4) No gold bonds were to be sold in foreign markets. 

3. More prosperous conditions beginning about 1878 somewhat quieted the agitation.

C. The silver movement was closely related to the demands for cheap money. 

1. In response to suggestion of the international monetary commission, because many of our coins were obsolete, our mint laws were revised in 1873. 

2. The standard silver dollar was dropped from the list of coins to be minted. a. At the time of passage this attracted little attention because for forty years silver had not been brought to the mint and coined. [ 114 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE 

b. The release of silver through the adoption of the gold standard by many countries and the discovery of rich silver mines lowered the value of silver until in 1874 it was worth more as coins than bullion. 

3. Demands arose from many quarters to remonetize silver. a. The owners of silver mines called the demonetization of silver the "crime of '73" and demanded its remonetization with free coinage at the old ratio of 16:1. b. Farmers, debtors and others suffering from low prices attached an exaggerated importance to "cheap money" as a panacea for their difficulties. c. In 1878 the Bland-Allison Act resulted in more silver becoming_money. (1) The Treasury was to purchase not less than $2,000,- 000 nor more than $4,000,000 a month and coin it into silver dollars. (2) Silver certificates were to be issued against these dollars and put into circulation. (3) The minimum amount provided by law was purchased and coined. d. In 1890 the Sherman Act provided for the purchase of 4,500,000 ounces of silver each month to be paid for in treasury notes. (1) Gold began to be crowded out of circulation by silver. (2) The gold reserve was rapidly depleted and even fell below $100,000,000 which has been considered the margin of safety. 

4. The panic of 1893 led to further agitation for cheap money. a. Several factors led to the panic of 1893. (1) People lost faith in the ability of the government to redeem its promises. (2) Extravagant appropriations dissipated the treasury. (3) Revenues were falling off. (4) An inflated credit came from speculation and overinvestment. b. Panic disorganized every department of our economic life. (1) Price of silver fell greatly, western mines closed with consequent unemployment. (2) Failures of banks and commercial institutions were numerous. (3) One-fourth of the railroad capital in the country was in the hands of receivers. [ 115 ]

(4) Failure of corn crop in 1894 increased the distress of the farmers. (5) Strikes and riots evidenced the widespread unrest. (6) Government revenues fell off and gold was being exported from the country. (7) Government sold bonds to secure gold to maintain specie payments. c. Democrats in 1896 carried out the campaign on a free silver issue but were defeated. d. Large increase in gold-production remedied the money shortage and the Currency Act of 1900 definitely established the gold standard.

D. Panic of 1907 was largely distress among financial institutions. 

1. Rising prices had caused much expansion with borrowed funds. 

2. When banks began to contract loans, payment could not be made and failures resulted. 

3. Many banks suspended specie payments until confidence could be restored.

E. Panic of 1920 was an aftermath of the World War. 

1. European demand for our products fell off considerably. 

2. With fall in demand prices slumped. 

3. Many enterprises were operating on credit basis and with falling prices were unable to pay. 

4. The panic was really but the hardship which may be found in a transition period. 

F. Panic of 1929 has led to monetary experimentation. 

1. Causes and effects of panic were much the same as that of 1893. 

2. Several efforts were made to stem the tide of the crisis. a. National credit corporation was formed to rediscount ineligible commercial paper. b. Federal Reserve Banks were instructed to purchase securities in the open market to stabilize credit. c. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation was organized to loan money to private industry to create more employment. 

3. Banks and businesses continued to fail and President

Roosevelt sought partially to remedy conditions by change in monetary system. a. He was interested in raising the price level and easing the burden of debt payment. [ 116 ]

b. By presidential proclamation in 1933 a heavy penalty was placed upon hoarding gold or gold certificates and all gold must be surrendered to the federal treasury. c. Export of gold was also prohibited. d. The gold clause in all private and public contracts, providing for payment of principle and interest in gold, was abrogated. e. Act of May 12, 1933, gave the President broad powers. (1) He could authorize Federal Reserve Banks to extend credit operations. (2) Up to $3,000,000,000 in United States notes might be issued. (3) The dollar might be devalued by as much as fifty per cent. (4) Silver might be accepted in payment of war debts. (5) He could fix a bimetallic ratio between gold and silver and provide for their unlimited coinage. 

4. In October President Roosevelt fixed the purchase price of gold at $31.36 per ounce (the former mint price was $20.67) which devalued the dollar to about 66 cents. 

Later it was lowered to 59.06. 

5. By executive order on August 9, 1934, all stocks of silver in the country were ordered to be turned into the mints at a fixed price of 50.01 cents an ounce.

II. DEMAND FOR BETTER BANKING FACILITIES A. The National Banking System was established in 1863. 

1. At beginning of Civil War bank notes issued by about 

1600 banks, operating under state laws, were in circulation. 

2. National banks were established to meet apparent needs. a. One aim was to replace mass of state bank notes with a safe national currency. b. Another aim was to create a market for government bonds. Banks were required to purchase bonds upon which, as security, they could issue circulating notes. c. Issues of national bank notes did not drive out issues of state banks, as was expected, so a prohibitory tax was placed upon the latter which gave a monopoly of note issue to national banks. d. National banks encountered strong opposition from those who favored the issue of legal tender notes by the government. (1) Banks were private monopolies and should not be entrusted with issuance of currency. [ 117 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE 

(2) Bank-notes were an agency of monopoly and of oppression and extortion. (3) Since notes were based upon public debt, bankers were detrimental to best interests of the nation in opposing payment of public debt. (4) They were distrusted as being agencies of the "money power." 

3. The national banking system functioned with little change in the original plan until the adoption of the 

Federal Reserve System.

B. The Federal Reserve System was adopted in 1913. 

1. Recognized evils of the national banking were responsible for new system. a. Note issues did not provide elastic currency. b. Banks were independent institutions and were not integrated into a system. c. Reserves concentrated in money centers and became frozen there. 

2. The Federal Reserve system attempts to remedy the evils of national banks. a. Elasticity of currency is provided through the Federal 

Reserve note. b. National banks are required to affiliate themselves with the System. c. A Federal Reserve bank is established in each of twelve districts to accommodate the banks of the district. d. Federal Reserve banks extend aid to member banks through rediscount of paper. e. National integration of credit is attained by requirement that one federal reserve bank must rediscount paper held by another. f. Entire system is under control of the federal reserve board at Washington. 

C. Panic of 1929 brought emergency banking legislation. 

1. Because of the distrust of banks and the avalanche of bank failures President Roosevelt, on March 4, 1933, declared a banking holiday by closing most banks until emergency legislation could be secured. 

2. Emergency Banking Act was passed on March 9, 1933. a. Large additional issues of federal reserve bank notes were authorized. b. All persons were required to surrender their gold to federal treasury. [ 118 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE 

c. Conservators were provided for closed banks. d. National banks were permitted to issue preferred stock to be sold to Reconstruction Finance Corporation for cash. 

3. The Glass-Steagall Act of June 16, 1933, is more funda mental and revolutionary. a. Commercial banking is divorced from investment banking and no institution may carry on both types. b. Provision is made for the guarantee of bank deposits. c. Payment of interest on demand deposits is prohibited. d. Member banks are allowed to establish branches in those states which permit branch banking.

III. SOURCES OF GOVERNMENT REVENUE 

A. Customs and excises were used following the Civil War. 

1. High Civil War tariffs were continued but about onehalf of the federal revenue came from this source. 

2. The number of commodities upon which excises were levied was greatly reduced until the important revenue producers were those upon tobacco and liquor. 

3. The income tax, inaugurated during Civil War and held constitutional, was given up in 1872. 

4. An attempt to adopt income taxes in 1894 was frustrated by the Supreme Court. Such was declared unconstitutional as being a direct tax.

B. Other taxes have been added during present century. 

1. In 1909 a tax was levied upon the net income of corporations. This was held not to be a direct tax since it was levied as an excise tax upon the corporate franchise. 

2. Personal income taxes have been developed under Sixteenth Amendment. a. Amendment gives Congress power to levy taxes upon incomes from whatever source derived without their being apportioned among the states on basis of population. b. First income tax under the amendment was in 1913 with high exemptions and low rates. c. Rates were raised and exemptions lowered during 

World War and because of large incomes, the tax was productive of much revenue. d. With less need for revenue after the war rates were lowered and exemptions raised, only to be changed again with increased need for funds since 1929. [ 119 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE 

Shrunken incomes, however, have made such taxes less productive. e. Taxes upon corporate net incomes have been increased with a rate of 1334 per cent is now levied upon them. 

3. Estate taxes have been levied since World War. a. During war such taxes were used to produce revenue but have been kept largely to force the states to make more use of such revenue. States are allowed a credit up to 80 per cent of the federal levy for taxes levied by them. b. To break up large fortunes has also been a motive in the levy of estate taxes. c. During the economic depression processing taxes have been levied while excise taxes have been greatly extended.

C. States and localities have extended sources of revenues. 

1. For many years the important source of state and local revenues was the general property tax. 

2. Property tax has waned in importance as source of state funds and has been replaced by corporation taxes, income taxes, inheritance taxes and taxes upon commodities. 

3. As an emergency measure many states have adopted sales taxes. 

4. States receive increasing amounts as aid from the federal government, especially for highways, development of natural resources, and, in recent years, for emergency relief. 

5. Most local revenues still come from property taxes although increasing aid comes from the states for educations, highways and other functions.

D. Public indebtedness continues to be large. 

1. Indebtedness was decreased after the Civil War. a. Federal bonds were refunded at lower rates of interest and gradually repaid. b. Approximately $1,000,000,000 of indebtedness was retained at low rate of interest, largely as a basis for circulation of national bank notes. 

2. By end of the World War federal indebtedness had been increased to about $26,000,000,000 but this had been reduced to about $16,000,000,000 by 1929. 

3. Borrowing has been extensively used since 1930 and by 

1935 the indebtedness has reached the highest point in our history, about $28,000,000,000. [ 120 ]

PRIVATE FINANCE 

4. State and local governments have also borrowed but the total of such indebtedness is small in comparison with that of the federal government. 

IV. TARIFF ACTS 

A. The protective feature has dominated most tariffs. 

1. The tariffs enacted during the Civil War were presumably temporary measures, but the protective features were so pleasing to manufacturers that few reductions in rates were made. 

2. Growing dissatisfaction was expressed with tariffs but the McKinley tariff increased the protective features. 

3. As a protest the Gorman-Wilson tariff was enacted with lower rates but the depression of 1893 caused a reaction in the Dingley tariff which contained higher rates than any previous tariff. 

4. Protest resulted in the Payne-Aldrich tariff in which level of rates remained about the same as in Dingley tariff. 

5. Underwood-Simmons tariff provided lower rates but the almost immediate advent of the World War prevents any appraisal of its economic consequences. 

B. All tariffs since the World War have been highly protective. 

1. The Fordney emergency tariff was designed to extend aid to depressed agriculture after the war. 

2. The Fordney-McCumber tariff embodied the highest schedule of rates yet known in American tariffs. 

3. The rates in the Smoot-Hawley tariff of 1930 are still higher and have resulted in much retaliatory tariff legislation by other countries. 

4. The Roosevelt administration pledged itself to tariff reform but as late as 1935 nothing has been accomplished. [121]

THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA I. EXPANSION OF TERRITORY 

A. Expansion before 1898 was largely expansion of continental area. 

1. More land was needed to satisfy desire of settlers. 

2. Louisiana territory was purchased primarily to get control of the Mississippi. 

3. Alaska was purchased in 1867 to quiet the pretensions of 

Russia to greater claims on the northwest coast. 

4. Texas was annexed upon her own request. 

5. In all this expansion the United States was involved in but one war, that with Mexico in 1848.

B. The year 1898 introduced a new phase in the territorial expansion. 

1. The Hawaiian Islands were annexed in 1898 because of 

American economic interests there. 

2. As a result of the Spanish-American War the United 

States secured the Philippine Islands, Puerto Rico and Guam. 

3. As important coaling stations some of the Samoan islands have been annexed. 

4. By treaty with the Republic of Panama territory of the 

Panama Canal Zone is under control of the United States. 

5. Virgin Islands were purchased from Denmark.

II. DEVELOPMENT OF A FOREIGN POLICY 

A. Foreign policy was enunciated by early statesmen. 

1. Washington formulated first policy when he warned against permanent alliances with foreign nations. 

2. The Monroe Doctrine, formulated by President Monroe, has frequently been invoked. a. President Monroe warned European countries that "we should consider any attempts on their part to extend their system to any portion of this hemisphere as dangerous to our peace and safety." (1) Doctrine anticipated a growth of American republics freed from alliances with European countries. (2) Assumption was that conditions in Europe and America were different and that there should be no alliances between them. b. Monroe Doctrine has been invoked to restrain European countries from enlarging territorial possessions on western continent. (1) Great Britain sought to obtain territory along the coast of Nicaragua for purpose of constructing a canal. [122]

UNITED STATES OF AMERICA 

(2) France attempted to establish a colonial empire in Mexico. (3) In a boundary dispute between Great Britain and Venezuela, President Cleveland insisted that the matter be settled by arbitration. 

3. Monroe Doctrine has been extended in our Caribbean policy. a. Policy asserts that in the Caribbean the interests of the 

United States are paramount. b. The United States acts as a protectorate and has assumed the authority to maintain civil order in the countries and to compel them to fulfill their international obligations to prevent interference by European powers. c. In some cases it has landed marines, appointed officials, and administered finances until conditions were put in order. d. Policy has frequently been called "dollar diplomacy" as if it were built entirely around economic gain. 

The extent to which this has been true is hard to measure.

B. The United States has sponsored the open door policy in the Far East. 

1. With the development of our interests in trade in Far 

East we have been successful in maintaining the grant of equal opportunities of trade with China. 

2. We have not extended same policy to Philippines but have granted preferential tariffs to their imports, have given a monopoly of their carrying trade to American shippers and favorable opportunities were granted American investors.

II. THE UNITED STATES AS A WORLD NATION 

A. Several factors cause the United States to rank with other nations of the world. 

1. Extension of merchant marine gives it world-wide shipping interests. 

2. Territorial interests are extensive. 

3. United States is now a creditor nation with financial interests in Europe, Asia and the other Americas. [123]
