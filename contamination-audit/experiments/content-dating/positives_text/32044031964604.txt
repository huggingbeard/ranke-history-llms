The Constitution of Florida

Introduction 

In a democratic government, public power is wielded most directly by the citizen as he chooses his epresentatives and accepts or refuses constitutional change at regular intervals. The voters of Florida vill go to the polls in November 1954 to select their Jnited States Congressmen and a number of state nd county officials. They will also be asked to pprove or reject seven proposed amendments to the tate constitution.

This is not a "presidential election year" and pubic interest generally is low. The May primary elecions in a one party state like Florida pretty largely ecide the November result and this narrows the ppeal of the general election. Except for the unimely death of the governor last year, no major state ffice would be at stake. Selection of a candidate to ill the unexpired term of the late Dan McCarty rovided an active spring primary race, but by fall, is fair to say that the amendments will be the most nportant items on the ballot. 1

Interest in state constitutional problems is likely to be in direct proportion to the amount of contro versy they arouse. Two years ago in November 

1952, at least four of the eleven proposals caused much discussion and difference of opinion. The amendment to "earmark" state motor vehicle license revenues to assist in providing capital funds for schools under the Minimum Foundation program was approved after an intensive campaign by its sup porters; the proposals to enlarge the Supreme Court, to permit Counties to receive municipal-type char ters, to create an additional state senatorial district and five local amendments of lesser importance failed to be adopted.

Brief Constitutional Facts. The Florida Cor situtional Convention of 1885 prepared the present constitution which became effective in 1887. Since that time, almost 150 amendments have been sub mitted for ratification with a total of 88 adopted. 

The amendments are now about as long as the origi nal document and, over the years, have added to the complexity and contradictions of an out-of-date basic law.

The amending process in Florida is not compli cated. Amendments may be proposed in either house of the legislature and, when approved by three-fifths of the members of both houses, are submitted to the voters at the next general election. The governo takes no part in the amending procedures. A simple statewide majority of votes cast is all that is neces sary for the proposed amendment to become a part of the state constitution. A special emergency method of amending the Florida constitution requiring three fourths of both houses of the legislature and a special election has never been used.

The Florida Voter's Problem. State constitu tions generally tend to be longer, more amended, an more detailed than the concise federal model. Ofte they impose negative restraints on legislatures an prevent easy, flexible adjustment of such matters a the system of courts, county and city governmen organization of state agencies and compensation 2

officials. Matters which might be resolved by elected representatives at regular intervals become constitutional problems to be dealt with by the cumbersome amending process. So it happens that changing conditions make long, complex constitutions even longer in an almost vain hope of simplifying obsolete provisions. Often this situation invites further constitutional change at the next election and the cycle continues. It is an axiom of state government that legislatures tend to be less effective as state constitutions grow more elaborate.

Until the Florida constitution is thoroughly overhauled and brought up to date, it will probably be necessary to continue with a patchwork of amendments from time to time. For many years in Florida, it was customary for most proposed amendments to be adopted at the general election after they had been approved by the legislature. In late years, the trend appears to be away from accepting further amendments, notably in 1952 when 10 out of 11 proposals were rejected by the voters. Nevertheless, it would be possible to renovate the Florida constitution by a process of amending it Article by Article resulting in a virtually new document over a period of years; thus the amending process must not be disregarded and the alert citizen will study carefully all problems of constitutional change. th

Extended Legislative Session— Additional Compensation I. House Joint Resolution No. 579: Proposes an amendment to the Legislative Department Article III, of the state constitution to permit the legislature by three-fifths vote of the membership of both houses to meet for an additional 30 days each two years.

Retains existing 60 day regular session each two years and 20 day special sessions when called by Governor. 

Extra 30 day sessions called by legislature itself need not be consecutive; recesses may be taken by joint action of both houses; no extended session to last beyond September 1st following regular session. The amendment also provides an increase from $600 per session to $1200 per year as regular compensation for legislators, and per diem expense and travel expense money at a rate not to exceed the regular allowances for other state officials under general laws which apply.

Discussion: At present the Legislature meets ev ery two years in a regular session limited to 60 days. 

Members receive $10 per day in salary ($600 per session maximum) and $7.50 per day subsistence

allowance plus 7½¢ per mile to and from homes for not more than four round trips per regular session.

Special sessions of not more than 20 days may be called only by the Governor. Pay and allowances are similar for special sessions with a limit of mileage for 

2 round trips from home.

The proposed amendment provides for a continuation of the regular 60 day biennial session starting in April and special sessions when called by the Gov- 

C ernor for not more than 20 days. However, by 3/5ths vote of the membership of both houses, the legislature may extend a regular session up to a limit of 30 additional days, not necessarily consecutive, not to extend beyond September 1st. During this additional 30 day period, recesses may be taken by joint action of the two houses. There is no provision for a recess during the regular 60 day meeting. New legislation may be introduced into the extended session upon consent of 2/3rds of the members of the House where it originates.

A number of studies of legislative problems in Florida have concluded that the current legislative session is too short for the thousands of bills and issues with which it must deal. It has often been el suggested that, with certain safeguards, the legislature should have the power to call itself into session or to extend its deliberations. This proposal is offered as a method of solving one of the problems by permitting the legislature to have a 30 day session, if it desires, immediately after the regular 60 day session. Often, one portion of a "split session" will be "estricted to tax, revenue and budget matters so as ot to confuse finances with other state issues and pcal bills. The proposal to increase the compensation of leg. islators appears to be a recognition of the increased responsibility which they bear. It is certain that state 

Senators and Representatives have to work at their representative jobs throughout the year, not just for a few weeks in Tallahassee during the session. Increased compensation has long been urged for Florida legislators and this amendment will increase it to a fixed $100 per month.

There is always a problem in providing salaries for policy-making representative officials such as city councilmen, county commissioners, and members of legislatures. Should the job pay enough to attract persons who may seek it for the money or should public service of this kind be a civic duty which may require occasional personal sacrifice? There are many who feel sincerely that it is unwise to provide regular salaries for this kind of public position. 

It is possible, nevertheless, that the present, low compensation for Florida state legislators keeps able persons from serving who might otherwise offer to run for the office. The small additional compensation which is suggested does not appear to be enough to attract persons for its own sake but may take a con siderable burden from those who otherwise might have to seek funds from friends or special groups. If an extended session is desirable as proposed in this amendment perhaps some additional compensation should match the extra service which is contemplated. 

Whether the proposed amount is too large or too small is left to the voter's opinion.

However, this may be an oversimplification be cause of several other matters which have a bearing on the situation. First, it will be noted that the peo ple of Florida amended the state constitution in 1930 so that "The Legislature shall have the power to provide for issuing State bonds only for the purpose of repelling invasion or surpressing insurrection,..." 

As a result, Florida has no "general obligation bond" state debt. 

Second, to finance some state and local highway and public works construction since World War II, Florida has borrowed a considerable amount of money (more than $70,000,000) through a device called "rev enue certificates" issued by an agency known as the State Improvement Commission. As authorized by the legislature, these are interest-bearing, debt obligations on which the interest and principal is to be paid from specific revenues such as bridge tolls, dormitory rentals or earmarked gas taxes. The "full faith and credit" of the state is not pledged to them and the word "bonds" is not used although the net effect of the "revenue certificates" is to permit the state to borrow money and to pledge to pay back fixed interest and principal as in the case of bonds. 

Although to the investment bankers who purchase these evidences of indebtedness, a well-planned issue of state revenue certificates is an attractive investment based on the potential earning power of a particular public project (the bankers have stated privately that they do not expect the state to allow its revenue certificates to go into default), they invariably insist on a higher rate of interest for revenue certificate or revenue bond securities. Thus, the debt service charges on projects constructed in this way will be higher than if built from general obligation bonds, current oper ating funds or from any surplus which may be on hand. Governor's Veto Additional Time III. Senate Joint Resolution No. 179: Proposes an amendment to Article III, The Legislative De partment, of the constitution relating to Executive approval of bills by providing that after final adjournment of the Legislature the Governor shall have twenty (20) days in which to approve or veto bills.

Present 5 day limit during legislative session un changed. 

Discussion: At present, the Governor has ten days in which to study bills which reach him after the close of the legislative session. During the session he must accept or reject proposed measures in five days, but he has ten days after adjournment to approve or veto any bill passed within the final five legislative days.

The proposed amendment would give the Governor an additional ten days, or a total of twenty days after the close of the legislature, to consider the flood of bills which customarily come at that time. During a legislative session in Florida, a Governor may be called upon to consider as many as 1,000 separate bills and it is not unusual for more than 500 to reach him after the House and Senate have finally adjourned. The present limit of ten days was estab lished when the demands on the Governor were much fewer. The object of the amendment is to allow ad ditional time for the hundreds of measures which must be studied and on which considered action must be taken by the Governor.

No change is proposed in the procedure for passing a bill over the Governor's veto. A two-thirds vote of 10

each house is required to over-ride a veto in the present constitution, and the same stipulation is provided in the proposed amendment. If the Governor's veto comes within five days of the end of the session, and adjournment prevents the legislature from acting, or if the veto falls within the twenty day period following the session, then the next session of the legislature may consider passing the bill over the veto by a twothirds vote of each house.

There appear to be no strong reasons why this additional time should not be allowed the Governor in the discharge of this important function. There are reasons for believing that the present time is too short and that the consideration of important issues of public policy would be aided by the adoption of this amendment.

Conclusion 

No easy task faces the citizen in deciding upon Florida's proposed constitutional amendments this year. The process of piecemeal amendment to an old and somewhat confused basic law does not usually get at most of the basic problems. 

Most of the proposed amendments have merit when taken alone. Each is designed to accomplish some specific objective of public policy and has stood the test of legislative approval before being offered to the voters for referendum.

Yet, it is difficult to consider these proposals i single fashion because they must become woven into the fundamental framework of our state and loca governments if approved. There are some specific 18

udga questions in the minds of many about these proposals and this paper has attempted to single out the major issues for objective comment.

Someone has stated that a constitution should be sail and not an anchor for a democracy. By that s meant a constitution is the basic instrument for guiding and directing the forward progress of a repreentative free society and is not intended to keep govtrnment at rest or fixed in the shallow bays of the of bast. world ars Yet it must be observed that a democratic constiution is an important reference point which together with the will and high purpose of the citizens deternines the substance and character of public policy. t cannot shift with every breeze, for citizens and "udges alike look to their constitutions as fundamental nstruments of self-government. il cr has ent lity There will always be forces contending for change and advantage just as there will be those who seek to reserve a favored position. It has been part of the 

American constitutional system to give wide scope to hany points of view in a framework of orderly govtrnment and to resolve most difficulties by equitable ompromise. 

It is usually possible to propose ideal solutions to onstitutional problems, but experience teaches that hese ideal solutions are hard to achieve and practical roblems of the moment often cannot await the work inf idealists. ent to!

In considering this year's constitutional proposals, he citizen will keep in mind the work of the Judicial Council which is to report later on means of improvng the Judicial Article of the Constitution, and the various other proposals which have been made from come to time for revising and overhauling the constihaution by (a) a Constitution Convention, (b) a Contitutional Commission and (c) Article by Article reision.
