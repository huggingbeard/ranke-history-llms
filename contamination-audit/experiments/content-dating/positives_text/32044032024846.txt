Tax Accounting For Tots and T-Men Introduction 

There is no doubt that a lawyer can draw a deed or a will without having the slightest knowledge of accounts. He can sue for negligence or trespass and defend his client against such suits even though his knowledge of accounting is so limited that he does not know a debit from a credit.

On the other hand, a lawyer cannot fairly advise his clients about investments unless he can read financial statements; and he cannot do tax work unless he can read a set of books. 

Then, too, let us not forget that, even in drawing that deed and that will, the lawyer encounters, and generally creates, tax problems, some of them of horrendous proportions, and even there, a knowledge of accounting would help him. 

Accounting represents a bugaboo to many a lawyer. On the one hand he considers it a minor profession, like, maybe, the "tonsorial art." And on the other hand he knows that it would take him two years of intense study to become even a fair accountant. The lawyer knows he cannot cut a head of hair, and he doesn't want to know how to do it. He can get a haircut for seventy-five cents or a dollar. He knows he cannot keep books, and he says he doesn't want to know. He claims he can hire a bookkeeper for fifty dollars a week.

Some day, however, he is forced to admit that a good law practice involves clients with money, and that money has to be invested; and money breeds income and income involves accounting and taxes; and rich clients die and death involves accounting and taxes; and finally he concludes that he needs to know accounting. 

As soon as he finds out that he must know accounting he finds that there is very little literature on accounting addressed to him. 

Then he finds out that as soon as he admits he needs to know accounting, all the people who know accounting look down their noses at him, and play hard-to-get when he asks them to teach him. Even if he goes down to day-and-evening college in his own home city, the professor of accounting tells him he is launching on a three year project, and maybe his mind is not trained enough to absorb such an intricate subject anyway. 1

The lawyer needs be a sturdy soul if he be not discouraged here. 

That idea prompts me to write this monograph. Maybe I can help you a little. Without preliminary, I attack the subject. 

A set of books is kept for the purpose of recording all the financial transactions of a business. A good set of books shows. the true financial status of the business as it was on certain given dates, and it shows the profits and losses over given periods of time. A good set of books also shows the financial responsibility of the business, and the ability of the business to meet its obligations. It conveys some idea of the value of the business; but reliance solely on the books to determine value is unwise. 

You will never see a perfect set of books. 

As a practical matter you will find that usually there are adjustments which must be made in the books before the books reflect all the facts, and before they accomplish the ends that I have just said books are meant to accomplish. 

In an effort to improve this situation, a set of unwritten and thoroughly misunderstood rules called "Best Accounting Practice" has been devised. No two accountants agree on these rules, on what they mean, or on how to apply them. 

The "Best Accounting Practice" means, or was devised to mean, that you must "Make honest entries reflecting the true facts." 

Let's leave it there.

The temptation not to follow such a rule is often great.

Bookkeeping Systems 

Since books of account are meant to show the value and the earnings of a business, they must follow some logical plan, and must do it consistently.

They may be kept upon any one of many plans, but there are two generally accepted methods, the Accrual System and the 

Cash System.

ACCRUAL SYSTEM 

Under the accrual system all obligations are entered as they arise, without regard to their discharge. Their discharge is, of course, noted, but, so far as income is concerned the creation of the obligation and not its discharge is the determining factor. 2

Every money transaction creating a right or a duty is entered as of the time the right or duty becomes fixed, and against the time to which it logically applies. 

For example: Under this method, if the principal borrows money at interest, an entry will be made in the period to which it applies showing the interest owed and to whom it is owed. 

This will be done without regard to whether or not the interest is paid.

For another example: The payroll for the week ending 

Saturday night is entered as of Saturday as an item of expense, and this is done whether the payments are made at that time or not.

The accrual system is, by far, the most accurate method of setting up accounts; and it is the most popular. 

Most businesses may adopt any system of bookkeeping the owners may choose; but, at least in tax practice, any business having inventories is required to be on the accrual basis. There is no choice in the matter.

CASH RECEIPTS AND DISBURSEMENTS SYSTEM 

The cash system of keeping books is the simplest and least scientific method. It accounts for money received when received, and money paid out when paid out. 

For example: The interest on the loan referred to above, even if due or past due, would not be entered until it was paid; but when it was paid it would then be entered as "interest paid." 

The payroll referred to above would not be entered unless paid, nor until paid.

The cash system takes no account of outstanding and unpaid items, nor does it take any account of outstanding receivable items. That is, at least, in theory. In practice, however, the cash receipts and disbursements basis must take into account certain events and certain items even though they are not evidenced by the receipt or the disbursement of money. It takes into account capital items just as is done on the accrual basis. Thus, on cash receipts and disbursements books, as on accrual books, one hundred dollars spent for a desk is charged as a capital item. It is not charged as an expense. And the desk, on the cash receipts and disbursements basis, as on the accrual basis, is set up as an asset and depreciated over the period of its useful life. 3

In addition to that, people on the cash receipts and disbursements basis who lend money must charge the loan to the debtor and not to expense. When the loan is repaid the money received is not income. It is credited to the debtor's account. In this regard the entry is exactly the same as it would be on the accrual basis; for the loan is an account receivable, and the payment is a credit to an account. 

Cash receipts and disbursements books necessarily reflect the problems of the proprietor of the business. In bad years his charges remain unpaid, whereas in good years he pays the past accruals and the current bills. In tax work this system allows a certain amount of shifting of income from one year to another. 

The cash receipts and disbursements method is rarely used except by individuals. It does, however, fit nicely into the needs of law and accounting firms and professional men generally. 

Constructive Receipt Basis 

A very important feature of the cash receipts and disbursements method of accounting is known as the Constructive Receipt Basis. This is not a different system of accounting. It is part of the cash system.

A good example of constructive receipt is wages. Suppose on 

December 31st a given taxpayer was entitled to be paid what was due him for December. On accrual books he would accrue it as income, a December item in the accounts of the expiring year. But suppose he is on the cash receipts and disbursements basis. He might not be paid until January 2nd. A question then arises as to the year in which he should be charged with the income. The rule is that if, on December 31st, the money was unqualifiedly subject to his demand, he must still report it in the year earned, instead of the next year during which he actually drew it. Constructively, he received the income in December. 

The classic example is bond coupons. Bond coupons, if good, are income when due whether the owner clips and cashes them or not. The interest is constructively received when due. 

Summed up, it might be said that the constructive receipt basis covers the theoretical receipt of money which could have been reduced to possession but was not. The rule is that if the taxpayer could have closed his hand on the income, but turned his back and walked away, the income is assumed to have been

his when he failed to take it. It is a necessary part of cash receipts and disbursements accounting. If this were not so, taxpayers would shift their collections from one year to another at will. Some shifting is inherent in the system, but not such artful shifting as this. 

MIXED BASIS OF ACCOUNTING 

Sometimes in tax work it is found that books are really kept partly on an accrual method and partly on the cash receipts and disbursements method. It is not entirely settled just how such accounts should be adjusted. It has been said that if the books are generally on one basis, that basis should be used, and all items not on that basis should be put on that basis.¹ 

It sometimes happens, however, that one or two very large items are on the accrual basis and four or five dozen very small items are on the cash basis. It would seem probable, in such a case, that the treatment of the big items would govern.2 

A general rule regarding mixed accounts is that they must be properly adjusted to reflect income.³

In such cases the gain is taxable, or the loss is deductible, in the year of completion.5 Just when a contract is completed is a very troublesome question. Acceptance is probably the test. Accounting Periods 

All accounts are kept as of periods of time. They show what the business was worth when the period started, what was earned during the period, and what the business was worth when the period ended.

Almost all accounts are kept on a basis of a period of a year. 

Most accounting statements are as of the beginning and end of the proprietor's year. You will sometimes see books "closed" monthly or semi-annually. That means the proprietor makes monthly or semi-annual formal calculations of the worth of the business and of its profits and losses, in addition to annual statements.

Some taxpayers select years that start on some day other than January first, but January first is the most popular starting day for the accounting year. The Government insists, for income tax purposes, that no period may exceed one year, and that every period must end on the last day of a month. 

In order to show how a business stands, or is progressing, Time and the Accountants have worked out what might be described roughly as a "Universal System for Stating Accounts." 

When this system is honestly followed, anybody can tell, in a rough way, after a few minutes study of the statements, the worth, the responsibility, and the profits and losses of any business.

Do not be misled by what I have just said. In one crafty way or another, in almost all cases where it is important to know these things, the accounts may be manipulated so as to deceive you. 

It takes more than a few minutes to decide whether to buy the stock of a corporation, or whether to lend money to a business. 

BASIS OF TAX RETURN AND ACCOUNTING PERIOD 

Tax returns are required to be made upon an accepted accounting basis. Corporations generally keep their books on the accrual method. Almost all books of any moment are kept on that basis.

Doctors, lawyers, and other professional men generally keep their accounts and report on the cash receipts and disbursements basis. 

In Federal income tax practice, the general rule is that the accounting with the Government shall be on the basis of a year,

ending the last day of a month. That year must correspond to the fiscal basis on which the books are kept." The calendar year is favored in all cases of doubt.8 When the taxpayer's year does not end on the last day of a month, he is automatically placed on the calendar year for Federal tax purposes. 

There are provisions in the tax law and regulations allowing changes from one period to another, and provisions allowing short periods, provided the consent of the Commissioner has been obtained.⁹

PERIOD OF LESS THAN ONE YEAR 

Any taxpayer who is required or allowed to make a return for a period of less than one year must compute his tax on the annual basis. This is done in order to prevent accidental or intentional reduction of surtax or excess profits tax brackets by the use of short periods.

Computing the tax on the annual basis means that the income is divided by the number of months in the period and then multiplied by twelve; then the tax is computed; then the tax so computed is divided by twelve and multiplied by the number of months in the period. For example: Assume an individual reporting on the basis of a fiscal year ended October 31st of each year decides to change to the calendar year, thus creating a short period embracing the months of November and December.

Assume his income in those two months is $20,000.00. His tax for those two months is not figured on $20,000.00. It is figured $20,000.00 

X 12 or $120,000.00. Then 2/12 of the tax so 2 computed is charged to him. The tax on 2/12 of $120,000 is a lot more than the tax on $20,000.00 would be. on:

This rule does not apply to the case of the return of a decedent in the year of death; or to the return of the estate of a decedent for the balance of the year in which the death occurred.

How Books Are Kept 

If you hope to read a set of accounts, and the books from which the accounts were compiled, you surely must know how books are kept.

The general scheme of bookkeeping is that every financial transaction must be entered on the account books. 

The theory of making the entries is that the source from which the money or value came is credited with the money or value in dollars; the source to which the money or value moves is charged with the money or value in dollars. 

In this connection you must remember that expense items are charged; income items are credited. That is because expense items are sources to which the money or value goes; income items are sources from which the money comes. 

There follows a discussion of simple bookkeeping, on the accrual basis. Don't forget. These accounts are on the accrual basis. 12

CASH BOOK 

Most sets of books have what amount to additional journals. 

They are called the Cash Book, the Sales Book and the Purchase 

Book. 

So many business transactions deal with cash, or with the bank, that a special account for cash or the bank is warranted in the books of original entry, and that is the reason for a cash book. 

The cash book is not always a true cash account. It is often an account with a bank.

The real cash transactions, which, in a well ordered business are usually not great or frequent, are run through an account called Petty Cash.

Most businesses have so many sales and so many purchases that separate journals are kept for them. 

This is enough concerning these journals for now. I shall tell you more about them later. You do not have to worry about these angles right now.

So far, all you have to know is that every financial transaction requires an entry in the journal or in a book that serves the same purpose as a journal. Such a book always has a charge (debit) in the left hand column, and a credit in the right hand column in the very same amount as the charge. The charge is to the source that gets the value or money, the credit is to the source from which the value or money comes. Sometimes the debit is to several accounts, sometimes the credit is to several accounts, sometimes both are to several accounts, but the fact remainsthe debit equals the credit.

We now come to the secondary books, which are, in practice, the important ones. 15

If we assume thousands of such transactions, or even hundreds, it is clear that some more concise method of keeping the accounts had to be devised, else a set of financial statements would be such a cumbersome thing that nobody could ever figure out anything.

To meet this need the accounts are collected in what is called a Ledger.¹7

A ledger has a separate account in it for every "name" in the journal. Into the ledger all journal entries are transferred and charged or credited to the particular accounts just as the journal entry indicates they should be.

In the ledger, as in the journal, all charges are on the left hand side of the page or in the left hand column, all credits are on the right hand side of the page or in the right hand column. 

In the ledger, as in the journal, the sum of all the left hand entries must equal the sum of all the right hand entries. There is and must always be a balance (unless there are errors, and if there are errors, they must be caught and corrected at once).

How to Use These Books 

The next step is to consider further what or whom to charge and what or whom to credit.

In the example given above we have real things or real people to charge and credit.

There was a typewriter charged to office equipment. 

There was a debt due Remington Rand. 

There was a loan at a bank and Remington Rand was paid. 

But many accounts do not involve real things and real people. 

They involve transactions relating to nonexistent things—such as "Expenses," "Depreciation" and "Wages."

So we have what are known as Real Accounts-Typewriter, Remington Rand, Bank; and we have what are known as Nominal 

Accounts-Expenses, Depreciation, Sales, and Purchases. 

In my thinking I divide nominal accounts into Nominal Accounts Affecting the Current Period, and Permanent Nominal Accounts. The latter are such accounts as Reserves, Depreciation Reserve, Surplus and Net Worth. This is my own idea. You do not have to adopt it. It is explained below. 

A set of books, before it is what we call "closed," always has in it both accounts with things and people and accounts with mere names; that is to say, a set of books, before closing, always has real accounts and nominal accounts. You will observe, on looking back or by reading on a few pages, that a trading statement is made up solely of the nominal accounts affecting the current period. None of these accounts carries over to the balance sheet. Not one of them is anything the company owns or owes.

After reading this and examining a trading statement, you might note that inventories are shown on a trading statement as well as on a balance sheet. That is true; but they are shown on a trading statement only for the purpose of figuring out the goods that are sold-not there-gone. The goods that are gone are not anything the company owns. 

When we make up a balance sheet it contains only actual or real accounts, something the company owns and something or someone the company owes, plus one or two permanent nominal accounts, usually Reserves, 20 and Surplus.

Before we can show gains or losses, or net worth, various "adjusting entries" have to be made. Then the "closing entries" have to be made. Then a balance sheet is reached. 

The adjusting entries are those entries which the necessities of the case require to be made in order to show all the transactions of the period, and to take into account those things which must be recorded before a fair statement of the operations of the period can be made.

One of these adjusting entries is to record the year's wear and tear-depreciation. Depreciation takes place, it must be recorded. But there is no transaction that happens so that the bookkeeper would have to make such an entry. Yet the necessities of the case require such an entry and it must be made. 

Another adjusting entry must be made to substitute the actual 24

count of the inventory for the figure shown on the books as a charge to inventory. All goods purchased are charged to inventory; or, better perhaps, the opening inventory figure remains on the books throughout the year, and goods purchased are charged to an account called purchases. All goods sold are credited to sales. So a count of the inventory must be taken and the actual inventory on hand, properly priced, must be substituted for the book figure. There are many other adjusting entries.

Closing entries are different from adjusting entries. They come after all the adjusting entries are made, and show the final results.

In a trial balance, even after adjustments, you have all the debits and all the credits but you cannot tell how the business fared during the period.

In order to close out the accounts and ascertain the results of the period's operations, you close out all the expense items (charges to current operations) by a credit to each of these accounts and a charge to current operations, usually called Profit and Loss. Then you close out all credits to current operations such as sales-by a charge to such items and a credit to profit and loss. Thus you collect in profit and loss all the charges and credits to operations and the gain is the excess of the credits over the charges. If the charges exceed the credits there was a loss.

Reserves 

Reserves are very tricky things. I shall attempt to explain them to you. 

Reserves are a means of: (a) concealing the true facts; or (b) setting up provisions to meet contingencies; or (c) showing the provision made out of profits or surplus to record depreciation or other actual or fancied changes or lessening of values; or (d) all of these noble or ignoble purposes.

Reserves are the bookkeeper's hip pocket in which to conceal his wallet or his gun. 28 

Except "Reserves." 37

Many reserves show on the balance sheet, but many do not. 

If you are dishonest in your bookkeeping, or "ultra-conservative," you can set up a "reserve" in inventory by the simple device of a miscount, or a discount, or by mispricing. If you do this 

Uncle Sam will not like you any more. There are other ways of setting up completely disguised reserves. I shall confine my discussion to reserves which show on the balance sheet. 

When you make a charge (debit) you must make a credit.

If there is nobody or nothing to credit, the credit may be made to a reserve. 

Let me take the best example, and the most usual one.

You are closing the books for the year. You know the plant has worn out some. It always does. So you must make a charge to depreciation expense and take that charge out of income. 

Otherwise you would overstate income by that amount.

I know this is not an insurmountable difficulty, but the usual custom is to credit a reserve. 38

A reserve for depreciation is an honest reserve, and represents a loss of value. It is therefore a reduction of assets when you figure the worth of the business. Charges to depreciation expense (sometimes called "depreciation" for short) are credited to this reserve. They are a reduction of income, and a deduction on the tax return.

Such is not the case with many other reserves. 

Let us assume, for an example, that the management feels that it will take $25,000.00 at current prices, to replace the $10,000 machinery when it wears out. So it decides to set up a reserve to cover this extra $15,000.00. To this end the actual depreciation of $750.00, based on cost of the machinery, is augmented by a further $1,125.00 to cover the added cost of replacement.

This added $1,125.00 would not be chargeable to current expenses or deductible on the tax return. It would not be a reserve to be reckoned with as a reduction in computing net worth. It would be what is called a "Surplus Reserve," that is to say, a part of surplus, a mere "savings" pocket. Such a reserve should not be treated as a debt or as a reduction when surplus and net worth are calculated. It would be part of net worth. 

So, when you come to reserves you must decide whether they record losses, or whether they are mere surplus reserves and really a part of surplus and therefore part of net worth. 

Analysis of Accounts 

You may be able to conduct a tax practice for a lifetime and never make an entry in any set of books, but it is indeed an extraordinary tax case that does not require you to have a sufficient knowledge of bookkeeping and accountancy to analyze the accounts.

For the tax lawyer it is more important to know how to analyze a statement than how to keep the books from which the statement is drawn. There is no great harm in knowing both. 

Analyzing accounts is a serious matter requiring skill. College courses on the analysis of accounts take a full year or more. It is not possible to cover a year's college course in twenty pages, but it is possible to state the rules. 39

Taking the problem from its broad base, no taxpayer can owe a tax on income unless he achieved that income, and every taxpayer who has realized income either has it or has spent it. 

Every case, therefore, requires an analysis to see whether or not the taxpayer had income, and if he had it, what he did with it. You may take it as settled that if you know what a taxpayer spent and what he saved, you can get very close to what he earned.

The equations are quite simple. Let me state one of them. 

If you know what a taxpayer ended the year with, and deduct from that what he started the year with, you will have what he saved in the course of the year. If you add what he saved to the amount he spent in the course of the year, you will have his income for the year, provided you deduct any gifts and any inherited money and all other nontaxable receipts. 

The rule is even simpler than I have stated it: 

What a man had in the beginning, plus what he earned, plus unearned receipts, minus what he spent, equals what he had at the end. Or, what he had in the end, plus what he spent, minus what he had in the beginning, minus unearned receipts, equals what he earned during the period. 

It is clear that when you have the accounts of any taxpayer in connection with any tax case, you should analyze those accounts. A method of analysis is discussed below. 

Before I start showing you how to analyze the accounts, I will tell you that most lawyers do not do what I am now advising you to do; and I will whisper to you that most lawyers can't do it; but it should be done, and sooner or later it will be done, probably by the Government auditors to the detriment of the taxpayer. 

The method of analyzing tax accounts is as follows: 

(I am here assuming that the taxpayer is a corporation. If it is a partnership or a proprietorship, multiply your caution by 

10 and your fears by 100.) 

You assume that your client is going to tell you the truth, but you must be careful not to be too strong in your conviction. 

If there is something to hide, he will probably attempt to hide it from you, and, unfortunately for him, he may be successful in doing so. He will not be able to hide it from the Government for long. It will probably come out at the trial table where you will not be able to offset its maximum effect. 

Your first inquiry to your client is made at a time when the answers do not appear to be important, while you are engaging 40

him in casual conversation. You ask him, first, casually, how business is, and how it has been for the last two or three years; whether the year in question was extraordinary in any way, and, if so, why and to what extent. You ask him these things while he is off guard, because it is going to be important a little later to know whether the year in question was a bad year or a good year, and how competition was going, and whether things were easy or tight, and the general circumstances of that year's business. 

You then ask for the books, and you start with Sales. 

The books will show a sales figure. You ask your client if this covers all the sales. He will tell you "yes," but there's no harm in going a little deeper into the matter, and seeing whether or not the sales of some odd lots of scrap, or something of the sort, maybe through the back door and for cash, are omitted from time to time, due to an agreement concerning such matters. This is not the common practice, but you occasionally find cases where there were such sales. You may even find that the superintendent, or maybe the treasurer, or possibly the cashier, or even the principal stockholder, put that money in his pocket, on some theory or another. If any of these things happened, you have to make proper adjustments. The proper adjustment might not affect the income of the particular taxpayer, but you have to make it.

It might be that the superintendent, as part of his job under an established custom, is entitled to pocket the cash from sales of scrap. If that be true, he owes an income tax on it. It might be that the treasurer of the company, being a large stockholder, pocketed the cash from sales of odd lots. If that be true, it might be a question of added compensation for him, or it might be a question of profits by the company, plus a taxable dividend to him. But, whatever it is, you have to work it out. 

Now, after a few hours' work, we've gotten past the first item in the year's accounts-sales.

The next item is the Cost of Sales, leading up to what is generally called Gross Profit.

The formula is as follows: 

Take the inventory at the beginning of the year and add all the purchases, and you have all the goods that could have been sold. Deduct the inventory at the end of the year, and you have the cost of all the goods that were sold. Deduct the cost of the 41

goods that were sold (generally called Cost of Goods Sold) from the sales, and you have the gross profit.

If you then deduct all other business expenses from the gross profit, you have the Net Income. The formula is simple, but this puts us ahead of our story.

Some bookkeeping systems take the inventory at the beginning and add all items purchased, at cost, deduct the inventory at the end, and arrive at a figure for cost of goods sold. Others (and probably the better bookkeepers, although I do not myself do it) add to purchases all such items as "freight in" and manufacturing labor, and sometimes even manufacturing overhead, arriving at a figure from which the closing inventory is deducted to arrive at the cost of goods sold. 

You might point out that moving the "freight in" from the other expenses which are deducted after you reach gross profit, and moving the manufacturing overhead from the items which are deducted after you get gross profit, is just shifting the accounts around and leaves the same figure of net income. In other words, if you figure gross profit on the bare cost of material, and, after arriving at gross profit, deduct these items, you get the same figure that you would get if you added these items to purchases and deducted them as part of the cost of goods sold. 

You would not be entirely correct in reaching this conclusion. 

It is true that it doesn't make any difference whether you deduct these items before or after reaching gross profit. That is, unless the closing inventory basis is changed. Do not forget that the closing inventory is taken physically, then priced, and then substituted for the inventory figure shown on the books. If properly priced there would be no change in income as a result of shifting the above described item. The case might be different in regard to manufacturing overhead. Such overhead is often ignored in pricing the inventory. But as a rule, if you add manufacturing overhead to purchases, before arriving at gross profit, you reflect those same items when you come to value the closing inventory. 

That does shift income. The shift is sometimes material. 

I will illustrate, but before I do so let me say that whichever method of inventory the taxpayer has adopted is all right if consistently followed. Be that as it may, neither the taxpayer nor the Government is justified in changing it in any one year, for that would effect a material shifting of income in or out of that year. Now for the illustration: 42

If the opening inventory were $10,000, not counting manufacturing overhead, and the purchases were $30,000, the total would be $40,000; and if the closing inventory were $12,000, not counting manufacturing overhead, the cost of goods sold would be $28,000. But if the closing inventory were increased from $12,000 to $20,000 by adding to it the manufacturing overhead, then the cost of goods sold would be only $20,000 instead of $28,000, and the income of that year would be increased by $8,000.

I now repeat that shifting the manufacturing overhead from "items deductible from gross profit" to "items added to purchases" does not in and of itself affect income at all. It is the resulting closing inventory value that affects income. Every dollar added to closing inventory increases income a dollar, every dollar deducted from closing inventory decreases income a dollar. 

Inventory adjustments are compensating. A dollar added to this year's closing inventory increases this year's income a dollar and decreases next year's income a dollar.

Once you have arrived at the gross profit, you then make a schedule covering a period of years, showing the sales, inventory at the beginning, purchases, inventory at the end, cost of goods sold and gross profit. Put these down in parallel columns and look them over.

Let's assume, for the sake of argument, that, over a period of years, the sales show an increase in some years, a decrease in others; or that they show a steady increase very much slacked up in the year in question; or that they show other irregularity. 

You would inquire about such matters and find out what caused them, not being too quick to accept a glib answer. Such inquiry is most important. If there is any irregularity, something caused it. What was it?

After you have satisfied yourself as to sales, you then look at the opening inventory for each year and see what the fluctuations were there, and you find out why there were fluctuations. 

Then look at the purchases with the same calloused eye. 

Then you consider the closing inventory.

When you have satisfied yourself on all of these items and have reconciled all irregularities, you look to see what the ratio of gross profit was to sales. 43

Let's assume, for the sake of argument, that it was 30% one year, 32% another year, 31% another year, 33% another year, and 22% this year. That situation would require a great deal of explaining; and you would have to investigate it to the very bottom, for you have an abnormal picture. 

As a rule, something is wrong in a case like that, and, also as a rule, the error is found in the inventories, but it need not be there. Inventories or not, the chances are, in such a case, that something is wrong somewhere. The error could be in business conditions affecting the taxpayer, or it could be found in an honest or dishonest mistake. You have to know the answer, now.

It could be, for example, that invoices were entered against purchases and that the goods were in transit and, as a result, not counted. The difference might result from a deliberate or accidental miscount. Or, it could be in an error in pricing.

I remember seeing an inventory once where 3,000 pounds of a material, inventoried at $20.00 a ton, were erroneously priced at $20.00 a pound. Three thousand pounds is 11½ tons, and, at $20.00 a ton, that's $30.00; but 3,000 pounds at $20.00 a pound is $60,000.00. Mistakes like that do occur; but if there is a mistake you have to find it out and locate and correct it, always hoping, of course, that it is an honest mistake.

After you have got down to gross profit, you analyze the deductions one by one. You see whether each item is a proper deduction, and whether it is this year's deduction, and whether it is this taxpayer's deduction. This is not as hard as it seems, but it must be done. It takes time. Somebody has to pay for your time. This client, however, may not do it.

Many things turn up in such an investigation. For instance, in repairs there's an item of $1,000.00 for "patching the roof" on the main mill. There's also an item of $1,000.00 for "patching the roof" on the steam plant. Investigation shows that the main mill was a big building, and that $1,000.00 was really the cost of patching the roof. Investigation also shows that the steam plant was a small building, and that the $1,000.00 was really a new roof. One item is an expense; the other is a capital charge to be depreciated over a number of years. And so it goes, all the way through the accounts. This, too, is slow. But doing the job always produces results. Doing it will prove you to be a good tax man. 44

When you come to officers' salaries, you make a schedule showing the officers' salaries over the past ten years, and you compare them to sales and to profits for each year. You also make some inquiry as to what the officers really do, and you reach a conclusion, in your own mind, as to whether they earn that salary.

The Government's theory that officers' salaries should follow profits is not entirely sound. If there is a general picture of profit, with a few lean years, the officers who made a business pay a profit over the long period of time should not have to take a cut in salary just because a bad year came along. They probably worked twice as hard in the bad year as they worked in the good ones. On the other hand, a concern that doesn't do very well year after year should not pay high officers' salaries, even in an occasional good year. The officers just aren't worth big salaries.

In arriving at a conclusion on officers' salaries, you should not allow yourself to be fooled by the fact that an officer drawing a big salary wears a pink corduroy coat with a gardenia in his buttonhole, or that he raises orchids in a greenhouse behind the tool shed, or that he sits all day in his office with his feet on the desk, apparently doing nothing. A man who can so organize a plant that he can do these things has got to be good. 

Compare him, if you will, with the officer in the plant where everybody in sight is rushing around screaming, busier than three bees, waving blueprints to the right and to the left. The officer in such a factory has his coat off and his sleeves rolled up. 

He, too, is tearing around, screaming and waving blueprints, perspiration rolling off his chin. You can have him if you want him; I'll take the man who is raising orchids behind the tool shed of a smooth-running plant, where nobody's excited, and where the only too busy place is the shipping department. 

It is not the custom for lawyers doing tax work to do any of the things which I have just told you about. All of these things take time. You cannot do the things which I have just told you to do in a plant of normal size in less than a week or ten days hard work, and if you do that hard work, you have to be paid. 

It's hard to collect. Do it anyway. If you can't collect, charge it to experience. You will win in the end. For in the end, the results of the case will show whether you did this work or not. 

If you don't do this work, you may not have any occasion to regret that you didn't do it until the decision in the case comes 45

down. You'll regret it then. If you lack the skill to do it, you ought not to attempt to do tax work.
