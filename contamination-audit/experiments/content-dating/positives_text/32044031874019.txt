The Comptroller General: 

There is attached a report of investigation of the sale of Governmentowned timber in the Pacific Northwest during the calendar years 1947-1951, by the Forest Service, Department of Agriculture, and the Bureau of Land 

Management, Department of the Interior. 

The summary section of the report is a resume of the points developed by the investigation. In brief, all tracts of timber offered for sale by the agencies are, with one exception, open to competitive bidding. However, the following elements have a distinctly adverse bearing on the degree of effective competition for each individual tract of timber offered for sale and on the net amount of revenue accruing to the Treasury: (a) "gentlemen's agreements" among operators to restrict competitive bidding, (b) private control, through private ownership of contiguous land and access roads, (c) administrative policies of the agencies with regard to the method of acquiring access roads, (d) timber selling practices of the agencies, and (e) the effect of the application of the capital gain and loss provision of the Internal Revenue Code (26 U.S.C. 117(k)). 

It is believed the basic factors underlying the application of the elements resulting in inequities and losses to the Government might be controlled and markedly corrected principally by (1) authorizing the financing and construction of all main access roads by the agencies themselves, rather than by the purchasers through an allowance in the timber sales price, and (2) reappraisal of Section 117(k) of the Internal Revenue 

Code. 

The field drafts of the reports have been considered by the respective agencies, who have cooperated fully with the investigation and have already undertaken steps to improve certain matters brought out. Appropriate consideration by the Congress would appear to be in order.

II

SUMMARY OF REPORT 

Investigation has been made of the sale of Government-owned timber by the Forest Service, Region 6, and the Bureau of Land Management, Region 1, during the calendar years 1947-1951. The 

Forest Service, Region 6, administers the national forest lands in the States of Oregon and Washington excepting that section in northeastern Washington under the administrative jurisdiction of the Regional Office, Missoula, Montana. The Bureau of Land 

Management, Region 1, administers the public domain lands in the States of Oregon, Idaho, and Washington and the revested and reconveyed Oregon and California Railroad and Coos Bay Wagon Road grant lands in the State of Oregon.

All tracts of timber offered for sale by the agencies are open to competitive bidding with the exception of that timber in the Shelton Cooperative Sustained Yield Unit. The cooperator in this unit is privileged by agreement authorized by law to purchase all offerings of national forest timber within the unit at the 

Forest Service appraised price.

While competitive bidding is sought by both agencies, it is restricted by administrative regulations promulgated in accordance with legal authority by the Forest Service in two areas (Federal Units) in region 6 to prospective purchasers within the boundaries of the units, and it is restricted by the Bureau of Land Management in O&C timber sales to purchasers within the administrative units classed as "marketing areas" to only those purchasers within the respective marketing area in which they are located. 

In addition to administrative jurisdiction affecting the degree of competitive bidding, there are additional factors which must necessarily be considered collectively in their effect on the degree of competition for each individual tract of timber offered for sale, namely: (1) increased utilization of the timber resource by the more advanced manufacturing plants, (2) the effect of the application of the capital gain and loss provisions of Section 117 (k), Internal Revenue Code, (3) agreements among operators to restrict competitive bidding, (4) private control through private ownership of contiguous land and access roads, (5) administrative policies of the agencies with regard to the method of acquiring access roads, and (6) timber selling practices of the agencies. 

The most important factor affecting the degree of competitive bidding, where the timber is sold on the competitive bid basis, is

access roads. At the present time, the major portion of access roads is constructed by the purchasers of Government-owned timber and an allowance therefor is made in the appraisal calculation. 

The unit road allowance is based on the cruised volume of standing timber applied to the estimated cost of the road construction, and the total actual allowance to the operator varies with the ratio of timber actually removed from the sale area to the estimated (cruised) volume of timber. Based on approximately one third of the volume of national forest timber sold during the period and considering only the appraised cost of access road construction, the additional allowance to the purchasers, or loss to the Government, was in excess of $1.5 million because the volume of timber cut exceeded the estimated (cruised) volume. Based on 6.4% of the cruised volume of Bureau of Land Management timber sold during the period, the additional allowance to the purchaser, or loss to the Government, was in excess of $83,000. 

In two drainage areas in the national forests the main access roads were constructed with appropriated funds. This made it possible to sell the timber in smaller sales and increased the competitive bidding for the timber. Receipts from sales of approximately 6% of the timber in these two drainages are in excess of $6.2 million which the Forest Service estimates is $3 million more than would have been received had the main access roads been constructed by the purchaser(s) of the timber. This $3 million practically liquidates the Government's basic investment in the two roads. Government construction of access roads makes it more practicable to salvage current losses due to blowdown, fire, or insects and diseases. Also, it provides more operators with a supply of timber and higher stumpage prices to the Government.

Both agencies are using a conversion return method of appraising the standing timber to compute the stumpage value which is the advertised offering price. The appraised stumpage value is considered by the agencies to be the fair market value. It might be more or less than the fair market value determined by the prospective purchasers. The fair market value of the timber determined by the purchasers is reflected in the degree of competitive bidding which is present in the sale of each tract of timber. 

The average bid value of all national forest timber advertised and sold during the period was 35.3% more than the appraised value and on Bureau of Land Management timber was 23.2% more than the appraised value. Some of the timber was sold during the period at the appraised price and some of the timber was sold at bid prices ranging to approximately three times the appraised price. 

IV

The Forest Service sells national forest timber on the basis of the cruised volume but payment therefor is received at the unit bid price applied to the scaled volume actually removed from the sale area. The Bureau of Land Management sells timber on the basis of the cruised volume and the lump sum bid value is the total amount received, without adjustment. Based on the bid stumpage value for each sale on approximately 9% of the total volume of Bureau of Land Management timber sold during the period, a loss in revenue in excess of $625,000 is indicated because the sales were made on the basis of the cruised volume without the benefit of a log scale.

Matters covered by this report have been discussed with Forest Service and Bureau of Land Management representatives at both the Washington office and the regional office level. 

The Forest Service representatives advised that (1) an audit of operators' stated road costs on the Shelton Cooperative Sustained Yield Unit and the Oregon Central Military Road would be made at an early date, (2) they are definitely going to discontinue issuing special use permits for road construction (prior to sale) which develops only national forest timber, (3) they are giving consideration to a change in their appraisal procedure to insure that operators will not in the future receive an indicated duplicated road construction allowance, (4) they would intensify checks to determine accuracy of estimated grade recovery used in appraisals, (5) consideration would be given to a revision of contract wording to require reappraisals if a fixed percentage of change is indicated, and (6) they have issued new control procedures to insure that all timber removed from the sale area is scaled.

The Bureau of Land Management representatives advised that (1) scaling of sample areas would be intensified to comply with the regulations, and (2) they will continue study of the appraisal system to improve present methods,

SCOPE OF INVESTIGATION 

This investigation included an examination of the advertised timber sales during the calendar years 1947-1951. Size of sales and appraised and bid values were documented on 1,224 Forest Service sales and 912 BIM sales. Practices with respect to advertising and sales layout were analyzed as to the effect of the various factors influencing competition. Consideration was given to the effect on competition of contiguous and intermingled private holdings, the establishment of Federal sustained yield units, cooperative sustained yield units, marketing areas, private roads, road right-ofway policies, checks established by the Forest Service to insure that all timber removed from the sale area is scaled, and checks adopted by the BLM to insure that the sales method results in full payment to the Government for the timber in the sale area. Also, consideration was given to the effect of the advantage to some operators through the application of the capital gains tax authorized by Section 117(k) of the Internal Revenue Code. 

Administrative policies and procedures were reviewed at the field level and discussed at the Washington offices and the regional offices Portland, Oregon. Basic facts were documented and analyzed at the regional offices, 19 national forest offices, 16 national forest ranger districts, and three BIM district offices. 

Various items involving specific sales were discussed with purchasers of timber from the Government. 6-

TIMBER UTILIZATION 

Complete utilization of the timber resources is not practical because of the high cost of cutting the timber and delivering the logs to the mill. For this reason "chunks", broken logs, and snags are left in the forest to decay. This is happening today although present processes of the more advanced lumber mills utilize every portion of the log once it is delivered to the mill site. It has been stated that in addition to products such as lumber, hardboard, and paper products, it is possible to extract on a commercial basis, molasses for livestock feed, wax closely resembling beeswax, drugs, vanillin, wood sugar, ethyl alcohol, and many other items. 

Thirty years ago only the desired species of timber were harvested. Of the desired species only the most desirable logs in each tree were removed from the forest. 

Since World War II, logging operations have been carried on in Oregon and Washington in burned-over Douglas Fir areas in which much of the timber has been dead and down for as long as 35 years. 

Relogging operations are being carried on in previously cut-over areas. It was recently reported that a giant Douglas Fir which had blown down 25 years ago was salvaged and sold to the mill for $750. -7-

CAPITAL GAINS AND LOSSES 

It is generally conceded that the application of Section 117(k) of the Internal Revenue Code greatly influences the competitive bidding for Federal timber. Two main reasons for this added competition are readily apparent: (1) a high bid for Federal timber influences the establishment of the base from which to compute the capital gain on private-owned timber in the area, and (2) a high bid on small sales of Federal timber establishes a base from which to compute the capital gain on other Federal timber purchased under a long term sale. 

The only requirement for an operator to claim the benefits under Section 117(k) is that the timber was owned or held under contract for six months prior to the beginning of the taxable year and that an election has been made to take advantage of its provisions. 

At the hearings prior to the passage of Section 117(k) in 1943, it was pointed out that its benefits would apply equally to large and small timber operators. This is true where both the large and small operators own their timberlands and if Section 117(k) applied only to private-owned timber, in which case its benefits are available in the same ratio as the ownership and cutting of timberlands, but it does not bestow the same benefits on large and small operators who do not own the timberlands. The average small or medium-sized operators purchase timber in quantities which can be removed from the sale area and converted into the finished product within a relatively short time because they do not have the capital to finance a long term operation. The small or medium-sized operators generally compete among themselves for Federal timber, and while their competitive bidding assists in forming the basis on which the large operators compute capital gain on Federal timber purchased under long term contracts, they do not receive the benefits of Section 117(k) because their sales generally do not carry over from one year to the next if they have been in effect more than six months prior to commencement of the taxable year, at which time the election must be made. However, the large operators will have several sales of Federal timber under contract at the same time and most of them will extend over a sufficient period of time to enable them to apply the provisions of Section 117(k) to nearly all Federal timber processed.

AGREEMENTS AMONG TIMBER PURCHASERS TO RESTRICT COMPETITIVE BIDDING 

Representatives of the Forest Service and the BLM stated that they are generally aware that agreements exist between certain operators, often referred to as a "gentlemen's agreement", whereby the operators will refrain from purchasing Government timber outside of their respective areas. They stated that this condition is constantly in review in planning their sales program. An example of this practice is set forth in a recent civil action for damages in the amount of $273,000 for an alleged breach of such a contract. 

The plaintiff alleged that there was in existence an agreement whereby the parties in the action would not compete with one another for the purchase of a particular tract of BIM timber but that the defendant did so compete and bid $428,136.50 on timber appraised for sale by BIM at $314,810.25.

PRIVATE CONTROL OF ROADS AND LANDS AFFECTING COMPETITION 

Competition for the purchase of Government-owned timber varies by areas, not primarily because of the quality of the timber but to a great extent due to the control exercised by private operators of roads and contiguous or interspersed private-owned timber. 

Some of this private control can be directly attributed to an administrative policy of the Forest Service whereby a large tract of Federal timber is sold in one transaction to one operator for constructing and operating necessary transportation and manufacturing facilities. As a result of this policy, it is difficult to foresee any appreciable degree of competition in these areas in the future.

For example, in the 1920's the Forest Service in a single sale opened up a large tract of timber, over 800,000,000 board feet. In computing the appraised value of the timber, it allowed in excess of $2 million for the construction of a lumber mill, 50 miles of railroad, and purchase of railroad equipment. Due to this transaction the present operator enjoys an advantage of purchasing additional timber at the advertised price, with only minor exceptions, and the new appraisals do not take into consideration that the capital expenditure was in large part returned to the operator on the original sale, 1/

It appears that a practical solution to obtaining competition in this area is for the Government to build main access roads from the sale area to connect with existing public roads to processing centers.

ACCESS ROADS 

The standing timber on the national forests and the public domain is valueless, except for scenic and watershed purposes, without its access to processing areas. 

Both the Forest Service and the BLM require the purchasers of Federal timber to construct and/or maintain the roads necessary for access to most of the timber with control of the roads in the selling agency after completion of the contract. An allowance is made in the appraisal of the standing timber for both maintenance and construction of the access roads. It is reflected as a reduc tion of the stumpage price.

In some quarters it is believed that the purchasers should retain control to some extent after the contract period. The 

Justification of this was expressed in a recent article appearing in the WOOD INDUSTRIES, Weekly, titled, "Who Really Pays For It? 1/ 

It was expressed that when a sawmill man has bought timber to which he has to build a road, that road is essentially a part of his equipment comparable to the sawmill or planer owned by him. It has a first cost and a maintenance cost, like an axe or a logging truck. He amortizes it on the basis of the quantity of manufactured products he expects to sell, not on the quantity of timber he has to buy as the raw material of those products. 

There is a difference of opinion within the industry as to a feasible means to resolve the contentions. One side favors the present policy and the other proposes that access roads be con structed by the Government from appropriated funds. The Forest 

Service and BLM have not committed themselves to a preference but are continuing to require the purchasers of Federal timber to construct the major portion of access roads. 

Numerous bills have been introduced in the Congress to authorize a program for the construction of access roads from appropriations.2/ 

There are shown hereafter some of the results of the requirement that purchasers of Federal timber provide the access roads as a condition of their timber purchase contracts together with the general policies being followed by the Forest Service and the BIM with respect to the access road problem.

Rights-of-Way and Rights-of-Use 

The Act of June 4, 1897, 1/ provides that nothing therein shall be construed as prohibiting the ingress or egress of actual settlers residing within the boundaries of the national forests, or from crossing the same to and from their property or homes. The Forest 

Service follows the policy that the Act does not deny anyone, particularly timber operators with contiguous or intermingled holdings, the right of ingress or egress over national forest lands. Generally, the Forest Service attempts to obtain reciprocal rights-of-way. 

However, this is not always attained without considerable consideration granted to the operator. The operators are very reluctant to relinquish rights-of-way over their lands and thereby give up an advantage on certain tracts of national forest timber. At the same time the Forest Service is rather liberal with the consideration which it grants for these rights-of-way through overcuts which result in allowances in excess of the amount agreed to. As an example, one operator constructed four miles of road over intermingled private holdings to tap his own and national forest timber at a cost of $60,000, over which an estimated 40,000 MEM of private timber and an equal amount of national forest timber had been logged. In 1950 the Forest Service decided it should sell an additional 12,000 MBM of national forest timber tapped by this road. The road owner agreed

to grant right-of-way over the road, upon condition that if he was not the successful bidder, he would require $30,000 for the rightof-way.

The right-of-way cost was allowed in the appraisal in accordance with the road owner's terms at $2.77 per MBM on all species except hemlock or 10,800 MBM. As was expected, the owner of the road was awarded the timber sale but instead of the $30,000 which he had demanded, he had, as of December 31, 1951, received $35,029 by reason of a permitted overcut. On March 6, 1951, the

Forest Service received an easement deed for the road. 

If the operators do not wish to grant rights-of-way over their holdings, a right-of-use is negotiated for the particular sale. 

This requires the successful bidder to pay a toll or a tariff for crossing the land or road. If the successful bidder is the owner of the land or road and there is no payment of the tariff, it is still considered as an allowance in the appraisal calculation. 

Such a policy further restricts competition because as a general rule it is left to the successful bidder to negotiate an agreeable toll with the owner of the land over which the timber must be logged. Wherever a right-of-way or a right-of-use is involved, an allowance therefor is made in the appraisal except in rare instances where the negotiated cost is minor and the payment therefor is made by the Forest Service from appropriated funds. 

Operator Construction of Access Roads 

Timber sale contracts generally require that the purchaser improve and/or construct roads and other developments used in the protection and management of the national forest. 

The contracts are generally vague with respect to the road requirements and never state an amount for road costs, which is merged in the selling price of the timber. In one instance it was found that the road location was not even decided upon prior to the sale although an allowance in excess of $140,000 was made therefor in the appraisal. Immediately after the sale was awarded the purchaser complained that he would "go broke" building such a road at the price he was paying for the timber, which incidentally was the appraised price, and submitted his estimated costs. Within 

10 months of the date of the sale, a reappraisal was made and an additional amount of $114,000 was allowed for road construction costs. It is not unusual for road construction costs allowed in appraisals to be 50% more in reappraisals. While no audit is made to verify the operators' stated costs, it was explained that they are reviewed to determine that they are not too far out of line with experienced costs within the area. Such a procedure makes it extremely desirable for the operators to obtain reappraisal clauses in their contracts. -13-

TIMBER SELLING PRACTICES 

The Forest Service and the Bureau of Land Management use two methods of selling timber. The Forest Service appraises and advertises practically all of their sales on the basis of an estimated volume (cruise) and makes no guarantee that their estimate is correct, but payment for the timber is received at the contract rate applied to the scaled volume cut and removed from the sale area. The BLM appraises and advertises all of their sales on the basis of the estimated volume (cruise) and makes no guarantee that their estimate is correct, but payment is received at the contract rate applied to the estimated volume. 

One other major difference in the timber selling practices of the two agencies is that the Forest Service awards timber sale contracts with the cutting period extending over a period of several years and providing for periodic reappraisals. The BLM has adopted the general policy of limiting their sales to small volumes with a cutting period not exceeding two years. 

The Forest Service contends that their large sales are necessary to provide sufficient timber to defray necessary development (road) costs.

The BLM policy of selling a tract of timber on the basis of the cruised volume, without benefit of a scale measurement after cutting, has often been the subject of controversy. It has been contended that the cruise method is sufficiently accurate to protect the interests of the Government although occasional instances have been found in small sales where actual volume cut exceeded the volume sold by more than 200% and unverified statements have been heard concerning overruns of 300% and more.

The policy of selling timber on the total estimated volume in a given tract has been defended by the BLM 1/ on the grounds that the checkerboard pattern of land control (intermingled private and public ownership), scattered throughout three states on O&C and public domain lands, would necessitate the hiring of more scalers than would be economically feasible. Also, that inasmuch as cutting of adjacent private timber is often carried on simultaneously with a Government sale, it would necessitate the presence of a scaler at the sale location rather than at a central point where scaling could be performed on more than one sale. 

Neither the log scale or cruise method of selling timber is an exact science since they are both dependent on the application of the personal judgment of the scaler or cruiser to a measurement formula. 

1/ BLM, Region I, Procedures letter, dated November 3, 1950. -29-

The BLM contends that cruise sales promote better utilization because a purchaser pays for all timber in the sale area and is "interested in doing the best job possible in falling and logging in order to obtain the highest recovery. In a scale sale the man pays for what he actually takes off, and there is usually evidence on scale sales that the upper portion of the tree which contains low-grade logs is deliberately smashed in the felling operation so that it will not have to be removed and paid for at the regular contract price. In other words, scale sales tend toward high grading as you pay for what you take out and on an average price, not a graded price." 1/

The results of the examination of advertised timber sales by both agencies during the calendar years 1947-1951 are set forth hereafter.

Forest Service 

The first action in a timber sale is that an operator asks that a particular tract of timber be advertised for sale or else the tracts of timber are offered for sale in accordance with the forest plan of disposal. Generally the two methods will work together. Cruising

After a sale area is agreed upon, the district ranger or the timber sale officer effects a cruise of the area to determine the volume of merchantable timber. Generally a cruise consists of a partial estimate of the standing timber in the area and the figures obtained applied to the entire area. As the size of the sale areas diminish the percentage of cruise intensifies until a total estimate or 100 per cent cruise is made of areas less than 40 acres. 

While this is the generally accepted method of cruising standing timber for sale purposes, the volume of standing timber in many of the current sales is estimated by applying to the current sale area, area cruise figures obtained many years previously. In some few sales it was stated that the estimated volume was arrived at by scanning the area from a vantage point. These practices result in large overruns and an occasional underrun of the actual volume of timber removed from the area. For example on contract A6fs 15366 the advertised volume of timber was 105,200,000 board feet. The latest reappraisal estimates the volume at 142,020,000 board feet. 

On contract A6fs 15367 the advertised volume was 67,245,000 board feet and the latest reappraisal estimates the volume at 94,143,000 board feet.

Advertising 

The Act of June 4, 1897, 1/ requires that prior to the sale of timber notice thereof be given for not less than 30 days by publication in one or more newspapers but provides that sales of timber, cord wood, and forest products not exceeding $500 in appraised value 2/ may be sold without advertising. The Forest Service does not require advertising where the sale is classed as "administrative use" or "timber settlement". 3/ "Administrative use" sales are defined as sales of the residual timber resulting from improvements, fuel, investigative use by the Government, and for use in relief work conducted by public agencies and such sales by regional foresters are limited in volume to 5,000,000 feet in any one transaction. "Administrative use" sales are also defined to include sales for protection, stand improvement, and investigations primarily for benefit of the timber stand. Under this interpretation there is the same limitation as to the volume or value of the timber which may be sold as is applicable to regular advertised timber sales. "Timber settlement" sales are generally associated with rightsof-way for the construction of roads, power transmission lines, canals, etc., and there is no limitation in the regulation as to either the volume or the value of timber which may be sold. Appraisals

The authorization for selling national forest timber 1/ provides that it shall not be sold for less than the appraised value. In arriving at the appraised value of the timber two different methods are employed in Region 6: 

1. In the pine region (east side of Cascade Mountains) the appraisal starts with the selling price of the finished product and deducts logging, milling, and selling costs and an allowance for profit and risk to arrive at the appraised stumpage value. 2.

In the Douglas fir region (west side of Cascade Mountains) the Forest Service maintains that the re is a competitive log market and bases its sales on the log prices quoted by the industry from which logging costs and an allowance for profit and risk are deducted to arrive at the stumpage value.

The first step in arriving at the appraised value is to estimate the grade recovery for each specie of timber and on the basis of the estimated grade recovery determine the weighted average selling value. Of the 10 national forest offices in the 

Douglas Fir region that were visited during this examination, five were questioned as to their practice of determining the estimated grade recovery and it was found that only one had consistently checked the grade estimates used in the appraisals with the actual total recoveries. First checks, initiated by this forest office in 1944, showed variances up to 44 per cent, but with the application of the actual grade recovery to specific areas, recent checks indicate only a minor variance. Practically all of the forests have made mill studies to check mill recovery but this only covers a small portion of entire sale volume involved and does not appear to be an adequate che ck to determine the actual recovery from the entire sale. 1/

After the weighted average selling price is determined, selling, milling costs, and logging costs are deducted to determine the conversion return. A profit and risk allowance usually between 

10 and 15 per cent of the weighted average selling price is deducted from the conversion return to arrive at the stumpage value. An estimate is made of the amount required for KV (cooperative deposit required to be made by the purchaser for sale area betterment and authorized by the Knutson-Vandenberg Act 2/) and this is deducted from the stumpage value to determine the advertised appraised value of the stumpage. It is not uncommon for the profit and risk allowance to the operator to exceed the appraised stumpage value. 

The regional office furnishes the forests average milling and logging costs for their particular areas. The cost figures are obtained from time studies and from costs submitted by the purchasers. Notwithstanding the fact that the costs used in arriving at the appraised value of the timber are for the most part based on the operators' costs submitted, no audit has been made to determine their accuracy.

The regional forester stated that appraising timber is not an exact science, and, of necessity, the judgment of the appraiser must be heavily relied upon.

Reappraisals.

It is the policy of the Forest Service to specify in its timber sale contracts the period during which the timber must be cut and removed. On the smaller and average size sales this is generally one or two years after execution of the contract. Generally, if all timber in the area is not removed at the expiration of the contract period, an extension is granted for sundry stated reasons even though more timber has already been removed than was estimated, and stated in the contract. On the larger sales extending over a period of five years or more, the timber sale contracts provide contracts provide for reappraisals at specified dates after a minimum cut has been scaled It has been repeatedly stated that a reappraisal clause in a timber sale contract influences the competitive bidding, and that this is particularly true where the sale is in an area which an established operator regards as his exclusive territory and desires to restrain a competitor from entering. Further, a reappraisal clause in a contract in effect eli inates the monetary risk generally associated with private enterprise because the purchaser is well aware that the Forest Service apprai sals and reappraisals are conservative and that any costs which he may submit to influence the reappraisal will be given consideration in negotiating the reappraised timber value. 1/

No particular trend is perceivable in reappraisals. In one reappraisal the regional office interceded with the forest office and reappraised the timber at less than the forest's recommended reappraised price because it was felt that the purchaser on location was at a distinct disadvantage, in that he has no choice as to whether he will or will not cut the remaining timber. 2/

A reappraisal is in effect a negotiation with the purchaser at intervals stated in the timber sale contract or at the time of extending the contract for the purpose of adjusting the contract stumpage price. It does not necessarily reflect experienced selling values or costs but appears rather to be a device to insure that the operator will receive a reasonable profit for purchasing Federal timber. An example of such a negotiation is borne out. in a sale contracted in 1942 and on which periodic reappraisals have been "negotiated" at times specified in the contract. In 1950 the

Federal and Cooperative Sustained Yield Units

The national forests are managed on the theory of sustained yield. This theory specifies that the annual cut of timber will be limited to insure an available supply of timber for the future needs of citizens of the United States. The present annual cut of national forest timber is approximately 4 billion board feet. The maximum annual cut desired under sustained yield management is approximately six billion board feet. Sustained yield management of the national forests has been practiced in the past and will continue to be practiced in the future.

The Sustained Yield Management Act was passed by the Congress in 1944. 1/ This act, although containing features of sustained yield management, undertook the granting of a certain monoply in order to attain its goal. The Forest Service contends that its application has at the present time been extended to such a small area that it is inconsequential as an overall factor affecting competitive bidding for national forest 

1/Act of March 29, 1944 (16 USC 583-583i) -41-

timber. There is, however, the possibility that its application could be extended to cover the entire national forest area. The Act provides two methods of attaining its goal; (1) the establishment of a "Federal Unit", and (2) the establishment of a "Cooperative Sustained Yield Unit." The effect on competitive bidding of the establishment of each of the two types of units is set forth briefly hereafter.

There are in Region 6 of the Forest Service two Federal sustained yield units, Lakeview, Oregon, and Grays Harbor, Washington, and one cooperative sustained yield unit, Shelton, Washington, established under the Sustained Yield Management Act. 1/ The 

Shelton unit is the only unit established thus far which involves both national forest lands and private lands. All other units involve only national forest lands. 

The primary purposes of establishing Federal sustained yield units as stated in the Act are: (1) to stabilize the communities, forest industries, employment, and taxable forest wealth, (2) to insure a continuous and ample supply of timber and other forest products, (3) to secure the benefits of forests in regulation of water supply and stream flow, prevention of soil erosion, amelioration of climate, and preservation of wildlife. Aside from the stated objectives of the Act, the establishment of the Federal unit appears to grant a monopoly of large tracts of the national forest to certain operators by limiting the area in which primary manufacture of the logs may be accomplished and thereby eliminating competitive bidding for the timber by prospective purchasers outside of the stated manufacturing area. The establishment of the cooperative sustained yield unit directly confers to the cooperator the right to purchase national forest timber in the unit at the appraised price without competitive bidding.

The Forest Service has on some occasions stifled possible competition on sales of timber by restricting the manufacturing area prior to the establishment of a Federal unit. In one instance, the manufacturing area was limited to an area within five miles of a sawmill town. In another instance, the manufacturing area was limited to that area of a particular valley above the junction of two rivers which effectively limited the sale to only one possible purchaser. It was stated that this authority to so limit the manufacturing area is contained in the broad authority granted in the Act of June 4, 1897, as amended, 2/ but that this practice has been discontinued since May 1948.

It is conceded by the proponents as well as the opponents of Federal units and cooperative sustained yield units that units

established under the Act grant a monopoly to those favored at the expense of others in the industry as well as the general public not receiving the direct benefit. The proponents argue that the monopoly granted is not a complete monopoly since it does not control the market but only the raw material. 1/

It is a generally recognized fact that it was the growing monopoly of the raw material which prompted the farsignted conservationists of fifty years ago to establish the forest reserves for the benefit of the citizens of the United States and curtail further growth of the monopoly and exploitation of the natural resources. 1/ 

Gifford Pinchot, first Chief of the Forest Service and recognized as one of the greatest conservationists of the era, in his book "Breaking New Ground" said: "Since conservation's objective is the ownership, control, development, processing, distribution and use of the natural resources for the benefit of the people, it is by its very nature the antithesis of monopoly *** Monopoly of resources which prevents, limits or destroys equality of opportunity is one of the most effective of all ways to control and limit human rights, especially the right of self government. *** A moral and intellectual price, a price in knowledge and understanding, in education, in degradation of standards, and in limited freedom of thought and action, must be paid also. Here may well be the heaviest cost of all." 2/ 

The Lakeview Federal unit was established October 10, 1950, and permits an annual cut of 50,000 MBM of timber which may be averaged over a ten-year period. The limits of manufacture are the community of Lakeview, Oregon, which is defined as the town and adjacent area within a radius of six miles of the Lake County courthouse, and the community of Paisley and the adjacent area within three miles of the center of the town. The manufacturing limits established effectively limit the purchase of Federal timber to three operators in Lakeview and one in Paisley. The unit is further divided into four blocks or tracts and each operator has stayed very closely within the confines of the block in which he has started operations and has purchased all national forest timber for sale at the advertised price with the exception of one sale.

Scaling Logs are scaled at stated locations by Forest Service personnel at Forest Service expense or at the option of the purchaser, at his expense by a local scaling bureau. 

The Forest Service has not exercised an internal check to determine that all timber removed from the sale area is scaled and paid for. It is generally the district ranger's responsibility to lay out the sale area and assemble the data necessary to appraise the timber. 1/ Both are reviewed by the forest office and if the volume of timber is greater than their authority to sell, review is made by the regional office. Either the forest supervisor, regional forester, or the Chief, Forest Service, depending on whether the volume is within their authority to contract, signs the contract on behalf of the Government. After the contract is approved, the administration of the sale is the responsibility of the district ranger. He establishes such controls as he deems necessary to insure that all timber removed from the sale area is paid for. Neither the regional fiscal office nor the administrative assistant on the forest in their periodic inspections has included such an item to verify, although the timber sale record cards and the scale sheets are audited to determine the correctness of contract prices and extensions. Sale areas are at the present time planned one year in advance in accordance with a predetermined plan of disposal taking into consideration requests for specific sales by prospective purchasers. 2/ After the sale area is determined, the cutting areas or settings are laid out and marked. Generally, in the 

Douglas Fir region the settings involve an area of 40 acres or less although a few sales have involved a considerably larger area. In the pine region selective cutting is practiced and each tree to be cut is marked, whereas in the Douglas Fir region all trees within the setting are cut. Cruising

In settings of less than 40 acres a 100% cruise is made and the lumber recovery for each tree is estimated and tallied. 3/ 

For settings larger than 40 acres a percentage or strip cruise is made, usually covering 20% to 40% of the total area, and applied to the entire area. In some larger areas where the stand is not consistent and of high value, a 100% check is made of all trees to be cut.

The judgment of the cruiser is of primary importance since he must estimate the lumber recovery making allowances for breakage and defect. The volume is determined in the pine region on the basis of the Scribner Decimal C Log Rule with a maximum log length of 16 feet and in the Douglas Fir region on the basis of the Scribner Decimal C Log Rule with a maximum log length of 32 feet (two 16-foot logs to reduce the allowance for taper). The cruiser estimates the number of logs, 16 feet long in the pine region and 32 feet long in the Douglas Fir region, recoverable from each tree and records the diameter of the tree at breast height together with the grade of the log to be recovered. For example, a tree might contain five or six 32-foot logs to a specified top diameter but he estimates that only two or three of the logs will contain merchantable recovery in lumber volume, the balance will contain rot and defect or will break in the logging operation to such an extent as to render them valueless. 

It was stated that check cruises are made of the cruiser's tallies, but that a check of the cruiser's tally by scaling of logs on sample areas has only been done in a few cases. 1/ Appraisals

After the cruise has been completed, the cruiser's tally of tree scale is converted to lumber recovery by grade for the entire sale area on the basis of past studies to determine lumber recovery for each type of log. Value factors are applied for each grade of lumber estimated to be recovered to arrive at the lumber realization value. Selling, milling, logging, and road costs are deducted from the lumber realization value to arrive at the value of the standing timber, which is referred to as the conversion return. The conversion return is split 50% to the purchaser for profit and risk and 50% to the

Government as stumpage which is the advertised value of the timber, except that on pine sales 60% of the conversion return is the advertised stumpage price.

It has been acknowledged by BLM that the division of the conversion return as shown is not in any manner related to the risk involved or an equitable profit to the operator under the high stumpage values received subsequent to World War II. The amounts paid over the appraised value may be attributed to several factors: 

1. Desire of one operator to keep others out of an area of Government timber which he considers to be for his own operations. 

2. Establishment of a high market value to be used as a basis of computing capital gains tax. 

3. The operator is in need of timber to maintain a milling operation and would be willing to accept only a small profit or no profit on his logging operation. -52-

4. The timber is actually worth much more than its appraised value due either to a greater volume than that which is shown as the cruise or erroneous cost or selling values used in the appraisal. 

5. Scarcity of timber in some areas reflects the desires of purchasers to continue operations notwithstanding a small margin of profit. 

On one sale in 1951, the purchaser paid $401,720 for timber with an appraised value of $120,587.75, 233% over the appraised price. An official of the regional office explained that he believed this was a case of the purchaser desiring to keep other prospective purchasers out of a certain territory but acknowledged that the purchaser probably would not lose any money on the overall operation over a period of time as he had a very efficient mill. Another possible consideration is the application of capital gains tax on contracted Forest Service timber at a lower sale value. 

Many sales at the appraised price may be attributed to marketing areas 1/ restricting the primary manufacture of timber to a limited number of prospective purchasers; road ownership and control, although there may be an agreement providing access to other purchasers at a stipulated amount; and agreements between purchasers to refrain from bidding against each other on the purchase of Federal timber.

1. Accuracy of reports submitted by the operators to the Bureau may be questionable. and the remaining species in the sale entirely omitted from the report. Inasmuch as a cruise is only an estimate of volume, it is highly improbable that the volume cut would not vary to some extent from the cruise. Further, some companies may consider it advantageous to report only a nominal overrun or no overrun when they were actually cutting a much larger volume. This is borne out to some extent by a statement of one operator that it would be to his disadvantage to report to the BLM the actual volume cut when there was a substantial overrun. 2.

BLM was 2,708,586 board feet. It is the practice of the BLM to make a 100% check on smaller sales while the larger ones are often times based on a cruise of 20% to 40% of the total sales area. A relatively small error made in a percentage cruise can result in a substantial overrun or underrun on the entire sale. 

3. Each of the 62 sales compiled by the BLM was, almost without exception, made to different operators while the 65 sales compiled independently of the BLM records were to nine companies and included all sales during the period for which information was available.

The contorted thinking which justifies this procedure stems not from Portland but from Washington. It is founded upon the bureaucratic philosophy which looks at the complicated system of production, distribution and marketing built up the hard way---costly trial and errorby competitive private enterprise, and considers that system automatic in its operation. From that it concludes that the value of any commodity at any point is also automatic and inescapable. 

In timber that bureaucratic philosophy starts by assuming that since a certain tree on a mountain-side, owned by the government, can be sold for cash to a logger who will spend more money on road-building to reach the tree and haul its logs to a mill--therefore the true value of the tree, out there on the mountain, includes the cost of stumpage, falling, bucking, and hauling plus part of the cost of the road. We have encountered this in the Forest Service as well as BIM, and just about every other government agency that seeks to prove that the essential value is in the trees which the government owns. Starting with this comfortable premise they easily conclude that if a road has been built to log off a thousand acres of timber, then the timber on those thousand acres has paid for the road. "The timber pays for the road" has become so ingrained into the thinking of government timber agency policy makers that they assume it was derived from the Ten Commandments. 

Believing thus firmly that the timber pays for the roads, it is an easy next step to their next conclusion, that as administrators of the ownership of the timber they should control the roads giving access to this public timber, regardless of who went through the formality of actually putting up the money to build the roads.

Loggers and lumbermen typically view the situation a mite differently. 

They do not recall ever having been repaid road and logging costs by a tree. 

In their view a tree has no cash value at all, for harvesting, unless and until there is some economical means for bringing its logs to a mill. They believe that the road adds value to the tree. They look to their customers to reimburse them for the money they spent in buying the tree, in getting to it and bringing out its logs, and in converting them into lumber or plywood, or poles or paper. All the way back to the ultimate consumer they look to the customer to pay the cost of everything done up to that point in supplying what he wants. When a sawmill man has bought timber, to which he has to build a road, that road is essentially a part of his equipment, like his sawmill or his planer. It has a first cost and a maintenance cost, like an ax or a logging truck. He amortizes it on the basis of the quantity of manufactured products he expects to sell, not on the quantity of timber he has to buy as the raw material of those products.

Factors to Consider in Determining the Need for Constructing 

Roads with Appropriated Funds. 

First, construction costs on some projects are high and in order to provide an equitable recapture of such road costs during logging operations it is necessary to make extraordinarily large sales. This tends to limit opportunities of bidding on Government timber to fewer and larger operators. In addition, the high rate of cutting dictated under such circumstances tends to restrict the rate of cutting elsewhere in the working circle in order to not exceed the allowable annual cut under sustained yield. This stands to displace established operations elsewhere in the working circle and upset dependent communities. Occasionally we are forced to make large sales in order to secure the construction of these costly roads. 

There are many things which contribute to high construction costs: Sometimes the road must traverse through several miles of non-forested land before timber is reached; sometimes the construction entails expensive rock or bridge work; and sometimes the total amount of timber available in the initial harvest is small in contrast to the total length of road needed. 

Another factor considered is the need to increase the harvest of national forest timber up to the annual allowable cut under sustained yield and to maintain such cut. 

This is important for two reasons: First, installed mill capacity exceeds the timber supply from all sources and the national forest should contribute its full share of resources; second, if the full cut of the national forest. is not harvested each year, it is lost - sustained yield cannot be stored and taken later. The forests now are being cut at the rate of around 1.8 billion board feet per year, which is about 70% of capacity. Lack of timber access roads forms one of the important reasons why only about 45% of the working circles are producing the full allowable cut, but such lack is not the sole reason. In almost every working circle, there are recurring combinations of circumstances other than the lack of access roads which interfere with full production. Some of these circumstances are: Shutdowns due to weather, strikes, lack of markets for less desirable species, railroad car

Quite commonly, as a result of inadequate access roads, we are concentrating our cutting activities in those places where we do have roads. In other words, for lack of a road, we shift cutting in the same working circle to a place where we do have a road. Thus, through repeating this practice over the region, we are cutting up to 70% of capacity, and it might be said that we are accomplishing this amount of harvest by robbing the face of the national forests and by inching our way into the forests with such roads as can be built by operators. 

This situation is the root of many difficulties. Government construction of roads to the back of more of our working circles would put us in a better position to shift the annual harvest over wider areas and capture losses which occur as a result of natural mortality, insect and disease epidemics, and catastrophic blowdowns and fires. We would be in a better position to use winter logging shows as such and help prolong the working season of the logging industry. From the standpoint of fire protection, we would be in a better position to disperse timber harvest and break up the continuity of logging slash hazard. Also, from the standpoint of fire protection, we need better access to the back country to facilitate fire suppression. We need roads to the back portions of our working circles in order to take better advantage of changing markets and thereby work towards a better balance in the harvest of various kinds of tree species. Last year, for example, over 60% of our cut was in ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir. These species are the most desirable ones, but they are the least abundant in the national forests.

Clackamas River Road System 

Prior to 1942 there were no roads suitable for the hauling of timber within the Clackamas River drainage. Such roads as existed had been constructed for forest protection and administration and were too narrow and poorly located for log hauling. In spite of the fact that the Clackamas drainage comprised in round numbers nearly 450 thousand acres and contained approximately 9 billion board feet of national forest timber, practically no timber cutting had been done. The annual sustained yield allowable cut was computed at 125 million board feet per year, and this source of raw material was badly needed by industry to supply material both for the war effort and domestic use. 

During the three-year period of 1941-43 the total cut from the area was but 583 thousand board feet, or an average of 194 thousand board feet per year. A major and costly road construction job was needed before timber cutting could be made possible on any reasonable scale. Access road construction was begun in September 1942 with an allotment of Defense Highway Access Road money and continued later with National Housing Administration funds as well as more recent appropriations of special access funds, with the result that to date 24.8 miles of road have been built with government funds at a cost of $2,362,000. During this same time timber sale purchasers have constructed 59 miles of permanent type road at an estimated cost of $1,027,000. They have in addition constructed many miles of temporary road. 

As of January 1, 1952, approximately 451 million board feet of national forest timber have been cut and hauled over the road system, and to date receipts from sales in the area are in excess of $3,500,000. The cut during the past three years has averaged about 80 million board feet per year.

What Were Alternatives as of 1942? 

The alternatives to the program carried out thus far in this area were (1) to let the watershed remain undeveloped or (2) to have advertised for sale, with a requirement

that the purchaser construct all of the main roads, an excessively large volume of timber. 

Under alternative (1) the important timber resource of this drainage would have remained unavailable and a public need would not have been served. From the financial standpoint, the returns for the timber returned to the 

Treasury-to date would have been lost for all time since under sustained yield the productive capacity of a forest cannot be stored. If alternative (2) had been adopted, a sale of 500 million board feet or more would have been necessary in order to amortize the road cost as a charge against stumpage. Even a volume of 500 million may not have made a sale attractive as of 1942 because at that time the average value of timber, even with the main road constructed with government funds, was only $4.00 per M. 

If the initial sale required main road construction estimated to cost $1,000,000, an allowance of $2.00 per M for road construction cost would have been made in the appraisal, leaving a net of $2.00 per M as the appraised value for stumpage. A transaction of this magnitude would have limited possible purchasers to a very few operators, if any. Therefore, competition would have been unlikely and the sale if consummated probably would have been at the appraised rate.

Summary of Benefits 

Under the plan adopted for construction of the main access road with government funds the Clackamas River drainage has contributed a substantial volume of timber in increasing amounts since 1942. An average of approximately eight sales per year ranging from one to thirty million board feet each have been made. The average appraised value for the period has been $7.00 per M and the average price bid $10.98 per M. Competition has been had on nearly all sales, with an average of two bidders per sale. In contrast to this, if one large sale of 500 million board feet or more had been made in 1942, the initial stumpage price may have been as low as $2.00 per M, since competition would have been unlikely at that time. This price would have been increased in later years through annual reappraisals, but during the entire period the stumpage price received would have been at the appraised rate rather than at a competitive bid rate.

Assuming that the total cut during the eight-year period would have been the same total amount as has been cut, the total loss in revenue to the United States would have been nearly $2,000,000. This loss would have been the result

Greater financial returns as a result of this road construction is not the only benefit that can be claimed. 

Construction with government funds has permitted a number of operators to obtain a supply of timber rather than just one. It has permitted a distribution of cutting in the watershed, which is beneficial from the standpoint of management and would not have been possible if one large sale had been made.

Fall Creek Road System 

The Fall Creek drainage is much smaller than the Clackamas River drainage since it comprises only about 100. thousand acres. It contains more than 4 billion board feet and has a computed annual allowable cut of 50 million board feet. 

Like the Clackamas area, this drainage was completely undeveloped with access roads prior to 1942 and no timber had been cut from the area. Access road construction with government funds was begun in February 1943. The first sales from the area were made in August 1943. To date 13.6 miles of road have been constructed with government funds at a cost of $630,000; 16.2 miles of operator-built roads have been constructed at an estimated cost of $1,086,000. 

Up to January 1, 1952, a total of 331,322,000 board feet of national forest timber have been hauled over the road system. The total stumpage value for timber hauled to date is $2,753,637.

What Were Alternatives as of 1942? 

The alternatives in this case were the same as those indicated for the Clackamas River drainage-either to make no sales or to advertise a sale large enough to amortize the necessary road development. In this case a sale of from 

150 million to 200 million board feet perhaps would have been adequate to amortize the main road by appraisal allowance, but, again, this course of action would have meant one sale with an annual cut of 40 to 50 million per year rather than a number of small sales made each year with a large number of operators benefiting. Whether or not competition for a large sale would have materialized is problematical, of course, but it is certain that the degree of competition would have been materially less since the number of operators who are financially able

to undertake the construction of a road of this nature is relatively small.

Summary, of Benefits 

As a result of the government financed access road construction in the Fall Creek drainage timber was made available to a large number of operators during the war period and since. The average appraised price of timber sold during the period is $7.96 per M and the average price bid $10.86 per M, or an average increase due to competition of $2.90 per M. If the road development had been attained through operator construction necessitating a large initial sale, very little, if any, competition could have been expected. Also, as in the case of the 

Clackamas River example, the contract price would have been very low due to the low level of stumpage prices during 1943 and due to the necessity for charging the cost of the road development against the value of the timber sold. As is customary in long-term sales, stumpage prices would have been reappraised annually, but here again, as pointed out for the Clackamas area, the prices received would necessarily have been those determined by appraisal rather than by competitive bidding. It is entirely reasonable, therefore, to assume that for the timber cut to January 1, 1952, amounting to 331 million board feet, the government would have received approximately $1,000,000 less in revenue.

In addition to providing more operators with a timber supply and returning a greater revenue, government construction of access roads in this case, as in all other situations, makes it more practicable to salvage current losses due to blowdown, fire, or insects and disease.

This is partly because when roads are constructed with government funds there are no restrictions as to the number of operators who may use the road. When losses occur, prompt salvage is possible through sale to the small operators engaged in this type of business who, of course, are not financially able to construct roads as a part of their operation. When roads are constructed by purchasers the same freedom of action in making numerous small sales is not possible because the purchaser who constructs the road has the primary right to its use during the life of his contract.

Date: Nov. 20, 1947 SUBJECT: S- SALES, 

Reference is made to your memorandum of November 14.

Your reappraisal calculation has been reviewed, and while we consider that the prices developed would be entirely equitable if this were an appraisal for a new sale, we believe that some downward adjustment in indicated prices is justified because this is a reappraisal. It is a well established policy that consideration must be given in reappraisals to the fact that the purchaser is under some disadvantage due to the fact that he has already established himself on the sale area and often expended considerable sums in improvements and as a consequence is not in a position of having a free choice of accepting or rejecting the sale. In other words, in reappraisals we do not feel justified in asking the top price that might be developed by an appraisal calculation unless we are absolutely sure that the facts of the case justify it. 

In this case, and likewise in the case of the and reappraisal which we will comment on today, we feel there is some uncertainty with regard to the estimated selling values for both ponderosa pine and fir and larch. With respect to ponderosa pine we note that you have used the full price as indicated by the Western Pine average, whereas in the recent appraisal for your area a downward adjustment was made because your quality analysis indicated a lower than regional average quality. We are not convinced that the average quality for pine on the 

Forest is lower than for the 

Region as a whole, but if the timber is below average, there is a good chance that the same would hold true for the areas covered by your and sale areas. For this reason, and because this is a reappraisal, we recommend using a profit ratio of 16% plus in both calculations. This results in an indicated stumpage price of $9.25, including K.V., for ponderosa pine in this case. For fir and larch we feel that the Regional average price may be too high. Recent price quotations, of course, are even higher than the 

Western Pine reports, but the Western Pine average is based on a production of 10% plus of selects, and we doubt that the fir and larch harvested from the or the sale areas will produce this proportion of high quality lumber. 

We hope that you will be able to check this matter of recovery for use in future appraisals by securing reliable grade recovery data from one or more mills in your territory, but for this appraisal we suggest the use of a profit ratio of 16% in order to allow for this uncertainty. A profit ratio of .16 results in a stumpage price, including K.V., of $3.80 in this case. Our suggested prices are considerably lower than those developed by your calculation, but we believe they fully care for the Government's interest; also we believe they are equitable to the purchaser.
