Luck and Alaska

UCK gives zest to life. Luck is a determining factor, but we cannot account for luck. We cannot trust luck." Luck "Wherever man struggles with his environment, he prays for the favors of the chimerical goddess of luck. The more intense the struggle the more rosy does imagination depict the features of luck."

In a country where boats ply among hidden rocks, deadly reefs and treacherous glaciers; where trails are wearisome, perilous and uncertain, the element of luck is ever in the foreground; and this, like the Sirens of Old, beckons and calls and challenges. There is the ever-present lure of the unexpected. 

Alaska is Nature's toy shop, her playhouse, her retreat for moods and tantrums. She paints with a lavish brush, she builds in magnificent dimensions, and she unlooses her destructive forces with care-free abandon. 

Hyder, "the gate to Alaska", is a location of natural beauty. Nestled at the foot of green-feathered, snowcrowned mountains of indescribable beauty and grandeur, this rugged, unpainted town pushes a 3,000 foot wooden pier into a wonderfully sculptured bay. But its outlook is most alluringly upwards, above and beyond the snow-capped mountains, past scenes comparable to those of the Swiss Alps, to mountains of gold and silver from which $4,000,000 was taken out during the past nine months.

American vessels make the port at irregular intervals. It was a gala day for the quiet little, out-of-the-way town of 

150 inhabitants when the steamer, "Jefferson", landed at the wharf. It seemed that most of the men, women and 1

children in the town were waiting at the pier—and with them were the dogs, the waggish ravens and the persistent hotel porters and hack drivers.

With true American egotism, we accepted the whole show as a compliment to our boat and its distinguished passengers; but our smug vanity was destined to receive a jolt. The English boat, "Prince of Wales", had just arrived with the weekly mail from the States.

We walked across a mile of wooden piers and platforms to the rows of wooden houses that were built after the plan of "straddle bug houses" and the "house boats of the Styx" as described by John Kendrick Bangs, while the large ravencrows were calling a sort of saucy welcome from the mud flats which were fully twelve feet below the board walk. 

On one of these "stilted cottages", there was a sign: "No 

Office Hours Tonight". A little farther along we read, "Bing's Lunch", "Simon's Grill", "C. H. Sundamacher, Lawyer", "Fuzzie's Store", "Golden Gate Hotel" and the "U. S. Postoffice". The postoffice was but a unit of a general store. Here postcards could be had at "3 for 25 cents"; potatoes at $2 a bushel; shoes at $6 a pair; baled hay at $8 a ton; soft collars at "3 for $1"; and brass jewelry at any price that you would pay.

If big talk exaggerated values and gross prevarications could build a town, Hyder would be a city much larger than 

Seattle or San Francisco. Fictitious prices and imaginary gold mines are such common subjects of conversation that some of the citizens must believe the "oft-told" tales. 

There is much intrinsic value below the fermenting surface of "boost", day-dreams and unbridled graft. These foundations of solid fact are reckoned with by a conservative element of the citizenship, and these public-spirited men must carry the heavy burden of lessening the inflation, eliminating 2

the graft and establishing confidence-confidence in a true and lasting valuation, and confidence in the intent and honesty of enough leading citizens to make the town a place where investments are safe and where a person would care to live. 

The president of the Salmon River Bank represents the conservative type. He was ready to tell us of the opportunities and resources of the town, but he was careful to make distinctions between possibilities and facts. 

We walked past the obelisk that marks the boundary line between the United States and Canada; we were in 

Canada but no immigration official met us. We were free to come and go at will. But on the American side, it was different; an official was on duty. Canada welcomes immigrants, while the United States has restrictions. 

The road around the cliff has been blasted out of solid rock. From the rim, two hundred feet above, not less than fifty crows kept up a clatter of friendly nagging. They would answer to "Hello" and "Good-bye", and sometimes to "shut up", but when answering the latter, some old fellow would give an explosive "cawk" or "ca-ak".

Of the eight hundred mines in the district, probably less than a dozen really get out enough ore to pay expenses; the rest are continually buoyed up by "indications" and by man's willingness to barter real money for the uncertainties of luck. At one mine, where three hundred men are employed, they had not taken out a penny's worth of ore for three months; but they were hoping for the "lucky strike". 

There are no degrees of luck—just two kinds: good and bad. Any man who works with his head and with his hands to the best advantage that his ability can comprehend, 3

somehow becomes one of Luck's favorite children, but can "prospecting" miners claim an even chance?

The Premier mine employs about 900 men but no other one of the eight hundred mines employs half that number. 

Since there are less than 2,000 men actually engaged in mining in the district, it is evident that the Premier is the real center of the industry. From this mine the ore is brought over the mountains by the longest single tramway in the world. The distance is ten and one-half miles. It requires forty-four miles of wire cable. The ore, which is smelted in Seattle, is said to run from twenty to thirty dollars a ton.

This district is not especially noted for "pocket" mines, very rich deposits that are soon mined out. The Salmon

River is formed by a glacier which terminates a few miles above Hyder, and which pours a stream of muddy and metal-bearing water into the bay. Were is possible for tourists to reach the top of this glacier by automobile, this town might easily become a resort. It is the border town of Alaska and it is the first town on the western coast that really has a glacier. As the mountains on all sides of the bay are snow-crowned, the scenery is beautiful.

The water of the bay is badly discolored, it is dirty white, like dirty soap suds. This is due to the streams discharged by two glaciers, the pollution caused by the mines and the drainage from the mountains bringing ore-laden water. No doubt the water of this bay contains valuable minerals in solution. The fact that the Premier mine yields both gold and silver would tend to strengthen this supposition; and some day a method may be found for extracting these minerals from the water.

Bears are common in the mountains in this section. Black bears and brown bears are frequently seen along the trails, 4

but to some extent, they have been accustomed to man so that under ordinary conditions they keep a safe distance away. While they are not aggressive, they expect fair treatment. The lineman, who keeps up the telephone line between the mines and the town, related an experience: "I was humping it over the line one day, just hiking for all that I could put out, when I came 'plunk' upon an old brown bear and her two cubs. She raised her head and gave an 'oo-oof', and the cubs shinned up a little tree in no time. The old lady gave just one look to see where they were, then she turned and started for me. But I was already beating it with a few laps lead. Things were terribly interesting, and we were both moving fast. When I came to the first pole, I was right up to the top. I do not know how I got there, but I was there all right. "And the pole was short, too short; I did not know that there was a telephone pole on the whole line that was so short, so terribly short. The old lady came up to the pole, looked at me and growled, then she rushed around the pole and growled some more. "She did not try to climb the pole or to pull it down; she could have done either. But I was rip-roaring glad that a bear has no sense. Well, she did not stay above three minutes, and then she went kind of loping back to her cubs. 

I saw them come down to meet her, but it was over my shoulder, as I was humping it for a bigger tree down the trail. She kind of turned her head sideways, but that was the last I saw of her. I waited about fifteen minutes for her up a good-sized tree, but she did not show up, and I came slipping down and went gingerly about my business. 

But say, I keep my eyes open when I go up in that mountain now-a-days." 5

It is quite probable that if this bear had seen the man in time, she would have taken her cubs out of sight. 

Alaska is a land of glaciers. There are two kinds of glaciers, "dead" and "alive". "Live" glaciers move forward through well-defined channels. Sometimes these channels extend hundreds of miles back from the real face of the glacier. Taku Glacier is "alive", it moves forward. Its rate is about eight feet a year. But this is considered very fast for a glacier, since some of the most famous glaciers do not creep forward more than a foot a year. 

As soon as our boat turned off of the channel into Taku Inlet, we noted that the wind was a little stronger and that it held a well defined "bite". But the sun was so warm, the towering green-clad hills so inspiring, and the regularly-arranged series of snow-capped peaks so beckoning that we were feasting on anticipations.

It is about nine miles up the Inlet to the glaciers. There are three of them. Twin Glacier, Windham Glacier and 

Taku Glacier, the latter termed "the beautiful". 

Twin Glacier and Windham Glacier are "dead", but

Taku is very much "alive". When we were passing the first cakes of floating ice, some of the passengers began taking pictures and the pilot announced, "you need not waste your films, you will see plenty of blocks of ice and icebergs, too, in about thirty minutes."

From a distance of several miles, the glaciers compared favorably to the pictures we had seen, both paintings and photographs; but as we came nearer we became aware of the depth and colorings, of variations and changes, the elements and characteristics that define life. It is an immense force in motion; a rushing river, one hundred feet deep, pours out from under the perpendicular wall of ice which is three hundred feet high and two miles long.

The steamship proceeded carefully among the icebergs. 

These icebergs with their soft, changeable tints of blue, green, purple and old rose, ever presenting a different aspect when viewed at a different angle, majestically floating or slowly whirling around; these cloud-like masses of solid ice, they drew us away from the real wall of icy columns until the boat became almost motionless, then everyone trained their glasses on the upper edge of the glacier, where the afternoon sun was making the sharp points gleam into bayonets and spires and stalagmites, all of glistening silver but with flashings of gold and violet.

And this was the glacier! But there was too much to see all at once; the sweep was too comprehensive, the details too numerous. Here was a column of blue, exquisitely set in a frame of silver, and with a few touches of purple on either side and in exact proportion. 

Another angle would show a series of columns, formed in elegant but enormous proportions, which gave an impression of grandeur too elegant to sketch, too vast to comprehend. Occasionally a section of a column would break loose with many a crackling and shivering boom that would echo and re-echo from the surrounding hills only to be broken by others of more recent origin.

With a sort of smothered roar, this mass would sink into the torrent below, but not before sending splashes of foaming water high into the air.

The Captain asked everyone to be quiet, and, as the "gabblers" continued to "prattle", he called out the stern command, "Keep still or go into the assembly hall." The engines were stopped and the ship floated slowly among the icebergs towards the wall of ice. As it continued to float nearer, some of the passengers became nervous, and this nervousness increased when a large piece of ice came up near

the ship. When the ship came to a standstill, the captain called attention to a large section of ice that seemed to be just ready to break loose.

The deep-toned whistle of the steamboat that echoed and re-echoed above the roar of the water and the cracking of the ice, brought down several chunks and spars of ice, but the very large section did not fall. Three times they whistled for it, but it continued to hang as if "by a thread". 

The immediate presence of this gigantic force of moving ice, of roaring water, of whirling icebergs and of such immense sections of ice hanging ready to fall, produced a rather strange feeling of awe, a sort of littleness or helplessness, akin to that of being on the ocean in a storm, the feeling immediately following an earthquake, or even that helpless, trembly feeling that is so familiar, so unnecessary, and yet so hard to get away from, during a thunder storm. 

A sea lion rose to the surface and swam about a quarter of a mile across a clear strip of water to a large iceberg. 

After surveying it carefully, he went around to the other side to climb out.

Terns and shearwaters and little black-headed gulls circled around among the icebergs, above the glaciers and back towards a little cove where they had their nesting ground. 

They had chosen a picturesque home, but it would have been an easy matter for them to have found a warmer place. 

Slowly the steamer circled a large iceberg, crossed where the water was white with silt and bubbling foam, grazed several large cakes of floating ice and headed down stream. 

We were leaving the glacier, leaving Old Taku's lofty and inspiring columns, leaving the elegance and magnificence that was quite beyond our comprehension, but we were taking with us ideals and memories. Our ideals of beauty were enlarged and simplified, and our impressions of this

wonderful phenomena, continually playing during the lifetime of the oldest human being, impressions that must continue during the remainder of our lives. 

Tramping and mountain-climbing in Alaska presents many variations, but one thing that you need not expect to find is well-beaten trails or anything better than a mere winding path. 

The tundras are soft as the best carpets but you soon tire of walking over them; the blueberries, which furnish the famous "BB cobbler" grow in tangled masses that offer a continual impediment to walking.

Our guide was an "old timer", who knew the trails perfectly, and what was better, he knew how to get through the tangled mass. The hills on either side of the trail were about 6,000 feet high, but these were "just hills" for this section of Alaska. For the first 3,000 feet, they were covered with heavy timber, the next 2,000 feet was scrub timber and brush, and the last 1,000 feet was either bare or covered with snow.

The deep blue face of the glacier marked the vale between the tops of the larger peaks. Near the snow line is the haunt of the grizzly bear. He spends the day next to the glacier, but in the evening, he comes down below timber line to feed.

We pushed through berry thickets and tangled alders, crawled under spruce trees, climbed over logs and fallen tree tops until we reached a fan-shaped patch of big timber. 

Blueberries were plentiful. Almost anywhere there were bushes well loaded with the appetizing dark blueberries. 

Bears show refined taste when they eat blueberries. Then there were salmonberries, the last word in juicy, deliciouslyflavored, pulpy and all-sufficient ambrosial "food of the gods".

Created by the glacier and the gods of Neptune, salmonberries are too frail and delicate to be transported. 

We crossed over the divide and came down towards the fiords. Here we encountered a very abrupt canyon which made climbing difficult. When we attained the divide, the old abandoned mining camp could be seen near the edge of the water.

It is said that in its palmy days, this city of luring prospects and coy golden pockets, once boasted of being the "richest town in the district" and that no less than 5,000 people resided in Gold Rock. But the remains of this city and the extensive operations are but rusted machinery, jungle-like paths, and houses in the last stage of decay. In some instances, good sized trees were growing through the rotten floors and extending above the roofless walls. Evidently the main street had two sharp curves. 

The story of Gold Rock runs like a fairy tale, the story of a typical "pocket mine". Two prospectors were working around a rocky point and by force of habit just happened to try panning a little sand from the bed of a swift glacial stream. They found gold and very naturally followed their lead up the gulch; here they found a "pocket of gold". The ore was so rich that they took out $20,000 worth in three days; but the pocket was empty. After another day of searching, they went out with their ore. 

When the news spread, others came. More "pockets" were found, not so many rich ones, but in several instances, they ran from $5,000 to $10,000. In every instance, the ore was so rich that it could be taken out in a few days and a man could carry all of the worth-while ore in the "pocket", even if some of them did run above $20,000. These deposits might be found either high or low along the sides of the canyon and anybody might be lucky. 10.

Gold Rock was once a busy town. Where the timbers have fallen and have been covered with vine moss, wild sweet peas and trailing beechweed, a saloon once stood. Evidently it was a saloon of splendid proportions, with kegs of whiskey arranged in rows along the bar, each with a spigot in place.

Here the news was told; trades were made; bets were made and paid. Here was the scene of the arguments and brawls, the quarrels and the threats, the fights, and the killings that occur in these new countries. The saloon was something of an institution. Fights and killings occur where men who have worked long and intensively take a respite, a relaxation from the strain. Men who have been through all of this say that it is a real test of a man, to be able to control himself after he is released from the strain. 

Three bats flew away from the old hotel, "Mike's Luck".

And the story of this hotel is typical, in a large way, of the radical changes, the ups and downs, that make "luck and 

Alaska". It seems that Mike was a good-natured Irishman, an impetuous plunger into any kind of a venture, but somehow he seemed to differ from most Irishmen in having streaks of extraordinarily good luck. For a time, he ran a saloon, and prospered. Everybody liked Mike, and the saloon business was better than mining. 

Then he concluded to build a hotel. There was great demand for a hotel and everybody told him that he was just the man. Of course he could run a hotel; the popular saloon keeper could do anything; and they told him so. 

Besides, he had surplus money, a troublesome thing, even for a lucky Irishman.

Materials and labor were very high, and he built so well and so elaborately that when the hotel was completed, he did not have a cent to buy furniture with. Money was 11

not so plentiful now, the town was beginning to slump. 

Mike was not a hotel man, and no one had any real confidence in the venture. Since he could not borrow money to buy furniture, the hotel stood empty.

There was nothing left for Mike but his luck, and it did not forsake him. Just at the psychological time, for Mike, the great ocean liner, Clara Bell, went on the rocks at 

Eagle Reef, some thirty miles distant from Gold Rock.

About two hundred people lost their lives and the vessel was a total wreck. By some peculiar turn of chance, the wreck of the steamship floated to the beach just a little below Mike's hotel. Here were beds and mattresses, carpets and steamer chairs.

The other items of furnishings included rugs, table linen and even silverware. With this equipment Mike furnished his hotel. Things moved fast, the hotel was opened for business, and the formal opening had touches of grand style. 

Guests came and things went well. The hotel barroom became more popular than the big saloon down town, and Mike was again the most important citizen in town. 

But luck is uncertain. The "pockets" of gold became fewer and fewer. The prospectors drifted away and the miners were leaving. When mines were abandoned, Mike held on to "Mike's Luck" until there were not more than a dozen people left in town. But finally, after all hopes of re-opening the mines were dissipated, Mike traded most of his property to an Indian for an old boat and paddled good naturedly down the fiord, "Ach, but I may hit it again, I'm a young mon yit."

Probably there are many other stories fully as interesting, that might be suggested by these old ruins, if the right party could be found to relate them. There was an old graveyard with three marble slabs that must have been 12

imported "from the States". But most of the graves were marked with irregular native stones. Some of these bore initials, rather unskillfully carved. One grave was surrounded by a stone wall, the grave being covered with rows of shells laid in mortar so as to form a terrace which was surmounted by a cross.

Some of the best buildings seemed to have been destroyed by fire. There was a large cellar with a layer of good building stones around the top. On one side of this wall, there was a sort of built-in safe or strong box. 

One of the largest stones had been smoothed on one side, and the inscription could be easily read, "Here Dick Died". 

Here was a chance for the imagination. Whether "Dick" was a noted character or desperado, who died with his "boots on", or a pet dog, we had no means of knowing. 

A good set of stone steps led up to the spring, and the water was good. After spending about three hours in this graveyard of great hopes, we re-entered the neglected trail and began the ascent of the mountain range. We hoped to get a good view of the location of the city, but the growths of alder, spruce and hemlock were too thick and tangled. The last evidence that we noted of "Gold Rock" was a heap of stones that was supporting some rusty machinery. Here was the lure of gold that intoxicates, that drives men to poverty. 

The story of Douglas and the "Old Treadwell Mine" just across the bay from Juneau, is another bit of history that has many romantic side lights.

It would seem that $100,000,000 worth of gold would entitle a mine to some respect, but gold mines, like rich relations, lose their particular charm when they no longer produce gold. Although the books of the company show a 13

loss of $11,000,000, it is quite probable that the Old Treadwell mine made good interest on the investment. Of course it lacked $11,000,000 of meeting expectations. 

The production was slow, the ore was low grade. Then the mine caved in and it was finally abandoned. The tracks and most of the cars were left in the mine. Much of the heavy machinery was left in the stamp mill. The shops were abandoned rather than moved. Instead of being moved or sold for junk prices, the entire plant was left to rust and decay.

A city of eight thousand people was reduced to a few hundred. The mining buildings, three stories high and from one hundred to three hundred feet long, were left to the owls and the bats. Not only these, but store buildings, hotels, churches and dance halls were left with the empty hope that sometime the mine would be reopened. 

On one street, there were two theaters among the rows of frame buildings. While they were of cheap, frame construction, they were large, and in that country, they must represent a heavy expenditure of capital. Some of the boarding houses were large, twenty to forty rooms, but now they did not have caretakers.

When we landed at the pier, we were told to follow the boy with the mail sack, for he was going "straight" to the 

Treadwell mine. But he seemed to be trying to keep out of sight. We threaded our way along wooden platforms, among the Indian houses that stood on the water front and we climbed steps and meandered over bridges. All along the way we were passing old abandoned, wooden houses that were in various stages of decay. 

A few of the houses had the doors and windows covered with corrugated iron, but in most instances the roofs were beginning to fall in; sometimes the roof would be covered 14

with a thick layer of green moss. One might think that this moss would cause the roof to rot, but on the contrary, it seems to help preserve the shingles. There were many holes in the board walks, and a person had to be continually on the watch lest a mis-step would land him in the mud or shallow water a few feet below.

We walked over the hills to the place where the mine caved in and looked down the "hell hole"; it presented a neglected, forlorn and forsaken suggestion of an abode of evil spirits. You look down a large crater-like hole into thick darkness that seems to possess substance. The ground around the edges must be safe, for it is solid rock, neverthe-less it is difficult to assure yourself that your footing will hold. Instinctively, you draw away from the edge. 

Visitors go only once, there is no urge to return. 

No attempt was ever made to get the tools and wagons that were buried in this place. Just as the men left it on the shift before the cave-in, the mine has remained all of these years.

Although there were 800 men working on this level at the time of the cave-in, the fall happened to occur just as they were changing shifts and not a man was in the mineand this may be called luck.

The ore is not very high grade. Our guide picked up a few pieces of the best ore that he could find and we brought them out of the mine, thus violating the usual rule of a gold mine, which does not permit anyone, not even the miners, to carry away the smallest piece of ore.

After a very good "fish dinner" at a little restaurant, we visited the store and the postoffice. With whole blocks of vacant buildings, it recalled some of the old stories of "dead cities".

Among those who came to work in the mine was John 15

Peccola, a native of Sweden. He came to America to make a home for a flaxen-haired, blue-eyed "Marie". They had been engaged since they were schoolmates. This is John's way of telling it: "She cried a little and told me, 'God speed', and when the boat pushed out, I could see her waving a red scarf. The country faded out but I could yet see the girl, and for years after. And when I was dreadful tired, those blue eyes would shine across to me and I just kept on working".

It is no wonder that John was called a "piker" by his less provident fellow workers when he refused to spend his money and would not join in the ordinary carousels of the camp. John had little time for the amusements and even the companionship of the other miners; he saved his money.

It was not long until he had bought a little patch of ground on the side of the hill, very close to a bubbling stream of mountain water. He had selected a beautiful location, but it was rather high to carry lumber and other building materials.

After his day's work in the mine, John would work on his house until it was dark, and some things he could do by candle light. He hewed the sills, he put up the frame, he reinforced the walls, he roofed it over, all in his careful and thorough manner. It took him four years but the house was well built.

Of course Marie was kept informed as to the progress of the "home in America", and it was John's dream to have the house completely furnished and then save enough money to go back to Sweden.

They would have the wedding at the old church, a great party at the old estate, and then Marie and he would take the boat in grand style for their new home, "and won't 16

those blue eyes shine when she looks out of the front door and sees Juneau under the mountain with the green trees and the snow above?"

In a first-class story, the woman always waits, ever so patiently and coy, but in real life many a good story is badly wrecked. Marie's letters were getting a little longer. In one of them she said: "John, I believe that I hear your hammer pounding and pounding after it is late. You ought to get your sleep. You should have some one to tell you when to quit" and in the postscript she added, "O, I can go alone on the boat".

Although John was slow-thinking, he easily read the meaning. He had $80 in the bank and the banker readily loaned him $60. On the next boat, he sent the $140. Marie must have had her little leather telescope trunk already packed, for she engaged steerage passage on the next boat. 

In this way she saved the difference between steerage passage and second class passage.

She arrived sooner than John had expected. As he came out of the shaft, some one said, "There is a woman at your house; she came on the afternoon boat". While he was crossing the long wooden bridge with the other men, she singled him out. As John expressed it, "she was pointing both blue eyes at my dirty face".

The wedding ceremony was short; the town clerk happened to be standing on the bridge. He took their names and ages on a scrap of paper, wrote their names on the back of an old envelope which he handed to John. "Give this to the Rector and tell him that he can go ahead with the ceremony and I will fill out the papers tomorrow." 

The Rector was at supper, "but that just made things a little hurrieder". He joined their hands, pronounced a blessing and hurried back to his blueberry cobbler. 17

But Marie was practical. When they got to the house "a complete supper was ready. It was the best meal that 

I ever ate, but I guess that I did not eat much though." 

Four days later was the "great cave-in". Things went bad for the miners. There was no work and no money. 

John was worried about the $60 that he had borrowed. 

Out of the last pay check, he gave the banker $15. But bankers, like everyone else, were uncertain. The future held no prospects for money and the banker informed John that the money must be paid "when it is due, or three days after". 

But when John talked of selling out, Marie said, “John,

I cannot leave this house. It is my wedding ring, 'the little spruce shanty that you must wear for a wedding ring'. If everybody leaves Douglas, we can stay. We can raise cabbage and potatoes and celery and strawberries and rutabagas and lots of things. We don't want money or fine clothes. We just want ourselves, just us in this little spruce shanty. Oh, it is home, home, to me, John!"

The thought of selling the house or of leaving Douglas was abandoned for all time.

Being thorough-going and a good worker, John got work across the bay at the Alaska-Juneau mine. He has worked there ever since. He goes over in the morning and comes back in the evening. While other miners were rushing from one strike to another-and usually "dead broke"- this practical man kept his place. Years to him mean the satisfaction of living and enjoying modest blessings, and the systematic saving of a small amount against that "unforeseen circumstance" which seldom comes to the well-prepared but which is so certain to arrive where it can be least reckoned with.

On the side of a mossy-green hill, half draped in climbing 18

vine moss and trailing red fireberries, there stands a little spruce cottage modestly painted a pale blue, "just to match the eyes", and with every part in perfect order. In front is a flower garden with a row of forget-me-nots forming a sky-border of blue, and a low picket fence also painted blue. 

This fence is draped in sweet peas. And all this is a "little paradise" where blue-eyed Marie, and little "blue-eyed 

Marie" await the coming of John every evening.

Gold is not all there is in life. When gold takes wings, real life values stand out for their full worth. 

Twelve years ago, the Old Treadwell mine was the richest and the most extensively worked gold mine in the world, today as we walked about the wrecks of old buildings where fishermen were tearing off pieces of siding and prying off sills, the simile of buzzards tearing the remaining flesh from the bones of an animal that happened to die on the plains, seemed to apply.

Nothing about the old mill is worth current junk prices. 

In an abandoned cabin, near the edge of the brush, there lives a dog. The dog was abandoned when the house was left by the unfortunate occupants. "Old Boz" keeps house alone. Every day he walks down to a high point on the rocks where the path turns off to go to his house. He looks expectantly down the board walk as the people pass, but none of his folks come home. He is a very old dog, grizzled about the head, especially the nose. After a long wait, he goes slowly back home with his head down as though sorely disappointed. Once a day, he goes down to the beach to get some fish. While he appears to be too old to fight, other dogs leave him alone. He is perfectly friendly until some one attempts to enter the house, then he is the watch dog guarding his master's property. But houses are so plentiful that no one cares to ask for this one. 19

"Old Boz" is a pioneer of the olden times, a great favorite of the Indian children, and a real citizen of this disintegrating relic of a once-prosperous community. 

Alaska is a land of luck, a land where things happen fast, and where the fairy footsteps of the things that almost happen are inaudible; but somehow you get the feeling that these things are about, that the "sword of Damocles, supported by a hair", may fall, or that you may have the luck to pick up a fortune anywhere any time. This uncertainty, this chance for big luck, or luck to get big things without expending the regular amount of labor and sacrifices, all this gives a zest to life; it is the lure of the unexpected. 20

The Canadian Rockies and the “Inside Passage"

HE Canadian Rockies have personality. Wild and rugged, they uphold a wild and rugged grandeur-a grandeur of newness, of freshness, of crispness.

After a rainy day at Moose Jaw, and a cold, windy day at Calgary, the climatic hospitality of Banff was quite overwhelming. When we walked out of the little railway station it seemed that warm, kindly hands were pulling at our overcoats and a soft voice was insisting that the air was balmy, and that the sun was hot, in spite of the snow-capped peaks that formed a tent circle, above and around the village, the bridge, the bubbling waterfalls and the blue-green lake.

From a fringe of feathery green trees, sharp lined mountains lift their shoulders of gray and brown patches of solid rock straight up to dizzy heights where displaced rocks have left a shelf. Here the mountain glaciers are held in check and they dispense tiny streams of foam-white water along the crevices and over the edges of the great rock patches. 

Above are the white tints of the sun, piercing the blue, sparkling the white diamond dust of frozen water. Bearing their loads of ice and snow, they are yet successful windbrakes for the little valley that basks in a surprisingly hot sunshine. With green-feathered trees, multi-colored flowers, a patch of the blue lake-like an eye half-asleep-the steeproofed hotel, like a castle set in the hills, and with mountains and glaciers serrating the sky line, the prospect assumes the superlative.

A mile above sea level, with a panorama of enchanted forests spreading out between the mountain peaks, and nar- 21

row defiles sparkling with blue glaciers, lakes of turquoise and foamy waterfalls hanging in the sunlight, a play of gleaming emerald, garnet and amethyst, the headquarters camp of Rocky Mountain Park becomes more than a health resort. It ministers to the mind, to the imagination, to the esthetic tastes.

Under the spell of the bigness, the massiveness of the surrounding and crowding mountains, one cannot feel old or weary or burdened with worries. The fresh, cool air forbids weariness and the grandeur of the mighty snow-capped mountain peaks drives away personal ills.

Where nature has done so much for the place, it is a distinct shock to come in contact with people who drive hard bargains, who impose unusual prices for ordinary services. On the standard bus tours, the fares are not excessive and in the hotels and resorts maintained by the Canadian Pacific Railway Company, the rates seem to be justified by a very high standard of service and efficiency, but the village swarms with parasites. 

When you come to the Canadian Rockies, drive your own automobile. The roads within the parks are excellent, camps are inviting and the officials are kindly and helpful. 

Picturesque, red-coated soldiers dash about on beautiful mounts. They are the famous Canadian Mounted Police.

They accost you civilly, their manners suggest culture and refinement. You will like them; you will want to take some of them home with you. 

Banff has been termed "the Yosemite of the North", but we found little to justify the comparison. Here is beauty, new and clear-cut, sparkling, dazzling beauty of artist's pigments and precious stones, but there is no hint of the quiet restfulness, the grandly-quiet sleep of centuries, the venerable time-defying, age-old mossy rocks and storm-scarred trees 22

that connect Yosemite with the life of centuries that seem to reach out and fold in the weary and the distressed men and women who have been wandering away from home. Here we find the dazzle of newness, the spell of grandeur. It is the beautiful city, not the old homestead.

Sulphur Mountain hot springs is a strange feature of a place where snow carnivals and ski jumping contests are regular events during the winter months. Hot sulphur baths are recommended for various ailments. There are open air swimming pools where a warm bath may be had in the bright sunshine as it glances from the glaciers and snow-capped mountains.

There is the famous "Cave and Basin" which may be regarded as a suggestion from Mother Nature that bath tubs are a source of health and recreation to civilized men. 

There was nothing strange or foreign about the place. 

Four out of every five tourists that we met were from New York City. The government maintains a wild animal zoo and a museum. There are bears and wolves and badgers and wildcats; then there are Canada geese and ducks and golden-eyed eagles, but who comes to a wild place to see wild animals behind bars? There is boating on the river and there are paths and trails through the canyons and up the mountain steeps.

The call of the mountain is irresistible. Here is life in all of its original beauty and splendor. On a narrow edge of a precipitous cliff, where the feet must be planted singly, yet with the head uplifted into the blithe air of infinite space, there is no room for small or mean egotism. Here is a field of mountains. In the Canadian Rockies, there are 

300 peaks that attain an altitude of more than 10,000 feet above sea level. Here is a country of massive mountains, red and brown, solemn and stately, grand in their sharply- 23..

drawn lines of contrasting color, their overawing sweep of irresistible coldness. How many centuries will pass before these peaks are worn down? How long until the green trees 

I will march across them?

Among the painted, towering snowpeaks and fields of glistening ice, there is a hanging lake, a lake 5607 feet above the sea. We go up by a tramway, three and one-half miles from the railway station. The trip is a delight. A panorama unfolds on every side. There are too many things to see: "Oh, see!" "Look!" "Look!" 

Lake Louise is a jewel, not a rainbow. It is not elusive and changeful. It is fixed and real. Without the charm of change or the lure of some unexpected revelation, it is sublime in its enchanting and awe-inspiring constancy. It is the full glow, the lasting blush of peaches swaying the branches with their weight of ripening fruit.

With all of its fixedness and consistent adherence to a set purpose, there yet remain the suggestions of mobility, of liquid, of fluid. It is a study in standard color, it is the flowing blue, the rippling blue, the waving blue of substance. 

The rugged and colossal massiveness of the mountain peaks that are mirrored in its blue depths, without disturbing the deep repose of the azure water, contributes a grandeur that is quite beyond interpretation. Set in limpid green, yet a polished emerald, clear green, deeply, darkly and overwhelmingly green or blue, just as the angle of light happens to fall. Sometimes the trail seems to wind and to linger irresistibly close beside a waterfall. We cannot go to the top of all the mountains, but we can at least try the trails. The 

Lake of the Clouds is bordered by trees and flowers. We can view it from several angles, and in every instance there are new beauties. Set directly in front of an ice mass hundreds of feet high that suggests a frescoed wall of dazzling white 24

and purplish blue, the glassy water seems to go through a series of opalescent changes as we ascend the trail. First, it is purple, deep and dark, then there are the olive, the violet, the amethyst, the pale violet, the magenta, pale blue and indigo. Probably the simplest element in visual experience is a result of light and color, but here light seems to determine shades of color, and even the bright, the shining, the glittering are pleasantly stimulating.

Paradise Valley is a land of flowers and waterfalls. Glacial streams moan and gurgle and foam and tumble over rocks in ever-changing shades of blue and green and white. 

The Great Divide, "the backbone of the world", is also the boundary line between Alberta and British Columbia. 

Here the waters of a single river divide, part going west to the Pacific Ocean and the other part going east to Hudson 

Bay. The railroad goes through Kicking Horse Pass. At

Ravelstoke, the train made a 15-minute stop, hardly long enough for us to get the details of a large catch of mountain trout that was displayed on the platform. Five of the largest would average a little over four pounds each, and "the fightenest fish that ever jumped out of the water." "9 

Vancouver is a beautiful city, but the "Inside Passage' to Alaska offers so many allurements that we forego most of the sight-seeing. There is bustle and excitement on the pier. The great cranes and pulleys are squeaking and thundering, ship officers in snappy uniforms are moving about giving orders; petty officers, stewards, apprentices, assistants, cabin boys and mess boys, in spotless white coats, are assembled in a receiving line at the gangway. 

Many and various are the different kinds of people assembled. There are nervous people making their first trip abroad, there are much-traveled people who have traveled 25

too much; and there are the "new rich", yet under the illusion that they are the "only rich".

After the gangplank has been taken up and the lines of confetti are beginning to break, a taxi rushes up and a party of five tumbles out. Two suitcases, a traveling bag and two packages are thrown aboard; a weeping woman is hanging onto a man who is dressed in a black party suit, while a cab driver and a shipping clerk are urging him along. "But Peggy, I'll be back." "Well, I should hope so." This from a middle-aged woman, probably the mother-in-law. The boat is slowly sliding along the pier, and getting a little farther away. 

The man hesitates, two bells are sounded, there is a sharp command, slowly the boat swings back to the pier. There are no doubts now. Wharf hands push the gangplank against the ship and the passenger climbs over the rail. 

Among the passengers, he is waving one hand and holding up a package in the other. 

But this boat was not to get away yet. A woman's scream from the pier! A commotion on the steerage deck! This space is crowded with little brown men-Chinese, Japanese and Filipinos and there is a fight, several against one. 

It seems that they are trying to throw him overboard, and they are about to succeed. "That's my boy!" The voice from a man on the pier, a commanding voice, and he repeats, "That's my boy. A first class passenger, stop that boat." His bearing is that of a substantial business man. 

It seemed that he was accustomed to giving orders. Bells are sounded; the engines are stopped and the big boat swings slowly forward, loses force, and trembles to a standstill. 26

Ship officers rush among the mob, the captain signals, and the agent calls out, "Take out your boat, captain!"

As we afterwards learned, a 17-year-old boy was making a trip to the north. When the boat was slipping along the pier, he got among the Orientals, cannery hands, and steerage passengers, where he hardly belonged, and they misused him. He was not really hurt.

As the city with its great buildings, some of them 40 stories high, fade into the distance, we are launched in the mystery of floating islands, green-fringed fiords and magic. mountains lifting out of opal seas, but reaching into that fairy cloudland where patches of glittering snow sparkle in the bright sunlight. Like immense white diamonds in a green setting displayed in a field of pale blue silk, these great whitecaps light up the sky.

Nature is in a lavish mood. It is a luxurious combination. 

All of the richness of jewels, precious stones and costly fabrics, an abundance that might be suggested by Midas, the Count of Monte Cristo or some of the imaginary characters of mythology. It is the spell of richness and grandeur. Just above tidewater line there is a strip of reddish-brown moss, and this is "tucked-in and basted down" with low-hanging bushes, blueberry, salmon berry, wild raspberry, and then the alders and other broad-leafed trees before the fields of spruce and fir and hemlock.

A little flock of black cormorants, flying close to the water in their peculiar letter "Z" formation, round the point and change their formation to a "check mark" or a letter "V" with one very long side. It is accomplished by the heavy, black water-birds with a facility that might suggest the lining up of brants or migrating geese. With a strange spreading motion, they become a semicircle before they drop themselves upon the water. 27

Sailing among the islands, the water is quiet in a little cove. There is hardly a ripple on the surface, but there is a large circling wave spreading towards the ship. It seems that a three-cornered rock, rather dark, has lately appeared above the water. It gets bigger, and as we continue to look, there is a large round body-not perceptibly connectedthat seems to be floating just at the surface. It gets larger. 

Bigger and bigger it grows as we continue to look. Suddenly a jet of water is shot into the air. It rises with the force of a fireman's great pump but the stream is not so large. 

It is a thin stream shot with much force. It rises straight up with one grand spurt, and one shot is all, not a continuous stream; it spreads into spray and steam, and settles back to the water. The great whale brings more of his body above the surface. He turns that immense tail, then there is a curve in the great tank of a body and the monster water mammal sinks like a submarine. Where the whale disappeared, large rolls of widening circles spread and roll away on the water. They are not large when they strike the path of the ship. The spouting whale is gone, but we have the lure of the primitive. We wonder what will be the next strange object.

The second day out is rainy, but the morning of the third day the sun seems to be struggling to get through the layers of broken clouds. As the sun breaks through the water becomes lighter. The blue dissolves into green. It is an emerald landscape. "Barring old Neptune's unwelcome movements, the tips of submerged mountains form a chain of green islands among which modern steamships meander to the land of the midnight sun." 

There are patches of snow. There are trim little spruce trees and we can easily recognize the old cedars, for they 28

are darker than the other trees. Some fleecy clouds, like loose wool, are being rolled down the mountains, but in some instances the clouds are rising like smoke and floating away. There are narrow strips of lighter green, beginning well above timber line and following a winding canyon down, getting wider as they approach the water.

These are the paths of snow slides that broke loose high up and slid down, taking trees, rocks, everything that was loose, and tumbling the entire mass into the sea. 

Ketchikan is much like other cities along the western coast of California, Oregon and Washington. Aside from the narrow streets, and being built upon piers, there is little to suggest Alaska. Wrangell is a graveyard of strange people that have about faded from present day civilization. 

It is the city of totem poles. A few half mendicant remnants of the old race of hospitable and self-supporting Indians that have been immortalized by John Muir, Doctor

Young and other popular writers, may be seen sprawled about on the docks, offering for sale a few such home-made articles as moccasins of moose hide and deer skin, beaded belts and wicker baskets. Some of the children are strictly in the store business, with a line of garnets, chipped from the native rocks and little bouquets of native wild flowers. 

There is nothing bright about this picture. These are the original inhabitants. Their ancestors owned the country. 

The typical grandeur of this country is in its glaciers. 

These are rivers of ice, flowing irresistibly, wearing away the hills, and when they reach the sea they present majestic palisades in hues of enchanting richness, and spirit-lifting cathedral spires, poised in the air, seemingly by a whim. "Like a sea of diamonds, poised in the hills, Old Taku sparkles and thunders and drops icebergs into the muddy blue water." 29

There is a thrill in seeing natural objects for the first time. But a deeper, more soul-satisfying feeling comes the second time. A feeling of acquaintanceship, of intimacy, of mutual sympathy, must be built up. No one can see Old 

Taku the first trip. The beauties and glories must have time to grow. It is typical of the grandeur of Alaskan glaciers. They are rivers of ice, flowing, continually flowing; ever so slowly but irresistibly, they flow over highlands, through mountain gorges, carrying away the loose rock, wearing away the hills.

Sometimes they score deep trenches in solid rock. These mighty forces have a charm that overwhelms. Taku glacier is not as large as Malaspina glacier. Several glaciers in 

Alaska are larger, but Taku is the best known of the really large glaciers. It is 90 miles long, one mile wide and 100 feet deep. However it is but an arm of a great glacier stream which is 300 miles long.

Our party was in a small launch and we had not proceeded very far up Taku Inlet before we began to meet icebergs. Some of them were blue and solid but most of them were whitened by melting until they suggested chunks of snow ice. Over waters of sapphire and jade, our little boat "put-puts" along and the spell of the sublime surroundings has quite overcome our wayward interests before we are in sight of the mighty palisades. 

A large, flat "berg" floating just above the surface of the water contains a black object. We hasten to get our field glasses. It is a seal, a hair seal, floating and resting in the sun. The captain swings the boat so that we will pass close to the "berg". Evidently the seal is sensitive to the sound of the motor. He raises his head, as though he was awakening from a sleep, and almost with the same movement, he slips into the water. 30

Seagulls are fluttering around a small piece of floating ice. When a bald eagle takes his place among them, several of the gulls fly away. Some kind of a land animal, a deer, a moose or a caribou, is frozen in the ice. This raises the question as to how long this piece of meat has been preserved in this natural refrigerator. It is not unreasonable to speculate that the glacier may have been carrying this animal along for the past 50 or 100 years. No doubt that the scavenger gulls recognize that meat is sometimes carried in the icebergs, for there is a large flock hovering around the foot of the glacier.

In the distance, there are the white palisades back of a stream of icebergs and filling in a space between two green hills.

Mountains soar on both sides of the picturesque channel, and the showers that have been intermittently falling during the forenoon are dashes followed by such breaking of the clouds that the sun almost breaks through. The color of the water changes with the degree of light, pale opal green when it is lightest, almost blue when there are shadows; but when it is the darkest, when the rain is falling, the water is so dark that it suggests black. 

In this dark water we meet some ice floes that are blue as indigo. Our captain heads the launch directly towards the wall of ice. The roaring increases. Soon we hear splashes and plunges, which are caused by chunks of ice dropping off the glacier. These chunks of ice, ice floes and icebergs, plunge out of sight and when they reappear they are outside of the roll of foaming water that is rolled up by the contact of the running water with the comparatively quiet water of Taku Inlet.

Closer and closer the seemingly reckless captain allows his launch to continue at full speed. Some members of the 31

party are nervous. They make inquiries. They suggest that we are close enough: too close, "Stop that engine", "Turn the boat", they shouted, but we continue until the icebergs begin to bob up around the boat. Then the boat is allowed to float. With our attention centered on the boat, we had not taken a real look at the glacier.

Here the majestic palisades, in hues of enchanting richness, lift their inspiring monuments, prisms and pyramids and proclaim a grandeur that is sublime. We are not prepared for the sight. We cannot comprehend it. The spiritlifting spectacle of cathedral spires, poised in the air, held by a whim, some of them tumbling down, sections of ice larger than our boat, the force accumulated in ages past brought to this supreme moment of dissolution, all crowded upon the mind at once. The ways of the universe are strange, awesome and fraught with mystery, but the "mills are grinding". 

When we are proud of our achievements, when we think that the human race has accomplished anything, we might tarry in front of a glacier and meditate. 

The face of the glacier is ever changing. Like a benevolent old seer, Old Taku looks down upon the world, not to chide or condemn, not to preach or admonish, but simply to show the triumph of natural law. When clouds come over the sun, some spots become blue-black. Then there are heavy lines, casements, gateways, cathedral spires that stand out from a darker background, grand old monuments that might have been erected to kings and to nations and have brightened the pages of the world's history. 

But the marvel of a drop of water, the mystery of an ice crystal, these are persistent questions, even when we are swamped by immensity.

The roar of water is more continuous but the gutteral thunder of splitting ice, the low, heavy and forceful booms 32

that come from the depth of the mighty mass of ice, these are the cannon shocks, the thunder roar that set a-tremble the most courageous human beings. Sometimes these thundering intonations are prolonged. They seem to trail off back into the glacier. Again, they are booming and sudden. 

The glacier is about a mile wide. Our boat is about a quarter of a mile from one side when a giant chunk of ice is dropped near the middle. There is a roar of breaking ice, a splash, a plunge and a great wave rocks the boat. It would not be well to have your boat too close to that one when it comes up. We wait a long time. "The big ones stay down longer", and when it comes up, it does not seem to be much larger than the others. Icebergs are deceiving. The large ones may not show so much above the water. 

It is surprising what a man can do with a little launch.

We landed on a rocky point and we were soon climbing over the slippery rocks up a little canyon that leads to the top of the glacier. This canyon is narrow and a small stream of water dashes and foams and tumbles down the steep rocks. There is a shower and snow flakes glide and hesitate and finally settle to the wet rocks. But this is July 

2. No difference, these are snow flakes. 

Here is a field of diamonds. The sun is shining. Here are contrasts. A few minutes ago, rain and snow flakes, but now the bright sun. Prismatic hues, rainbow colors, flash from the snow and the narrow points of ice that make up the field of a vast expanse. The first view of the jewelglistening field is startling and dazzling. The light is too strong for our eyes. On a field of ice and snow we have the feeling of warmth, of heat. Again must we take cognizance of the nice distinctions in the natural world--there is much difference between light and heat. The air is cold and biting. There is no heat in the dazzling light. 33

We walk out upon the diamond-spangled glacier, and— "Watch your step". The footing is uncertain. Mush ice at the points of the pinnacles easily gives way; the crevasses are deep; and we are not ready to be put in "cold storage". 

There is a regular snapping and popping. The river is far away, but the thundering roar of the big breaks, "the growl of Old Taku", comes occasionally, and it comes with a quality and a force that commands respect. Sometimes from far below there is a tinkle of running water. Somehow the water from the ice that melts on top of the glacier finds its way down through the mass of ice and joins with the continually flowing river that pours out its waters at the foot of the glacier. With all of their treachery, these crevasses are beautiful beyond compare; the most exquisite azure tones, the elusive dissolved opal richness and a delicacy sublime. 

One crevasse is 100 feet wide. Like a delicately tinted morning glory, its hue is faint at the top, but it gets darker with depth, and it seems that there is a black spot in the bottom. Near one side-what is called a lateral morainthere is a mass of stones, dirt and some pieces of board. 

Getting down from the glacier is not so much easier than climbing up. The rocks are slippery and it requires caution. 

A blue bird, an indigo bunting, blue as the bluest, swings past, alights on a low-hanging limb and watches our progress.

We are soon at the bottom of the ledge, and there is a smoke near the rocks where we landed. It is handy to have a "Harry" in the party. Harry is a Tennessee negro boy.

While we were climbing the glacier, he was boiling the wieners. Hot coffee and the little brown "puppies" served in buns that are just out of the steamer!

As we take another view of the hundred foot palisade, it 34

seems to have changed in every detail. The lower half is darker now, but the upper rim is even lighter than it appeared when we arrived some three hours ago. Then there are patches of color, deep shades, that seem to be framed in by lighter hues. But as we swing the boat to a position that is about a thirty-two degree angle to the sun, and look high up towards the other end of the mighty ice wall, patches of rainbow gleam from the enchanted pinnacles. In colors deep and contrasting, these rainbow patches fall in with lighter and darker patches of solid color. As we shift our position, the rainbow patches shift about. 

Artists are frequently accused of putting too much color into their mountain and glacier sketches. But it is more a question of time of the day and the angle of the sun's rays. 

Taken at this particular time, and from our viewpoint, it seems that the artist could use all of his colors and yet hardly do the scene justice. Painting with a lavish brush and in great daubs, the declining sun transfigures, transforms and glorifies the ice formations on the face of the glacier until the scene becomes charged with an unreality. 

In some places, it seems to be a wall of sparkling jewels, a fairyland that could not exist in reality. Yet if we wait and watch for the right conditions, if we take time and use patience, the superb spectacle will unfold before us. 

Old Taku is marvelous. Superb in her grandeur, enchanting in her whims and human in her universal appeal to lofty sentiments, she stands above and beckons-beckons to heights that are worthy, to thoughts and ideals of that perfect accord with a friendly universe. 35

Old Sitka, Mt. St. Elias and Mt. McKinley

T IS a rainy day at Sitka. The bay is an indefinite splashing of light gray waves. The hills, and even the great mountains, blend into the clouds. Even the patch of light is the dim, doubtful light of uncertainty. 

Like a grand old dame, basking in the glory of a strange and wonderful past, Old Sitka, the Russian capital of Alaska, offers a weirdly strange hospitality. Wrapped in somber grandeur, the half-asleep old city seems to breathe an aura, as though incense was being burned by some of the weird images that recall a romantic past. We could almost expect some of the frogs, sea lions or polar bears, figures of the totem poles, to be enjoying the rain.

A peculiar quietness, a silence that weighs down, so that we feel its weight, and a dignified reserve, almost verging on aloofness, seems to invest the old harbor, the pier and the deserted streets. It almost seems that our boat is rude, that we are violating some privacy or doing something that is unethical. Like entering a holy sanctuary during services, we approcah the main city only to find that every doorway and window is alive with sharp noses and gleaming eyesmen, women, children, dogs, pet bears, and at least one wolf.

In every shed and doorway, a motley array of Indian women is flung about, helter-skelter, each surrounded by children and dogs and some kind of a display of wares for sale. There are deer-skin moccasins, match holders, baskets and decorated belts and girdles. One old woman is doing some business selling rabbit feet. 

They are the feet 36

of the snowshoe rabbit-that wonderful adaptation that this rabbit has acquired for getting over the deep snow. 

There is a simple Greek church. It has not changed in 

100 years. A leaf from an old book, this church is a shrine and a relic, a monument to self-sacrificing people who believed something and had the courage, the fortitude and the strength of will to maintain their ideals. 

These pioneers in church work struggled against odds, but they maintained their ideals among the Indians of this strange, out-of-the-way place. We are about ready to think that the old priest belongs to the last century. With his tall cap, his heavy robe sweeping the ground, and his immense, spreading whiskers, he is hardly a figure of presentday life. There are several paintings that seem to have real merit as works of art, but a famous painting of the Madonna, brought over from Russia some time during the last century, attracts most attention. Everything about the church belongs to the past, yet it is used regularly. As one old lady told us, they have "the old-time religion".

Besides a city of relics, of monuments and totem poles, Old Sitka is a sylvan retreat, a "lover's lane", a shrine of old trees, historical trees, and trees of impressive beauty. 

There are spruce trees; tall, dark and tangled; pine trees, stately and graceful; and there are cedars, like Chinese temples, exhaling fragrance as a sort of benediction. The effect may be that of a park, a cemetery, a private estate or an abode of elves and gnomes, a place to conjure with. 

This is my second trip to Sitka; there has been no change.

In 1923, we arrived in the bay a little after midnight. We found the pier, the streets and every available space overrun with strange and wonderful people, all anxiously awaiting the arrival of President Harding and his party. It seemed that very little posing would be required to make 37

White Father". People were rushing about, talking in excited tones. Lights and campfires were everywhere. There was no place to sleep. 

That night and the next day, the town was seething with excitement. Today, the town is the same. The buildings, the trees, all the physical features have remained unchanged, but the life has gone out. 

Founded in 1799 by the Russian explorer, Banonaff, the old traditions have held sway. Fortune and favor, glory and pomp have come and gone, but Old Sitka has made few adjustments. Like wrapping the folds of a velvet robe about a very old family album, we leave the venerable relic of a city. Secure in the past, heeding not the present and fearing not the future, the old town sleeps in peace, with her relics, her memories, her triumphant resignation, she sleeps in a beautiful halo. This range, dipping into the sea, reaching into the clouds, and extending across a skyline of sea, offers one of the most extensive pictures of great land masses that can be found in this northern wonderland.

Mount McKinley is the highest mountain on the North 40

With all of its lofty grandeur and majestic bearing, it half conceals a sort of weird beauty, a beauty of change of dissolving-out and building-up shades when vivid tints follow each other, as in play, across ledges of rock and palisades of ice.

This great mass of earth dominated the entire region in ancient times as it does today. To the Russians, it was known as Bulshaia Gora or Big Mountain but the natives know it as Traleyka, or Tenally or Denali. The Great One and the Home of the Sun are given as the significance of these terms. A mining prospector, W. A. Dickey, suggested the name, Mount McKinley. It is the highest land mass in the world since it rises 18,000 feet above the plateau in which it stands.

Although mountain climbing is of little interest to the average citizen, the ascent of any mountain is a matter of absorbing interest to a small coterie of people known as mountain climbers. First ascents and the first ascent of the year are always chronicled, and they become a part of mountain history. This whole-hearted fraternity of healthy outdoor people is ever anxious to keep mountain history accurate.

When we take into consideration its immense height and the degree of rise, Mount McKinley offers no obstacles that are especially new or difficult to the trained mountain climbers. The best account of an expedition to the top of Mount McKinley is that published by Dr. Frederick A. Cook, but it is now questioned whether his party really made the entire ascent or whether they turned back after attaining a position high among the foothills.

In 1907, Dr. Cook's article, "The Conquest of Mount 

McKinley" appeared in Harper's Magazine, and the next year, his book, "To the Top of the Continent" was accepted as a faithful recital of facts by scientific societies, authorities in mountain history and the general public. For three years his claims were unchallenged by mountianeers and mountain clubs. Since it was far away from the usual haunts of man, there were few people who were in a position to pass judgment, and no one raised the question. When the controversy over how he "discovered the North Pole" was at its height, his "conquest of Mount McKinley" was questioned from several sources. It is doubtful if any later account of an expedition to the summit of this mountain can equal Dr. Cook's "The Conquest of Mount McKinley".

Strange though it may seem, the item that brought out the most criticism, the most scathing sarcasm, "the purple snow", has really been found. Truth is stranger than fiction and nature knows no absurdities; when the angle of light is just right, the snow on some of the great heights appears to be purple; this has been confirmed by several trustworthy mountain climbers. Some of the most severe 43

critics are faced with the question, "Did Dr. Cook and his companion really ascend the mountain high enough to witness this strange phenomenon, or, was he just lucky?"

Whether he attained the summit or just went part way up, as some of his critics aver, the fact remains that he gave an account that was both convincing and interesting; he made for himself the reputation for being one of the best story tellers of his time.

There is much to admire in the primitive fastnesses of this boundless region and we were sped about at a rate entirely too fast for a real impression of the mighty environs, but to the other members of our party, this was regarded as a side trip.

Sea Lions and Seals 

F THE STRANGE creatures that inhabit strange places, sea lions are even stranger than fur seals. 

It seems that they do not owe the world much for rent, they live where few other animals can exist. In the economy of nature it seems that some form of life has been especially designed to live in each particular type of locality. 

Hence alligators for the swamps, eagles for the mountain crags and sea lions for the barren rocks of the storm swept islands of the sea.

On ledges of wild sea cliffs, just above the edges of the flapping waves, on barren rocks that are regularly drenched by the spray, these gracefully formed, silken-coated, warmblooded animals sprawl themselves around, helter-skelter, to bask in the sunshine.

To most forms of life, these rocks are hard and repulsive. 

They are also cold and damp and slippery. Often there is a deep, restless and hungry sea lapping at the sides of one of these slippery rocks and threatening to engulf the unfortunate animal that happens to lose a foothold, and the sun, shining directly on a barren rock, often becomes very hot. With the reflections and accumulations of heat, the rock may become almost hot enough to burn tinder. Yet here on the barren rocks, under the blazing sun, a sea lion prefers to indulge in the luxury of a siesta. 

If it is true that: "Man wants but little here below, Nor wants that little long." a sea lion wants less.

The sea lion is a land animal that has taken to the water. 

His home is on the land, where the young are born. So far 51

as their life history is known, the water is but a work shop, a place to get a living. Aside from a little bubbling play, their great life functions are worked out on land. 

Their family life and love making, their jealousies, rivalries, fights and their unreasonable oppressions of one another, all become a part of that strangely wonderful pageant of sheer brute force and unsullied mother-love that is displayed from day to day on the cold, rough rocks, just above the pounding waves.

To the sea lion "back to nature" means back to the rocks. 

He knows a few strange birds that awkwardly sprawl themselves along to the edge of the rocks, and then drop into the water with a splash; birds that seem to fly under the water and they accomplish that feat. Using their wings as the sea lion uses his flippers, the wings become fins. The murres that accomplish this feat are a numerous kind of birds; but, since they nest on barren rocks, far out at sea, they are not so well known. There is no quarrel between these birds and the sea lions. The sea lions will not eat the birds nor their eggs. If an unlucky murre happens to fall among the sea lions, it simply scrambles and flutters until it gets into the water. 

The Stellar sea lions of these islands of the rugged coast of Alaska are much larger than the California sea lions. One of the large old males will weigh about 1500 pounds but the largest female will not weigh more than 600 pounds. 

The average is much smaller. The largest of the old bulls, perched on the highest point of a rock, is usually surrounded by a number of females, resembling somewhat the organization of a fur seal rookery. Since he is the largest and is occupying the most prominent position, people are likely to get an erroneous notion as to the comparative number of the large sea lions. 52

Hobbling along "like a boy in a sack race", in continual danger of falling in almost any direction, this great mass of weird prehistoric life presents a strange show of awkwardness as he bumps himself along from one rock to another. 

It would be hard to find another instance in the animal world where so much muscular force, grace and agility in one element becomes so awkward and useless so quickly. 

Every movement seems to suggest that feet and hands have been tied together. The front flippers are of little use except to balance the body and hold on to the rocks. The fin feet are set at right angle to the body and they are quite useless as feet. They must be hitched along as though the whole body must go at the same time. With the gait of a merry-go-round horse, he rocks and bows and hitches himself up and down and a little forward. The first question asked is, why doesn't he fold his flippers and roll himself along?

But when he splashes into the water, he is a marvel of grace. Have you ever watched the sea lions at the zoo when they were at play? How they glide and flash, loop above and around one another, sometimes rolling over and over as they turn and circle, bending a sinewy body into a graceful curve, twisting from side to side, up and down or round and round as though no joints were needed, since the whole body is made up of soft, satiny and pliant material so nicely balanced that no bones were necessary and the entire body was but a nicely organized bundle of minute rubbery strands. 

But the animal pays a price for this grace in swimmingawkwardness on land.

The ideal home is on barren rocks that extend but a few feet above the highest waves, where no plant life can exist because of the frequent splashing of the salty waves and the intense heating whenever the sun is permitted to pour its 53

direct rays upon the bare rocks. The luxury of a soft bed is never sought by a sea lion. However sharp or uneven the rocks may be, he drops down upon them as though it was a feather pillow. In a sense, he is "padded" on the outside by a layer of fatty tissue. Just under the skin, this thick layer of soft, spongy material serves as a protection against cold and also forms a protective cushion.

A herd of sea lions on the rocks is a spectacle that borders on the grotesque. Sprawled about among uneven rocks, their bodies in every conceivable position, they hardly suggest living animals of any kind.

Sacks of sand or saw dust might be more easily thought of, or slim bunches of seaweed of various sizes, but the usual features of animal life, they do not appear, not even a head, for there is no neck. 

In Western Alaska, especially near the seaport of Nome, there are hummocks of seaweed that cover small rocks so as to give them the appearance of shocks of brown hay. From a distance it is difficult to distinguish a group of these mounds of seaweed from a herd of sea lions. And to make the distinction we must look for the pointed heads of the old leaders above the others, which from a distance appear sharp and pointed. Statue-like, he is usually maintaining his pose.

Our boat was in the shadow of a great cliff against which the waves pounded. It would be dangerous sailing if the sea was rough; but very frequently, a sea lion would roll on the waves, almost to the rock, but with his head turned so that a plunge sent him under with the return wave. They were fishing for the squids that were floating around the high point of rocks from a comparatively quiet cove which was almost covered with jelly-fish, squids and cuttle-fish. 

Of course the first question to ask was, why not go into the 54

quiet water and take a meal instead of chasing occasional stragglers around the dangerous reefs? There were a few sea lions in the quiet water, but we saw more of them along the wave-pounded reef. It is possible that the sea lion prefers to catch his prey in the open sea where the waves are rough; and that he enjoys the sport of the chase. 

As we launched a small boat, the roar of the great old leaders could be heard above the flapping and dash of the waves against the high rocks. Our boat was landed among the rocks before there was a stir in the herds that were basking on the higher and more rugged rocks, some thirty or forty feet inland.

First heads came up by the dozens. It seemed that pointed heads protruded from rocks and above rocks. The rocks were bristling with heads. There were so many sea lions. 

Odd and senseless were the first seals seen by man, odd and senseless are the seals of today. They are slow to make adaptations. In a world by themselves, they would thrive, but in this world of many forms of life they must perish unless man continues to protect them.

The seal world is a world of might, a world of despots; the strong individual is the supreme ruler. He is master of the rocks and master of his household. He chooses his place on the rocks, he chooses his wives. 

The only important seal rookery in the world is located. on the lonesome, storm-swept islands of the Alaskan Peninsula. The Pribilof Islands, about 500 miles south of Nome,

Alaska, are the summer home of a colony of about 800,000 fur seals. This colony is about all that remains of the millions of seals that covered the lonely rocks during the early period of our country's history, during the days of the British Fur Company and John Jacob Astor.

Inherently wild as the untamed savage, fur seals can- 55

not be tamed. All efforts to domesticate them have failed. 

Much of their life history, their migratory habits, and even the exact location of their winter homes has so far baffled the researches of man. We hardly know the fur seal. 

But this we do know, about the last week in June of each year, big old bull seals appear on the rocks of the Pribilof 

Islands. No one sees them come. One time you look, there are no seals on the rocks, but look again, there are seals all over the rocks. Sometimes they may be seen slipping out of the water, but they come from below the surface. There is nothing to indicate the direction from which they come. 

These are the big old warriors of the herd. The largest weigh about 600 pounds. They take up their positions, "their home claims", about 15 feet apart and each one scrupulously regards the boundary lines of his neighbor's claim. 

During the next week the herd begins to arrive. Unheralded, and without the slightest indication of the tremendous mass of moving life, one by one, they begin to slip out of the water. The first day of their arrival only about 

35,000 may appear, but in three or four days there will be 400,000 seals on the rocks, and in another week the entire herd of 800,000 will be at the rookery. There are times when it seems that there is a steady stream of seals pouring themselves out of the sea and up along the rocky shores. 

But the organization of 800,000 seals into households, or harems, of 30 to 90 cows each, is not accomplished peaceably. 

While a great bull seal may weigh 500 or 600 pounds, an ordinary cow seal weighs from 90 to 100 pounds. When the cow seals begin to arrive, it is the business of each lordly old bull to get as many of them as possible for his harem. Taking a position on a high point of his territory, he surrounds himself with cows. In this case, the cow has no choice, he simply takes every cow that comes close 56

enough. If she is undecided, or even hesitates, he uses force. 

He thinks nothing of biting her severely, tearing a big gash in her silky hide or even thrusting his terrible tusks into a vital part. When a cow tries to break away, she is so severely handled that she is not likely to try again, and the older cows seem to have trained themselves into submission. 

Some of them will rush headlong towards the first big bull they see when they thrust their heads above the water. Such "love at first sight" may be pleasing to the "lord of the household" but he does not reward her with a gesture or even a pleasant look. She must take her place among the

HE WALRUS is a stupid mountain of flesh. "Live, raw meat by the ton" is suggestive; the animal is lowly organized. From pictures and from stories of walrus hunts, we had formed opinions of an efficient animal with some degree of fierceness, but actual contact with the animals-when we were able to survey them in great herds at close range, acres of great flabby animals, sprawled over the rocks our preconceived ideas had to be reconstructed. 

A drove of very large, fat hogs, ready for market, sprawled about a muddy trough sunning themselves, would be as suggestive of animal intelligence, efficiency or fierceness as a quiet walrus herd.

After leaving Bristol Bay with its hummocks of seaweed, kelp fields and sea meadows, its teeming life of sea birds nesting on the tundra, and its clouds of mosquitoes, our course led across lonesome stretches of wildly tossing sea.

There was a strong wind and the sea was rough enough to be uncomfortable. The experiences of a rough night at sea and the landing at 4 a. m. without breakfast might have added something to our personal prejudices, but the first view of the immense herd was something of a disappointment.... 

The "sea horse of Bering Sea" is a great hulk of flabby awkwardness. Timid and uncouth, the immense cowardly brute becomes a study in the most primitive forms of life. 

Sleeping on the waves, floating vertically, with only the nostrils protruding above the water, the great mass of flesh and fat seems to get its full portion of "pig satisfaction" out of just "forgetting the world". How can an animal live in 71

the sea, survive storms, escape its enemies, acquire 1500 or 

2000 pounds of fatty meat and bone, and yet never attain the intelligence of a mouse?

Black islands of flesh, they haul up on the ice floes or rocky islands in herds of several hundred, a patch of flabby, animate life-alive, but just alive, a little above mere protoplasm. 

Is the walrus hunter a butcher? Can there be as much sentiment disturbed by slaughtering a whole herd of these lowly animals as that called out by the cutting of a forest of great pine trees? The Eskimo is neither poetic nor sentimental. To him the walrus is the biggest part of the living, the crop. What the crop of wheat or rice is to some people in other parts of the world, the walrus is to the Innuits of Alaska. They discuss the "run of walrus" with as much anxiety as the prairie land farmers discuss crop prospects. 

When the walrus herds fail to appear on the rocky shores, it is a calamity. "No walrus" is a plaintive cry, it indicates distress as surely as a "flood in the cotton fields" or a "drouth in the wheat belt".

It is difficult to comprehend these extraordinary, grunting, bellowing brutes that allow themselves to be rolled and shoved ashore rather than make the effort to get away. 

They hardly get above high water, but usually lie awash of the highest waves. No other form of animal life supports such an immense bulk of flesh in proportion to the strength of limb or means of self-protection. About the only method of escape from an enemy is to plunge into the sea, and they are not especially active in the water.

When a walrus comes to the surface for breath, it blows or "snorts" like a whale. This "blowing" is known by sailors as the "walrus fog horn". In times of heavy fog the old-time mariners were frequently warned by the "wal- 72

But even when one of them bobs up beside a frail rawhide boat, there is little danger. If the boat is capsized, it is by accident or awkwardness; the walrus is not aggressive just inquisitive.

The skin of the walrus, which has been put to so many uses by the Eskimos, does not make a very favorable impression upon a person viewing the live animal with field glasses.

There is little suggestion of strength or durability in this loosely-folded skin. It is extremely raw and naked, rough and irregular, and there are warty eminences that suggest pimples or boils, but the hair is hardly noticeable. There are clusters of these swollen, warty pimples. Some of them are yellowish, suggesting that the animal is diseased. It is an unwholesome wrapping for such a large, misshapen mass of flesh, but appearances are deceiving.

The skin of the walrus is used to make the best boats, the boat that the hardy seal hunters must risk upon the open sea when the waves are rough; it is also used to make the summer tent-the movable tent that must withstand the dashing rains and the storms that are severe even during the open months--and it is from this hide that the parka, the principal garment of the Eskimo, is made. Then it must furnish the strongest shoes for the big hunts. 

The tusks are "something to sell". Most of the ivory cutting is done in the large cities of the eastern part of the United States, but on account of the cheapness of labor, much of this work has been shifted to Japan. The native

It seems that the walrus uses his tusks for digging clams. 

Even the largest males do not fight with any degree of violence. They growl and threaten and make slow, desultory strokes at one another, but they seldom bruise the skin or draw blood. The tusk is so loosely set in the jaw that a hunter simply gives it a few raps with a stick of wood, which loosens it so that he can pull it out. A hunter will remove a tusk in two or three minutes.

A large bull walrus will swallow a bushel of clams at one meal, and how he contrives to digest such an unwieldy meal remains a mystery. But the greater part of the food of these immense mammals consists of large water plants that grow in the Bering Sea. Some of these plants have bulbous roots which the walrus digs out; they are strong with sea water, iodine and bromine but that makes no difference to the big brute.

There are great stretches of marine grasses upon which this animal feeds, and he feeds largely for his wants are great. On the whole, it seems that the walrus is essentially an herbivorous or plant-eating animal, but, like the domestic hog, he is glad to eat most anything that can be had without much effort.

The kind of food that he eats has a bad effect upon his flesh. Most white people readily agree that the meat of the walrus is not toothsome. It is rank and raw, strong and "fishy", and it can be compared to no other kind of meat, unless cuttlefish might be classed as meat. But the Eskimo eats this meat with oil that is as rancid as stale butter.

Since he can eat the oil, even this vile meat is taken with relish.

These immense pinnipeds are mated once a year. In many instances, the same pair happens to go together several succeeding years, but this is hardly the rule. After a pair has gone together, others do not interfere. The one calf, an awkward and almost helpless youngster, is born in the early days of June. On the ice floe, the mother cares for it after the fashion of most mammals. She feeds it upon milk for almost a year and shows much parental love for her calf. Instances have been known where the calf was carried several miles inland; the awkward mother rolled and hobbled herself along over rough rocks until she died of exertion.

The young seem to retain their affection for their mother until the end of the second year. After the young bull gets his tusks, he forgets her. But by that time, the mother probably has a new husband, and she is no longer interested in grown up children.

There is no individuality in a herd of walrus. There are no overlords or underlings. The aged and the cripples are not noticed. If the herd moves and some individuals are not able to go along, not a head will look back. When a herd rolls and tumbles into the water, there is plenty of noise and splashing to awaken the most sleepy individuals; the awkward rolling and struggling of the big brutes cannot be compared to any kind of a stampede of domestic animals, but tumbling, rolling, sprawling, flopping, they lose no time in getting into the water. 76

ANDS that sink beneath the sea, rise again and change their form and contour, and that in a day, these volcanic islands are a violation of our usual conception of land forms.

We think of land as stable, not liable to sudden change. 

Terra firma, the firm earth, represents the Roman's idea of the unchanging earth, and we have grown up with the idea of an unchanging landscape; hills that are eternal-this has ever been the prevailing thought of human beings. 

If we do not go back of the flood, the oldest island must be somewhere about Mount Ararat; but the newest island is likely to be among the Aleutian Islands; somewhere along that uncertain string of volcanic islands where there is a break in the earth's crust and the heat from the interior is being liberated.

Coral islands are formed slowly, throughout a long period. of time, an island is brought above the surface of the water by the work of tiny living things. But these are volcanic islands; in forming islands, it seems that the gods may work fast or slow; one island may be two centuries or five centuries in building, but another may bubble out of the water in a few minutes. It is said that one large island was formed in less than thirty minutes.

On the map of North America, the Aleutian Islands are mere specks. Storm swept and lost in an expanse of water, they seem of little importance. But they are lively; they have surprises that startle the world. A slumbering crater 77

may spring into life without warning, and fill the air with smoke and ashes; islands may appear suddenly; and they may disappear as suddenly. A smoking, quivering landscape may give off detonations and subterranean rumblings, may "growl and threaten" for years, and it may "blow up" with all of the spectacular pyrotechnics of prepared explosives.

These islands, and even the sea about them, are uncertain; even to watch them is uncertain; it is difficult to keep from thinking of "what might happen". This is a land of volcanoes. The Aleutian Islands are of volcanic origin; they came up from the sea. Sometimes an island is the result of one disturbance, but more frequently, there are several eruptions. Under the ocean, many fires are burning; masses of complicated compounds are being reduced. 

From United States Government reports, it appears that Bering Sea is getting shallower. Many glacial streams are adding deposits but that can hardly account for such a change over a wide area. It is believed by scientific men that the floor of the sea is rising.

The bottom of this great sea is little less than a level valley covered with shallow water. Some geologists think that Asia and America were once connected by a strip of land. Here is a chance for more speculation; an upheaval might raise the sea floor, might connect Alaska and Russia, might build a continent in the broad expanse of the Pacific Ocean. There is a cloud of volcanic smoke; there is the rumbling of deep-toned thunder; is this a continent in the making? And what are the chances of its appearing just now? Well, let us go back to the states and read the reports.

There is a story of one ship that passed along its regular path on its way to Nome; the crew noted nothing unusual; but a few days later, on its return trip, the ship almost 78

dashed head on against an island that had just appeared above the water. It proved to be a formidable island; the molten rock had solidified and pressure from within had pushed and broken large chunks of rock; and these chunks of solid rock, jagged and sharp, frowned from the edge of the water. A lake of molten rock covered most of the island. 

This is the land of bubbling islands. Our boat pulled up beside a new, fresh island; it had lately been thrust out of the sea; it is steaming hot, just off the fire. 

This new island is yet smoking and sizzling, steaming and sputtering, and giving off waves of heat that are stifling. 

Dead fish are floating near the shelves of rock, and some of them seem to be actually cooked. There are a few large fish but most of them are small; it is almost a winrow of minnows. It is hardly conceivable that fish will continue to swim into the hot and acid-polluted water from the open sea. This is an uncertain world; the life of a fish like that of any other form of life, may be snuffed out at any time. 

Probably when conditions are regular, the fish avoid the island of death; but when there is a disturbance, when the currents vary from their regular course, the fish follow their regular route, and they arrive at destruction. Where a mass of rocks jutted close to the surface, a flock of herring gulls were picking up the floaters.

The "land of the youngest island" is Bogosloff; it is an island or islands, "erratic, impulsive and hysterical". The history of this freakish and changeful bit of land is strangely fascinating. This land was first noted by the Russian 

Admiral Bogosloff in 1790, and it was charted as one island. But when the United States purchased Alaska, there were two islands. The islands were then known as 

Bogosloff and Castle Rock, the second island was a rock formation that suggested a castle. But Old Bogosloff grew, 79

and later New Bogosloff appeared; and then there were three islands. The strip of land connecting the two islands had suddenly thrust up from the sea, and this to the amazement of sailors who had been in the habit of sailing between the two islands.

New Bogosloff is four miles from Bogosloff and it remains the same as it appeared in 1887. In 1905, a new island, "Metcalf-Perry Peak", came up between these islands; and in 1907, a small mound-shaped island appeared; it was named "McCulluch Peak", but it disappeared in October of the same year.

In 1908, "Metcalf-Perry" split into halves, and one-half sank. Then by stages that were not carefully followed, a long, narrow strip of rock joined Old Bogosloff with New Bogosloff.

The crew of the United States steamship, Albatross, while trawling in this section, observed a peculiar disturbance of the surface of the ocean; the water would rise in large dome-like swells, much as a soap-bubble rises from the surface of suds. These bubbles would rise and subside, and with the subsidence, a great quantity of gas would escape.

These puffs and swells grew in immensity; it was the effect of a subterranean volcano in action. 

In 1910, the United States revenue cutter, Tahoma, noted a terriffic electrical storm. A black cloud was hanging over the island and a column of smoke and flame was spouting like a geyser from the sea. There were sharp flashes of lightning and crashes of thunder. Here was the "birth of a Bogosloff". Red hot, glowing rock was rising from the sea through swirling clouds of steam and smoke. This was Fire Island; a mountain of molten lava, rock, steam, smoke was shot into the air. From the center of a salt lagoon that had been formed on the island, there was an erup- 80

tion of cinders; it was an explosion that covered the country and the sea for many miles with volcanic sand and pumice stone. Out of this smoke, these terrible earth convulsions, this pyrotechnic display, masses of fine dust, seemingly afire, good-sized sparks and bits of red hot rock, there arose an island. "Old Moses", on Nunivak Island, emits a small stream of smoke; he is not violent, just a "fire eater". For a long time "Old Moses" was used as a beacon light for navigators from the northern Pacific Ocean into Bering Sea; the location is strategic; but as time went on, the light was not dependable and there is now a lighthouse at Unimak Pass. "Old Chernarboro" sends up a light smoke, but with it there is a strong sulphur gas, enough to kill the birds and animals that happen to come in contact with the fumes; but as we were passing along the channel, it seemed to be one of the most peaceful of the active volcanoes. Just a wisp of blue smoke rose gently above the rocky summit, and this played itself out before attaining a great height. "Akutan", on Akutan Island, is spasmodic and uncertain; it may sleep for weeks or months, then, without the slightest warning, there is a violent explosion. Electric sparks shoot out, and with each outburst of sparks, puffs of smoke roll out and ascend in clouds; after what seems a very long time, the clouds begin to drop dust and volcanic ash over a large area. 

The "Eighth Wonder of the World-Katmai” is situated on the mainland of the peninsula directly across from Kodiak Island. With a freakish mock modesty, it almost escapes notice, even though it dominates the entire chain of Alaskan volcanoes. Our visit to the region was more than a series of surprises. We had read Dr. Griggs' graphic account of "The Valley of Ten Thousand Smokes", but somehow 81

the impressions did not grow strong enough. No criticism of the article; the story is just too big to tell. "The roof of a powder magazine, sputtering fuse, and 

Kingdom Come", and this is the "Valley of 10,000 Smokes".

It is the rendezvous of devils; we look for Satan with his pitchfork. The first view of the smoking valley, the steaming inferno, is startling and un-nerving; and this is putting it mildly. It is unearthly. We have no comparisons; such similes as "the devil's kitchen" and "Satan's soap factory" carry a little suggestion, but they are weak. 

We look around to see Old Satan, himself, busily cooking his creatures over different fires, the degree of intensity varying with the wickedness that they practiced-all roasting and sizzling and burning, and giving off steam and smoke of various shades and colors. The odor of scorching meat, of burning feathers, of burning eggs, is not a fancy; we recognize burning sulphur and carbon-bisulphide gas; there are jets of fire and little puffs of steam. Dante was correct; his descriptions check with the facts.

We find nothing inspiring or sublime in the first close-up view of the weirdly strange and unearthly phenomenon. It is awesome, intimidating and fear engendering. We are astounded, stunned, rendered incapable of making intelligent observations.

This is the great earth factory; here is another great natural force at work. We know something of the forces of wind-tempests, cyclones, hurricanes, and of water-floods, swirls, moving ice-but this is fire, the great reducing agent, the force that reduces complex and complicated compounds and mixtures to their simple elements, that changes coal to gas and ashes and liberates heat.

With all that we had read about this valley of steaming geysers and smoking caldrons, we were not prepared for the 82

immensity of the display. Acres of smoking fissures, some in jets and puffs of steam or heavy smoke, others with a more steady upward flow, sometimes attaining great heights, but forcefully and unceasingly releasing pent-up heat energy, some of them working like safety valves; but it is a furnace, a field, a plain, a vast extent of burning lands. What feeds these fires? We speculate. All of this heat came from the sun; it has been conserved, stored-up, and now it is being released; yes, we read some of the outline, a few of the more common first principles, but the spectacle is too large; man is but a speck-even a fat man is but a sizzle. 

What of our great factories, blast furnaces and heating towers? Compared to this terrific energy, these titanic forces, man's endeavors are dwarfed to insignificance. 

We were told that the trails, and even the shelves, "so long as you use caution", were as safe as Broadway, New

York; but even that was not sufficiently assuring for a person of a conservative temperament. Where the smoke is jetting out and the edges of the rocky fissures are red hot, and it sounds hollow as you walk over the ground, it takes nerve-fool nerve-to walk up to the edge and stamp. In some places you can feel the earth tremble as you walk across it, and even the thump of a stick will sound hollow; and there would be no use to pull a man out. 

The feeling of "gazing into the bottomless pit" is intensified by the terrible presence of fire and will the earth be melted in a fiery furnace?.

We were also told that there was no danger from the effects of the "loud-smelling" and irritating gases that come from the fissures. With the wind in our favor, we would cautiously approach the edge of a great fissure; in some instances it was like gazing into a white-hot furnace. A little knowledge of chemistry is sometimes annoying. We 83

could recognize the odor of burning sulphur, and sulphur is burned to kill germs; it is highly poisonous. In the jets that arose from the fumaroles, it might be easy to recognize some of the gases as they separate from the big bubble and spread out and settle down in accord with their weight but for the fact that everything seems to come in big mixtures; the compounds are broken down or re-arranged, but the whole residue is sent out in one puff.

We got some consolation from one explanation of the action of the molten materials around the fumaroles. According to the explanation of this Old Timer, substances like iron and glass, when white hot and molten, are brought up with the bubble of the fumarole and there encrusted to the hollow undersurface around the vent, thereby reinforcing with a jug-like form the ground around the vent and making the fissure regular while building a roof over the cavern. It is nature's plan to cover up these mighty fires by building chimneys, well reinforced, that may in time become filled. Volcanic mountains are such chimneys. 

These fumaroles were deceiving. Sometimes a little jet of steam or smoke issuing from the ground would seem to indicate just a little opening-one that could be examined with safety. But when approached it might prove to be a monster vent; lighted from below by immense fires, it would seem like gazing into the inferno. Legions of leaping tongues, rolling sheets of red fire, white-hot patches gleaming with a fire that stunned the eyesight like looking at the sun. We learned to use caution when looking into these fissures, even though they were giving off comparatively little smoke or steam.

In one instance, it was a wonderful spectacle; bottomless, the subterranean cavity seemed to be; and it was glowing with intensity; the internal furnace seemed to be boundless; 84

it seemed to undermine the entire hill and the surrounding country. But what if the whole top should fall in?

According to our guide, who was something of a talker, one of these steam fumaroles becomes a model steam cooker. 

From a casual observation it seems possible. According to this version, "the pots are surrounded by an atmosphere of live steam. It is just at the point of condensation and nothing ever boils away or cooks to pieces. It does not matter how long we are away or if we forget about the supper-on-cooking; there will be nothing burned". No doubt he got his idea from Griggs, for photographs of food being cooked were published in the National Geographical Magazine with Dr. Griggs' article. 

It was the National Geographical Society that entered the field in 1912 to determine the exact cause of the terrific earth tremors and the shower of ashes that covered the whole Island of Kodiak with dust and ashes. Although Mount 

Katmai is on the mainland, a hundred miles from Kodiak Island, sufficient dust and ashes fell during a period of three days to bury the entire island to a depth of nearly two feet. At that time, dust and ashes fell at Juneau and also at Sitka. Nine hundred miles was considered too great a distance, and, while Katmai was mentioned in the early reports as a possible source, many people were skeptical. 

From careful researches, it is evident that the "Valley of 

10,000 Smokes" really opened before the mountain blew up.

Something had been going on in the interior of the earth. 

Beneath the valley, the river, and the lakes, beneath the mountain, great heat had been pent up. There was much fire, rocks and heavy material compounds had been burned, reduced to cinders. This dust had accumulated beneath the surface of the earth and that with a considerable pressure 85

from beneath. Then the valley opened, split apart, and the mountain discharged its accumulated materials. 

An area of more than fifty square miles was broken open. 

Such volcanic eruptions were common in older geological ages, but nothing like it had occurred in historic times. The 

United States Government made a national monument out of Katmai; it comprises 1,000,000 acres, much of which is barren, sand and ash-covered islands. It is surprising how quickly the various forms of plant life crowd back into the cinder-covered areas. Even in the vicinity of the great volcano, the trees have made remarkable progress. Next to the glaciers, vegetation has all but covered up the effects of the storm of ashes. On the Island of Kodiak there are just patches of trees of any size, but the island is covered with grass and flowers; it is a green island, and it requires real investigations to find the effects of the blighting shower. 

No doubt the volcanic ash now serves to add to the fertility of the green island, the home of the famous Alaskan grizzly bear.

While hot springs, mud pots and geysers are not the rule here as they are in Yellowstone Park, there are abundant reminders of all of these. There are minute hissing jets of steam issuing from areas of mud; such areas of "mud hissers" are more active after a rain. Sometimes this material suggests steaming-hot asphalt as it is sometimes applied to city streets. In some of this mud, it is easy to detect the rusty brown of iron oxide, iron rust. After a very heavy rainand such rain had fallen only a few hours before we arrived -there are many hot springs and pot holes of hot water. At this time there were "temporary geysers", and these were uncertain; they might spout out from an unexpected place, and that with little warning. We were told that after a 86

few days these steaming geysers and boiling springs would be displaced by mud pots and "growlers". 

Organic odors were present in the air. There was the strong odor of iron sulphide and ammonia, and some members of the party were sure that they detected coal tar and hydrochloric acid. According to our guide, ammonia can be found in some parts of the valley in the from of ammonia salt, sal ammoniac; it is so clean and white that it seems to be ready for use.

After walking around a while in a rather muddy section, our attention was called to the colorful mud that was adhering to our shoes. Some members of the party had accumulated enough multi-colored mud to decorate an artist's palette; one pair of shoes was daubed with a confused medley of deep-tones and half-tones, a galaxy of parti-colored muds. 

But when we began to seek the kinds of materials that made up the colors, we were soon at the end of the inquiry. 

Af course there was the red of iron with oxygen, iron rust, and the black of iron with sulphur, iron sulphide; but we were confronted with pearl-gray, brown, yellow and ochre. 

Falling Mountain, Novarupta, seems to be an example of a standing mountain that can continue to fall, never regain any lost materials, and continue to stand, and that with little apparent change in her general bearing. We were told what would happen; that there would be a fall of stones, and we did not have long to wait. As if an oracle had spoken, there was a crash; an avalanche of stones broke loose from high up in the face of the mountain, and these broken stones came crashing and rolling down. It is said that these avalanches of falling rocks occurred at five-minute intervals in 1916 and that the interval has been getting longer until now it is about forty minutes. 

Not the grandeur and sublimity of mountains and gla- 87

ciers, not the splendor and dash of a cataract or the eternal throbbing of the wildly splashing and tossing ocean; this is a scene of desolation; life's substance has been burned out; it is the inferno; every feature tends to instill dread and fear. Like a nightmare, it is fearful and wonderful, but we cannot get away from it. It is a graphic lesson, taught with overmastering force; we must respect natural forces-we must tremble when it thunders. "Old Pop-Off" gets her name from her eccentricities; this is one of the most strenuous and uncertain of all the unreliable volcanoes of the Aleutian Peninsular Chain. Only one thing is certain about this volcano-it is likely to "popoff". Whenever it has one of its "pop-off" fits the island of Shumagin, sixty miles away, is covered with ashes. "Makushan", near Unalaska, is continually smoking; it is near Dutch Harbor and a landmark of the vicinity. The natives are very much afraid of it-according to tradition. 

After much shaking of the head and much demonstration of fear, the price of a native guide can be talked down from $50 a day to $5 or even less. Some of the natives of this section are the most unreasonable grafters that can be found outside of New York City. But the country is really dangerous for people who are not familiar with its treacheries; there are pot-holes, thinly crusted over with icy snow, and underneath the thin crust the pot is boiling. Beneath some of the glaciers, there are springs of boiling water.

There is terror in the earth convulsions. When smoke and flame belch from a mountain or pour from the sea, birds and beasts and human beings must flee for life. It is like the end of the world. 

Inky-black clouds roll forth, blot out the sun in mid-day; showers of dust and cinders are falling; there are darting flashes of lightning and the whole earth trembles with the deep-toned rumblings of thunder; irritating and strangely stupefying gases fill the air; red paint is changed to brown, and metal mountings of silver and brass are blackened. Breakfast, consisting of sausage, bread and coffee, was taken rather deliberately. When someone asked Mr. Petersen, our guide, if it was sun-up, he replied that "About 5 hours, I dank". Sunrise occurs about 3 o'clock at this season,

The climb of the day before registered with some of us in stiff knees and sore muscles, but by the time that we were out of the canyon this was forgotten, especially since we had something else to complain about. The glare of the bright sun on the ice and snow was blinding when we emerged from the shadows of the deep canyon.

We were soon across the narrow field, but when we began to descend the cliff we were confronted by a new difficulty.

The general shape of the "hanging valley" is oblong, with one end abruptly broken off. Clear-cut, as though it was carved by hand, the effect is surprising.

It is wonderfully regular and the different layers of "shelves" next to the rim might be compared to some sort of elaborate decoration on a fancy tray. All of the ridges and concentric rings and half-moon circles of gray and white and transparent blue seem to form a sort of framework or setting for a turquoise jewel, a bluish-green sparkling lake that reflects sky and cliff, blue glacial facades and banks of glistening white snow. Bottomless as a mirror, but with a sparkling brilliance that could fairly dissolve and restore with but a change of a point in the angle of refraction, this jewel of joy, this iridescent and half-elusive glacial lake hangs in the mountain heights well above timber line. 

Geology writes with a slow pen. About 6,000 years ago a great, blue glacier extended the full length of this valley, but its work in rolling over precipices and grinding out basins could be compared to that of a stream of running water. Something like 9,000. years ago, this whole country was a vast field of ice extending from Taku Point across Norris, Taku and Twin glaciers, over the ice fields to the 

Canadian glaciers and west to a point near Lake Atlin. The marks, and we might almost say the date lines, are lastingly carved in the solid rock.

A half-moon shaped ridge extends from Taku Point around to about opposite Twin Glacier. To go directly 91

While two parties had been over it this year, one a United States government survey gang and the other some European climbers with Alpine guides in attendance, we classified ourselves as amateurs and trekked half way across the semicircle where we could skirt the high part of the ridge and descend a more gradual slope into the lower level. 

From the rim of the second level, this hanging lake appeared as a very deep setting in an elaborately decorated dome inverted on a side hill, but so clear and transparent that the edge of the water could hardly be traced among the concentric rings of rocky ledges. 

The concentric rings of rocky ledges were supplemented by icy cascades and overhanging ledges of snow. Like the brilliant centerpiece of this elaborately decorated cirque, reposed the lake. Deep blue, almost black in the center, the reflection of the sun's rays on the steep cliff directly opposite our position made a half-moon of indigo blue next to the columnar wall of immense icicles which formed a palisade for hundreds of feet where the water had been frozen as it slowly dripped down the steep cliff. Not a ripple disturbed the placid surface of the peaceful lake. Instead of crystal clear water, the whole firn might have been solid ice so far as our sense of sight was concerned, but for the fact that an eagle happened to be winging his way across these dizzy ice fields and his shadow dropped from the shelf upon which we were standing to the blue lake below and traced a somewhat distorted image that seemed to glide along below the surface of the water in the lake. It was this that gave the quality of liquid to the surface of the lake. 

This, the second hanging lake, was at the open end of the first, but at about a thousand feet lower elevation. For 92

From the open end of this hanging valley and some 600 feet lower, a third circular cavern extended in a broader and more flattened-out elevation just below a series of secondary depressions. This lake was more life-like; it seemed more like a real body of water, not like a painted picture or an etching in solid stone or marble. No doubt this hanging lake represents a much longer period of glacial history, more glacier action, than either of the lakes above. It is a young lake; for a glacier lake, it is very young; yet it is old in years.

According to the way that they reckon time, it seems that no less than 2,000 years were consumed during the time that this depression was being scooped out by the glacier. Years rolled on, and if the work of the glaciers was slow, it was no less sure. As the ice-level slowly changed to another position of comparatively stationary equilibrium, another tributary little glacier was left marooned at a lower level, and when this melted away, there was a fourth cavern with a hanging lake in the bottom.

Grandeur superb, unreal and unrealizable, mountains and monoliths, columns and frescoed images in ice and snow, blue walls of ice, fringes and shelves, overhanging snow curtains, sometimes almost transparent, of thin, snow-sprinkled ice, bearing tracery that would astound a student of ancient architecture, domes and cathedral spires built by the fairy hand of nature, all of this crowded into a landscape as 93

boundless as it was astounding and mystifying. This was the spectacle left by titanic ice forces when they carved their history in the igneous rocks that formed the mountain ranges.

If a man is troubled with egotism or self-importance, if he thinks that he is really big, that he is a determining force of any significance, let him study a glacier, or one glacial pebble. Some of these scratchings and scorings in the solid rock represent forces that were at work about 6,000 years ago, and the glacier, nature's first attempt at a refrigerator, has kindly preserved them for us to read.

Huge ice masses covered this part of Alaska about 9,000 years ago. The disappearance of this ice was not a steady process; it was punctuated by stationary periods in which the general level of the ice has remained the same but small tributary glaciers scoured out their valleys in the rock masses that projected above the ice. Other climatic conditions caused the general level of the ice to lower itself. When it became stationary again, some of the little tributary glaciers were left marooned up along the sides of the mountain. 

When these tributary glaciers melted away, they left basin-like cavities in the solid rock where the pressure had been the greatest, and basins became blue lakes. Evaporation continues even to this time but the loss of water is made up by the melting of snow and ice. This process can be traced at the present day. There is an offshoot of Taku Glacier just opposite the Twin Glacier camp that is forming its basin-lake. Slowly but surely, it is melting down; in the course of comparatively few years its firn will delight the first hikers of the season.

Following the valley for a few miles, there was a fifth level with its inverted dome and a lake of clear, blue water in the cupped-out rock. Here, then, is a series of five hang- 94

ing lakes, and even a casual study of them reveals that there will be a sixth, and the exact location of the sixth hanging lake. How long a time? Two or three thousand years. In geology, that is but a span.

There is a lure about the trails made famous by John 

Muir. A really great man may leave a finger print on anything that he touches. The paths of greatness are largely a mental attitude. Big and profound, yet cultivated in the sublime art of simplicity, John Muir made a lasting impression upon the people with whom he camped and climbed glaciers. He is remembered and loved by these natives as well as by those for whom he wrote the beautiful descriptions of Alaskan scenery.

So many of the "old timers" are ready to tell of their excursions, their camps and their record-breaking climbs over the glaciers with the celebrated "naturalist of the wild places" that a stranger is first led to suspicion the accuracy of the details.

John Muir's work in Alaska extended over a period of several years. He was very active, and he was associated, in various ways, with many of the inhabitants. While there may be a tendency to put extra coloring in the detail, the stories, in the main, accord closely to the authentic accounts of the trips. 95

The country northeast of Wrangell, and especially that immense glacial field that offers Taku to the astonished world, its ice fields, rugged mountains and immense glacial torrents that continually drop icebergs into the sea and offer a suggestion as to what the country must have been during the glacial period, all of this ice-bound wasteland area, might be known as Muir's Alaska. 

According to the "old timers", the "Twin Glacier Trail" led straight across the divide in John Muir's time. Now it makes something of a rainbow curve. This is due to the shifting of the ice banks. The upper reaches of the trail were always winding. Millions of them, in blue and green, in rainbow colors, they revel in dazzling brilliance. 

Here the great naturalist stood and gazed and thrilled at the prospect-green-feathered mountain sides, a band of blue just above timberline, snow caps, sparkling and white, serrating the blue heavens. Other people had been there before, many of them, but they thought of the difficult climb, of their need of refreshments and of the necessity of getting to some particular place. But the naturalist of glaciers took time to see the beauties, to enjoy them and to bring them back to a rather indifferent world. As it takes one especially trained or if specially interested, he will soon become trained-in the matchless school of intimate association with earth sculpture to appreciate, to read a glacier. It takes an artist to see a painting, and a glacier is more than a work of art.

Ten thousand restless, wayward and overwrought human beings thrown together without the organized force or the recognized restraints of society could not be expected to behave well-and they did not. Saloons and gambling houses and dance halls got started some way. We have been told that there were preachers and missionaries and men of good repute among the "Klondikers"; that is undoubtedly true, but the history of the times is not concerned largely with churches and religious meetings, or many serious attempts to reform battered and broken humanity.

From the "old timers", we get the picture of a wicked, wide-open mining town at its worst. There was boisterous noise and hilarity. There were brawls and fights; men allowed themselves to become beastly drunk, and they acted like beasts. They would impose upon one another, and the worst nature was unrestrained. The big brute would lord it over his weaker fellows until someone would put an end to his insults with a lucky shot. The announcer "bellows", and the roulette wheel clicks; the whisky glasses clink and the beer mugs come down with a thud; and the loud songs and boisterous laughter almost drown the "crack" of the assassin's pistol.

Men coming in from the lonesome trails where they had struggled with the snow and ice for weeks and months, lonesome men who had with great difficulty succeeded in gatherin a little "dust", these were men plunged into a dizzy whirl of vice. ODERN changes and ways of life have in some way reached the remote sections of the earth, and the lone trapper of Shinik Lake, Yukon, is not an exception to the rule. The old time trapper who was a child of the wilderness almost as much as the animals that he trapped, has been displaced by a modern, professional trapper who is an outdoor man skilled in the art of meeting conditions, and courageous to face difficulties; but he is also something of a scientist; possibly not from the technical or academic standpoint, but from the practical application, gathering scientific data and applying natural laws. He reads books and he reads tracks, tooth marks on the bark and empty egg shells; yet, above all of this, and what seems to be the most important, he is something of a business man.

The Alaskan Brown Bear 

T HE great brown bear of Kodiak Island, Alaska, is the largest bear in the world. It is also the largest carnivorous or flesh-eating animal. There is some controversy as to the size of the largest specimens. Some big game hunters say that the living representative of their trophies weighed as much as 2000 pounds. Such questions are difficult, but living specimens have been exhibited in museums and their weight has been ascertained to be near the 1500-pound mark. 

John Kendrick Bangs discusses this question: "It is all in the point of view. Behind the iron bars at the zoo, the bear may seem small and tame. But out on the bare hillside, after you have fired your last shot, and missed, after you have been running at highest speed for a time, have thrown away your gun and find that you have increased your speed just a little, you take a careful look across your shoulder and see that he is actually gaining on you, then he is a big bear, a big, big bear."

Since he is the biggest bear in the world, it is not worth while to quibble over his exact size. Then they raise the question as to his courage. Here, again, is "point of view" important. The Story of Babydoll 

A LASKA was no place for a woman during the gold rush days. But women have a way of getting in on all occasions. Danger, excitement, privations and the most severe strain of physical endurance only seem to be a sort of incentive.

The women who appeared in the rough mining camps. were quickly divided into two classes: the weak, which included those who were lacking in the moral fibre and the strength of will to endure privations and resist temptations, and the strong, who could meet the stern conditions of "lifein-the-rough", who could maintain their standards. 

The annals of those turbulent days record a few strong, enduring women, who could work, and fight, and even die without yielding a principle. A name might represent chance, a whim or a combination of circumstances. The name, "Babydoll", might represent contrast. A short, powerful woman with a heavy chin, she added no feminine touches to her make-up. An equal among men, her cold blue eyes were not a threat, but a danger.

Dressed in the rough garb of a workman, and with a sixshooter hung to her belt, she appeared among the miners at Chilkoot Pass. She was friendly with everybody, but she had no intimates. She asked no favors. She endured the hardships of the trail without complaining. As one of the miners expressed it, "she done rather less cussing than the rest of us." 

With the "greenhorn's luck" she got one of the good claims, and with a common sense that was rare in those days she refused to dicker and sign away her rights.

There is an element of mystery about reading a totem pole; it is something like interpreting feminine beauty in that it may require imagination and that it always depends upon a personal equation; however, to the clansman who has been instructed in the mysteries and endowed with the proper mental attitude, the totem pole tells of his ancestors and of their great achievements and of their mysterious visitations and embodiments of spirits of the animal world. 

At Ketchikan we found two famous totem poles, the Johnson totem and the Kyan totem. For the reading of these poles, we are indebted to Mr. Hansen of Pruell's Gift Shop, a curio shop which is patronized by tourists. Debunking the Arctic

ILHJALMUR STEFANSSON went into the frozen north and found that thousands of Eskimos never saw an igloo.

Any man who has accomplished a strange or difficult piece of work becomes a subject of special interest. As the populace in the fifteenth century were thrilled with stories of the travels of Sir John Mandeville, so people from every walk of life in our modern civilization have been enthused by the recital of Doctor Vilhjalmur Stefansson's explorations in the "Friendly Arctic".

Inasmuch as Doctor Stefansson represents experience rather than "research", his was an acquaintance rather than an introduction. Cutting loose from supply trains and communication with the known world, he passed into the mysterious land of the Arctic night. He went to the Eskimos as a wayfarer dependent upon their friendship and hospitality. He entered the unknown. 

North of the Arctic Circle there is a land of romance.

From early childhood we have been taught that the North 

Pole suggests privations, strange difficulties and daring feats; and that is a challenge to aggressive men. It appeals to the heroic. Deeply imbedded in the native character of the most civilized man there is an element of daring. We crave excitement, exploits, strange experiences. We are ready to take risks to attain the unusual. Doctor Stefansson's work touches a responsive chord; it appeals to the universal. 

With other men of world renown, he came to Houston to lecture at the convention of the Texas State Teachers Association November 24 to 29, 1930. During a part of his stay it was my privilege to act as a sort of host, a guide about 163

the city and something of an adviser as to the interesting features of plant life in the immediate vicinity. His schedule was stretched to include six public appearances in one long day.

Two of his lectures were before very large audiences. 

Six thousand people heard his address at the City Auditorium. We saw him in a strenuous speaking campaign that might compare to one of the best of the late William Jennings Bryan.

While dodging back and forth between speaking dates, it was not possible to have a real outing with him in our Southern woods, although he had expressed a desire to partake of "the hospitality of a typical Texas woods". Even with a few minutes along the bayou below the Country Club in the Forest Hill area, there was little opportunity for a real appreciation of the "columnar cypresses and the trailing Spanish moss" that might be termed "typical features" of the natural and unspoiled beauty of the bayou. 

Genial, modest, but thoroughly efficient, this modern adventurer possesses many of the attributes that have gilded the old-time heroes and emblazoned their names on the scroll of undying fame. We are impressed with his reserve power. 

Physically, he is almost a giant among ordinary city men. 

He lacks only one-half inch of being six feet tall; his shoulders are broad, his muscles well knit, and his body well proportioned. A football coach would appraise him as a good prospect.

Probably there is no connection between his thick mass of silky tan hair and his long residence in the Arctic region, but his hair attracts favorable comment. Such a growth of fine, wavy, almost curly, hair would be the pride of a society belle.

In addition to physical power, there is evidence of mental 164

He knows the importance of small details and he struggles to keep the large and the small in the correct perspective.

Doctor Stefansson has spent ten winters and thirteen summers in the arctic regions and it would seem that he is in a position for "debunking the Arctic". This he proceeded to do. "I first want to remove from your minds the imaginative North that never was in this world on land or sea," he said. "The Arctic Circle is twice as large as the United States and it contains many lands and many seas," and there are about 40,000 Eskimos in the Arctic Circle." Much of his theme of "The Friendly Arctic" hinges about the prevailing custom of many authors of textbooks in making generalities from isolated instances. He thinks that a serious injustice is being done by allowing children to think that all Eskimos live in igloos, made of blocks of ice. 

More than half of them have as little knowledge of houses made of blocks of ice as the people in Texas; "thirtytwo thousand of them have never seen a snow house". Some of them do live in igloos of ice and snow and he gives a most interesting account of his experiences while he spent "one winter in the same room with twenty-seven Eskimos".

They were cozy and warm, so comfortable in fact that most of the men went without shoes, shirts or socks. Some of them even reduced their wearing apparel to a single strip or body cloth about the hips.

In these snug ice houses, men perspire freely and it is necessary for them to provide themselves with a box of shavings, something like the excelsior used in packing, with which they carefully wipe and dry the body before they don their fur-lined oilskins for venturing outdoors. While his experiences are unusual and sometimes highly romantic, they are given without color or a hint of egotism. 

He went to the Arctic and found it friendly. Under strange conditions he was an industrious student, a faithful seeker after the facts and a resourceful traveler. He was able to meet unusual conditions in getting about in a strange land, a land in which the prevailing language is even stranger than the land itself, where certain words have been warped into a conglomerate dialect. 

He is the "Lucky Swede" whose luck is made up of 95 per cent hard work and 5 per cent "stickability". This, with a will power to doggedly struggle through difficulties without considering an alternative, has enabled him to attain some wonderful results. With this attitude of mind, success came with such ease that he was inclined to minimize his exploits. His "Friendly Arctic" would not seem so friendly to the casual traveler who started out to seek a little excitement.

It took pluck and determination and a willingness to endure hardships, but he was able to meet the conditions. To mingle with the native Indians and Eskimos and to live off of the resources of the country required a peculiar type of tact, much perseverance and an adaptability that few people would care to cultivate. It is refreshing, it is inspiring to meet such an old-time story book character of this day and time.

The spectacle of a man wrestling with the forces of nature and the uncertain element of meeting and mingling on even terms with strange people, these are the features of the legendary heroes; and modern men have inherited a romantic instinct. 

We are strangely moved by the recital of commonplace incidents when we have invested the individual with the element of romance that springs from our inherent nature. 

We are not surprised or thrilled so much as we are fascinated by the congenial and commonplace of this modest fellow man. From Doctor Stefansson we learn much about the Arctic Circle, the Eskimos and the problems of life in the barren northlands, but we also learn something of the charm of modesty and the friendly pulling power of an honest recital of experiences just as they occurred. 168

HE toothsome halibut has attained fame. Even in lands where the choicest sea-foods are served every day, halibut steak is a strong contender for popular 

Lobster, caviar or oyster crabs may be tried for a meal or two, but juicy and white sections, yielding and flaky sections, meat with the flavor of strawberries and cake, and packed in musical rhythm, with beats and gurgles of delight, halibut is the old-time but ever-new cradle song for people whose gustatory tastes have been cultivated. It is not whipped cream or egg-and-sugar frost, not angel cake; it is buoyant but refreshing. A halibut steak is an anthem that wafts a hungry man into realms of blissful repose-but from this dreamy stupor he will return to practical affairs with a wonderful rejuvenation of body and soul. 

It is a flat fish, a deep sea flounder, a soft-bodied aristocratic fish that sprawls out, flat-sided, on the bottom of the ocean where there is plenty of room; fed by the waves it is like a corn-fed pig or beef; it grows and fattens and grows some more until it attains an immense size. Only four kinds of fish are larger: tuna, tarpon, mola and swordfish.

The largest halibut on record weighed 720 pounds; it was recorded by Neilson, a Swedish authority; but most of the records for the largest halibut are decidedly less. Captain 

Atwood gives the figure as 359 to 401 pounds; Stern gives 

350 pounds for his largest; Dr. Anderson, Inspector of Fish for British Columbia, says 200 pounds for his district, but 179

Dr. Goode gives a record for 10 or 12 of the largest halibut taken on the New England Coast, and they range from 300 to 400 pounds.

For such a large fish, a halibut is surprisingly thin and flat; a 350 pound fish is about eight feet long and four feet wide. There is a great difference in the size of the sexes; the female is much the larger under the same conditions of growth; where a male would attain a weight of 50 pounds, a female would pile up and spread out until she tipped the scales at 150 pounds. The largest individuals are not the best for the table; halibut weighing a little less than 100 pounds are the most in demand; a fat female weighing about 

80 pounds seems to be the choice in most markets. The males are a little less desirable than the females. Small halibut, called "chicken halibut", weighing from 10 to 20 pounds, are particularly sought by epicures in New York and Boston; "chicken halibut" tops the market in those cities, but that is a local condition, and it was probably brought about by chefs who served these steaks in a very attractive setting.

Like the other flounders, the halibut has indulged the lazy habit of lying on the sea bottom until his two sides have become adapted for "upper" and "lower"; the upper side is dark gray and brown, just the right color to blend into the general shade of the sea floor. Like the skates and rays, the halibut basks comfortably on the sand while his hungry enemies swim around above him; even if they search for him, they have little luck; there is much space, and the halibut blends into the landscape.

There is little sign of age and decrepitude among halibut. 

Like alligators, it seems that they just grow big as they grow older, and, until they have attained a very great size, become awkward, and are burdened with an increased demand for food, but they are never aged.

The under side is white, just what would be expected to result from lack of light. But the eye that belonged on the lower side has wandered around the head, and it gazes from the edge of the upper side as a sort of supplementary to the larger and centrally-located eye that stares straight up from the reclining fish.

When moving about, it is an active fish; it catches small fish by quick, wolfish bounds. When hooked or gaffed, it puts up a real fight; but its peculiar shaped and unwieldly body furnishes opportunities for the skilled fisherman to take advantage; an amateur usually lets his first large halibut get away.

There is only one member of the genus: the common halibut (Hippoglossus hippoglossus). It ranges from Bering 

Sea to San Francisco; the most important fishing grounds for the Pacific halibut are in the Gulf of Alaska from Yakutat to Kodiak. Here they take the choice halibut for the large city markets. This is very cold water; glacial streams pour in ice-cold floods and icebergs float during the entire year. Some day, we may find out why it is that the temperature of the water makes a difference in the flesh of the fish that swim in it. "Left-handed halibut" are a reality; about one in every 

5,000 individuals is left-handed; it lies on its right side and it has its eyes in the left side of the head. 

The "Halibut Express" carries nine cars of fresh halibut 181

from Vancouver to Boston; it has the right-of-way and it is depended upon to supply certain meals, much like the milk trains; a delay in transportation or a failure to get in a supply of fresh halibut would mean a disappointment to the hotels and restaurants and to private families. 

The halibut catch of Alaska amounts to about $3,000,000 a year. The halibut boats are small craft; there are usually one or two men to a boat; the best fishing grounds seem to be in deep water that is close to the shore; in the deep bays and fiords about the rocky islands along the southwestern border of Alaska, the choicest halibut are taken. 

Halibut are invisible to other fish when lying on the sand at the bottom of the sea, and it is possible that they simply waylay and eat some other fish; but they do not depend upon their aggressive resemblance to their surroundings; they are colored like the sand or the mud upon which they rest but this is more a protection from their enemies than a method of getting a living.

They are active when they are hunting; and they are voracious, sharp-toothed, large-mouthed, wolfish hunters of fish. When such food is at hand, they feed upon crabs, mollusks, lobsters, and they are not above such scavenger fare as fish-heads and the backbone of cod that are thrown overboard; but to call them the "hogs of the fish world" is something of a slander; they are not scavenger fish. They come to the surface often; they pursue and feed upon skates and rays, cod and haddock, mackerel and menhadden, herring and grenadiers, flounders and sculpins, and even some of the smaller sharks; they are active, lively hunters when they are hungry, but they seem to have mastered the great lesson of sprawling out and resting comfortably when they are not hungry; they are not nervous, or playful; they simply rest "between meals". 182

Because of their solitary habits of remaining scattered over very large areas and the fact that they are not easily trapped, and when they are resting on the sea-floor, they are beneath the depth of the greatest nets, they are yet outside the reach of the immense hauls of gill nets, fish traps and fish wheels; the greatest commercial fishermen must go after them single-handed and on something like even terms. But even this can be overdone; the halibut have been literally fished out of the waters of the Atlantic Coast. Some fifty years ago, these great flat fish were plentiful in Massachusetts Bay and in the land-locked waters to the north, but a fisherman in that region could hardly identify a halibut if he happened to take one in these days of over-fishing. A member of the United States Fish Commission is authority for the statement that the great Alaskan halibut fishing grounds is being over-fished and carelessly fished; he thinks that a careful survey should be made and careful plans worked out before the history of the eastern coast is reenacted on the western coast. 183
