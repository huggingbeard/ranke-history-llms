PREFACE 

The years 1937-38, during which these pages were written, were full of dramatic and far-reaching events. And yet it seems as if they formed only a beginning, an introduction to greater and far more important transformations. In East 

Asia a vast empire has risen out of the débris of the Sino- Japanese conflict. In India and in the Near East tension is mounting; storm clouds are gathering. In Europe a political drama is unfolding, so rapidly, so breathtakingly, that millions of radio listeners the world over follow its individual scenes with anxiety and panicky fear. The western hemisphere, too, is undergoing changes whose end cannot yet be foreseen. Oswald Spengler's words come back to mind: "We lived momentous times; the stupendous dynamism of the historical epoch that has now dawned makes it the grandest, not only in the Faustian civilization of Western Europe, but -for that very reason-in all world history, greater and by far more terrible than the ages of Caesar and Napoleon." 

This account attempts to portray the radiant and confusing colors and the burning problems of the present-day world as they were revealed to the writer on a journey, recently completed, through Asia, Europe and America. It attempts to show how intimately related, in cause and effect, are the events from East and West that fill the columns of the newspapers. It endeavours, furthermore, to present a solution-perhaps the only possible solution-for the gravest of our problems: war or peace among the Western nations and in the East. This solution may appear naive to some and shocking to others. It is not new. The author often heard it discussed in Europe and Asia, though it was mostly beclouded by fears and emotional resentments.

To form a realistic picture of the world at this time is vital to our very existence. The world must be seen as it is, not as it should be. Political realism-it must be stated at the outset is the supreme command of an epoch in which, irrevocably, politics is supreme. Political realism is the supreme command in our age that is an age of fear and uncertainty. Realism is not materialism. True realism recognizes the ideal forces that move man to do great and heroic deeds. Realism is not opportunism, a shrewd and cowardly

subservience to everchanging petty necessities. Realism is not short-sightedness; it recognizes great realities also in the distance. It is not lack of phantasy. The great realists in history were the great builders, whose minds moved forward to form new structures out of the existing materials. By recognizing the impossible, the realist will, with greater effectiveness, work for the possible. Only if paired with rigid realism can idealism be true to itself. 

But seek as we will for rational explanations and for causes, there always remain realities, unexplained and beyond reason. The rise and fall of nations, of dynasties, of civilizations transgress the limits of our rational comprehension. 

The will of a nation to create a culture, to expand, and to carry that culture to others, to become the center of an empire, nay, the center of the universe, is like the will of creative man itself. It is the universe that the painter wants, when the demon of creation forces his brush. The universe it is, that the sculptor molds with strong but trembling hands, that the poet writes about in painful silent nights. 

Do we ask: why? Do we ask: why just at this present moment? Is there a past and present in creation? There is only a future! Eternity becomes present when the forces of creation are at work. Men are like cities or nations, and nations-whole continents-are like men: tools, instruments, victims of the spirit of creation. With the vehemence of a spring storm the creative spirit sweeps over frozen fields. 

Do we ask why? Out spring the forces that were sleeping underground. Life wants only one thing: life wants to be lived! Trees and flowers grow in myriads of varieties. They grow and perish. Do we ask why? We may measure the fertility of the soil, label the qualities of the seeds, and regulate the temperature of the air; we may have reasons and causes, since our mind hungers for causes. Creative man does not ask why; creative man creates. To him the "why" is only a part-so small a part!-of the universe, whose pulse he feels beating in his veins, driving, tormenting, inspiring.

A nation starting out on its march to "conquer the universe" is as the sculptor carving the marble. What it does is neither good, nor bad. It is. We cannot explain it, we can only grasp it with awe. To seek its limits is to search for the horizon. The forms of expression that the driving, creatvi

ing spirit adopts for itself depend upon an unlimited and unlimitable number of factors and conditions, upon accidents and incidents. Those the historian, the sociologist and economist will explain. A nation that strives to rule over others and to impress its mark upon them, or a nation that strives to throw off a foreign yoke these are manifestations of the forces of life that only intuition can comprehend. When 

Rome built the Imperium, when Albion built the Empire, two cities, Rome and London, became the centers of worldwide regions. Latin was spoken in North Africa, along the 

Danube and on the Channel, as English is now spoken in 

India, Canada and Palestine. Is it "good" or "bad", what the English did and the Romans? Is it "imperialism" or "capitalism” that moved them—them and many others at all times?

The conviction-or hope that the "age of imperialism” is past is wide-spread among Americans and Europeans. 

But empire-building is an eternal urge in strong races. It is a cosmic reality that cannot be combated by wishful thinking. If the nations of the West have forgotten how empires are built and defended, there are other races who have learned it. If the nations of Europe and America consider empire-building a thing of the past, there are other peoples to whom it is an inspiring vision. In the present decades it may be decided who will be the rulers of the globe for centuries that come.

Chapter I 

Banzai

An Empire in the Making 

In the northwestern part of the Pacific Ocean, at the very end of the European shipping lanes that lead to East Asia through the Mediterranean or around the Cape, about India and Singapore-more than four and one-half thousand miles away from the western shores of America and almost in the shadow of the wide mainland of Asia-lies a group of small volcanic isles: Kyushu, Shikoku, Honshu, and Hokkaido. As the British Isles lie at the western end of that vast expanse of earth that stretches from the China coast to France and Spain, so the Japanese islands lie in the East, where the sun rises over Asia. Rigid and unyielding are the mountains and gorges that restless forces of nature have piled up and carved for hundreds of centuries on the islands now called Nippon, and the smell of the pine trees is sweet that the winds carry from the shadows of the valleys to lofty snowy peaks. Rigid and restless, sweet and soothing as the nature that created the islands is the spirit of that strange people who since time immemorial inhabit Nippon. In seclusion the islanders lived for centuries fishing and cultivating their fields, building and rebuilding their little wooden houses whenever nature, in a hard and cruel mood, through earthquakes, typhoons or fire, destroyed them. In seclusion, they lived through the ages, worshipping Amaterasu, the Sun Goddess, their avowed ancestor, and the good and evil spirits which reveal themselves to men through the elements of nature and through the spirits of the dead. When the teachings of the Lord Buddha were brought to Japan, after they had wandered from India to Malaya, to China and Korea, the Japanese people made Buddhism their own, amalgamating it with the old beliefs of Shinto. Praying before the sacred shrines that stand in quiet loneliness in the dark forests of 

Nikko and Nara, bowing to the smiling majesty and allsaving power of Amida Buddha, the Yamato race lived on its passionately-beloved isles. Fighting bitter and bloody wars amongst themselves, struggling in never-ending feuds, instigating and crushing rebellions, the feudal knights of

Japan led an heroic and dangerous existence. Spartanic simplicity, a readiness to bear hardships and pain silently, a willingness to sacrifice everything-first of all, life-to honor and the higher duties, combined with a sublime capacity to enjoy the most refined pleasures that nature and art can offer-this is the spirit of the Samurai. The Japanese home, with its sleeping mats, with a simple and delicate flower arrangement as its only decoration, placed unsymmetrically, almost casually, yet with deep and harmonious forethought in a corner before a paper window, is a symbol for the living spirit of ancient Japan. 

Seven decades ago, with the suddenness and vehemence of a typhoon, Western industrial civilization broke over feudal Nippon. The Emperor Meiji reached the decision that his nation, if it was to maintain its national existence and independence, must accept the industrial achievements of Europe and America. Although social and political conditions in 

Japan were favourable to such a decision, the speed with which it was carried out brought many bitter hardships. 

The feudal knights struggled in vain against the new age. 

Thousands of toll gates, an important source of income for the noble families in the olden days, were abolished. The power of the Shoguns, a group of mighty feudal families, was broken. Japan, politically and commercially, was to become a centralized state. Many impoverished knights or their descendants eventually found refuge in the fighting services that the new régime built up, following the lead of the Anglo-Saxon nations on the sea and the Prussian model in the army. In the officers corps of the imperial army and navy the old fighting spirit of the Samurai, the uncompromising spirit of honor and self-sacrifice, is preserved in its truest form. But in the social and political life of the nation new classes and new types of men have come to the foreground since Meiji Tenno reached the historical decision to "modernize" Japan. The evolution from medieval feudalism to a capitalist industrial society, a process which occupied the West for several centuries, was achieved in Japan in several decades. In spite of the drastic changes, however, Japan remained overwhelmingly rural; the small farmer and craftsman constitute the basis of her economy, many feudal institutions and a great portion of the feudal spirit were carried over into the new age. Old Japan has preserved itself in spite of electricity and the automobile; it persists in the towns and villages with their tiny shops and inns, gaily illuminated with lamps and lanterns in the evenings when the families sit around in their half-open homes, enjoying the cool breeze after a laborious day in the rice fields and watching the bathers stroll to and from the bath houses in their kimonos a picture peaceful and infinitely joyful.

Upon the foundation of a semi-feudal peasant and craft 10

economy with the smallest units of production there has been built a gigantic structure of capitalist enterprise during the past half-century. The feudal lords who owned the land that their "followers" cultivated in the old days were replaced by modern capitalists. A few families today own a high percentage of Japan's farm land and lease it to the tenants. The most important of Japan's great factories, steel and cotton mills, mines and shipyards, many of the great banks and insurance companies, the large shipping and trading corporations which experienced a development fully as spectacular as that of the North American continent in the second half of the nineteenth century-all this belongs to one of the few giant holding companies and concerns controlled by Japan's great commercial families. The Mitsui and Mitsubishi are the Morgans and Rockefellers of Japan. The capitalist plants, bristling with energy, the department stores and bank palaces, adorned with rows of Corinthian pillars, rose rapidly above the flat bamboo roofs of the old towns. Blinding advertisements flash into the night sky of Tokyo from fashionable stores along the Ginza just as they do in New York, Melbourne or Paris. Legions of Japanese scholars and engineers have studied in the West, and the genius of the country absorbed the new ideas and forms and adapted them with astonishing ease to the different spiritual and physical conditions at home. As Buddhism once has been brought to 

Japan from the nearby mainland of Asia and was absorbed, transformed, and assimilated by the religious spirit of the nation, so the institutions of the West: factory organization, democracy, parliamentary government, civil, commercial, and penal codes, stock exchanges and modern schools, were introduced from far-away countries into the life of Japan, to experience there the same process of transformation and amalgamation that the teachings of Buddha had experienced a millennium before. There is something extremely rational and planful in the way in which the Japanese selected and copied whatever was presented to them by the various Western countries. But the genius loci-contrary to most other nations of the East-proved to be stronger than the foreign ideas that inundated the Japanese cities. Untouched remained the ancient beliefs, untouched the old customs and traditions. The God-Emperor remained the sacred center of 

Nippon even throughout the era of industrial democracy. 

Whenever the wave of modernism or Westernism seemed to grow too strong in the habits and tastes of the people, a reaction from the very bottom of the Japanese soul, carried forward by those who considered themselves the heirs and guardians of the ancient Samurai tradition, counteracted the cosmopolitan and all-too-rationalistic tendencies. 

Thus the features of modern Japan are molded by a powerful dualism: ancient traditions and myths, rising from the 11

depths of a soul that reaches beyond time and space, are met by an activistic and rationalistic determination to master the problems presented by the age. Melancholy and wise is the eternal smile of the Buddha of Kamakura before whom men and women bow; deep is the reverence and intense the awe inspired by the sound of the temple gong as it reverberates through the dark forests. This is one face of Japan. 

The other need not be described; it is that of the world-city of the twentieth century with subways, bars and smoke stacks. The one is as real as the other, and the contrast between them appears infinitely stronger and more vital than are the contrasts between "old" and "new", between tradition and modernism within any other great nation in the world. 

The power of this dualism is an indication as well as a reason for the mental strength of the Japanese people. It is one of the covert forces that has made Japan a world power. 

When Japan, in a few decades, raced through the stages of industrial capitalism, she experienced what America and Europe had experienced before and were experiencing at the same time: big cities grew up rapidly; many hundreds of square miles in the metropolitan areas of Tokyo-Yokohama and Kobe-Osaka were covered with plants, storage houses, poor workers-quarters and slums. An industrial proletariat was recruited quickly from the lower middle classes and the peasants. The population has increased until now more than a million Japanese are born every year. Unemployment, however, a typical phenomenon of Western industrialism, accompanying every major depression, remained practically unknown in Japan. Unemployment is no problem for Japan largely because of the broad rural basis of the national economy and the patriarchal family ethics that prevail everywhere in the East requiring the members of a family to support each other in times of need. The self-contentment of the masses permits the low wage level that gears Japanese industry to highest speed and causes Japanese competition to be feared on the world markets. As in the West, social contrasts became more pronounced and were felt more sharply under capitalism, though they were perhaps actually not stronger than under the feudal system. But, whereas the 

Western states today are confronted with the difficult task of integrating their nations politically and spiritually and of counteracting the disintegrating tendencies of industrial capitalism, Japan has a firm basis upon which to build her modern political organization: the political-religious belief in the divine rule of the Emperor and the world-historical mission of the Yamato race. The strength of this faith is the spiritual basis for the self-contentment of the masses; it is the root of Japan's political and economic power. 

The love and devotion that the Japanese feels for his family, his native town and his country culminates in his 12

love and awe for the God-Emperor, the direct and chosen descendant of the Sun Goddess, from whom the entire nation springs. On rare occasions the Tenno is seen by his people. 

From the Imperial Palace, which lies like a medieval fortress surrounded by a river and ancient walls of heavy rock in the middle of the city of Tokyo, he may be seen riding on a white horse to the modern Diet building to open his parliament. All houses bordering the streets through which the ruler passes are evacuated. Thousands of citizens stand for hours lined up at a respectful distance, and finally, when the 

Emperor appears, they bow in reverent silence. To mention the Emperor in debates or conversations is not good taste in Japan. The modernization and rationalization of the country, together with the growth of big cities, have done their part to weaken the traditional religious spirit. The layers of an uprooted cosmopolitan intelligentsia which exist in Paris or New York exist also in Tokyo. But Japan seems to be far "behind" the West in this development. Even with rationalism at its height the Japanese realizes by intuition what the preservation of ancient beliefs and forms means for the life of his nation. Although, or just because he, as an individual, has gone through a process of rationalization, he preserves the old forms and customs, conscious of the strength that they lend him and the nation. His bow before the Emperor is the expression of his love and devotion for the fatherland, a symbol of patriotism. 

The fact that the spirit of religious devotion for the Emperor has survived so strongly gives the state leadership a firm grip over the masses. This does not imply that in Japan there exist no revolutionary underground movements. Movements of communistic or supernationalistic character do exist, as can be seen from frequent police reports on the arrests of people for political offenses. Japan is a country of secret societies and conspiratory clubs, a trait that has been preserved from the days of feudalism. In the history of the 

Empire conspiracies and palace revolts often have not come to a halt even before the throne. But the throne has always emerged from the troubles a sacred institution. Several years ago violent opposition to the policies of the government and the social order of the country, expressing itself through conspiracies, attentats and revolts, centered most strongly in the circles of the younger army officers. These "fascist" revolutionaries were feared more by the men in power, prior to the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese war, than any communist group. As the fascists in the Occident, the so-called fascist groups of Japan are anti-parliamentarian; they despise the political parties who "talk" instead of acting. They are anti-capitalistic, though not, in principle, against private property and private initiative. They hate the big concerns; they want to redistribute property and especially to improve 13

the lot of the small tenant farmer. They are anti-communistic, strongly nationalistic, ultra-conservative in their cultural views, and militaristic. Their ideology is "socialistic" in the sense in which the Prussian army spirit has always been socialistic: complete subordination of the individual interests, including those of bankers, traders, and manufacturers, to the interest of the state. The principal opponents of this trend in Japan are the big financiers, backed by sections of the civil bureaucracy, the party leaders and the intellectuals. 

Their readiness, however, to coordinate all energies of the nation under one all-powerful command which will consider sufficiently their particular interests has increased in recent years. Japan is moving closer and closer towards a totalitarian régime, a trend that is accelerated by the war with China. Far-reaching economic mobilization and utility nationalization laws, modelled after the German example, were adopted by parliament in the spring of 1938. Japan, thus, experiences what many countries of Europe are experiencing: the era of democratic parliamentary government is being followed by an era of authoritative state leadership; state planning is taking the place of a free economy; discipline prevails where liberties have been abolished. The old theocratic concept of the state, blurred by liberal-democratic influences, has been reborn.

II 

After Japan had entered upon the road toward industrial capitalism under the Meiji in 1867, she soon became fully conscious of the fact that she could maintain her new achievements and her very existence only by further and greater achievements. The laws that were governing the economies of Europe and America began also to govern hers. 

The dynamics of industrial capitalism demanded expansion. 

Japan needed raw materials for her industries, and markets for her finished products. She needed room for her growing population. Her islands are small and mountainous; her soil is poor in minerals and raw stuffs. The road for Japan led towards imperialism just as England, France, Germany and the United States were led toward imperial expansion. But can the rise of a people from the status of a small nation of contented islanders to a first-rank world power in the short course of a few decades be explained in terms of "markets" and "raw materials"? Can the bristling energies of the Japanese, which keep the world breathless, be analyzed in terms 14

of "surplus population" or "capital profits"? Economic motives do play a strong part in Japan's undertakings on the Asiatic mainland. The north China area, which Japan conquered in the fall of 1937, is rich in coal, petroleum and metals which Japan needs. The project of developing China under Japanese leadership will create new markets for Japanese industry which finds itself confronted with high tariff walls in most parts of the world. Hundreds of thousands of square miles of fertile land, a practically inexhaustible supply of many important industrial raw materials, a population of several hundred millions, primitive and backward in most provinces, but eager and apparently capable of participating în industrial civilization under an experienced and energetic leadership-thus China presents herself to the Japanese entrepreneur. She not only lures Japanese enterprise, but she constitutes the major opportunity for the salvation of Japan as an industrially and technically advanced country. 

While the considerations that deal with tons of tungsten and iron ore and warehouses full of machinery and silk kimonos may explain some phases of Japanese growth and expansion, they do not explain all. Why is it that just Japan, not China, nor Malaya nor India is the leading nation in the evolution of the East? Japan's insular position, the character of her people, apparently better fitted mentally to adapt itself and better disciplined to fulfill the requirements of mechanized civilization than other Asiatic nations, the peculiarities of the Japanese soil, which hardened the people's resistance against misfortunes, the peculiar character of the Japanese religion and government-all these factors have been cited as contributing causes to the sudden rise of Japan. 

One reason for Japan's spectacular growth is that she possesses a ruling group of high quality in the military, diplomatic, economic and engineering fields, endowed with gifts that were inherited and developed through hard and persistent training. Strict, almost cruel from a softened Western point of view, are the exercises that the Japanese, though great lovers of children, force upon the young Samurai to harden body and mind for the tasks of a hard life. Spartanic and Prussian, truly aristocratic, is the spirit of the ruling cast of Japan. Greater duties, not greater privileges, mark the nobleman. The determination and skill of the carefully selected leaders are met by the willingness on the part of the masses of the people to obey the leaders and make sacrifices.

Probably nowhere else in Asia is the combination of qualities for leadership within the ruling groups and discipline on the part of the people developed with equal strength as in Japan. In China, in the Soviet Union, in India and Arabia, attempts are made to awaken a spirit of patriotism and selfsacrifice in the masses, and to engender confidence in themselves and their leaders. To train efficient and reliable staffs 15

in the military, administrative and economic fields is one of the outstanding endeavours of national leaders in wide parts of Asia. Chiang Kai-Shek pays as much attention to this difficult problem as the Shah of Iran, when he sends students abroad, founds universities, or calls in foreign advisers. 

Whereas in all other Asiatic nations the new political hierarchies have to be built from the ground up, very often against the outspoken opposition of the existing feudal aristocracies which are hostile to any innovations, whereas the political and even the most elementary patriotic spirit in the masses must, through great effort, be created or slowly awakened from centuries-old slumber, Japan had both at her disposal several decades-decisive decades!-ahead of all other nations of

Asia: a burning patriotism in the hearts of her citizens and the skeleton of a well-trained hierarchy. When the decisive hour for Asia's awakening came, following the first contacts with the industrial civilization of the West, Japan possessed an organic and strong central authority, a dynasty whose authority was on the rise after having been reduced to a shadow throughout long periods. With the exception of Japan, all large nations of Asia, including the Asiatic parts of Russia, have spent the past few centuries under the rule of foreign dynasties or colonial governors. No central authority of domestic origin could hope to be generally recognized, except after long and enervating struggles. Japan, alone, was a wellintegrated independent state, politically and spiritually prepared to play a leading part in world politics.

Manifold are thus the "causes" that can be cited to explain Japan's sudden rise to the status of a world power. Needed raw materials and sales can be measured in tons and pounds. 

National energies, the strength of traditions and the stability of a political structure can also be rationally comprehended as contributing causes in molding a foreign policy. But the will to become a world power, deep and unconscious, then suddenly awake in the soul of a people, the will to have others share the fruits of one's own culture, the very will to have a culture and to exercise power over others, cannot be analyzed in statistics, charts and cycles. No cause exists for it. It is something that is, that comes and grows and withers away like the life of a plant. The question has been asked: why does 

Japan resort to conquest in order to fulfill her "mission"? Why does she not attempt to reconcile China and trade peacefully with her? That this is exactly what Japan wanted and attempted to do for many years, will be the answer of a Japanese spokesman. The Chinese will retort that Japan has pursued a policy of pressure and aggression against China ever since there has been a modern Japan. How can Japan expect cooperation, the Chinese will ask, when she follows a policy of imperialist oppression? This dialogue has occupied the foreign offices and the press of the two countries for several 16

decades; it has been handled with a skill and patience on both sides typical of Oriental diplomacy and hardly comprehensible to the Western mind. Many Japanese of moderate opinion, industrialists, intellectuals, diplomats, may admit that through greater patience more might have been achieved with China; but in Japan, too, time is a factor in politics, and after the policy of moderation failed to show tangible results the protagonists of a more drastic policy, especially the military, the successors of the ancient Samurai, gradually won the upper hand.

As important as may be the economic and spiritual conditions of a nation in intrepreting the "Why" and "How" of its actions, they do not explain the rise and fall of empires. What 

Japan is doing today, the British have done for centuries. The 

Romans did it, the Persians, the Babylonians and the Aztecs. "Europe" did it when it invaded the country of the North and South American Indians. Japanese is being studied and spoken in Manchukuo and North China as English is learned in India and Arabia, as German once was spoken far into the plains of European Russia when the German knights and the merchants of the Hanseatic cities penetrated the Slavic wilderness. Empire-building-whatever form it chooses-is one of the fundamental urges in mankind, one of the irresistible forces that the creative spirit has chosen at all times to make man try to reach beyond himself and to conquer the infinite. 

Nippon has started out to conquer Asia, as the dynasty of Akbar once conquered India, impressing its marks on the landscape and upon the spirit of the people; as the Manchus and many others before them conquered and ruled China; as the Turks, only a few centuries ago, subjugated southeastern Europe, reaching to the very doors of Vienna; as the 

Arabs conquered north Africa and Spain; as the Aztecs did and the Persians, the Romans and the Phoenicians, the Spaniards, the British and the French; as world-history has always been, so far as the searching mind can reach, and always will be so long as the driving spirit of conquest and creation persists in men.

III 

For a quarter of a century after the Emperor Meiji's historical decision to "modernize" Japan, the energies of the nation were fully occupied at home. Then Japan, driven forward by social and spiritual forces, began her march to become a world power. This march she has continued ever since. In 

1894-95 the first conflict with China broke out over Korea; in 1904-5, encouraged through her friendship with Great Brit- 17

ain, Japan fought Russia; again, ten years later, she joined the 

Allies in the World War and at the same time exerted further pressure upon China. In 1932 she created Manchukuo, and in 

1937-38-events moved now with increasing rapidity-she established the North China régime in Peking and the Central China régime in Nanking. Less than fifty years ago Japan was but a small island state, far removed from the Western capitals where the fate of the world was decided. She was of little international significance, merely a comfortable ally of the large powers which tried to widen and strengthen their influence in the "Far" East by checkmating each other on the chessboard of China. Today, Japan demands parity with the two largest sea powers in the world; she possesses an army prepared to fight the Soviets, who boast the largest land forces in the world; she is the center of an empire controlling wide areas of the Asiatic continent and far-flung possessions in the Pacific Ocean reaching from the Sea of Ochotsk and the plains of Mongolia to the very doorsteps of New Guinea and Australia. Half a century ago hardly more than fifty million people obeved the Mikado. Today the subjects and "allies" of the 

God-Emperor, in Sakhalin, in Manchuria and Mongolia, in 

China and Korea, in Formosa and the South Seas, number well over 300 million people and their number is rapidly increasing. The birthrate is still rising in Japan, though more slowly than in previous years. That Japan has her difficulties in Manchuria, that even in Korea a certain amount of opposition prevails against Japanese control, is well-known. It will probably take years even under the most favourable circumstances to pacify the provinces of North and Central China where the Japanese armies have set up new governments. But all empires

But all empires have experienced difficulties of this nature. British troops to this very day fight regular little wars against hostile tribes on the northwestern borders of India; the Ethiopians have not been completely subdued by their Italian conquerors. A realistic observer of far eastern developments can hardly doubt the inner stability of the Japanese empire, at home and overseas, regardless of sporadic outbreaks of fighting in the conquered areas. Even before the conquest of China is accomplished, the 

Japanese have begun construction projects there that open the path to undertakings of unparalleled magnitude.

No signs are to be seen as yet that Dai-Nippon has reached its zenith. Japan appears determined to go further along the road of empire-building. Tokyo's statesmen, on several occasions, have proclaimed Japan to be the guardian of peace in East Asia. As vague as this statement, in itself, may be, a policy in accordance with it bears great potentialities; it has, perhaps justly, been called the Monroe Doctrine of Eastern Asia. Interpreted in the most moderate sense, Japan's rôle as a "guardian of peace", as a guarantor of pax Japonica, 18

includes her right and obligation to preserve peace and order in China and to protect all well-established rights in the zones where Japanese influence is sufficiently strong. Interpreted in the missionary spirit of the descendants of the Samurai it means nothing less than: Asia for the Asiatic nations under 

Nippon's leadership! In the latter form the reverberations of

Japanese policy penetrate to the most remote cities of Mongolia and Chinese Turkestan, to the Philippines, to Malaya and Siam. The French are worried about the growing unrest in 

Indo-China as much as the Dutch in the East Indies; in India and Burma hundreds of millions of people, awaiting the day of freedom, may someday find Japan a welcome ally in their struggle. Several of India's prominent leaders, irrespective of differences in religion, in philosophical and political outlook, look avowedly with gratitude and satisfaction upon Japan's endeavours, which they consider to be directed against the West. They express pleasure and encouragement in the achievements of Japan, regardless of strong emotional sympathies with the Chinese people. Wherever Asiatic nations dream of their day of freedom in the plains of Central Asia, in the temples of India and in the deserts of Arabia, the call “Asia for the Asians” is heard, one day like a fanfare, another day like a whispered prayer, but always with the burning intensity of which the souls of Asiatic people are capable. 

To the remotest little ports on the Persian Gulf, to Muscat and Bunder Abbas, come vessels that fly the flag of the rising sun. Japanese exports to the Asiatic countries rise from year to year in quantity and value. This is not only because Japanese commodities are inexpensive and by no means as inferior as Europeans and Americans please themselves to believe (they are, at least, sufficiently good to meet the needs of the native populations in Asia!), but also because the Japanese producers and traders study the particular needs and tastes of the communities to which they sell, manufacturing their products accordingly with a care that leaves the European and American competitor often far behind. Wherever Japanese light bulbs, daggers, or toys are sold, in Damascus, where "real" Damascus silk is imported from Japan, in the Hedjaz where the Arab women wear veils "made in Japan", in Baluchistan, in Bengal or Kutschbehar, where the bands on the ankles of the women, the vases in the temples and the prints on the walls not seldom have been manufactured in a Japanese shop, there are being told all over Asia tales of a nation of small brown men, living on little volcanic islands in the distant East, where the sun rises, a people very industrious and kind in manner, but clever, tenacious, ruthless, and unyielding in the pursuit of their aims. One story tells that this nation was the first Asiatic people to defeat a nation of white men, the Russia of the Czars, and that she again is engaged in a great war with the final aim of driving the white man out of 19

Asia and putting herself in his place. Interest in the Japanese 

Chinese struggle is strong in wide parts of Asia among those who have learned to think politically. To the mind of the Asiatic observer, however, the underlying conflicts between Japan and the West and between Japan and Russia which are fought out in this war overshadow by far the conflict between Japan and China. There may be little love for Japan among the 

Mohammedans of western Asia. In his inner soul the Hindu has, perhaps, no understanding for Japan's driving spirit, which seems so closely related to that of the hated white man; and also the Buddhists of eastern Asia may feel closer to the kindred Chinese than to the proud and Prussian Japanese.

But there appears to be unlimited admiration and a great deal of envy for Japan even in the hearts of her most bitter foes in China. There is a secret, often almost fearful, conviction widespread among Asiatic people that Japan's advantage over all other nations in Asia is so strong that she has become the destined leader in Asia's fight for liberation from the white man's rule, that she will irrevocably become master over Asia when the white man will have been driven out, and that she will remain so at least until other Asiatic nations will have risen to her level.

The national awakening of the Asiatic peoples is the key to Japan's greatest potential power. Only through it, can the 

Japanese-Chinese conflict of 1937-39, the Japanese-Russian tension, and Japan's relations to the Western powers be understood. The nations of Asia demand freedom from the white man's rule and the cry for liberation rings louder every year. 

The leaders of Japan know it and the Japanese people know it. They know it better than the peoples of Europe and America. With the white man's capital, through his skill and experience the wonders of Euro-American mechanization have been exported to the shores of Asia. The Asiatic countries were "developed" by means of Western techniques to serve the interests of the white man, to make profits for him, to protect him strategically, to give him power over hundreds of thousands of square miles of Asiatic land and over hundreds of millions of Asiatic men. Where it was too costly to annex land, "influence zones" were created and "police stations" were established, outposts at strategical points from which the hinterland or the neighboring oceans could be controlled. Shanghai, Hongkong, Singapore are such police stations. The genius of the white man spread them like a net all around the sleeping colossus of Asia.

The European powers came as competitors and rivals to Asia, as they were rivals in Africa and in America. It took 

Asiatic man some time to realize what the lack of unity among the invaders meant for him. For the Asiatic, who looked with awe and amazement upon the "white devils" who conquered time and space with their steam engines and roaring planes, 20

all this was at first part of one great and overpowering development. Great and everwhelming it was, in fact, this latest, if not last achievement of the white race on the road to conquer the universe! Men in India, in Arabia and China, hated it, but they bowed to it. It was irresistible. Asia's march towards mechanization and "progress" had become inescapable. Together with the white man's tools the white man's spirit had come to Asia. Slowly and gradually, but, from the point of view of Asian history, with an incredible speed the Asiatic nations awakened. They realized that the white man and his tools were not one and indivisible. They realized that the wheels might turn also in their own hands, and they tried to turn them. With the joy and pride of children the Chinese built their own first railroad. The nations of Asia also realized that the white man was not quite so strong and united, not quite the young God they once thought could make the light rise over Asia from west and north. The sun was still rising in the East! The leaders of the Asian peoples began to be possessed by one idea: freedom; and the statesmen of Asia began to develop the one type of diplomacy that an infinitely weaker opponent in a game can hope to play successfully against several stronger opponents: they played one rival against another. They have played and still do play this game with equal skill in Teheran and Baghdad; they played it in Nanking and Gualalulpur.

In 1905 there occurred something that even the most daring among the dreamers for freedom and independence in Asia did not expect at that time. An Asiatic nation defeated a European nation: Japan defeated Russia. At the time of the

Russo-Japanese War of 1905 the Russia of the Romanoffs was a European power in her spirit and the inner dynamics of her policies as well as in the minds of the nations of Asia. The victory of Tsushima established Japan's claim for leadership in the Asiatic mind. A few years after the Peace of Portsmouth was concluded, Kaiser Wilhelm II launched his famous appeal: "Europe, preserve your most sacred rights", and the ominous word of the "yellow peril" was first pronounced; this call did not go unheeded in Tokyo. Again, a few years later, the great war broke out among the powers of Europe; Sikh and Punjab regiments were led against the 

Central Powers; China and Japan were allies of England and Russia against Germany and Austria; the Arabs from 

Hadramauth to Alexandretta were aroused against the Turks and their friend, the Kaiser. For the first time in the history of the world all Asia took part in a struggle among European powers. Asia emerged from this struggle strengthened, rejuvenated, pulsating with new energies; 

Europe emerged weakened, tense and tired, looking forward fearfully to new battles, since the great battle of 191418 had failed to decide the issue among the members of the 21

European family and the "armistice" of Versailles had failed to settle their quarrels. Louder than ever resounded over Asia the cry for freedom.

The very same development that had swept over Japan with the vehemence of a typhoon, but yet in a strikingly wellordered and well-organized fashion, was now gradually stirring everywhere in Asia; a national or racial awakening, linked inseparably with the demand for technical progress and social improvement, began to take place in the middle classes of many Asiatic nations. It would, of course, be utterly misleading to draw comparisons too far; the circumstances in the different countries, nay continents, of Asia differ so widely that general terms such as "national" or "racial awakening", "industrial progress", or even such terms as "nation" or "independence" must be used with utmost care if they are to have any meaning at all in a world of realities where no two things are alike. A 

Chinese coolie who, for a few cents a day, runs like a tired and beaten dog before his rickshaw (usually not his, but the property of a gangster-profiteer), until, worn out and sick, often still in his early thirties he lies down to die—this human being thinks very little of national "independence" or "progress".

His material lot and that of millions of others in the great cities where they live crowded together and covered with disease-often ten or fifteen in one small room-or in villages where there is actual famine ten out of twelve months every year-the material lot of these creatures is so miserable, so indescribably pitiful, seemingly so hopeless, that beautiful abstractions such as "independence", "self-determination", or "democracy", when suddenly thrown into their world, sound like the effervescence of the most cruel hypocrisy or of the most stupendous ignorance. The coolie in Shanghai or Hankow, the tenant around Bombay and Benares, wants to live; he wants only to survive, to hold his own against the forces of bad demons who may become stronger than he any day of his short life. As individuals they do survive these millions of the poorest among the poor-by praying to their Gods and hoping for the day to come, by chewing betelnuts and drinking in the wonderful smoke from the poppy that, for the price of a few days earnings, makes them see castles of marble and gold.

Collectively, as nations, as races, they survive simply by multiplying faster than they die of exhaustion or disease. There seems to be nothing, absolutely nothing human, in the outer lives of these "misérables" of the Orient; they are born like cattle, are treated like cattle, and like cattle die. A kind and good-natured smile, flashing out of a face in which fear and hopelessness have dug their marks, a fresh and cunning witticism from a mouth that is used mostly to squabble over a few coppers of bakshish or rickshaw fare, are the only reminders that these thousands of living beings, with their expressive animal-like eyes, are human. 22

In spite of the enormous difficulties in the way of the national and social emancipation of the Asiatic peoples, in spite of the differences in the signs and symbols of East and West, the indications are unmistakable that Asia is awakening, that Asia has awakened to demand freedom from the "white oppressor", regardless of whether he came with Bible, gun, or account book in his hand. The Asiatic nations have grasped the meaning of airplanes, electricity, modern accounting, and economic planning for their national existence. They acquire these tools and the ability to master them as quickly as circumstances in the individual countries permit. The cry for independence and modernization goes out from individual centers of power and learning. Parties, such as the Kuomintang in 

China or the Congress Party in India, outstanding personalities as Gandhi or Tagore, schools, families or enterprises represent and propagate the new thoughts that radiate to the remotest cottage or oasis in their countries. Political leaders: Chiang

Kai-Shek in China, the Shah of Persia, some of the rulers of Arabia, and many others have become protagonists of the new spirit of technical progress and nationalism. It is the spirit that molds Asia's destinies in the twentieth century. The nationalistic awakening and the growing feeling for pan-Asian solidarity in the consciousness or subconsciousness of many peoples in Asia—as an already strong and articulated reality in some parts, as a mere possibility in others constitute the great opportunity for Japan as the most advanced Asiatic nation; they provide her with a world-historical task. The "Nipponese man's burden" is a slogan, a program, a sacred goal that can sound as inspiring, as obliging, as destiny-laden to Japanese ears as the word of the "white man's burden" once sounded when a feeling of inner solidarity and an urge for evangelistic spreading and powerful expansion was awake in the white man's soul.

IV 

The nations of Asia, awaiting their day of freedom, are as little united among themselves as were the nations of Europe when they invaded Asia, and the contacts between the different regions of Asia are as yet slight. But there is, beneath all separating issues, a deep underlying parallelism in the interests and endeavours of the different nationalities. There is growing among the most alert leaders of the Asian peoples the conviction that whatever their petty differences at the moment may be, and however far apart their ideas and interests will 23

lie after the morning of freedom will have dawned, the command of the hour is to make common cause against the common enemy: the man with the pale skin and the machine guns from the West. The fact that Oriental statesmen and cliques frequently side with Western powers, either to enlist their support for their particular ends or to play them against other Western powers, may becloud the issue; but it does not alter the fundamental "intention" of twentieth-century history in 

Asia, that is, to drive the white man out of Asia.

Several difficult and weighty questions present themselves: what has the white man done, that he is so hated? Is he hated everywhere in Asia? Is he truly considered the enemy of the 

Asiatic peoples? Is not the word "white" an abstraction, a meaningless abbreviation for French, American, British or Dutch? It is impossible to answer these broad questions briefly. The white man came in a thousand forms and in a thousand disguises, and thousandfold are the reactions of Asiatic men to him. He came not seldom as an invited guest or helper, often as a self-styled assistant and saviour, sometimes as a robber and usurper. He brought with him capital, energy, vision, and ingenious methods of mastering men and nature. 

He built hospitals, roads, railways, and humane prisons. He took away raw materials and the results of cheap labor-labor mostly better paid and more humanely treated by him, the foreign exploiter, than by the native princes and magnates. 

But his mere presence frequently helped to undermine, if not to destroy, ancient customs and orders. The white man came in his own interest; he, the stranger, came as a superior, because he was superior in everything that mattered in a society bound for mechanical organization and material progress. It was the white man's mission to bring the gifts of his civilization to Asia; it was his destiny to carry "the white man's burden" in the most sublime and in the most ridiculous sense of the phrase to the most remote corners of the globe. 

Whether the Europeans brought more to Asia than they took from her-the much debated question of the "squeeze" -has been and will be the subject of many intellectual battles between scholars and politicians on both sides, fought with the aid of all possible fallacies from statistics on trade and opium smuggling to moral arguments. It matters little. Nor does it matter much whether the fate of the Shanghai coolie or the farm-serf in Ceylon would be any easier if the white man should pack his cases and leave. The one thing that does matter is that the white man came as a foreign master to Asia. 

Foreign masters are never and nowhere loved where there is a remnant of pride in men. Foreign masters become intolerable when in the hearts of the servants there has grown to full strength the desire to become masters in their own house. The position of a foreign master becomes precarious when the servant has learned enough about the master's art 24

to be able to become master himself, and when he, skilled in the art of observing weaknesses, has discovered the frailities behind the superior front of his master. It is for very good reasons that to lose "face" is one of the most dangerous things that can occur in the Orient. When British and American gunboats and transports were bombed and machine-gunned by Japanese planes during the Sino-Japanese War, lives and money were lost. Men and money can be replaced; even wounded ambassadors can be replaced. But to restore lost face and prestige in Eastern Asia is a difficult task. Only strenuous efforts and great sacrifices can accomplish it, if it can be accomplished at all.

The peoples of Asia want to be masters in their own house. 

This is one of the strongest single impressions that a journey to Asia can convey to the observer. The urge for freedom has its roots in the deepest recesses of the human soul. Freedom is power over one's own self. The desire for freedom is the urge for power in its most primitive and also in its most sublime form. The white man is an obstacle in the way of this most fundamental drive for freedom on the part of the Asiatic nations, and as such he is hated. As an individual, he may be sincerely welcomed in the rôle of a partner or teacher; he may be royally entertained as a guest with a hospitality that exists only in the East; he may be admired as an engineer or soldier; he may be treated as a father by a houseboy and served with loyalty and childlike devotion; he may be openly and frankly received with that distinguished modesty that only noble individuals in noble cultures can call their own when he comes as a student or wanderer to Asia's shores. But as a white man, as a foreign master who comes to rule and to exploit, he is intensely hated by the native man with the darker skin. "White" may be an abstraction just as "coloured", or "Oriental" are abstractions, or "Eurasian"-the name given to that numerous and sometimes deeply unhappy class of people who descend from European and Asiatic parents and, therefore, belong socially to neither camp. For the scholar who tries to grasp the multitudinous variety of the world's forms and races, these abstractions "white" and "coloured" may be empty and meaningless, as meaningless as they often are for the physical passions of men and women that glow under the hot sun of Shanghai or Singapore. But regardless of the findings of the ethnological student, regardless of the ideal of tolerance and understanding with which the humanitarian approaches the ports of the Orient, regardless of the urges in men that break the colour-barriers, melting together and mixing races, the concepts of "race" or "colour", and the hatred and fear between the races are strong and vital realities in the East. Life does not move as scholars and idealists want it to move. It is deeper than rational convictions, 25

business motives, and the passion of sex. Racial pride and its effervescences, race hatred and contempt, are dominating realities in many civilizations. Race-consciousness, as it confronts the traveller to Eastern shores with unforeseen strength, is an outgrowth of man's love for his own self; it is the work of a cosmic instinct for self-preservation, when the self comes into inimical contact with a foreign self. Race-feelings cannot be measured; they cannot even always be explained causally. 

To explain them economically, in other words to argue them out of existence, is one of the comfortable fallacies in which the present age indulges itself, though economic interests undoubtedly play often a strong part in crystallizing racial antipathies. Race-hatred is a potential energy slumbering deeply in men. With frightening intensity it can be seen burning in the eyes of Asiatic men when they are humiliated by their foreign masters. Suddenly it is awakened: the Boxer rebellion, the Indian mutiny, the massacre of Cawnpore were some of the many events in the history of the East in which the abstractions: "white" and "foreigner" played a part that was uncomfortably real to those on the spot. It is futile to ask whether the race instincts and the instigators who try to direct the emotions of the Eastern peoples are right or wrong. 

Realities of these proportions reach beyond right and wrong. 

It only should be asked-and in good time: what situations can arise and how can they be met?

V The conquest of China by Japan may today be accepted as an accomplished fact. The Japanese-controlled East Asian empire covers the entire coastal region of China and most of her interior provinces. How is the spectacular conquest of 

China to be explained? China for a long time, has been an object, not a subject in world politics, regardless of the tenacious resistance that she has offered to Japan. The Chinese could not be but pawns in the hands of the great powers that play the political game of East Asia. There are many reasons for China's weakness. Her position as a national state, in many ways, was just the opposite of that of Japan. Whereas 

Japan is small and integrated, the Empire of the Middle, called China by foreigners, is wide and open. Whereas the history of Japan is like the constant flow of one stream that rarely meets with different streams throughout many centuries, Chinese history is the history of many streams, of conquerors and invaders meeting and melting together on the plains of 26

Cathay. Down to the time of the Manchus, who sat on the throne of heaven until China was reorganized into a republic in 1912, the great dynasties of China were invaders and conquerors. China's history, like that of India, is the history of a people that for many generations lived under the rule of foreign usurpers. When the foreign blood and spirit eventually were weakened and absorbed, new conquerors came, attracted by the opportunities for wealth and power. Whereas the Japanese are truly a "nation" in the Western sense with all the characteristic features of a national conscience and spiritual unity, the Chinese-even today, in the throes of a national awakening-are still very far from being a nation in the Western sense. The Chinese masses, the coolies, the small peasants in the villages, the craftsmen and shopkeepers have taken little, if any, interest in the fall of Shanghai and Nanking which was decisive for China's national existence, in spite of what was reported in Western countries to the contrary. Peking lies far away from Canton! The North and

South speak different languages. Military staff conferences among Chinese officers have sometimes been carried on in the 

English language in order to make understanding possible.

The little political and spiritual unity that China possessed was kept alive by the throne. When this shadow of a link disappeared, nothing could prevent the huge body of China from rapid disintegration. The efforts of Dr. Sun Yat Sen and of the wealthy and influential Soong family, and the endeavours of the "national" government under General Chiang to create a new united China out of the members of the old body might eventually have borne fruit. Most foreign observers, in fact, agree that the unification and recovery of China progressed with surprising rapidity. But China was too far behind Japan.

The Nanking government realized from the very beginning that China could not modernize and industrialize herself, and thus become a strong power, without foreign assistance. It decided that China should take the necessary assistance wherever she could find it, without binding herself too definitely to any of the assisting powers. Chiang Kai-Shek's policy was to keep the influence of the competing foreign powers balanced until China would be strong enough to steer her own course.

His chances, however, to play one "assistant" against the other, until China had gained sufficient strength, were slight. 

When the Nanking government was driven more and more into the arms of the Western powers, which were only "commercially" interested in China and therefore least dangerous to China's national existence, then Japan struck. Japan struck before it was too late. This is the justification for the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese War in 1937.

It is one of China's tragedies, from the viewpoint of the Kuomintang and Chinese nationalism, that China started thirty years later than Japan on her road towards mechani- 27

zation, and even then only haphazardly; it proved to be a tragedy for the Kuomintang that the Chinese people are thoroughly individualistic and particularistic in character, whereas the Japanese readily accept discipline and centralization. It was a tragedy for China that her provinces were exploited and looted by gangsters who called themselves "generals" or governors, by bands of robbers and tramps who call themselves "soldiers", by "armies" that fought each other on Chinese soil (though more through checkmating marches than through actual battles), by officials who lived from bribes and betrayals-all this at a time when Japan was organizing a thoroughly modern, efficient and reliable centralized government. Dumbfounded by the contrast between the splendour of the past and the misery of the present, the Chinese threw overboard their age-old cultural traditions, whereas Japan clung to her ancient institutions. In China women became influential in politics-a tragedy for China, at least in the eyes of Japanese commentators-whereas in Japan the affairs of the state were things sacred and the affairs of men. An ill fate it proved for the Chinese, that they had absorbed ideas from all corners of the world-Chinese students, like Japanese, swarmed into American and European universities-and that the new ideas flooded the Chinese soul, taking possession instead of being possessed and mastered by the native spirit, as was the case in Japan.

All the odds in the Chinese-Japanese game seemed to lie in favour of Japan when the conflict broke out between the two nations in the summer of 1937. The immediate reasons are not known why the Nanking government decided, nevertheless, to put up armed resistance against the island empire that was slowly entrenching itself in North China and digging its way southward. Chiang Kai-Shek reckoned with the possibility of a future armed conflict as early as 1927 when he had Nanking, his new capital, built as an "air-raid-proof" city by American city-planners; he, obviously, has been playing for time ever since. But the pressure against his policy of waiting and negotiating grew stronger: from rivals in various parts of China, who worked upon the awakening national emotions of the Chinese intelligentsia, as well as from Japan who insisted on carrying out old and formulating new and more definite arrangements. The Japanese, in this phase of the struggle, were interested mainly in three concrete aims: the lifting of the anti-Japanese boycott and the ceasing of anti-Japanese activities in general; the initiating of positive measures against the Chinese communists, Russia's stronghold in China; and the recognizing of Manchukuo. The fulfillment of these demands would have forced Chiang's government, if it could have survived internally, definitely to go over to the side of the Japanese. It would most probably have caused an estrangement, if not an open break, with Russia and the 28

Western powers. The Nanking government, whether encouraged by its domestic progress or by the promises of friendly powers which were eager to lend more "assistance" to China, did not yield to Japanese pressure. The increasing tension, on the contrary, created new anti-Japanese incidents; the Nanking government drew closer and closer to the Western powers, and also to Soviet Russia; its attitude towards Japan stiffened. In July 1937 the armies of the Rising Sun again marched into China.

China is the nearest and most convenient field of activity for an expanding Japan. The ruins of old Cathay, out of which the young plant of a new Chinese nation tries to break forth, are alluring material for the building of an empire. "China" can be called the first act in the great drama "Asia 

Awakens”, in which Japan plays, masterfully, the leading part. Back in the nineties the curtain rose on the first scene: 

Korea. In rapid sequence the scenes followed each other: 

Mongolia, Manchuria, Jehol, Chahar. The stage became wider and deeper-almost too wide and too deep for the eye of the European, who in this powerful drama, is an actor and a spectator at the same time. Russia, making her appearance at first in the regalia of the Czars and later in that of the Soviets, Great Britain, the United States, and France are Japan's great counterplayers on the East Asian stage. Many hundreds of thousands of square miles of fertile land, hundreds of thousands of square miles of ocean are the stage setting. With the drama rapidly progressing, Japan, in addition to being the main actor, seems more and more to assume the position of a self-styled director of the play, determining the rhythm on the stage and the actions of the cast. "Shanghai" and "Nanking" were two of the most effective climaxes of the China act in the great Asiatic drama. All main actors were present on the stage. With burning Chapei in the background and the rumbling of light artillery-already far inland from Fort Woosung-as discreet stage music, everything in Shanghai went off perfectly according to the program. 

Night clubs were closed at ten o'clock in the International Settlement during the period of fighting. But whoever had an interest in contemporary world affairs, and a curfew pass, could drive out to "Windy Corner", where the Settlement borders on Chapei, and watch the fighting. Small brown men, their heads covered with big German-made steel helmets, were 29

to be seen running back and forth and crawling behind barricades and sandbags. They were Chinese soldiers of the Central Government's divisions. Other men, they too small and brown, their heads covered with strangely-round steel hats, were hiding behind shelled walls or trying to cut their way through barbed wire fences: the fighters of the 

Nipponese army and naval landing party. Behind solid walls of sandbags a few Tommies and tall boys from Iowa or Nebraska, tanned by the sun of Shanghai, kept watch, with bayonets fixed, over the borders of the neutral Settlement area. On their dugout between deserted Chinese quarters they had posted a sign: "Dancing every night-Fancy dress desired-Come and join". It was a bit of trench humor, as it once bloomed in 

1918 in the Argonne and in Flanders. A few of the tall boys were killed-besides many little brown men-before Shanghai fell.

The China war is rich in bizarre details. When the Japanese were asked why so many older men were seen fighting in their ranks, the favoured answer was: "Family fathers fight more quietly and in a less self-sacrificing way than youngsters". Another story, however, had it that Japan kept her best divisions at home and in the north with an eye towards Manchukuo's Russian border. Several times it occurred that 

Chinese attacks, scheduled to take place simultaneously at several points, for unknown reasons failed to take place.

Later on it was discovered that the Chinese officers in charge of the smaller units did not possess watches! For several months Japanese and Chinese troops were battling in the Chinese parts of Greater Shanghai and in Yangtsepoo, a part of the International Settlement. After Yangtsepoo was made a battleground, Europeans and Americans preferred to call it "the Japanese settlement"; it sounded better to sensitive Western ears. When the Europeans finally were permitted by the 

Japanese military authorities, after the Chinese had been repulsed, to re-enter their old homes, to get out what was left of winter bedding and personal belongings, many of them realized for the first time, that with the year 1937 a new age had started in East Asia, an age in which Japan was to be master in China, and Europe and America only tolerated guests.

Towards the end of the sunny month of September in 1937, Admiral Hasegawa, the Japanese commander on the Shanghai front, gave friendly warning to the powers that, starting with a certain day, Japanese planes would bomb Nanking, then the capital of the Chiang Kai-Shek government. The diplomats of the powers accredited to the government of General Chiang thereupon quietly packed their trunks and retired to Woohoo, a few hours up the river. One of them, to the great chagrin of the nervous but tenacious Chinese, even made a public announcement of his departure. After the ambas- 30

sadors had gone, not many foreigners stayed on in Nanking. 

Among those who remained there were a few missionaries and teachers, idealists who had thrown in their lot with the 

Chinese, and a few "sewing-machine salesmen" representing firms from all corners of the globe. Some of them had met -a strange coincidence the previous year in Madrid, and two years before in Addis Ababa. Brave and loyal to the cause their cause!-they remained "just in case General 

Chiang might need some sewing machines." A few "news hawks" remained also to tell the world, in thrilling words, exaggerated stories about military events, of which, at least in the opinion of the military men, they understood but little. "Sewing-machine salesmen" work on a commission basis, and newspaper men mostly are paid by the line. So the Chinese soldiers were supplied with guns and the world with fascinating news. Remaining also, almost to the very last, were the desperately-resisting General Chiang, his wife, and the German military instructors.

The "dialogues" in the Shanghai-scene were well declaimed. 

England spoke hers at the Nine Power Conference in Brussels, which Japan did not attend. The President of the United 

States made a "strong" speech in Chicago advocating a quarantine against aggressor nations, which caused Tokyo some "surprise". Only on the northern side of the Amur River did everything remain ominously quiet. There, General Vassily Bluecher is reported to command "500,000 well-equipped and loyal Soviet troops"-the same Bluecher, who, under the name of Galen, in the short interlude of Borodin's communist régime in China, once before played a significant rôle in the Chinese drama, until Chiang Kai-Shek and the Shanghai bankers drove him off the stage. The dialogues were wellspoken. The audience of the Western world, kind-hearted and sympathetic for the "underdog" as audiences are, nodded approvingly, and sanctioned it, when their governments lent "moral" support and money to the "poor Chinese". Only a few, though important, onlookers in Europe and South America found it advantageous to side, openly or covertly, with the 

Samurai. The main actor, smilingly bowing, expressed regret at the eloquent reverberations from abroad. He apologized that he could not make his intentions better understood and steadfastly marched on towards Hankow, where the fleeing Chiangs had organized their new capital. "Cathay" is the theme of the first act in the drama "Asia 

Awakens". Whether other acts will follow and whether Japan, openly or behind the scenes, will remain the managing director only the future can tell. Directors usually remain the same during a play, especially when it is successfully directed, in spite of ambitious aspirants who may be waiting for the post.

China, because of her size and wealth and number of people, may also prove the most important act in the drama portraying 31

the transformation of the East. The latent energies resting in the continent of China are incalculable. As China goes, so 

Indo-China and Siam must also go eventually, following a law of political gravity. From Saigon and Bangkok, however, it is only a short distance to neighboring Burma (that is, to India!) and to the Malayan states, at whose southern end the small island of Singapore lies just across the straits from the Dutch East Indies. A strong and economically-developed 

China is the key to southeastern Asia. Wide regions there, especially Cambodge, Annam and the Malayan states are in the process of being "conquered" economically by an increasing number of Chinese immigrants. A silent war is tenaciously carried on, in which the Chinese seem to be the winners over the Annamites, Sais and Malayans. It is a conquest, less startling, but not less momentous than the conquest of China by Japan. 

Southeastern Asia offers gigantic opportunities to the genius of enterprise. Two projects in the field of communications await the pioneer of the twentieth century. The one is the constuction of a canal across the Malay Peninsula (the Isthmus of Kra), which would shorten the route from the China Sea to the Gulf of Bengal by 1400 miles. Such a canal would render Singapore, the domineering sea fortress of southeastern Asia, practically worthless if it were in the hands of an enemy of Britain. That Japan is interested in the construction of such a canal has often been reported and just as often been denied as "unfeasible" or, at least, "premature".

The other project, greater by far in scope and potential effect on many hundreds of millions of people, is the establishment of a modern land route from China to India along the old caravan trails from the upper valley of the Yangtse River and the province of Yunnan over the rigid mountains to the fertile regions of the Irrawaddi and Bramaputra Rivers. China is reported to receive increasing quantities of arms via this route. 

The possibilities of an air route over these vast regions, covered by treacherous jungles and ranges of steep mountains, were explored not long ago by German aviators. Fantastic dreams have been dreamt, before and since the days of Marco Polo, by princes and adventurers about the power of one who could join the various "continents" of Asia together by links which he alone would control. Napoleon conquered the St. Bernard; 

Lesseps the Isthmus of Suez; the Americans mastered Panama. Who will play their part in East Asia?

A transformation of eastern China will produce changes in the northern, central, and western parts of Asia equal to those envisioned in southeastern Asia. Eastern China, if politically and economically developed, may someday spread mechanized civilization over her western provinces with the same breathtaking rhythm with which European-American civilization spread from east to west on the American continent 32

and with which Japan is moving westward on the continent of Asia. With Chiang Kai-Shek's retreat to western China in 1938 this development has actually begun. Prince Teh, the ruler of Mongokuo, a land nominally under Chinese, but actually for some time under Japanese sovereignty, has proclaimed repeatedly that the goal of the Asiatic peoples must be the establishment of a federation of Asiatic nations under the political and economic leadership of Japan. When he speaks thus, the ruler of Inner Mongolia thinks not only of his own country, but also of neighboring Outer Mongolia. 

Outer Mongolia is dominated politically and strategically by Russia, with which it has a common defense pact, although nominally the Soviet Union has recognized it as an integral part of the Republic of China. The ruler of Mongokuo, in visualizing a federation of Asian nations, is concerned not only with Mongolia and the Empire of Manchukuo, with the North and Central China régimes in Peking and Nanking, but it is reliably reported that he is also thinking of an independent Siberian Republic under Japanese patronage with Vladivostock as the capital, reaching from the Kamtschatka to Lake Baikal. East Asia, as a political unit, is not complete as long as Russia, with a menacing arm, embraces China from the north, reaching almost to the Japanese islands, to the very heart of the East Asian empire.

The perspectives that Central Asia presents are gigantic, the opportunities enormous. Superlatives and only superlatives are adequate to summarize in a few words a situation that pages of statistics on area, population, raw materials, water power and farm land, cannot accurately describe. The importance of Central Asia for world politics becomes clearer every year. From Sinkiang, roads lead to India. The "roof of the world" is no longer unconquerable. Afghanistan and

Iran are, in air distance, not all too far from Chinese Turkestan. Many of the inhabitants of Sinkiang are militant 

Mohammedans, as are the Persians and Afghans; and the

Mohammedan world is longing for greater spiritual unity. 

The interior of China is today removed by a hazardous journey of many weeks or months from Peking and Hankow. Once it is politically solidified, it can be reached by air in a few days from the shores of the Pacific. As a possibility for tomorrow the project of opening up Central Asia is a political reality of today. The political search-lights and power currents that emanate from Toyko via Harbin and Peking, from 

Moscow via Tashkent or Lake Baikal, from London via Delhi and Lasa, meet in this vast room whose roof is the sky, whose walls are the horizon and whose floor is the unexplored ground of Asia, rich in materials in many regions, rich in possibilities everywhere. The odds in Asia are favourable for the courageous and ingenious player. "Where are men with courage 33

and ingenuity?" was the question that history presented in the days of Cortez and Francis Drake; history presents the same question today.

The main rival of Japan in China is the Soviet Union. 

Russia and Japan have been rivals in East Asia ever since czarist and Japanese imperialism clashed in Manchuria about the turn of the century. The Soviets, compelled by the geography and the political dynamics of the Russian empire, have continued the policies of the czars. Two decades of Soviet imperialism in Asia are a striking illustration that the foreign policy of a great power does not change merely because the régime, the ruling groups and the ideological "dress" of the nation change. The Soviets prove apt pupils of the czarist diplomats. To old diplomatic weapons new ones were added. 

The appeal of the communist ideology to the poor and downtrodden Chinese masses is strong in many parts of China. 

Russian propaganda in China is nationalistic and pan-Asiatic, in other words, anti-European, just as is Japanese propaganda. 

But it is also anti-imperialistic and anti-capitalistic and, therefore, anti-Japanese. The Soviet Union exercises through

The rulers of the Kremlin are fully aware that not only their influence in Sinkiang and Outer Mongolia is at stake in the present struggle, and their opportunity to assume someday the leadership of an awakened socialist Asia, but also Siberia and the Russian possessions in Central Asia and their very existence as a great power. One of the purposes of the Moscow trials in the spring of 1938 was to demonstrate that there were movements under way after the World War and in recent years to separate Bokhara, Uzbekistan, Turkestan and other Asiatic Soviet republics from the Soviet Union and to turn them into independent states under the influence of other powers. The liberation of the Asiatic provinces from the Soviet yoke appears also today as one of the great opportunities rendered less distant by Japan's advance on the continent of China.

China is the diplomatic battle ground on which Japan and Russia have been meeting for several decades. By giving "advice" and "assistance" to the Chinese both powers attempted to entrench themselves in China in order to have an operating basis for further diplomatic and military advances regardless of whether the forces making for unity or the forces making for dismemberment would keep the upper hand in China. Only when Japan advanced in 1937 was a non-aggression pact between Moscow and Nanking finally concluded, and it was then that the Chinese Red army offered its assistance to the national government.

When two powers such as Soviet Russia and Japan rival for control over territory that may prove essential to their very existence, every increase in power of the one will be a potential threat to the other. Not only the construction of tanks and battleships and the training of soldiers, but also the increase of population, growing industrial output and road mileage become a potential menace. Whether and when, under such circumstances, war between the rivaling powers will break out is a matter of secondary importance. Japanese- 35

Russia is not the only rival of Japan in China. The 

Western powers, especially England, the United States and France have interests in China. Millions of pounds and dollars have been invested by Europeans and Americans in utilities and industrial corporations in China; for several decades trade with China has been a profitable business for some of the largest and most influential concerns of the Western world. The Western powers, moreover, that have possessions and influence zones in the Pacific area cannot be disinterested in any important change that occurs in the political status of China. The British crown colony of Hongkong, the Inter national Settlement and the French concession in Shanghai are outposts of Western influence at the very doors of China. 

The Philippines and French Indo-China are situated in the immediate neighborhood of southern China. Even the dominions of Australia, New Zealand and Canada, Holland as the owner of the East Indies, and the Latin American nations that border on the Pacific, will be affected by a political transformation of China that is tantamount to a shift in the balance of power in the Pacific. The British know that the organization of China into a modern nation under Japanese leadership will not remain without effect upon India, Burma and Malaya.

One of the great questions of world politics is: what can and what will the Western nations do to protect their interests in East Asia? Large sections of public opinion in the Anglo- 

Saxon countries seem to rely on the old truism that "trees are not meant to reach the sky". They await the moment when Japan will have overreached herself and will either stop her march of conquest or break down under the strain of economic difficulties. But public opinion in the democratic countries does not seem to be fully aware of the immense efforts of which nations under so-called totalitarian régimes are capable. If the Western nations choose to uphold their interests exclusively through wishful thinking and through the loans and ammunition with which they supply the Chinese, they will find themselves confronted in the near future with the fait accompli of a united China, that is, a federation of Chinese states under Japanese leadership, directing its energies southward directly against Western possessions. The Western nations had a similar experience in the Far East not long ago. Their clumsy attempts to "help" China under the disguise of League of Nations action were condemned to failure from the very beginning, since 36

no understanding concerning a joint exploitation of China could be reached with Japan. Japan, even more clumsily, but successfully, moved her troops to China, hereby indicating to the other powers that she considered the period of rivalry in North and Central China as ended and herself as master.

If the Western powers are determined to uphold their interests in East Asia, only two courses seem to be open: they must prepare themselves to fight Japan or they must attempt to reach an understanding with Japan and permit her to expand in a direction where Japanese activities do not conflict with Western interests. Short of a complete withdrawal of the Western powers from East Asia, which may have irreparable consequences upon the position of Europe and America in the world, and short of a collapse of Japan in China, which is highly improbable in view of her consistent progress, there exists only the alternative of either crushing Japan through joint action or of diverting and canalizing Japanese energies towards aims that can be reconciled with Western interests. 

The chances for the Western nations to come to an understanding with Japan seem to be better today in view of the fait accompli in China than ever before. One thing, however, has to be kept in mind quite clearly: the only region into which it seems possible for Western diplomacy to divert Japanese energies, without hurting British, American or French interests, is northern Asia, especially Siberia. A Japanese- 

Western conflict can be postponed or probably entirely avoided without catastrophic damage to Western interests and to the balance of power in the Pacific, if Japanese expansion, instead of going south, could be pressed northward. In fighting Soviet Russia and, if victorious, in developing Siberia, Japan would be tied up for a long time, financially and strategically. Southern China, where Great Britain has her greatest investments, French Indo-China and the southern

Pacific would be relieved of Japanese pressure. The colonization of Siberia may, in a limited way, even open up new opportunities for Western enterprise.

A conflict between the Western powers and Japan, on the other hand, involves even graver problems. The first prerequisite for the Western nations to fight a distant enemy of the strength of Japan is to be united. The question of unity among the Western powers, however, encompasses the entire European scene with its many unsettled problems. The West is not united. A Far Eastern conflict, in which England and 

France, for instance, would be involved as the two European powers with the largest holdings in East Asia, would find Germany and Italy ready to bring about the readjustments in Europe that they consider desirable, possibly at English and French expense. The Far Eastern problem is inseparably linked to the European problems. This link between East 37

Asia and Western Europe constitutes Europe's most difficult political task. It matters little whether the friendship between

The three nations have concluded a treaty that is directed against Communism and the activities of the Third Internationale that emanate from Moscow. That this treaty, though officially concerned only with the Third Internationale, will be brought into operation against the Soviet Union as a state, is a possibility with which European diplomacy must reckon. If any Western power should become involved in a Far Eastern conflict against Japan, she would find herself almost certainly allied with the Soviet Union and this would mean, in terms of European politics, in a state of war with both Germany and Italy. 

Quite apart, however, from the European ramifications of a Far Eastern conflict brought about by the German-Italian- Japanese cooperation, friendship of a Western power with the Soviet Union encompasses many other difficult problems. 

Soviet Russia is not only a Far Eastern power, but also a strong factor in the Middle East. In Turkey, Iran, and Afghanistan Russia is the most noteworthy rival of Western imperialism. If Russia comes out of a Far Eastern conflict with increased strength, she may turn her attention again to central and southwestern Asia. If victorious in a war against Japan due to the assistance granted her by Western powers, Soviet Russia would make herself the undisputed master over Asia and eastern Europe. The Kremlin would become the center of a communist All-Asia. The slogan “Asia for the Asians", used today competitively by Russians and Japanese, would find an even stronger echo, if both powers would utter it jointly in a united front against the West. 

Whichever course the Western powers choose in China, their attitude is still a formidable factor in influencing developments in East Asia. Many thousands of miles away from

As a diplomatic event the Japanese-Chinese conflict causes anxiety in the chancelleries of Europe and America where terrible blunders have been committed during the past decade. As one act in the great drama of "Awakening Asia" -perhaps the greatest drama of the twentieth century— 38

Chapter II The Rising Tide of Indian Nationalism 

South of the icy rims of the Hindu Kush and Himalaya mountains lies India. Vast and rich is the continent that had no name until the conquerors from Europe named it India. 

A part of Asia, but separated from the other Asia by the highest mountains of the earth, India's wide plains reach from the valleys of Kashmir to the jungles of Ceylon, from the sandy hills of Baluchistan to the shores of Malaya. Sparkling is the sky vaulting over southern Asia. Sparkling are the golden roofs of Hindustan's temples, the marble walls of the Moghuls' mosques and the ivory statues of Burma's pagodas. Vast and rich is the soul of India. Ages come and go. Empires rise and fall. But the stream of India's soul flows on eternally. In the West time marches on and with it marches the Western soul, chosen, destined, condemned to march on forever or to pause, to rest and die. 

In the East time flows, and with it flows the Eastern soul like the great rivers that come from the mountains and go to the sea; from birth to death, from eternity to eternity; to rise again, as the fog rises from the ground at the sun's command, moving on like clouds before the wind, rising and falling, living and dying and being born anew, to live forever, one with the great stream that is life, that is the living God-Self.

India is the East. All that the West called East when it became conscious that there were civilizations different from its own, civilizations with a different approach towards the finite and the infinite, with a different conception of space and time, all this is more clearly perceptible in India than in any other land of Asia. When the West, through contacts with the 

East, became conscious of its own existence as a culture reaching beyond the borders of nations and language communities, its building spirit framed the dualism East-West around which it thought world history evolved. All that is "Eastern" to the West, and strange, seems stranger and deeper in India than anywhere else in the Orient. India is the homeland of Gautama Buddha; in Sarnath he first preached to the deer. In India the urge of men to master life through the 40

strength of the meditating soul has reached its fullest bloom. 

The message of Nirvana and the tale of Yoga were told to the world from India. "Satya-graha", resistance without violence, appears to be the way to freedom to millions of Indians to this very day, whereas Europe and East Asia are trembling under the impulses of force.

To the West the soul of India is ununderstandable and unconquerable, because neither through understanding and analysis nor through conquest can it be reached. Only intuitive vision opens the way to its depths. For the brief moments that the scholar or poet from the West is allowed to look inside the deep miracle that he calls East-if only to grasp and hold it fast by a name-he ceases to be a Western man. Far away, forgotten forever seem the creations of the Faustian-Western soul in these moments of submergence into another world: the cathedrals of the Gothic, the facades of the Renaissance, the palaces and the sky-towers of the twentieth-century metropolis that are the West-man's "home". There may be similarities between forms and ornaments from East and West; the two worlds, in fact, touched each other closely. From the days of Darius and Alexander there were contacts, often intimate, between the Mediterranean civilizations and that of Persia and India. Ideas, forms and ornaments migrated from one world into the other as did merchants and pilgrims, pirates and soldiers. But there is a deep metaphysical difference even between two samples of the very same ornament, when the one is placed into Western and the other into Eastern surroundings. This difference between the Eastern and the Western version of a flower in a garden or on a marble wall is lost as soon as the intellect starts to compare and to dissect in the light of midday. But at dusk, when the bells of the cathedral call the burgher for evening prayer in the West, at dusk, when the gongs of the pagoda sound out in praise of God over the lotus ponds of the East, then East and West, as two great realities of the world, as two great ideas that move mankind, receive again their full meaning. A symphony by Beethoven-driving, urging, developing, moving on, even at the moments of seeming rest and quiet, dynamic to the last fibre of its inner existence, and a music-poem of the East, one of Rabindranath 

Tagore's songs-floating restfully, even in the most bizarre climbings of the melody, always being, awaiting, rarely awakening, never commanding or forcing into action-these are, perhaps, the clearest illustrations of the "being-otherwise" of East and West.

The wanderer from the West is bound to make two great discoveries in the East, revelations that time and again will strike him with new force: the intensity of the religious spirit and the direction of religious and philosophical thought. It 41

is in this respect that the East, and especially India, differs most widely from the West. In the West, a man deserves highest praise if he removes the obstacles that lie in the path chosen as the right one. In the East, perfection is achieved by trying to overcome all pain or ill-will resulting from wordly obstacles and troublesome trifles through inner strength. Hinduism, Buddhism, Taoism and Confucianism are, in this respect, rooted in the same world-feeling. The picture of the man from the West who kills or drives away the insect that bothers him, and the man from the East who ignores such trifles, illustrates wisely the difference between East and West, which is fundamentally a difference in religious outlook. 

Regardless of its significance for an attempt to understand religious, social and political life in the Orient, the formula "East-West" may be too simple, too naive to cover life's myriad forms, and yet not simple, not naive enough to cover all. 

The West committed many sins-sins of the intellect-when it pictured the East, especially India, as it wanted it to be. Indian life does center around the transcendental; its "direction" is towards the Beyond much more so than anywhere in the West. Ethics, politics, economics are intimately interwoven with metaphysics and subjected to it. Life in its daily appearance is, to a great number of Indians, only an expression and symbol of the transcendental will. But to suspect for this reason that India's life is not as full of "real" earthly problems, wants, urges, desires and struggles, is a grave error nourished frequently, however, throughout the enlightened West. The West began to like and to enjoy this "idealized" and somewhat anaemic picture of the East which it had made for itself, and stuck to it because it was so beautiful and consoling in the midst of never-ending troubles to imagine that there existed a land and a people that lived removed from all worldly struggles, with no purpose but to cultivate the soul, as monks do, in intimate contact with the Eternal. This picture of the East was fostered by Indian lecturers who found fertile ground for preaching their Utopia when they traveled over Europe and America. When the Western eye suddenly found itself confronted with some of the realities of Eastern life: the caste system of the Hindu communities, untouchability and invisibility, suttee, or certain rules of Hinduism, unforgivable to men with even the most primitive hygenic knowledge, the West, which had been so ready to picture Inda as a distant fairyland, began to measure her according to its own standards. The disappointment and confusion and even bitterness regarding India in the Western mind was great.

Vast and rich is the continent of India, as rich in color and design as an oriental carpet. The multitudinous varieties of religions, and social and political forms is a source of con- 42

fusion not only to the Western observer who watches the spectacle of Indian life, but also to the Indian himself who tries to formulate a program, a policy or plan along which India will fit herself into a world governed largely by the laws of the_mechanized dynamic West. Three great religions meet in India: Buddhism, Hinduism and Mohammedanism. Two of them originated in India. The wanderer from East to 

West meets them gradually, as gradually as he meets the dif ferent races and languages, the different customs and ornaments, as his steps follow the path of the sun. Burma, the land of pagodas, is the only part of greater India where Buddhism is still the leading religion, although Hindus and Mohammedans, streaming in growing numbers from Bengal into the Burmese port cities, have brought with them their gods and temples. In the northern parts of India, Hindus and Mohammedans live among each other, peacefully one day, in bloody riots and uproar the next. Towards the West, near Arabia and Persia, the believers in Allah become stronger and more numerous than the Hindus who, however, outnumber the Mohammedans more than three to one throughout the entire country. More than two hundred languages are spoken on the continent of India, and many are the races that dwell there. 

Mongoloid are the features of the native Burmese, who inhabit the valley of the Irrawaddy, where the Kings of Mandalay once ruled. Strong and muscular are the men from the Punjab and from the Kashmir mountains. Arabic-Semitic and Median 

Persian influences are noticeable in the western parts of the peninsula towards the Persian Gulf, and even African traces are visible in many faces in the coastal cities. All colors and forms of human life, of sorrow, joy and passion have been woven into the Indian carpet by a long and eventful history, overabundant in phantasy. The ascetic worshipper of Gautama Buddha can be found near the ornament of the fullbreasted Hindu-goddess, a symbol of sensuality and earthliness. The Maharaja who measures his weight in gold and diamonds lives among masses of half-starved serfs. The loftiest ideals of human freedom from all worldly ties, as taught by the Brahman, lie together with the narrowest and most cruel restrictions of the caste system. The broadest concept of humanity meets with the most rigid barriers against untouchables and lower castes. The Moslem conquerors of

India have entertained themselves by watching the blood of the slain citizens of Delhi run down a marble wall. But in the same land many men will not kill vermin because their religion forbids them to take life. The plains and mountains of 

India are overabundant in startling forms and colors. But even richer in extremes and contradictions is Indian life. 43

II 

The history of India is the history of many and bloody invasions. From the northwest, from the Afghan plateau, tribe after tribe, army after army swept down onto the Indian plains. From the northeast the people of Burma, a mongol tribe, are said to have come over the mountains, subjugating the populations they found and settling in the fertile valleys. 

The history of Hindustan is the history of conquerors and invaders who came to India from foreign lands with foreign languages and foreign creeds; they came and subjected the native populations to the rule of their swords. They built palaces and temples; they built empires. But most of the empires declined as rapidly as they had grown. The conquering spirit withered away, and with it the vigor of the conquerors. The palaces decayed and the temples were destroyed by the armies of new invaders. Cities were sacked and hundreds of thousands slaughtered. New cities rose; new palaces, new fortresses were constructed. Seven times was the city of Delhi built. 

Proudly and with silent melancholy the pillars of ancient fortresses and castles tower into the evening sky, the only visible marks of the grandeur of empires that stretched over wide parts of India only a few centuries ago. 

India's story is the story of haughty invaders and conquerors; but it also is the story of a strong and irresistible power that forced all conquerors to bow, that absorbed and assimilated all foreign usurpers. Through the sweetness of her air and the smell of her incense, India is said to conquer her armed conquerors. Alexander the Great marched into

Hindustan over the mountain passes from Kabul. The Seleucides invaded India. The Huns swept over northern India, ravaging and pillaging; but even they were rapidly Hinduized. Tamerlan's hordes made India their playground. Arabs, Turks and Persians pushed down into India far to the south. 

The empires of the moghuls, which reached at times from the Oxus to Bengal and from the Himalayas to Gwalior were among the most resplendent kingdoms in the history of Asia. 

Great men have occupied the moghul thrones; Akbar was but one of them. In wealth and splendour they lived, surrounded with the most costly things on earth; in wealth and in splendour they buried themselves. Their tombs still stand and speak to the descendants of their subjects and to their successors, the present masters of India from the West. The invaders of India always succeeded in conquering her, but, in the end, India always conquered her invaders. And never was the end far ahead. Shorter than in most other Asiatic civilizations seem the life-spans of the empires that followed each other on Indian soil. A few centuries, often only a few decades of might and splendour, and everything that once was virile effort, overbearing pride and evangelistic deter- 44

mination, became a part of the past, nothing but shadow and dream. Long before the Aryan conquerors in ancient ages pushed their way from the North into India-light-skinned and tall, the Rigveda pictures them, beer-drinking and beefeating-dark-skinned men had built up high civilizations with cities as large as the metropolis' of Egypt and Mesopotamia. 

The conquerors had contempt for the cities and the civilization of the darker men; but gradually they were absorbed and, still ruling, but already enervated, they were drawn into the circle of the conquered world. In the great basin of Indian civilization they were submerged. Empires rose and fell, but the soul of India lived on. 

India is one of the important examples in world history to demonstrate that human migrations and human cultures do not always and inevitably, as it is sometimes claimed, move from East to West. Wave after wave swept from Asia to 

Europe, from East to West; the Franks and Goths moved West and after them Huns, Slavs and Turks. The most momentous example of human migration in recent centuries, the migrations of Europeans into America, and entire American history are an effervescence of the great East-West stream. But in 

India the great movements have been from the north to the south; most of the invasions of India, in fact, were forced by steep mountains to take a direction from the northwest towards the southeast. The latest among the conquerors of 

India, the British, have come from the west. Contrary to all prior invaders the Europeans, however, came to India not as settlers in masses, but only as governors and merchants, few in numbers, to rule India in the name of an empire that had its center thousands of miles away. 

Four empires of Europe have rivaled in India for power and wealth: the Portugese, the Dutch, the French and the 

British. When, towards the end of the fifteenth century, creative unrest and an urge for conquest and exploration seized Europe, the Portuguese held the monopoly on Oriental trade. 

A few minute colonies in India and in the Far East, and the ruins of fortresses that once overlooked the Eastern seas like eagles' nests, are the only remnants of the bygone days of Braganza's glory. The Dutch, setting out from the solid centers of Antwerp and Amsterdam, drove the Portuguese away and established a net of trade centers and "factories" from the Red Sea to the East Indies. But their might soon vanished too. New rivals had appeared in the merchants from across the Channel. In 1600 the East India 

Company was founded under Queen Elizabeth of England, a powerful rival to the Dutch traders. The early history of the Europeans in India, up to the end of the seventeenth century, was chiefly the history of commercial rivalries between the Portuguese, Dutch and British. When the French began to set foot on the Indian continent-French-India 45

(Pondicherry) is a reminder to this very day of French power in India-the rivalry became predominantly political. The governments in Europe increasingly came to the assistance of their commercial colonists. In the great struggles of European diplomacy and arms for supremacy in Europe, which filled the eighteenth century, the colonial possessions, especially India, began to play a large part. General Bonaparte's expedition to Egypt at the end of the century, with India as his final goal, tried once more to undo the defeat that the French had suffered in India at the hands of Clive, Alexander of Macedonia, who had moved to India more than 2000 years before, was the model of the young Napoleon who sensed clearly the great possibilities of the Orient for his personal power and for France. After Holland was made a part of the Napoleonic empire she lost all her Oriental holdings to England. It was not until Holland became sufficiently cemented into England's European system that she recovered part of her East Indian possessions. 

When Clive became governor of the Bengal settlements of the East India Company in 1758, the English were already firmly entrenched in India. The earliest of the British settlements were more than a hundred years old by then. Immense was the wealth that flowed to the British Isles from India, and decade by decade the company-controlled territories grew. One war followed another. In a truly empire-building way the British proceeded: treaty-making, treaty-breaking, they advanced. When the neighboring princes did not yield, they were deposed. Where rulers proved uncomfortable, revolutions followed with precision. Where it was deemed necessary to round out territory, the native treaty-partners were secretly incensed to break commitments, and punitive expeditions were then organized to uphold the sanctity of treaties. 

The Mahratta wars were followed by the Mysore wars; the 

Mysore and Gurka wars by the first Burmese war. Wider and wider became the British domain. The Afghan wars, the Sind wars, the Butan war, the second Burmese war followed. Still in 1939 the British forces are engaged in fighting regular small battles against the "unruly" tribes of Waziristan on the northwestern frontier.

In the second half of the nineteenth century, once more a powerful rival to British rule in India appeared. Russia, rapidly expanding east and south, had annexed Bokhara and Taschkent. Her influence over central Asia was felt strongly in Afghanistan. When the British advanced in 1878 over the 

Khyber Pass into Afghanistan, their real enemies were the Russians, not the Afghans. At the turn of the century England made an alliance with the Empire of the Rising Sun, not only to prevent further Russian expansion in the Far East, but also to dislocate Russian energies that pointed towards Persia and Afghanistan and threatened the northern borders of Brit- 46

ish-India. After the World War, Russian influence continued in south-central Asia and caused anxiety in India. The Soviets now commanded a new weapon in their nationalist-communist and anti-imperialist propaganda among the native populations. 

The "Russian bear" presented himself as friend and liberator to the oppressed nationalities of Asia and the subtle weapons of Soviet propaganda in central Asia have proved, in several instances, more effective than czarist guns. 

Through the past centuries English power and might grew in India. It spread from the "factories" and trade centers of the Company practically over the entire Indian continent. 

India, in the geopolitical and strategical sense, has become one unit whose military outposts lie in Singapore and Aden, in Jerusalem and Baghdad as well as on the Tibetan border and even in Hongkong. As England's frontier, according to Stanley 

Baldwin, has to be defended on the Rhine, so India's borders have to be defended in Palestine and southern China. The shadows of the moghul empire gradually withered away until in 1858 it was formally abolished. British government expanded from the administration of the trade centers that were originally but isolated points with little, if any, hinterland, to the powerful vice regal régime in which the governments of the Indian states and the provinces of British India find their natural center. British policy, however, was to interfere as little as possible with native government, with native institutions and customs. Many rajahs in the Indian states kept their own governments, their armies and the insignia of sovereign rulers. They are treated as princes, federated to the 

King of England within the British Empire, who have transferred certain rights and powers to the ruler of Britain in exchange for benefits and guarantees. The British proceeded as the Romans once had proceeded, with firmness and brutality when it appeared necessary, but with the lenience and equanimity of great colonial builders when anything but the vital interests of the empire was involved. 

Throughout the centuries during which British power grew in India and in the world, there grew also this unique plant that for so long has been the pride of England: the aristocracy of blood, spirit and ability that has formed the ruling caste of Great Britain and throughout the British

Empire. The growth of England's political aristocracy is intimately linked together with the development of India. 

Service for the East India Company and the government of India attracted the most enterprising, the most daring and the most adventurous among British youth. Service in India was a test, not only for the courage and endurance of a man, but also for his wisdom and ingenuity. The problems that confronted the colonial administrator, the soldier, and the clerk in India, far away from home, required skill, shrewdness, decision. Big and luring were the spoils; formidable 47

wealth was acquired by almost all of the Company's governors and officers; great honors were bestowed upon the successful ones, and success in India often opened the door for an even more splendid career at home. India became one of the main training centers of the British Empire in which young men learned those arts in life that are rarely learned from books or in the class room, but that are bred into them as part of the culture and tradition of nobility or that are acquired through challenging tasks and hard experiences. That the British 

Empire is what it is, in spite of all storms that make the structure creak in its joints, England owes, more than to any other single factor, to the existence of this highly-skilled, wellbred and well-trained stock of political men. 

The type of man that England has sent to India and received back from India throughout the past two centuriesregardless of faults and shortcomings that individuals or even the entire service may have had-belongs to the finest and most highly-developed achievements that Western civilization has produced and probably ever will produce. Clive was a clerk, who became a soldier and a statesman; Cornwallis came to India, a defeated general from the American war. Neither of them, nor any of their followers may, perhaps, be called truly great men in the sense in which the epithet 'great' is generally applied to statesmen and soldiers. They, like the Cato's and Scipio's in the Roman republic, were parts of a long line of public servants in high office of an unusually good average. 

It is the consistency of the high average more than individual achievement and greatness that counted in the colonial history of Rome as well as of Britain. Clive, Warren Hastings, Cornwallis, Lord Minto opened the long line of men who increased the might and splendour of the British Empire. This they did not for unselfish reasons, not without using devious means of deceit and brutal force. But nowhere in the world have empires ever been built, nowhere in the world, at the present day, are empires being built, nowhere is political life carried on without such means. Britain produced warriors when she needed them to fight, administrators and reformers when she desired reforms. Wellesley, Hardinge, Dalhousie were outstanding among them. None of these men is called a genius by historians; but the nature of the task and the temper of the human material involved did not demand a genius; it perhaps would not have tolerated the overbearing lone greatness of a Bonaparte. It demanded a long line of men consistently experienced and skillful in the art of governing, and Great Britain, with unique fertility, produced these men. When the government of India was transferred from the Company to the Crown in 1858, following the mutiny, the public-political and governmental element came even stronger to the foreground in the Indian administration than was the case in preceding decades, and the private-commercial, businesslike char- 48

acter that had marked the early enterprises of the Company vanished. But even now when the government of India rests on a constitution, on acts of parliaments and royal orders-incouncil, it is made a going concern, first of all, by the ingenuity and skill and the gift to compromise of the "entrepreneuradministrators" who occupy the governors' chairs in the provinces, who advise the Indian princes, and who rule in the vice regal palace at New Delhi.

It is sometimes said that great personalities have become rarer on the viceroy's seat in Delhi in recent decades. It has been asked whether the young Englishmen who enter the Indian civil service today are still of the high calibre of prewar days. It has been charged that arrogance and hypocrisy in the behaviour towards the natives have increasingly taken the place of wisdom and real firmness. Whatever may be the amount of truth in these charges, it may give cause to think that in by-gone days many of the British officials in India and Burma were sincerely liked and often loved by the native population in spite of the firm hand with which they managed affairs. It is obvious to every visitor, and nowhere denied, that today, in spite of their readiness to debate and to discuss, the British officers, as a body and as individuals, and Englishmen generally, are rarely liked and frequently hated by the native population. Yet, some of 

England's most influential statesmen in recent years have service records in India: Lord Curzon, Lord Kitchener and 

Lord Halifax rose from Indian posts to important positions in London. Many leading English statesmen in India in recent years are descendants of families that have a long tradition or significant precedents in the Indian service. 

Lord Irwin, the later Lord Halifax, who is a grandson of

Sir Charles Wood, the outstanding reformer of Indian education, is but one example. If, in spite of these outstanding men, the fears expressed by some are well-founded, that the high qualities of Britain's representatives in India are becoming rarer, this might prove to be of more serious concern to England and to Europe than a second Indian mutiny. Only the highest type of statesmen and administrators will be able to solve the problems that are yet to be solved in India. 

III 

India's position in world politics is determined by her dual rôle as a country with its own inner problems and needs and as an integral part of the British Empire. Many of India's 49

present difficulties result from this dualism. The two aspects of Indian life are intimately related to each other. Important forces in Indian domestic politics, especially the princes, are dependent upon British support. The strongest popular movement in India, the nationalist movement, owes its very existence to the fact that India is not a united, free and independent country, but a federation of states and provinces that form a part of the British Empire. Indian domestic politics, on the other hand, play a rôle of considerable importance in the life of the British Empire. The strength of the Indian independence movement cannot be without influence upon the decisions of the statesmen in London concerning British policy in Europe or in the Far East. A relationship similar to that existing between Indian domestic politics and Empire politics is to be found in the economic field. India is a country or continent with its own economic interests and problems; but at the same time, she constitutes a part of the greater economic unit that is composed of England, the dominions, and the British crown colonies. Indian domestic interests and Empire interests sometimes cover each other completely, often they overlap, very frequently they seem to be diametrically opposed to each other. It need hardly be stated that with regard to most of the complex problems in political and economic life the Indian nationalist leaders consider the interests of their country as opposed to that of Britain and the Empire, whereas British diplomacy, on the other hand, attempts to harmonize the conflicting interests or, at least, to make them appear as parallel. To draw a brief picture of Indian politics, as far as it might bear directly upon world politics, is not possible without considerable simplification. To simplify the picture of Indian political life, however, may well mean to deviate from truth because it is just the element of complexity that is essential to Indian politics. 

Without the confusion in political thinking and organization and without the conflicting interests among the various religious communities and pressure groups it would probably be impossible for Great Britain to hold India within the Empire with the relatively small means that she devotes to this purpose.

In Indian domestic politics four powerful factors play a major part: the Hindus, who form the majority of the population; the Moslem minority; the princes, that is, the rulers of the Indian states; and the exponents of Great Britain, that is the viceroy, the governors, the British element in the Indian civil service, and the British garrisons and British business and propaganda. The relationship between these factors changes rapidly; it varies almost from year to year. India is in a constant flux of evolution, not to say of revolution. It also varies from province to province and from state to state. 

There may be satisfactory collaboration, at least outwardly, 50

between the governor and the National Congress Party in one provincial government, whereas in the neighboring province a state of tension exists, resembling the days of passive resistance. In one state the ruling prince and the British adviser may be in close understanding or even connected by an intimate personal friendship (though friendships of this nature have been found almost invariably to have a detrimental effect upon the mutual relations in the end); in another state the British adviser may find himself compelled to use his entire diplomatic skill to manoeuver the unruly rajah out of power and to put a more cooperative member of the ruler's family upon the throne. In some provinces Hindus and Mohammedans have, if only temporarily, achieved a working arrangement that makes possible a unified national movement. In other parts of the country Hindus and Moslems may battle each other in the streets with a bitterness and vehemence that often makes it impossible for the police to prevent bloodshed.

The most frequent cause for bloody rioting between Hindus and Moslems for many years have been Hindu musicmaking processions that pass Mohammedan mosques. The stubbornness with which the Hindus insist on carrying out their religious processions, and the fanaticism with which the Moslems object to Hindu music being played in the neighborhood of their mosques are at first difficult for the Western observer to comprehend, until he realizes to what an extent politics and religion are intertwined in the community life of India and how strong a forming force religion is in social life. Century-old jealousies between the two communities, resulting from the days of Mohammedan conquest, religious in nature, but frenquently interwoven with political and economic motives, sometimes strengthened through racial and linguistic differences, are carried over from year to year and from decade to decade with a great capacity for intense hatred on both sides. In bloody riots that break out over a song or a harmless village procession, the anatgonism between the two communities bursts into the open. Much bitterness and excitement has been caused in recent years by a song called "Bande Mataram". Its verses tell of green corn fields, of thrilling silvery moonlight nights, of groves with blossoms and flowers. A strong group among the Hindus wanted to have the "Bande Mataram" song adopted as India's national anthem; but particular references to the Bengal province and to a personified motherland in the song made it unacceptable to the strictly monotheistic Moslem who suspected "Mother India" of being a Hindu-goddess in disguise.

Indian nationalists frequently charge that the historical differences between the Hindu majority (approximately 230 million) and the Moslem minority (about 60 million) could 51

be ironed out, and would, in fact, be already settled if the British would not constantly pour oil onto the fire in order to keep the contrasts alive and, according to the old rule divide et impera, profit from the lack of unity among the Indian people. It probably holds true that restoration of complete harmony between the two communities would strengthen the nationalist movement greatly_and, therefore, would not be altogether desirable from the British point of view. The British have sided rather consistently with the 

Moslem minority whih holds a share of influence in the government considerably larger than its strength in numbers, although the Moslems are said on the whole to be less educated and materially less advanced than the Hindus. National feeling and the urge for independence is less pronounced among the Moslems than among the Hindus, since the Mohammedans fear that the establishment of an independent Indian democracy, where only numbers count, would deprive them as a minority not only of their political influence, but eventually also of their independence and freedom of worship. 

Under these circumstances it was quite natural for the 

British to become the protectors of the Moslem minority.

To live on good terms with Mohammedans in India is, furthermore, essential for British diplomacy in view of the effects that the attitude of the Indian Moslems has upon the Mohammedans in other parts of the Empire and in countries such as Iran, Iraq and Egypt. The growing inner unity of the Mohammedan world is being felt by the British in India as elsewhere. Strong resentment against England was provoked after the World War among the Mohammedans in India because of the terms that the victorious allies tried to force upon Turkey.

Clearer and easier to discern than British policy in the Hindu-Moslem row is the tendency of British diplomacy to support the Indian princes and to strengthen their position. 

It is the princes who, in the scheme of federation, counteract the nationalist aspirations represented most strongly by the popularly-elected delegates and by the nationalist governments in the provinces. The hundreds of Indian princessome of them sovereign rulers of large and wealthy states such as the rajahs of Mysore, Bikaner, Baroda or Hyderabad, many others hardly more than petty feudal landlordsknow that they owe much of their tranquil existence to the British. Most of the Indian princes are autocratic rulers and not many of them appear inclined to introduce modern political and social ideas into their states. The popular nationalist movement, on the other hand, which is organized chiefly through the National Congress Party led by Pandit Nehru and symbolized by Mahatma Gandhi, has strong social tendencies. A victorious national democratic movement in the provinces of British India would, if unchecked, hardly stop 52

at the borders of the Indian provinces. The status of the princes as sovereign rulers-sovereign in name, if not in fact -and the often fabulous wealth of their courts would be in serious peril of being gradually abolished or, at least, greatly reduced by a complete victory of the national-social-democratic movement. Great Britain also, for obvious reasons, is opposed to any drastic change in the political or social status of India. So mutual interests led the British and the princes together. In 1914 the Indian princes responded wholeheartedly to the call for the defense of the Empire. 

The English have not forgotten this. At the Round Table 

Conferences in preparation for the new India constitution the rajahs found reliable supporters in the British, especially among the conservatives, for the defense of their interests. 

The driving force in India's domestic politics are not the princes, nor the Moslems, nor the British; the force that moves India forward towards a new political and social development is the nationalist movement that is organized in the Congress Party. Indian nationalism is by no means exclusively Hindu. There exist nationalist aspirations also among the Mohammedans organized in the Moslem League, and the establishment of friendly cross-relations between the two religious-political communities is the aim of many political leaders. But essentially the Indian nationalist movement appears to be Hindu in character. In the choice of its political weapons, in its objectives and general outlook, through the character of its leading personalities and through the sentiment of its followers, Indian nationalism is thoroughly Hindu in nature. "Satya-graha", the policy of non-violent resistance that the Mahatma, that is the "All-soul", the "Great-soul" preaches, is the weapon of the Hindu religion.

Since the national movement is reformist in character, it is, nevertheless, opposed by a large number of orthodox Hindus. 

Gandhi's struggle against untouchability especially is meeting resentment on the part of conservative Hindus, although it is widely known among learned Hindus that untouchability is not an original feature of the Hindu caste system, but a relatively late outgrowth. Twofold are the aims of the popular movement that has its strongest political representation in the Congress Party and that found its most powerful symbolic expression in the figure of the "naked saint of India" at the spinning wheel, as Gandhi is frequently called by ridiculing Europeans. The objectives of the movement, called "Gandhiism", that despises rifles and knives as weapons, but believes in passive resistance, non-cooperation and similar bloodless methods, are national-political as well as social. As a national movement Gandhiism strives to make 

India a united independent nation. As a social movement it works to raise the material and educational standards of the masses of the Indian people. To define these aims more 53

precisely is very difficult mainly for the reason that little or no agreement exists concerning the concrete aims and objectives among the different wings and shades of opinion within the Congress Party. There is only a certain harmony with regard to the general direction in which to proceed, and even this harmony, established and maintained to a large extent by the mythlike personality of the Mahatma, often breaks down under the heated arguments of opposing ideologies, personal jealousies and rivalries. Whether, as it is often maintained, the ageing Mahatma has lost influence and prestige among the leading groups of the nationalist movement and with the Indian masses is hard to ascertain. Contrary to Gandhi's teachings, the nationalist movement is growing continuously more aggressive and militant in the choice of its weapons. But Gandhi's prestige is still strong enough to force every recalcitrant deputy of the Congress Party into line, if through no other means, then at last through the threat of a hunger strike.

To make India a united and independent nation means many very different things to different men in the Indian nationalist movement. Many of the younger leaders, who have been trained in the universities of America and Europe, foresee as a final status for India a completely independent republican United States of India modeled upon the United States of America. The writing of the leaders of the American independence period from Thomas Paine to Hamilton are widely read by the young political intelligentsia of India. 

Others again declare themselves contented with a dominionlike status for India within the framework of the British Empire. The word "dominion status" has become, in itself, a much-used slogan concerning which there is often little clarity in the minds of those who use it. But it, nevertheless, represents a program or approach, to which the Indian national movement has pledged itself, at least as a temporary aim, on the road to complete independence. In view of British promises made during and after the war, the Indian leaders consider themselves not only morally, but also legally entitled to demand dominion status or a dominion-like position. What they have received from Britain in the new constitution that came into effect in 1937 they consider as little or as no progress on the road to liberty. Some have even expressed the opinion that the new federal constitution removes the dream of Indian independence further into the distance, since it tends to deepen the contrasts and rifts within India, between states and provinces and between Hindus and Moslems. 

According to the charges raised by many Indians the new constitution gives to India what appears on the surface to be a free government, but what is, in reality, nothing but a clever and artistic scheme to enable the viceroy and the provincial governors to manage India's affairs through the 54

popularly-elected provincial and federal governments which have to bear the political and financial responsibility before the people. As to the future relations between the provinces and the states of India there seems to exist among the leaders of the Indian people as little agreement as to India's relations to England. All shades of opinion varying from centralizing republicanism to a loose federalism that would respect the rights of the sovereign princes are represented. 

With regard to social and economic policies the Indian nationalists seem to be even less united than with regard to their national and constitutional aims. Since the Gandhi movement is a mass movement that tends to mobilize the extremely poor Hindu masses in the villages and in the cities of India, great emphasis is laid upon the social problem, that is upon the liberation of the Indian masses from misery and poverty. 

The living conditions of the large majority of the Indian people are probably inferior to the conditions in any other large country with the exception of the famine-stricken provinces of China. The lowest types of negro slums in 

America and the caves and shacks in which the most miserable share-croppers in Spain and in the south of the United States are compelled to live resemble, to some extent, housing conditions that can be called "general" in the crowded and disease-ridden villages of India. Only through a promise to improve their material lot, can the interest of the Indian masses be aroused for any cause whatsoever. The ways and means proposed by different leading personalities in the movement vary from communism and state socialism after the Soviet-Russian model, to a type of "native" capitalism, to be nurtured behind the walls of protective tariffs and restrained by social legislation along Western democratic lines. Among the young social reformers of India every philosophy ranging from the economic liberalism of the classical school to economic planning and regimentation of the soviet or fascist types is reflected. In the field of agricultural reform, the socialization of land and the restoration of independent small farm lots instead of absentee ownership of large estates are two of the most prominent ideas. Both, of course, are in irreconcilable conflict, not only with each other but also with the present social structure of the country, and are bitterly opposed by the rich classes and the princes. Industrialization, as proposed by Kumar Sarkar of

Calcutta University, and the transformation of the foreignowned enterprises such as railroads, power plants, coastal shipping, mills and construction firms into Indian enterprises are other outstanding aims of many Indian nationalists. To many Indian leaders, political independence is only a means to achieve economic independence from foreign capital. They all realize that the two are inseparably linked.

In the center of the economic theories and proposals 55

which young-India debates stands Gandhi's own social ideology. Gandhiism, in this sense, has often been called socialism, although it has little to do with nationalization of the means of production which is usually implied in the term socialism. 

Gandhi's social ideal, as symbolized by the spinning wheel, is a typical product of India and only to the Indian mentality will its meaning be fully understandable. It does not call for organized effort and rationalization; it may not even be called an economic "program" in the Western sense; it is, in character, much more a psychological appeal, a symbolic action to stimulate the political consciousness of the Indian masses, than it is an economic program. The making of cotton clothes with their own hands by the masses of the Indian people, instead of importing British-made goods, the winning of salt from the sea instead of purchasing salt, on which the taxes of the "foreign exploiter" rest, the prohibition of alcoholic beverages now under consideration, and, most important of all, the very gradual improvement of working methods and the introduction of less primitive mechanical tools are some of the demands that characterize the direction of thought of the Gandhiites. Gandhi's thinking is, first of all, pro-Indian, that is, anti-British; it is essentially psychological-political and it is rooted in a social philosophy that sees as its ultimate goal not an urban, industrialized, progressing, and dynamic India, in accordance with Western, Soviet Russian, or Japanese ideals, but a self-contented static India, built upon the small rural unit and the village community as far as possible, self-sufficient and organized under religious-ethical, not under economic, selfish rules of behaviour. As revolutionary as Gandhi's ideas about caste and untouchability are in the life of the Hindu community, his social philosophy is deeply rooted in the Hindu tradition and in the soil and soul of India. Only as 

Only as a part of the spiritual traditions of Hinduism does Gandhi's economic ideology, decried as utopic by his opponents, have any real meaning.

Summarizing the internal conditions of India, the following can, perhaps, be said with a certain amount of accuracy. 

The forces striving to gain greater independence from foreign rule have been awake for many decades. Whether or not the Indian mutiny of 1858, following which the government of India was transferred from the Company to the Crown, was an early outburst of the new national feeling seems doubtful. But no doubt can exist as to the tendency of the new nationalism during recent decades: it is growing. Nationalism in India grows in spite of seemingly overwhelming difficulties resulting from a lack of unity that is the natural outcome of Indian history. A strong anti-European and anti-British sentiment is the major cementing and unifying factor in the new nationalism. In spite of the very strong localism and 56

regionalism, which is believed by some to be the predominant political reality in Indian domestic politics, in spite of the jealousy with which the princes guard their positions, and the bitter antagonism between Hindus and Moslems, in spite of the obstacles created by group, caste and sect rivalries even within the Hindu community, in spite of illiteracy and ignorance, the desire for Indian independence and the underlying feeling that there exists a binding tie above all separating issues is growing rapidly. The fact that the call for national independence is cleverly linked with the appeal for social betterment-a combination that has proved effective also in other parts of the world-is a major reason that nationalism has found access to the Indian masses. Though the direct influence of the national ideology upon the poverty-striken and illiterate millions must not be overrated, it cannot be denied that the leaders who have made Indian nationalism their cause have a very strong and, in some regions, unshakable power over the masses of the people. 

Indian nationalism centers around the idea of one Indian nation. The many races and nationalities, religious and linguistic communities are conceived as members of one great family, as children of one mother: Mother India. As it gains shape in the minds of the intellectual leaders of the nation, Indian nationalism is strongly influenced by Western thinking. Many elements in the Indian nationalist ideology resemble that of European nationalism during the nineteenth century. Since the principal languages of India are related to each other, the cultural tie of language that has been so important for European nationalism exists, to some extent, also in India. Indian leaders frequently see in Indian nationalism not an aim in itself but a means to higher achievements. Through the independent development of modern Indian culture some want to find a new form of life, a new approach towards things material and spiritual which may serve as a bridge between the old East and the West, a bridge for Asia and mankind between the past and future. A highly idealistic and humanitarian world-view inspires many of the intellectual leaders of Indian nationalism. Whatever the Indian leaders seem to lack in forcefulness and vigor to push their cause to a successful end, they make up in breadth of vision, strength of meditation and lofty humanitarian ideals. 

Whether the high idealism behind the Indian national movement would prove strong enough to hold India together once the supreme aim of independence is achieved, whether or not India would be capable of defending her independence after she once achieved it, may seem a rather academic question in view of the general world situation. According to the bestinformed European observers, including those who take a friendly and understanding view towards Indian aspirations, 57

the probability is that the divergent interests of different rulers and different communities would tear India apart and increase the misery of the Indian people. The contention that 

India is not "ripe" for full independence can, of course, be proved as little as the opposite view advanced by the more radical among India's leaders, that Indians if left alone would be capable of bringing their own house in order. Whatever the correct answer to this question may be, the movement for complete independence has many adherents, especially among the younger Indian nationalists, and under certain circumstances it may develop into a factor of paramount weight in world politics. For several years it has been said quite openly and written in the radical Indian press, that the next war in which Great Britain will be involved in Europe or in the Far East will find India better prepared to achieve her aim than the War of 1914.

IV 

What is the attitude of Great Britain towards Indian nationalism? Whether it is a symptom of British strength or weakness that open threats concerning India's attitude in a future war can be uttered publicly in a land under the British crown is a matter for speculation among Indians as well as among foreigners and also among British people in India. The fact is that the British administration, on the whole, shows a tolerance towards Indian nationalism that appears in extreme cases almost as indifference. It is one of the great surprises for foreign observers not familiar with British colonial methods. There exists a group of statesmen in England and also among the British residents in India who feel that the time of patience and conciliatory gestures has passed and that the moment for strong-arm methods has finally come. This group points out, and the utterances of Indian leaders may prove it to be correct, that Great Britain's readiness for compromise and conciliation is interpreted invariably as weakness by the Indians. On various single occasions the advocates of firmness have won the upperhand, and more than once the government seemed prepared for a supreme test of strength with the 

Indian nationalists. But the "voice of reason", that is, the advocates of moderation and conciliation, always triumphed in the end.

The motives of British policy in India appear to be rather simple. England wants to keep India in the Empire. She 58

wants to keep India not only because of the "squeeze" that she gets out of India from dividends and revenues, from freight rates, trade profits and positions-a "squeeze" that even in recent years has been very substantial-but she also needs politically secure markets in which to buy raw stuffs and to sell industrial products. England wants to keep India in the Empire because through India she is a major power in Asia. India is the reason and the justification for Singapore and Aden. From India the rays of British power penetrate into 

Malaya and Tibet, into Butan and Afghanistan and into Arabia. 

Without India the British Empire would not be the British Empire. Great Britain might, perhaps, be a world power even if she had never conquered India. But since she has conquered India and since she has, by doing so, distributed the red color that marks British possessions on world maps harmoniously over the entire globe, England will never be able to give up India without endangering her world empire. India has become a pivotal part of the Empire. The "squeeze" may be an important consideration in the minds of English businessmen; at the time when the East India Company rounded out its possessions, it was the driving element behind colonial imperialism. But to reduce the British Empire to an accounting problem is to reduce life to eating and drinking. The 

English have built an empire, not only to provide themselves with spices and rice and cotton, but also to carry the Union 

Jack over the world, to make Albion mighty and respected, to bring to other races the blessings of the English spirit and of English civilization. The English built their empire because they were bent to build an empire. Whether the individual 

Britishers who went out East went for the sake of profits or adventure or because of a feeling of duty, or simply because they had nothing else to do, matters little. They sailed East and West and drove their rivals from the scene. With British investments and guns, the British spirit, British prestige and glory went into India. England will never give up Indiaas no great country ever has given up or ever will give up its empire, except when forced to do so by superior might or inner degeneration-as long as the spirit of Clive and the verses of Kipling are alive in the hearts of Englishmen.

The English, as realists, recognize the difficulty of keeping a continent with 350,000,000 people within their empire in an epoch when the desire for independence is constantly growing in the native population. Altogether there are only 135,000 

Europeans living in India, 60,000 of whom are British troops -minute white isles in an ocean of brown people, which could be swept away mercilessly by a rising tide-and the Indian mutiny is not yet forgotten either by Indians or by Englishmen. 

Thus it is necessary for England to find a solution apt to reduce the chances for the repetition of such events, and yet 59

safeguard British prestige and interests. The British believe that they have found a solution in their policy of compromise and conciliation with the Indians. The constitutional development of recent decades is dominated by the idea of granting the Indians a greater share in the government and administration of their country, in the provinces as well as at the center. 

With the India Councils Act of 1909 the policy of "better understanding" was officially introduced. The Montague- 

Chelmsford reforms of 1919, the India-journey of the Simon Commission in 1927, the subsequent round table conferences in London and the Government of India Act of 1937 in which 'the development culminated, were further results of this policy. 

The policy of better understanding, which tends to reconcile the British and Indian viewpoints by granting, very gradually and "through successive stages", an increasing amount of self-government to the Indians, has encountered mostly bitter hostility on the part of the Indian leaders. The journey of the Simon Commission, undertaken to prepare the new constitution, was considered an insult by the Indians, since no Indian was a member of the Commission. But viewed retrospectively, the development of the past decades has considerably increased the share of the Indians in the government of their country. 

The National Congress Party forms the governments in more than half of the Indian provinces. The new Indian constitution may be an artistic and clever scheme to keep the various divergent forces of India carefully in balance and to prevent the accumulation of too much power in the hands of native officers. 

But, regardless of all the safeguards for British power that are built into the constitution, its unmistakable tendency is actually to give a greater share in the government to the Indians. The same development, though more slowly, takes place in Burma, which was separated administratively from India in 1937. More and more Englishmen are replaced by natives in the colonial service. The English appear convinced that self-government for India in the long run is inevitable, and a certain democratic instinct in the British political mentality seems to make it easier for Englishmen to reconcile themselves to this development. They recognize that an increase of tension through a revival of the boycott or of passive resistance on the part of the Indian nationalists would only hurt British interests and prestige. They know that through their collaboration with the Indian princes and through their influence upon the Mohammedan group they have a fair chance to prevent Indian nationalism from getting out of control. 

It is in view of these circumstances quite in accordance with British temperament and British political traditions gradually to relax British rule over India and to let consent and cooperation take the place of command and obedience. 60

In her conduct of Indian affairs England's attitude seems to be determined largely by rational calculations gained from a long experience in colonial government and from a profound knowledge of the Indian political mentality. But behind these matter-of-fact considerations there is also evident in Great Britain's conduct an ideal. The English who come out to India and to other colonies, and a great number of English people at home are deeply convinced that England is carrying out a mission by bringing Western civilization, English civilization, to nations with different or lower forms of culture. The 

English are convinced of the truth of their own message. 

They are "patriots". They have come to believe that they are the most able and best-suited among Western nations to colonize foreign lands, and the superiority of Western civilization and of the Western spirit is held as a postulate not to be questioned.

To bring to other peoples the advantages of Western civilization does not mean to the British mind to deprive these peoples of their own cultural patterns. On the contrary, British colonial policy is and, as a rule, always has been very tolerant concerning native religions, customs, or languages in British colonies. Where, as in India, the English found a highlydevoloped and complex civilization, their policy was not to destroy this civilization and to substitute their own, but to interfere as little as possible with native habits and to introduce Western forms and institutions only where it appeared advantageous to the material progress of the country or where native customs stood in shocking contrast to Western ethical convictions. "Suttee", the old Hindu custom according to which the widow jumps onto the pyre of her dead husband is one of the few customs in India against which the British administration proceeded with full vigor, so that cases of suttee have become very rare. With regard to other native habits the British have been extremely tolerant. In Calcutta the sacred white cows are permitted to promenade undisturbed in the midst of the city traffic. If native custom demands that visitors to temples must be barefooted, as in the Shwedagone in Rangoon, the British administration does not interfere. British people rarely visit the temple for that very reason; but if they do, they submit to the rules of the native Burmese. 

Concerning many Hindu customs of the most unhygienic nature, especially in the Holy City of Benares, the British have shown themselves so reluctant to intervene, partly because of general principles and partly because interference may cause political trouble, that even non-orthodox Hindus favor a stricter hygienic policy. The English hope that education will gradually achieve with ease what the police club could now achieve only under great difficulties. Time is one of the essential factors in British calculations. To be concilia- 61

tory at the moment but firm from a long-term point of view is one of Britain's great principles in colonial government, and one that has made Britain great. Perhaps rightfully,

British policy has been compared with the endeavours of a sea-captain, who steers a zig-zag course, but always keeps his eye on the final goal.

British colonial policy differs in several essential points from the colonial policy of other nations. Though the status of self-governing dominions has been granted only to those parts of the British commonwealth whose main bodies of citizens are made up of people of white stock and European culture, it is the tendency of British policy also in other parts of the Empire to have the native elements share in the responsibilities of government. A comparison of British rule in the 

East with French or Dutch rule illustrates this tendency. Both the French and the Dutch have relied much less upon the willingness of the native element to cooperate in the affairs of government. Their colonial régimes appear more authoritarian than that of the British. The difference between the "temper" of government in the British possessions and in the colonies of other Western powers seems the more remarkable since, socially, the British element in India lives absolutely aloof from the native population. There is hardly any social intercourse between the residents of the white "cantonments" and the Indians, including the Indians of wealth and education, except for those few contacts that are absolutely indispensible in the interests of government and business. A British officer and his Indian colleague will hardly ever be seen walking on the street together, and under no circumstances in the presence of a white lady. The "colour line" is strictly drawn and pitiless social ostracism awaits the transgressor. British mentality objects ardently to what it considers inhuman or unjust discrimination on racial grounds in other nations. But it does not mind that the educated Hindu-who at Oxford or Cambridge may have met his English teachers and colleagues. on an equal footing-suffers bitterly under the unwritten rule of social aloofness that guides British conduct in British possessions. The social attitude of other European nationalities towards the native_populations of the Orient, especially that of the French in Indo-China, is much less dictated by the command of absolute segregation than that of the British.

The rules governing social behaviour and intercourse in the different empires, seems in strange contrast, almost in reverse relations to the principles adhered to in the field of government and administration. 62

V India is one of the great focal points of the East. She sends her rays into the Arabic world through the vehicle of Mohammedanism, and into the Malayan Peninsula, where many Indians have settled. Even in East Africa large Hindu communities have been established that keep their contacts with the homeland, with Mother India. Spiritual and political shocks, originating from the densely-populated Hindustan, quickly reverberate all over southern Asia. The political separation of Burma from India in 1937 was meant, among other things, to isolate the spirit of India that has become restless. But such measures will not close India hermetically from the other East. India, besides being a center of the 

Eastern world, is a focal point of European power in Asia. 

The Indian empire is the largest single possession of a European nation in Asia. Without control over India, British power in Burma and Malaya, in Arabia and China would be endangered, and the road to Australia would be imperiled. 

The Dutch and French possessions in East Asia would be dangerously isolated without British-India to lean on for support. 

The British Empire is a well-knit and delicately calculated system of exchanges. Police and military forces are "lent" and "forwarded" from one corner to the other. Britain polices one part of her possessions with natives from another. The tall Punjab riflemen in Burma and Singapore, the bearded Sikh policemen in Hongkong and Shanghai are symbols of Empire organization. This well-balanced system would tumble without India. India's peculiar position results from the important dual rôle that she plays as an Asiatic country with its own national and cultural aspirations and as a European outpost, as a vital part of the British Empire. The tension resulting from this dualism is India's main theme of life in the present age; it is part of the great drama of Asia's awakening. 

For many centuries India has been under the yoke of foreign rulers. The last conquerors to come to India, the Europeans, did not settle and intermarry with the native population as previous conquerors had done. The so-called "Anglo-Indians" form only a negligable percentage of India's total population. 

But the European conquerors, too, brought with them their civilization and spread it like a magic net over India. India, as the entire Orient, was opened up during the past century to Western engineering and science, to Western forms, beliefs and pleasures, to European-American civilization with all the complex manifestations of its late and highly-developed stage. 

The tools that the Western engineer put into the hands of the Oriental changed the horizons of the East; the idea of individual freedom and autonomy, which Eastern men observed in the ways and walks of Europeans, produced earthquakes 63

in the mentality and social structure of the East. In India the caste system changed from a static and well-balanced social order into an unbearable reactionary device of semislavery. Thousands, in order to escape it, turned Christian; others became Mohammedan; many, under Gandhi's leadership, took up the message of social reorganization. European civilization introduced new wants and new desires into Indian life, and these desires-stimulated by the salesmanship of Western manufacturers-drove India forward relentlessly on the path towards modernization and industrialization. 

There is no way back to the former mental and social status, once an Oriental people has come into contact with Western civilization. The past has definitely been destroyed, and all attempts to restore it artificially seem condemned to failure. 

There is only the alternative between mental and social chaos or a new synthesis. The leaders of India struggle for this synthesis that is to incorporate Western technology and Western devices into Indian life. Through this gigantic task the 

Indian national movement becomes a part of the greater movement that is on foot everywhere between the Mediterranean and the China Sea, driving the Asiatic nations forward towards a new destiny. India's fate is irrevocably linked to the fate of all Asia. The problems to be solved, the enemies to be defeated, are the same in all Asiatic nations. The aim of a free Asia for the Asians cannot be achieved before the day of freedom has dawned also for India. The dream of a greater Asia that develops its own new world culture cannot be dreamt without a vision of the three hundred and fifty million Indians who inhabit the plains between Kashmir and 

Ceylon. 

As a powerful factor in the movement for freedom which is taking possession of the entire Asiatic world, India's fate is intertwined through many material and spiritual bonds with Eastern Asia. High mountains separate Cathay from

Hindustan. But the common endeavour of the nations on both sides of the mountains, the endeavour for an independent national existence on a socially advanced basis, creates a common psychology; it prepares the ground for a mutual understanding. The vital interest that political circles in India take in the events of the Far East is a symptom of how strongly the common character of the problems of the two parts of Asia is realized. India's sympathies may lie with China in the Japanese-Chinese conflict. But many Indian nationalist leaders avowedly wish for a Japanese victory, because a Japanese victory, in their eyes, would deal a blow to European prestige in Asia. The Japanese victory of 1905 had strong repercussions in India; another Japanese, that is Asiatic, victory, more than three decades later may have even stronger consequences upon India. As an Asiatic nation striving for 64

independence from European domination, India is of paramount importance not only for the many smaller nationalities from Syria to Malaya, whose aim lies in the same direction, but also for the nations that see themselves as the liberators of Asia from foreign rule and as the new rulers over Asia: Japan and Soviet Russia. It is a common saying in the Orient that on the very day on which the Union Jack is hauled down from Government House in New Delhi, the banner of the Rising Sun or the Soviet emblem will be hoisted. Though this may be exaggerated, it illustrates India's position. Countries such as India, China, or the Philippines, which have been ruled for many centuries by foreigners, will have unusually great difficulties in setting up their own government, should the foreign rulers withdraw. It is only natural that they would find themselves under the pressure of outside powers that have political and economic interests in their development. A country with paramount interests in the markets of India is Japan. Once outside Britain's orbit, India would be drawn almost inevitably towards the Asiatic federation that Japan has formed under her protection. Soviet Russia, weakened by internal difficulties, appears today less of a direct threat to India than several years ago. But as long as the Soviets exist, they will always form a potential danger to European interests in Asia. When 

Gandhi, in the autum of 1937, stepped from the train in Calcutta, to attend the meeting of the Nationalist Party's working committee, one of the first men to greet him was an humble pilgrim from Japan who had come to see the "Great Soul of India"-a minor incident, but perhaps one of deep symbolic significance for Asia's future.

Due to the general situation in world politics Indian nationalism finds itself in a very peculiar position. Demanding independence from England, it is a disintegrating force within the British empire. To the extent, however, to which it succeeds in weakening British, that is Western, influence in Asia it strengthens the position of the nation that has come to the helm of the Asiatic liberation movement. Japan's trade with 

India is already of considerable importance for both countries; it possesses even greater potentialities if India's commercial ties with the Empire are weakened. The industrialization of northern and central China under Japanese leadership is apt to change fundamentally India's economic outlook. The Eastern nations may be able to accomplish for each other what the white man is unable to accomplish for them, after he has given them, for many decades, the first impulses and the start to an industrial development. The distance between Tientsin and Bombay grows small in view of the possibilities inherent in the Asiatic situation. These possibilities may become realities in the course of a few short decades. 

Empires have risen and fallen in India; palaces were built 65

and destroyed. A great empire, the largest that the world has ever seen, defends itself today in India. Britain defends herself against the rising tide of Indian nationalism and against the invisible aspirants who are waiting abroad to continue the long row of India's conquerors. If granted many years of peace, Great Britain has a reasonable chance to direct the political forces in India into the channels that it has prepared through the new Government of India Act. India may, under 

British guidance, work out her difficult political and economic problems, and eventually become, through democratic processes, a member in her own right of the British commonwealth of nations. If many years of peace are granted, the optimists who hope for this development may once more be right. If the British Empire should become involved in a major war in Europe or the Far East, it is unpredictable which course India would take. To a clever anti-British "Lawrence" all roads seem to lie open. That India would emerge from a coming war in the form in which she entered it, is very improbable; for this the tension and the political hatreds have grown too strong. India, in such a war, would become most probably one of the major battlegrounds; she certainly would be one of the most important spoils. "I cannot forget that in the sky of India, serene as it is, a small cloud may arise, no larger than a man's hand, but which, growing larger and larger, may at last threaten to burst and overwhelm us with ruin". These were Lord Canning's words in 1856, before he left England to succeed Lord Dalhousie as governor general.

Two years later the thunder storm of the Indian mutiny burst. 

Eighty years thereafter dark clouds are again gathered on the sky of India. If the storm that hangs over India breaks while England is occupied with steering her ship through European gales, it may develop into an Asiatic cyclone which will sweep away the last remnants of European power from Asia. 66

Chapter III 

Storms Brewing in the Hither East

In the days when Charlemagne ruled over the empire of the Franks, the story of a great and mighty kingdom in the distant Orient penetrated to the Imperial Palace in Aachen. So great was the fame of the far-away Arab kingdom and its ruler, the Khalif Harun al Rashid, that the Emperor Charlemagne decided to send an envoy to the court in Baghdad with presents and imperial greetings. The envoy from the 

West was received in splendour by Harun al Rashid. Motivated by a common enmity against the Byzantines, Charlemagne, the most Christian king and successor of the Roman emperors in the West, and Harun, the Khalif over all Musulman, concluded a pact of friendship. Thin as it was, the political thread spun from the Rhine to the Euphrates by two far-sighted rulers more than eleven centuries ago was the first friendly link between the young awakening Christian Occident of Frankish-Germanic blood and the Eastern world of Islam. The followers of Mohammed were at the height of their power during these centuries. All over northern Africa and into southern Europe the Arabs had swarmed to spread the message of the Koran and to establish powerful states in the name of the Prophet. Only at Poitiers did Charles Martell succeed in defeating the Musulman and damming the Moorish flood that threatened Europe.

But as fast as the might of the Arabs had risen, so fast their strength and their political creations withered away.

For almost a millennium the Arab world sank into oblivion following the glorious rise after Mohammed's death. Centuries were filled with petty struggles between petty potentates and rival tribes. The Omoyades once more succeeded in creating a powerful dynastic state. But the Turks, pushing southward from Asia Minor, finally subjected practically the entire Arabic-speaking world, and the Ottoman sultan became the Khalif over all Moslem. For almost one thousand years the region between the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea and the eastern shores of the Mediterranean seemed half-forgotten to Europe. From this soil some of the world's oldest civilizations had sprung. 

Here Sodom and Gomorrha and the "Arabia Felix" of the 67

Romans had flourished, and not far from Baghdad stand the ruins of Babylon. But where once fertile fields and blooming gardens delighted the eye, there was now sand. Where once stood cities with highly-refined civilizations and prospering populations, there were now only poor and dirty shacks, inhabited by a poor and backward people, or sandy deserts, wide and hostile, silent and treacherous, the home of the 

Bedouins. 

In the age of the Crusades the Christian West made several powerful thrusts against the Mohammedan East. But the waves of Western vigour vanished as quickly as the Eastern storm over the West had lost its strength several centuries before. A few rocky walls, heavy and upright, are the only traces that the Knights of the Cross, from France and Germany, from Italy and Brittany, left in the land where Christ was born. In the early sixteenth century the Portuguese under 

Albuquerque established themselves on the Arab coast. To this very day the proud and sombre Portuguese fortress over

Muscat greets the sailor who approaches the mountainous shores of eastern Arabia. From Venice the traders came searching for carpets, spice and silver all over the East. Along with valuable commodities they took a piece of Orient with them in their souls and transplanted it to their homes on the Canale Grande. During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries "A Thousand-and-One Nights" enjoyed great success on the book marts of western Europe. But Arabia remained the mysterious land of the thousand phantasies to the West and the contacts between the two worlds were slight and superficial. Europe's attention, as far as it was not occupied at home, was concentrated on the Atlantic Ocean, on the Americas and on India. The Arab lands that lay between Europe and India were outside the general interest, since the land route to India through the desert was difficult and dangerous and the traffic with camel caravans was slower than the sea journey around the Cape.

At the end of the eighteenth century Egypt and Arabia were thrown, for a brief period, into the focus of great European politics. When Napoleon Bonaparte came to Egypt and 

Palestine in order to defeat the British in India through a victory on the Nile, it was as if lightning were revealing the political contours of a coming age. Napoleon recognized the importance of Egypt and Palestine as the key to open and close the road to India. He sensed that the contol over Akaba in the hands of a European power might mean hegemony over Europe and the world. But Napoleon was defeated at 

Akron. Throughout the nineteenth century the problems hinted at by Bonaparte's expedition to Egypt were gradually intensified and moved into the foreground of world politics. 

With the building of the Suez Canal the control over the sandy 68

deserts and friendship with the unruly Bedouins in the adjacent regions became of paramount importance for the European powers. When finally, at the turn of the twentieth century, Germany's Drang nach dem Osten found concrete expression in the project of the Baghdad railway, the imperialist competition of the European powers in the Middle East had attained its full strength.

In the twentieth century the Arab countries and the entire region of the Hither East, so long in oblivion, form one of the important centers where the interests and endeavours of world politics meet. The events that occur in this area have a vital effect on all the large powers. Developments in the states of the Arabic peninsula, in Egypt or Iraq have their repercussions wherever Arabs live, which is to the west all over northern 

Africa, throughout Tripolis, Tunis, Algiers and Morocco, to the north and west in the British mandates of Palestine and Transjordan, and the French mandates of Syria and Lebanon. 

The kindred spirit among the Mohammedan people transmits intangible messages from Arabia to Turkey, Iran and Afghanistan, into the southern parts of the Soviet Union, into India and even into western China. There exist two major reasons for the importance that the countries of the Middle East have acquired in recent decades in world politics, an importance that has increased with almost breathtaking speed during the past fifteen years and that is bound to increase further during the coming decades. One reason is the intensified interest of the Western powers in this part of the world: imperialism. 

The other reason, not less important than the former and intimately linked with it, is the nationalist awakening of the native peoples. The Middle East, like all of Asia, is in ferment. 

It presents essentially the same picture as India and the Far East. The European powers have brought the thoughts and tools of Western civilization to the East; in the course of their colonizing activities they have injected them like germs into the mentality and social life of the East. There they suddenly developed, becoming virulent after a short incubation period, transforming the bodies of the Eastern nations into organisms with new strength and new ambitions. The period needed to start the process of transformation in southwestern Asia was shorter than in other parts of Asia. The more advanced parts of the Arab world were spiritually prepared to accept the new ideas and forms prescribed by contact with Western civilization. Syria, the Lebanon, Egypt and Iraq, where the remnants of old civilizations lay dormant, took the lead in the new development.

In the evolution of the Middle East the World War proved to be a climax as well as a starting and turning point. It moved southwestern Asia to the very doorsteps of Europe.

Since the World War the Middle East has been playing a rôle 69

in world politics remarkably similar to that played by the Balkans before the war. The "Balkanization" of southwestern

Asia in this sense is today an accomplished fact with all the problems and dangers inherent in such a development. The potentialities of the situation in the Middle East, however, are infinitely larger than that in the Balkans. The Middle 

East is part of an awakening Asia. In the wide and thinlypopulated spaces between Ankara and Mossul, between Baghdad and Teheran, political and economic movements may not, at least for some time, accept the same sudden impetus and produce the same outbursts that are charateristic of the narrow and closely-knit Balkans; but their inner power, their vigour and tenacity, once they are fully awakened, may become as the deserts and mountain rims in these parts of Asia, wide, mighty, unalterable and invincible. 

The World War caused the final downfall of the Ottoman Empire. After the Porte had been pushed back from southeastern Europe and from northern Africa for several decades, Arabia was now "freed" from the Ottoman yoke. The defeat of the Central Powers led to the abolition of the Khalifate and made a progressive national state out of Turkey. In the 

Arab countries it brought into the open forces that had been latent for many years, unnoticed but by a few close observers. 

During the World War British and French imperialism stim ulated and used Arab nationalism against Turkish and German imperialism; Colonel Lawrence was the most famous among its many agents at work to incite and direct the Arab leaders. 

After the War victorious British and French imperialism saw themselves confronted with a rapidly-growing Arab nationalism whose repercussions were felt from Morocco to Aden. 

The unrest in the Arab world had at first proved a convenient weapon in the fight against the Central Powers and Turkey; now it was an embarrassing inconvenience to Western diplomacy. Soon it was to become a serious concern to the powers that consider themselves the guardians of European supremacy in western Asia and north Africa. When, with Italy's aspirations to be a world power, the Mediterranean ("the 

British Lake of yesterday, the Mare Nostrum of tomorrow") was made again one of the most contested areas of world politics, the Arab countries, bordering on its eastern and southern shores received an additional weight in international relations.

European investments, chiefly English and French, but also American, Italian and Belgian are of considerable importance in the industrial, agricultural and banking enterprises of the Middle East.

One of the earliest and one of the foremost imperialist interests is still today concerned with the opportunities that the Middle Eastern countries offer for strategy and transportation. 

Arabia is the "corridor" between Europe and India and between Asia and Africa. Already in ancient times the Egyptian kings attempted and Darius achieved the construction of a canal linking the Mediterranean with the Red Sea. In the nineteenth century, again, imperialism focused its attention on the Isthmus of Suez. Napoleon's expedition into Egypt and

There exist few more dramatic illustrations of how the spirit of the West has set out to conquer the universe than a map of the world showing the lanes of land, sea and air traffic. 

To the Middle East most lanes have come only in recent years; but they have been laid out with singular clarity and perfection. 

Emerging from the various centers of Europe, they converge net-like, crossing each other or running parallel for a certain distance, and then separating again, to lead in a bold swing to far-off destinations in East Asia or Australia. In the same manner the power lines of European diplomacy, spun over the Middle East, meet and cross each other, converge and part. 

Each of the great air, land, and shipping routes branches out through supplementary services into the areas adjacent to its course in attempts to bring the neighbouring regions under its influence. In the same way European diplomacy sends out its rays from the outposts that it has established strategically all over the Middle East, to hold control over the surrounding territories. The silver birds that are loosed from their cages in Europe take their course over the sandy deserts of Syria and Mesopotamia and over the hills of Persia like over great 71

The time tables of the European enterprises operating in Asia are a twentiethcentury epic of Western imperialism in the East, dry and precise, but of powerful rhythm.

The shipping lane extends from the Mediterranean through the Suez Canal and the Red Sea. With demonic power the spirit of the West has set to work to conquer the Hither East. To Western phantasy and energy the Hither East was only a part of the East, and the East was only a part of the Universe. Consciously or unconsciously, the inventors, the builders, the pilots and port mechanics who collaborate to knit the giant net of land, sea and air transportation around the globe are the agents of this spirit. The statesmen and businessmen who canalize and direct the energies of the Western nations, the diplomats who prepare the ground to which the nets of imperial power can be fastened, through leases to build canals, railroads or airports, and the soldiers who are stationed out there under the tropical sun and far from home, to guard the "hooks" against thieves and saboteurs, -they all are the tools of the spirit that restlessly, relentlessly, mercilessly commands to act, to build and conquer. The problems that Western imperialism encounters in the Hither East and the ways that it chooses to solve them are strikingly demonstrated by the conflict between the government of Iran and the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company that was brought before the League of Nations Council in 1932. The 

Shell interests of Sir Henry Deterding suffered a momentous defeat before the Council that had selected Eduard Benes, of Czechoslovakia, as reporter for the case. The history of the newly-awakened Persian nationalism and the seemingly successful struggle that the government of the Shah, Rezah Khan Pahlevi, leads against the invasion of Western, especially British, capital and political influence, are intimately linked with the story of Persian oil. Playing one great rival from abroad against another, the Shah of Iran has succeeded better than most other Near Eastern rulers in regaining control over his own country and its resources, or in bargaining for favourable returns wherever the exploitation of resources was leased to foreign interests. The main rivals from the West in Persia, so far, have been England, France and the United States. The British-Dutch "Shell" 

interests and the American "Standard Oil" trust fought some of their most bitter feuds over Persian oil. Germany also has made her appearance. During the World War 

Consul Wassmuss, the German "Lawrence", played a rôle 74

in Persia as daring and unusual as that played by Colonel Lawrence and the British agents in Arabia. In 1938 "Lufthansa" scored an important victory over the British-controlled "Imperial Airways" by securing access to Iran for German commercial aviation. Italy, too, is wooing Persia. Persian gunboats are being built in Italian shipyards. The great counterplayer of Western imperialism in Persia-before and after the World War-has been Persia's big neighbor to the 

North: Russia. Czarist imperialism and Soviet penetration into Persia and all over the Middle East have been directed in an outspoken manner against the Western countries. Though the political régime and the economic organization of Russia have changed, the dynamics of her foreign policy in the Hither East have remained essentially the same. To bring Persia into the Russian orbit, that is to prevent her from drifting under the influence of an anti-Russian combination of powers, is a vital question for Russia, apart from minor concrete problems such as Persian markets or access to the Persian Gulf. The rivalry between Russian and Western, especially British, imperialism in Persia, Turkey and Afghanistan, is one of the dominating aspects of international affairs in the Middle East. Carried on with the subtle means of diplomatic and economic penetration, it rarely makes front-page news in the West; but it constitutes one of the weighty unsolved problems in world politics, pregnant with great potentialities. 

The four independent nations of southwestern Asia, Iran,

Iraq, Turkey and Afghanistan, are in a similar position concerning their relations to the Western powers, and to Soviet 

Russia. They welcome Russia's existence as a counterbalance against the Western powers and vice versa. They have a common interest in defending their independence against Western imperialism and against the Russian "colossus of the north". 

Public opinion in these countries, especially in Turkey and Iran, is divided sharply between anti-Russian and pro- Russian factions, that is, between those who see in Russia and in Communist influence the major threats against their national integrity, and those who for material or ideological reasons advocate a closer leaning towards the great Eur-Asian power. The common element in their situation led the four nations of southwestern Asia to conclude a pact of friendship and consultation. This pact is an endeavour to form an axis with one end in Istanbul, --that is in Europe, and the other beyond Kabul-that is near central Asia. The four countries have little in common besides the Mohammedan religion and the endeavour to remain independent national states and to proceed with their industrial and social programs. But in an age of awakening nationalism in Asia, this parallel endeavour may prove to be a decisive factor. It may be capable of bringing about an entirely new 75

political grouping of states in western Asia. As the Balkan states for many decades have been striving for a pact of unity under the device "the Balkans for the Balkan peoples," so the Middle Eastern nations attempt to fortify and strengthen their independence through a common front of defense. The traditional causes of friction and hostility among them are still many. Between Iraq, a leading Arab state, and Iran, as well as Turkey, there exist a number of grievances. Iraq sympathizes with the Syrian Arabs in their disputes with the Turks over the Santschak of Alexandretta. French imperialism has cleverly managed to sustain the cleavage between Turkey and Syria over this small territory, inhabited and claimed both by Turks and by Arabs, thereby preventing the bonds of friendship between the parallel Eastern nationalities from becoming too close. Border difficulties between Persia (Iran) and Mesopotamia (Iraq) have been a long tradition east of the Euphrates. Iran has not desisted from claiming the island of Bahrein and other Arab territory along the Persian Gulf. 

But the modern leaders of Iran and Iraq try and seemingly succeed in ironing out the ancient Arab-Persian difficulties in the interest of higher aims. Through modern propaganda and demonstrations of good will the two governments work towards creating a mutual spirit of respect and friendship among the populations of their countries. The potentialities inherent in the combination of Turkish, Iraqian, Iranian and Afghan nationalism appear even greater when it is considered that similar nationalist movements are under way among the Asiatic populations in the southern regions of the Soviet Union, bordering on Turkey, Iran and Afghanistan. The greater the progress made by the latter countries on their path towards new national and social construction, the stronger will be the temptation set before the nationalities in the Soviet Union to secede and to form independent national states. The progress of the Soviets, on the other hand, has had and will have marked influence upon the psychology and the policies of the independent nations of the Hither East.

Iraq's position as a strong pillar of Arab nationalism and pan-Arabic ambitions tends both to weaken and to strengthen her part in the West-Asiatic four-power combination. Iraq's friendship with Turkey, Iran and Afghanistan has not decreased her interest in the cause of Arab nationalism. On the contrary, culturally and spiritually, the leaders in Baghdad feel themselves first of all as Arabs, akin to Ibn Saud in Mecca and to the Egyptians. But politically the young generation of Arab nationalists has learned to look beyond the narrow borders of racial nationalism and to make friends with all peoples that have parallel interests and do not constitute an immediate threat to their own national existence. The most far-sighted among the Arab leaders, therefore, wholeheartedly 76

welcome the rapprochement between Iraq and her northern non-Arab neighbors, regardless of minor dangers to Arab interests that may result from it. It is the realistic approach of modern enlightened, but fanatic nationalism-Realpolitik, as the East has learned it from the West-through which the leaders of Arabia try to solve the Arab problem. Behind the ties between Arabia and the non-Arab nations of the Hither East stands as a strengthening factor the common religion, the faith of Islam with its spiritual roots in the holy cities. Behind it, too, stands the vision of a broad front of struggling and rising nations, the dim but irresistible vision of an awakening 

East, of a marching Asia, throwing off the yoke of the hated 

European oppressor.

Geographically and spiritually the most Western among the four nations that are linked together through the axis 

Kabul-Teheran-Baghdad-Ankara is Turkey. The Turkish Re public, developing rapidly into a modern state with a state-capitalist economy under the leadership of the late Kemal Ataturk, still reaches with her northwestern frontier into Europe. But the Asiatic Ankara, no longer the European Istanbul, is the Turkish capital. Only a few decades have been passed since the Ottoman sultans ruled over large parts of the Balkans, only a few centuries, since the cohorts under the half-moon besieged Vienna. The Mohammedan mosques in Serbia and 

Albania, not far from the southeastern border of Germany, are a picturesque symbol of the age-old struggle between Asia and Europe, between the East and the West. Today Turkey is linked to the Balkan states through the Balkan pact. Through the "Balkan Entente", comprising Greece, Yugoslavia, Rumania and Turkey, the interests of Asiatic Turkey in southeastern Europe are recognized as legitimate. The 1914 war aim of "throwing the Turk out of Europe" has not been achieved. Two of the members of the Balkan Entente, Yugoslavia and Rumania, until recently were linked through the "Little Entente" to Czechoslovakia in central Europe. Thin and "unreal" as may be the thread that is spun through these various combinations and groupings of states from central 

Europe to central Asia, from Prague via Belgrade and Ankara to Kabul, the realities behind it cover political, economic and strategical possibilities of such magnitude that none of the imperialist powers can afford to overlook it. The "Little Entente" in Europe, the "Balkan Entente" bridging Europe with

Asia, and the "Middle Eastern Entente" on the Asiatic continent, were created to protect the independence of the member states and to strengthen them through collaboration and mutual assistance. From the point of view of the great powers, however, these blocks of smaller nations are not only devices to dam up their own efforts towards penetration but they are also, just the opposite, lanes of approach for imperialist ex- 77

pansion. By gaining influence in one or two of the joints of this long axis, it may prove possible for a great power either to split the axis apart or to strengthen and weld it further into one solid political and economic instrument and to make use of it, if not for actual expansion, at least for advantages at the bargain tables of European diplomacy. All great powers of Europe are viewing the possibilities of "political highway building" from Central Europe and from the Mediterranean to the East with more than theoretical interest. 

The diplomatic struggle that is being waged among the European powers to win Turkish friendship through loans and economic favors, in order to have a secure foothold on the Dardanelles and in Asia Minor in a future European war, illustrates clearly the part that the Middle Eastern nations are meant to play in world politics. Turkey lies geographically and politically between Italy and Soviet Russia. Constantinople is Asia's last hold on Europe.

IV 

Under the pressure of Arab nationalism and face to face with an unstable world situation and ill-balanced domestic conditions, Great Britain and France, the powers most strongly entrenched in the Middle East, have been forced in recent years to retreat several steps from the position they had acquired immediately after the World War. Some of the great schemes of a Middle Eastern empire that were nurtured in Tory circles in London during the War had to be abandoned or postponed indefinitely. But in spite of the growing force of Arab nationalism and in spite of the competition of rival European powers, the United Kingdom has succeeded in holding the areas considered vital for the Empire under British influence, if not under the British flag. Where it proved too costly to rule a country as a colony or protectorate or as a League of Nations mandate, Great Britain consented to give it freedom after she secured for herself a satisfactory position strategically and economically. Both 

Iraq, formerly a British mandate, and Egypt, a protectorate, have thus acquired their complete independence after having concluded with Britain treaties that protect British interests. 

On the basis of these treaties England is able to protect the Suez Canal, to exercise a dominating influence over the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, and to safeguard her political and economic interests in the Nile valley and in Mossul. The two Arab nations, before achieving their independence, had 78

enjoyed under the mandate or protectorate contact with modern rationalized administration; they still enjoy the advantages of British advice and economic enterprise. They have learned from the West the techniques of modern government and business and have begun to apply them themselves. The foundation of the Egyptian (Misr) airline and of the Iraqui state railroads, and the construction of modern highway and public utility systems in these countries are indications of the rapid progress that has been made during the post-war period through this form of cooperation between foreign and native elements. The advocates of the mandate system point with pride at Iraq and her achievements in many fields of modern civilization. 

One factor, however, seems to have been largely overlooked or underrated, when the mandated and protected territories were given their complete independence. Both 

Iraq and Egypt have become centers of organization for Arabic nationalism and pan-Arab sentiment; they are havens of refuge for political fugitives from the neighboring Arab countries under English and French rule. The bitter outbursts of Arab nationalism in Palestine, Transjordan, Syria and Lebanon, which disquiet the colonial offices in London and Paris, receive more or less open encouragement from Iraq, and Egypt also is in hearty sympathy with the cause of the Palestine Arabs. The Cairo and Baghdad press pictured the Mufti of Jerusalem as a martyr of the pan-Arab cause when he escaped from Palestine after hiding from British police for several days in the mosque of Jerusalem. 

Newspapers in Egypt and Iraq are busily engaged in working up sympathy for the Tunisian and Moroccan Arabs in their struggle against the French. The nationalist movements in all Arab countries receive financial support, intellectual guidance, arms and encouragement, from the two modern and most westernized independent kingdoms of Egypt and Iraq.

Arabia ("all lands where Arabs dwell") is growing into one spiritual unit regardless of the many-centuries-old tradition of jealousies and feuds which were largely responsible for her political weakness. The roots of pan-Arab nationalism, which has become a powerful movement wherever people speak one of the many Arab dialects, lie in the holy cities of Islam. The 

Kaba in Mecca is the heart of the Arab and the entire Moslem world. Out of the deserts of the Hedjaz and Nejd, which today form the kingdom of Saudi Arabia, flow the springs of mental strength for the Arab cause. King Ibn 

Saud, the founder of Saudi Arabia and ruler over the holy places of Islam, is one of the most prominent aspirants for the spiritual and political leadership of modern Arabia. As chieftain of the Wahabbi's, a fanatic, puritanical and belligerent sect of orthodox Moslems, and as Sherif over Mecca he is qualified to play a leading rôle among the Arabs in 79

spite of the relative backwardness and poverty of his kingdom. After uniting the Nejd and Hedjaz, Ibn Saud has undertaken a reorganization project of vast consequences in his country. He has settled a large number of Bedouins and turned them into farmers and, against the bitter resistance of his own Wahabbi followers, he introduced modern devices, motor cars and radio, into the most conservative of the Aarb communities. Also in international politics the ruler of Saudi 

Arabia proved a clever reformer. Though not successful in all instances, Ibn Saud made it his policy to achieve a lasting peace and to establish friendly ties between his kingdom and the other Arab countries. The greater the unity among the 

Arabs, the stronger could be, in his opinion, the pressure upon Great Britain and France to give in to Arab demands in Palestine and Syria. In this spirit Ibn Saud concluded treaties of friendship with King Feisal of Iraq in 1930 and with Emir Abdullah of Transjordan in 1932. Thus Feisal and Abdulla, the sons of Hussein Ibn Ali, whose place as Sherif of Mecca Ibn Saud had usurped, became reconciled to the enemy of their father.

Ibn Saud enjoys the benevolence of Great Britain, to which his kingdom is linked by a treaty of friendship, regardless of the grievances that exist between Saudi Arabia and Britain over Akaba as well as over general issues arising from the opposition of Arab nationalism to British imperialism. If Iraq should ever prove to be an untrustworthy ally to Great Britain after having been released from the mandate, the United Kingdom can hope, through a reawakening of dormant jealousies between Saudi Arabia and Iraq, to retain her dominant position in Mesopotamia. 

It so happens that the friendship between Ibn Saud and England is counterbalanced by a friendship between the king of Yemen, Ibn Saud's southern neighbor, and Italy. Whatever the nature of Italian activities has been in the Kingdom of Yemen, the "Arabia Felix" of the Roman Empire, the fact is that, after several years of increasing tension between Yemen and Saudi Arabia, war broke out in 1934 between the British-supported Ibn Saud and the Italian-supported Imam of Yemen. A peace of "Arab brotherhood", with the aim of defending Arabia against any foreign intruder, was concluded in the same year under the auspices of leaders from several other Arab countries. But Great Britain found it necessary, in rearranging her relations with Italy in April 1938, carefully to demarcate Italian and British activities in the Arabian peninsula with special reference to Yemen and Saudi Arabia. There have been persistent rumors, particularly at the time the pan-Islamic conference met in Mecca in 1926, that King Ibn Saud, like his predecessor, Sherif Hussein of Mecca, aspires to become Khalif. To give the 

Khalifate back to the Arabs after the downfall of the Otto- 80

man Empire was one of the war aims of Hussein and the other Arab chieftains who fought with Allenby and Lawrence against the Turks and Germans. Ambitions, however, to re-establish the Khalifate and to become Khalif, with or without foreign support, have been rumored in connection with almost every prominent Arab leader, including the former 

Grand Mufti of Jerusalem. Attempts to rebuild the Khali fate would probably weaken rather than strengthen Arab unity, as long as the still potent jealousies between the different tribes and prominent families of Arabia continue to exist. The chances that the Khalifate will be re-established in the near future appear smaller today than at any time since its abolition. 

Pan-Arab nationalism and the endeavours to establish political unity in the Arab world through the creation of a federation or league of Arab nations are based on the consciousness of a common destiny, a common race, a common culture and religion. The pan-Islamic ideology strengthens pan-Arab nationalism. The pan-Arab movement is also founded the practical conviction that progress towards independence and social improvement can be made only through cooperation. This tendency towards unity is strongest among the larger Arab states and in the more advanced communities. In the small and backward sultanates in Africa, on the Persian Gulf and in the interior of the peninsula, where Ibn Saud's efforts to settle the Bedouins and to modernize land and people show results only very gradually, petty feudal struggles, border raids and wars between sheiks and tribal chieftains are carried on as in olden days, and the idea of a greater Arabia penetrates only slowly through the deserts. The hatred of the foreigner is strong and universal in Arabia, but the love for the neighbor of Arab extraction is not great either. The conflicts among the different factions, tribes and families in Arabia offer convenient possibilities to the Western powers to safeguard their own interests in the rôle of mediators and pacifiers. The 

Sultan of Oman and Muscat, for instance, the "sovereign" ruler over the southeastern nose of the Arabian peninsula which controls the entrance to the Persian Gulf, would probably long ago have been driven from his throne either by the Arabs from the interior or by rival families in his own country, were it not for the British adviser who resides in the capital of Muscat and for the occasional visits of British gunboats and planes that leisurely patrol the coast. 

The Sultan's father, incidentally, who abdicated some years ago, found a refuge in Japan, which enjoys increasingly good trade connections with Oman as well as with most other Arab states. Also the island of Bahrein, situated a few hundred miles northwest of Oman and ruled by a "sovereign" sheik, would probably long ago have been annexed 81

by the Shah of Iran, were it not for the political agent sent from London. The Shah claims the island for his country, and his garrisons are stationed in Bunder Abbas and Bushire on the opposite shore of the Persian Gulf. But the British agent, who advises the Sheik of Bahrein, protects him against Iran's ambitions. Bahrein would be nothing but a poor sandy island with a few dirty and backward towns, of none but strategical importance for the domination of the upper Persian Gulf, had not the Standard Oil Company of California, in the shadow of British imperial protection, developed its modern cantonment on the island. Along the rest of the gulf coast of Arabia, where Britain is interested only to a lesser extent in Arab international politics, wars between the sheiks and Bedouin tribes break out from time to time, and peace settlements transfer territories of several hundred square miles from one chieftain to the other without disturbing the political balance. A piece of medieval Arab world has been preserved under the glowing sun of the desert into the twentieth century. A few miles away from the harbors where the steamers from London, Los Angeles and Livorno call, the tents of the Bedouins still stand, and their masters, mounted on camels and fast horses, consider the desert their kingdom. They are suspicious of anyone who intends to bring change into the hard and proud life that they have lived for many generations.

The contrasts between the advanced cities of Arabia near the Mediterranean coast and in the Euphrates valley, and the poverty and backwardness of the Bedouins and the small villages, are so strong that the educated Syrian, who has taken his degree from the American University in Beyrouth, or the modern Egyptian, who has studied in Cairo or in Europe, appears in all respects to be closer to the Europeans through whom he has come in contact with Western civilization than to his poor fellow countrymen. But many instances have shown that the bonds of nationality are stronger and more vital in an age in which nationalism is rapidly becoming the dominating idea in Asia than superficial ties of civilizing patterns and even deep-seated class conflicts. Pan 

Arab nationalism, backed by the rising middle classes and intellectual youth, finds its opponents not only in the poorest and most backward classes which have difficulties in understanding the new ideas, but, like nationalism in India and China and many other Asiatic countries, it meets also the opposition of the richest and well-established semi-feudal families. This opposition, however, is gradually being overcome. Wealthy and prominent families, as the Husseini in 

Palestine, are leaders in the nationalist movement. Others, such as the Nashashibi who are charged with taking sides with foreign imperialism, are viewed with increasing suspicion by their countrymen. Regardless of strong differences in 82

social development, regardless of religious differences-there are large Christian Arab groups in Syria and Egypt-fostered rather than suppressed by the foreign powers, regardless of all disintegrating traditions, Arab nationalism is growing constantly, and unity among the Arab peoples is becoming a formidable factor in world politics. 

The regions in which Western interests and Arab nationalism have clashed most sharply during the two postwar decades are the French and British mandates on the Mediterranean Sea. Here the Arab population is more densely settled than in most other sections of Arabia. Here live some of the Arabs socially most advanced. Syria, in fact, must be considered the home of modern Arab nationalism; from Damascus the doctrine of enlightened nationalism spread to Cairo and Baghdad. But in this area the Western powers have been unwilling to permit the same gradual development to take place that led Iraq from the status of a mandate to an independent kingdom. There are several reasons why Great Britain and France have been unwilling to give the nationalities of the mandated territories their freedom. The predominant reason for their firmness can be found in the special importance of the mandated areas for strategy and transportation. Palestine and Syria offer a broad approach to the Arab hinterland in case of an emergency. Palestine enables Great Britain to defend the Suez 

Canal; it is a vital link in the Mediterranean chain: Gibraltar. Malta, Cyprus, Haifa; it holds the key to India. In the hills of Jerusalem the highways from North and South and from East and West cross. Syria and Lebanon have, for many decades, been French interest zones. They give 

France a hold on Turkey from the South and a lane to Iran where French and Belgian interests strive to augment their influence. The opportunities of the early post-war period for an all-embracing settlement of Middle Eastern problems were missed, largely because of British-French rivalries in the Palestine-Syria area. Henceforth the chances for a peaceful adjustment between Western interests and Arab aspirations diminished steadily. In 1925 the Druze war took place in the region of Damascus and left a deep hatred in the minds of the Mohammedan Syrians against the French. 

Not to retreat under the pressure of events now became a matter of prestige for the Western powers in these regions. 

After Italian fascism, furthermore, began to develop the "Mare Nostrum" ideology and proclaimed the re-establishment of the Roman Empire as its aim, it may have appeared unwise to Britain and France to permit an increase of Italy's power in the eastern Mediterranean by a gradual withdrawal of British and French influence. After Mussolini declared himself in Libya the protector of Islam, and the wireless in Bari had begun to broadcast inciting anti-British messages 83

in the Arabic language, it may have appeared dangerous to the colonial offices in London and Paris to repeat in Palestine and Syria the same steps that already in the case of Egypt and Iraq had proved to foster Arabian nationalism and pan-Arab solidarity. 

Since the World War the passions of Arab nationalism are centered in Palestine. Palestine, more so than Syria, Tunis and Morocco, where Arab nationalism, too, meets European arms in frequent bloody clashes, has become the political focus of the Arab world. The waves of national emotion rise higher in the Holy Land, where the Mosque of Omar lies, than in any other disputed Arab territory. The situation in Palestine is more complicated than in any other Middle Eastern country, since, under British protection, a non- Arab minority which has acquired already much wealth, valuable land and influence in the country is being settled in Palestine. This minority has grown within fifteen years from about one hundred thousand people to more than four hundred thousand, and the Arabs fear that it will grow further if immigration is not drastically curtailed. They fear that it may push the Arab element, now only twice as strong in number, to the wall, especially since it has powerful financial backing in all parts of the world and is familiar with the tools and devices of modern industry and agriculture. This minority, the Jews, is the object of bitter and fanatical hatred on the part of the Arabs. 

The Jews have come "home" to Zion in growing numbers since the post-war settlements, not only from eastern European countries where they were unwelcome, from Poland, Rumania and the Ukraine, from Yemen and far away East 

Asian ports, but also from western Europe and America.

Between 1933 and 1938, 40,000 Jews immigrated into Palestine from Germany alone. When the British government, in the ill-fated Balfour declaration of 1917, promised the Jews, under Zionist pressure from England and America, to "view with favor the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people"-without, however, "prejudicing the civil and religious rights of existing non- Jewish communities"-British policy, besides yielding to Jewish pressure, obviously intended to drive a spearhead into the Arab world. There had been a period during the 

War when the Arab leaders themselves welcomed the prospect of an influx of Jewish population, Jewish capital and 84

enterprise to help develop Arabia. Several communications of Ameer Feisal, the later King of Iraq, testify to that. But 

Jewish immigration was then considered as an influx of scattered elements to be spread out over a united and strong Arab national state. That there is little anti-Jewish feeling in the Western sense among the Arabs, and that the possibility for a progressive development of Arabia with Jewish assistance actually did exist, is borne out by the fact that a considerable number of Jewish enterprises, old and new, do prosperous and undisturbed business in Iraq and several other Arab countries. These Jews in Iraq and 

Egypt, fearing a hostile Arab reaction in their own country, frequently view with disfavour the Zionist endeavours in Palestine. Arab-Jewish cooperation might have been possible along similar lines also in Palestine. But Zionism demanded more; it had hoped for the establishment of a "National 

Home", an autonomous Jewish community with its own Jewish culture, possibly even a Jewish state. The Jews were not content to play in Palestine the same insecure rôle of tolerated "guests" that they had played for centuries in many countries all over the world. Jewish nationalism demanded a "home" for the Jewish nation in a world where nationalism was supreme. With a strong Jewish community centered in one land, it was thought that the difficulties confronting the Jewish minorities in other countries, especially in eastern Europe, could be eased. The Jews, spread all over the globe, would thus spiritually be reunited and strengthened. Among the projects to settle Jews in closed communities that received prominence in the post-war period (for instance in the autonomous Jewish republic of "Birobidjan" in the

Soviet Union, and in French Madagascar), Palestine proved to be the greatest attraction to the Jews. Palestine was their ancient home before the diaspora, where once their temples stood. That for more than twelve centuries another people had settled in their old house and that this people, too, had nationalistic ambitions, the Jews overlooked in their joy of seeing their ancient dream of a "return" near realization. The new owners of the country, the Arabs, never gave their consent to the building up of a strong and organized Jewish minority in Palestine the minium demanded by Zionism-especially not as long as their country was under foreign domination.

In view of the hostile Arab attitude, British control over Palestine was declared by the government of the United Kingdom as well as by many Jews to be an essential means for carrying out the cultural task of establishing a Jewish "National Home", and to preserve peace between the two nationalities. When Arab ambitions for an independent united Arabia were shattered after the War and Jewish immigration into Palestine began to take place on a large 85

scale, the Arabs considered Zionism as a weapon of Western imperialism wielded against their national aspirations. 

They saw in it a spearhead of British interests driven into their national body, a high-sounding and humanitarian excuse for the British to dominate the Holy City, where lies the control over the highways from Europe to India and from Asia to Africa. Far-sighted Jews feared from the beginning that the link between Zionism and British imperialism might prove fatal to the establishing of friendly relations between Jews and Arabs in Palestine. Many Jews, furthermore, bore ill-will against the British government, since their demands to make "Palestine the Jewish Home" went considerably farther than what British policy was willing to grant, namely, "a Jewish Home in Palestine". Disputes between the British government and Jewish organizations over the immigration quota increased Jewish disappointment in British policy, especially since the government, under pressure from the Arabs, greatly restricted Jewish immigration. But, regardless of British-Jewish differences, the only practical prospect finally to establish a "home", and the fact that they were still a minority in Palestine forced the Jews to the side of the British, who, with their machine guns and airplanes, still are the actual rulers of Palestine. 

When Great Britain, after more than a decade of stormy experiences in Palestine, realized that the optimism of her earlier policy might no longer be fully warranted she decided upon a new deal for Palestine. Her early policy, as expressed through her dealings with the Arab leaders during the War (promising a united independent Arabia) and through her promises given in the Balfour declaration to the Jews, was based on the hope that it would prove possible to balance the conflicting interests of Arabs and Jews without great cost and without stirring up public opinion in other Arab countries. When the chances to realize these hopes vanished and, on the other hand, a distinct growth of pan- Arabic nationalism and an increasing political vitality of Islam was making a satisfactory three-cornered relationship between the British, Jews and Arabs impossible, the British government decided that the time for action had arrived. 

It sent a commission to Palestine to work out a plan which was to prove satisfactory to British interests and acceptable to Jews and Arabs. The commission, in 1937, published recommendations that found the immediate approval of the British government. The report advocated a partition of 

Palestine into three parts: the fertile coastal strip with the ports of Haifa and Tel Aviv was to be given to the Jews to form an independent Jewish state; the interior and less productive parts of the mandate were to be given to the Arabs as an independent state. Certain rights to maintain military establishments in both states were to be reserved 86

to the United Kingdom. The Holy City, several places in the neighborhood, and a corridor linking Jerusalem with the Mediterranean Sea were to remain under British rule. 

British control over Jerusalem appeared justified to the commission in the interest of peace since the city was a place sacred to three contesting religious communities. The report did not contain practical proposals as to how the considerable number of Arabs, who live today as a minority in the predominantly Jewish coastal region, could be transferred to the sandy plains of the interior.

The partition project met strong opposition from both Jews and Arabs. Neither community appeared to be satisfied with the share allotted to it. Neither community was willing to give up the claim to "its" sacred city. The Arabs considered partition a scheme to rob them definitely of the fertile coast of Palestine, and a storm of protest arose over the entire Arab world in support of the Palestine Arabs. Among the Jews, on the other hand, scepticism against the project of an independent Jewish national state had been growing for some time. First of all, many Jews began to realize that the creation of a Jewish state might have detrimental effects upon the position of Jewish minorities in other countries. 

In an age in which Jews have risen to positions of power and influence in many countries and that has produced, as a reaction, a growing tide of anti-Semitism throughout the world, many Jews feared lest the creation of a Jewish national state might lead to a crystallization of anti-Jewish feeling and serve as an excuse for anti-Semitic movements in other countries to demand expulsion of Jews to Palestine as members of an alien national community. Furthermore, since illusions about collective security have vanished, it was realized that it might prove to be very difficult to defend the small Jewish state in an international war. Under pressure from Arabs and Jews the British government finally dropped the plan and decided to take recourse to threecornered negotiations. Until a solution will be foundobviously an impossible task-it will be the unenviable task of the British High Commissioner for Palestine to maintain peace and order under the mandate system in the Holy Land, whose spirit has become even more tense and bellicose than before.

The land that is called the Holy Land has been a land of struggles and conflicts for thousands of years. In the twentieth century it is not only the battlefield for Arab and Jewish nationalism, but also a focus in which the awakened Arab nationalism, the rising East, clashes with the interests of Western imperialism. The capital of Palestine, called a holy city by Christians, Moslems and Jews, has become a city of fear and suppressed hatred. Irreconcilable appear the contrasts between the Arab and the Jewish city of Jeru- 87

salem, between the "old" Oriental and the "new" European city. Dark and narrow are the streets of the Arab town within the heavy city walls. Veiled women rush through the colourful bazaars that are as noisy as bird houses during the day, and as silent and oppressive as graves at night. 

More veils are to be seen in Palestine today than in previous years; their wearing is a protest of Arabic womanhood against the clothes and behaviour of Western Jewish women that are considered degenerate and immoral by the Arabs. 

In the midst of the old city, where the dark houses often are built one upon the other, lies the "via dolorosa" and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, said to hold Christ's tomb. The character of the old city, none the less, is thoroughly Arab. 

Its center is the wide open space where "al Aqsa" stands, sparkling and proud, and the Dome of the Rock from which

Mohammed, according to the tradition of Islam, ascended to heaven on his steed. At midday and at sunset the chants of the Koran sound over the wide stone-paved "harem" in praise of Allah the Almighty and Only, and Mohammed, his prophet. Near the edge of the Mosque, in a dark and narrow corner overshadowed by the surrounding buildings, stands an ancient wall of grey and heavy stones called the "wailing wall". Here the Jews remember the temple of

Solomon, which once stood on the site of Al Aqsa; for centuries the cries of black-coated Jews, imploring Jehovah to restore their destroyed temple, have resounded from the wall. 

Today even the opportunity to lament at the site of their destroyed temple has been taken from the Jews. The wailing wall is deserted. The bitterness and tension between Jews and Arabs is such that few Jews dare to penetrate into the old Arab city and particularly into the neighborhood of the Mosque. Like an impenetrable mystery, a remnant of ages long past, a piece of the invincible East, lies the old Arab city of Jerusalem on the hillside, surrounded by the heavy town walls which Hebrews, Romans, Arabs, Turks, and Crusaders and many more nations from East and West have built. 

Outside the city walls, a few steps beyond the Jaffa Gate, a new city has grown up, thoroughly Western in character, a piece of Picadilly or Kurfurstendamm with music-cafes, movies and elegant shop windows, transplanted into the middle of the Orient. This is the Jewish, the "modern" city of Jerusalem. Here Hebrew, like Arabic a Semitic language, is spoken in addition to English, much German, and Yiddish. 

The new city grew up rapidly during the short post-war period; an entire new garden suburb, "German Quarter”, was built mainly since the Hitler movement came into power in Germany. Simultaneously with the Jewish city of 

Jerusalem, there developed the port city of Haifa and the ultra-modern Tel Aviv, an exclusively Jewish community in 88

the fertile coastal region, and several smaller Jewish towns. 

Here the Jews live their own community life, largely undisturbed by outside influences. Regardless of the strong cultural differences between those from Yemen or the Ukraine, and those from Berlin or New York, the Jewish settlers attempt to build up a uniform national culture. In the urban communities this culture is predominantly Western and metropolitan. But in the rural areas, where Jewish villages and 

Jewish collective farms have sprung up, it is as if the spirit of the home-coming people remembered suddenly the longforgotten spirit of its Eastern homeland and attempted, refuge-seeking, to submerge itself in it. "Blood and soil" has become the ideal of Jewish nationalism in Palestine in the same way it has in several Western countries from which the Jews had fled. Jewish nationalism in Palestine has begun to resemble Western and Arab nationalism in intransigeance and intolerance. The Jews realize that they must become as strong as possible in Palestine in the shortest possible time, numerically as well as physically. They do everything, therefore, to increase Jewish immigration until new legislation may bar this; they cooperate with the authorities in training and building up auxiliary police; they acquire and irrigate land for farming purposes. In the neighborhood of Tel Aviv a number of collective farms, the so-called "Khibuzze", have been organized, each inhabited by several hundred young Jewish farmers from all parts of the world, many of them refugee intellectuals from Germany. Some of these Khibuzze are strictly communistic as a matter of principle; no private property whatever is allowed. Others have chosen the collective method of cultivation only as a preliminary device for the achievement of optimum results with small investments, and for training purposes; later on the collective method is to be replaced by individual farm ownership. Some of the Khibuzze are managed with almost Spartan discipline and simplicity. Many young families are compelled by lack of space to dwell together with other families or roomers in small tents.

Arabs come to the Jewish cities and farms to find work or trade. But from their old-fashioned, poverty-stricken quarters they watch the rapid development of the Jewish undertakings with bitter feelings, a mixture of hatred, envy and revenge, as if they were observing the works of evil demons. On the city walls of Jerusalem could be seen in the autumn of 1937 a large swastika, painted in black on the ancient rocks overlooking the valley of Hebron, together with two significant words: "Heil Hiteler" (sic!). This sign has become a symbol of Arab sentiment in Palestine. The forms, however, which the Arabs generally choose to express their feelings towards the Jews as well as towards the British are less harmless than this attempt towards Western symbolism. Attacks on 89

individual Jews or Jewish buses traveling over the lonely countryside are frequent. No less frequent are attacks on 

British policemen on duty in Arab villages. One day the 

British military found the valuable equipment of the airport of Lydda in flames, the work of the Arabs. The attacks of the Arabian guerillas-"bandits", as they are called in the official language of the Palestine government-are sudden. 

From nowhere they come, shooting or throwing bombs at their enemies from the ambush, and into nowhere they vanish. 

In several instances the Jews have retaliated by killing Arabs. The British try to maintain peace by imposing collective penalties on Arab towns and hamlets if the guilty individuals cannot be found. Planes are sent out to bombard the towns, and the homes of sheiks are frequently dynamited by government troops. Several Arabs and one Jew found guilty of terrorism before the military courts were executed. 

But these stern measures have not succeeded in restoring quiet. The feeling of uncertainty and fear persists, so much so that a number of Jewish immigrants have again emigrated to other countries. Palestine has become a political border land between Asia and Europe, a bulwark between East and West, and it appears as if no peace could reign in a land that is condemned through its geopraphy to be a crossing point of the highways of mankind, and through its history to be a sanctuary for three religions. In the first world war the nations of the Middle East have played a significant part as tools in the hands of Western imperialists. 

Should Europe live to see another great war, the mountains and deserts east of the Mediterranean between Asia and

Africa will be one of the main theatres of this war.

Quiet and peaceful, and of enchanting brilliance, are the clear summer nights on the Bosporus where the waves play on the shores of Asia and Europe. Quiet and peaceful lie the wide plains of the Ukraine in the moonlight, where Europe and Asia meet. The silvery light wraps East and West into the veil of eternity. At dusk, when the sun has set over 

Europe in the West, the sharpness and hardness, the uncompromising cruelty with which the bright day invests things, withers away. Soft and yielding are the contours flowing together from East and West, tenderly they embrace each other, sounding and resounding in a never-ending symphony of tone and colour. But each night brings another morning, and when the sun rises over the East the lines of things, and hopes, of intentions, and conflicts become sharp. East and West, Europe and Asia, again stand apart, and in the depths of the soul and soil of Asia men feel a fearful and inspiring trembling. 90

Chapter IV Europe: An Essay in Political Realism 

I The forces of creation were in a jubilant and overabundant mood when they made Europe. As in a bizarre dream the coast lines of the small continent wind. Bays and isles seem to be carved out from land and sea by a loving and capricious hand. Mountains and plains, rivers and lakes, are strewn over Europe with inexhaustible phantasy. From the silvery glaciers of the Alps the rivers rush down in all directions to the seas, each forming fertile and beautiful valleys, each telling its own tale of mystery. Invigorating air is carried by the winds from the mountains to the sea and from the sea to the mountains. Upon the lands between the green islands of Ireland and the grottos of Greece, between the forests of Finland and the sunny coasts of Spain nature with spendthrift hands threw everything that she had to offer.

Europe, north of the Alps, was covered with thick dark forests when, in centuries long past, tribe after tribe swept down from Asia towards the shores of the Atlantic and 

Mediterranean. From east to west the invaders wandered, incited by hunger or fear or lust of conquest. High civilizations were then flourishing around the Mediterranean Sea; but they could not stem forever the tides that were advancing from the north and east. Rome needed centuries to die, and several more centuries passed before a new civilization dawned over the ruins of the Eternal City and the wastes of Central Europe. Moulding together the remnants of Greek and Roman culture with the dim memories of their tribal past, adopting the Christian religion which had penetrated into Europe from Asia in the days of the Roman decline, the tribes of the Goths and Saxons, of the Franks, Alemans and Lombards laid the ground from which a new tree was to spring. Tall. strong, rich in form and colour, and of immense fertility throughout a millennium and more, this tree was to send its branches into distant lands and spread its seeds all over the globe: the culture of the West. 

The creative spirit of European men rivaled the spirit of nature; there was the same intensity, the same pulsating and driving rhythm, the same restlessness, the same density of 91

motives and ideas, and yet the same harmonious calm in the great manifestations of Western culture. Men's creations, from the fjords of Norway to the hills of Sicily, from the rocks of Scotland to the Black Sea, became so powerful, so colourful and multiform, that their mighty melody resounded over the entire world. Into their landscape the peoples of 

Europe built their churches, their boroughs and castles; throughout the centuries their towns grew into cities. The 

Domes on the Rhine, the cathedrals in the valley of the Seine, the castles on Thames and Danube, and the palaces on the banks of the Tiber are among the great symbols of the world-feeling of the West that many generations of Western men erected in stone. As manifold in form as the mountains and valleys of Europe, as various as the languages and poems and songs of the European peoples, so multiform are the stone monuments through which Western man expresses his longing for the universe, his urge for earthly fulfillment, his desire to find the eternally beautiful and to conquer eternity itself.

In spite of the diversity in form and colour, in language and spirit, between the regions of Europe and between the centuries that followed one another in hurried course each filled to the brim with new ideas, new discoveries and ambitions-there exists one proud and powerful melody, one majestic theme that runs through all epochs of Western history, and swings in all creations of the West. There exists an inner unity, beyond the ages, beyond the borders of language-groups and nations, a deep harmony underneath all rivalries and battles. It is a unity of feeling, often more of wanting and longing than of willing, of dreaming more than of awakened acting. It cannot be accurately defined through analysis. But no conflict of ideas, no war, no economic competition can destroy it completely as long as a Western culture exists. It is the life rhythm, the cosmic bloodstream of the West. In the early youth of Western civilization this unity was not merely inward, not merely one of feeling. The dome of the Christian Church of Rome and of the Holy Roman Empire of Germanic nations vaulted as the spiritual and political unifier over the diversity and multitudiousness of feudal life. Only for brief moments did this outward unity of the Occident last as a political reality; it was soon to be broken up by the intensity of diverse forces into smaller units, into kingdoms and duchies, into Orthodox and Protestant churches, and finally into nations and races and economic "classes". But as a longing, as a dream and faint hope, as a consciousness of common value and common destiny, deep beneath the endeavours and battles of the day, the unity of the West persisted. In rare moments when European rulers and peoples were made to see great common aims, or when serious dangers threatened from outside, this feeling of unity 92

and common destiny came forward in the European mind, and Europe attempted to rally politically. When the Crusaders sailed east to free Christ's tomb from pagan rule, when the Turks marched on Vienna, the call to unite was heard; but soon again the spirit of unity sank back into the realm of the subconscious.

Destiny made Europe the birthplace of the most powerful civilization that the world has seen. Far into the East advanced the spirit and the spears of the newly-born West. 

The thrust of European knighthood against the Musulman and the march of the Germanic knights into the Slavic wilderness, colonizing, converting and building church-fortresses along their path, were the first manifestations of the expanding and conquering vitality of the West. When, in later centuries, Europe had become too narrow to shelter the growing nations and the restless spirit of men, the white man set out to conquer other continents, the Americas, Africa, wide parts of Asia, and Australia. Carried forward by an insatiable urge to explore, to find and know, to dominate and exploit, to create and convert, European man began to conquer the universe. For centuries any resistance against his will seemed in vain. The inventions through which he mastered nature and the determination upon conquest, which seemed a refreshing necessity for his will-power, made him invincible. Wherever the men from the West went, exploring, conquering, trading, they carried Western culture to other peoples. The settlers, who came to stay in North and South 

America, in Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, brought with them the same spirit of activity, of restlessness and intensity. They were true children of the West. First as "colonials", then as members of independent nations and self-governing dominions, they began to build up their own national spirit and tradition. But the life-substance of Europe, the foundations of Western culture, remained also their foundations. They were offsprings of the great tree of Western civilization and rooted in the same soil as the national cultures of Europe.

The evolution of European civilization throughout the centuries was not uniform. Dynasties and peoples that in one century were the masters of powerful empires sank into political impotence during the next. Regions in which mighty and proud cultures flourished became the battleground for contesting foreign powers. Countries that for centuries had had no political will of their own suddenly grew into units of will and strength and began to conquer. European history is a constant rising and falling, an awakening and declining of its individual parts. And yet there exists this unity that is greater than the rising and falling parts, and that lasts longer than the individual dynasties and national empires: the cultural and spiritual community of the West. 93

II 

After centuries of fertile growth and forceful expansion Europe, and with it the entire Western civilization, has entered upon a crucial stage in its development. The problems that confront the peoples of the Occident in the twentieth century seem graver than any they have faced before. The crisis that the West has been undergoing since the beginning of the twentieth century concerns the entire political, social and spiritual structure of Western society and, perhaps, the very continuation of Western culture. The nineteenth century ended for Europe on August 2, 1914. The World

War marks the dawn of the new century with all its new difficulties, fears and uncertainties. Twentieth-century Europe is confronted with two categories of problems: the "internal" European problems that root in the very nature of Western civilization and that have developed there throughout the course of the centuries, and the "external" problems, arising from the fact that suddenly new powers and civilizations sprang up outside of Europe with their own claims for a share of the world. The European nations are no longer alone! It is the coincidence of the internal and external problems that makes the situation of Europe so grave. 

Nationalism and the idea of the national state are an essential part of the inheritance that the nineteenth century has left to the twentieth. As the dynastic state had, in preceding centuries, develoned out of the entity of the medieval feudal empire, gradually but not without bloody disturbances and painful adjustments, so the national states began, during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, to form themselves above and within the dynastic states. The American and French revolutions provided the pattern for the nationaldemocratic movements that determined the course of Western history for the coming one hundred and fifty years. The struggle of the nationalities for independence and self-determination filled the entire nineteenth century. All efforts towards an organization of the hopelessly scattered nationalities into larger units, all attempts in the direction of universal coordination failed. The Holy Roman Empire was formally dissolved in 1806. Napoleon, who had dreamed of organizing Europe around Paris-he considered himself the successor of Charlemagne-was defeated less than a decade thereafter. Prince Metternich's "Holy Alliance" broke apart under the pressure of power politics and nationalism. The 

Austro-Hungarian Empire, the last of the great supernational states of Central Europe, collapsed in 1918. The small nationalities, the Czechs, the Croats the Lithuanians and Latvians, fought for their independence, whereas the large nations that had already achieved their national unity 94

competed with each other to enlarge their possessions and spheres of influence. Nationalism and imperialism became synonymous where strong and expanding nations were involved.

The political and economic struggle among the great powers for hegemony over Europe and for world empire, and the endeavours of the small nationalities to achieve national unity and independence, were the main themes of European history during the pre-war decades. They were the themes of the World War and the "peace" settlements of 1919. They are the major themes of European international politics at the present hour. In this respect, nothing has changed in Europe. 

The World War was a battle in which two combinations of rival nations clashed. The Allied powers, victorious in the battle of 1914-18, achieved several of their aims: they reaped territories, colonies and reparations from the vanquished; they destroyed the Austro-Hungarian Empire, "liberated" many eastern European nationalities and, for the time, organized them through alliances and loans into subservient satellites. But if it was the aim of the Allies to eliminate 

Germany from the ranks of the big powers and from world markets, this aim remained unaccomplished. The young national state of Prussia-Germany was weakened, but not defeated. The "peace treaties" brought an armistice to Europe, but no peace.

If the treaties of 1919 would have been devised for the very purpose of sharpening the national conflicts within Europe, they could not have been made any more ingeniously. 

Hardly one single issue was "solved" by the peace conference.

European "stability" under the new order could exist only as long as the overwhelming combine of powers that had collaborated to defeat Germany remained united. This, however, was obviously impossible for any length of time. Already during the war differences of opinion as to the war-aims were pronounced in the camp of the Allies. England's interest in the continuation of the balance of power on the Continent was opposed to French plans for a complete dismemberment of Germany. Undisputed French hegemony on the Continent had appeared unpermissible to the island empire in the days of the first Napoleon; it did not seem any more attractive to England a century later, although France and Britain had fought shoulder on shoulder for four and a half years. Furthermore, America had entered the war for her own ends and ideals and, while playing her first short guest rôle on the scene of European diplomacy, sided with the British. French diplomacy was unsuccessful in its attempts to split Germany apart. The occupation of the Rhineland and the project to organize the predominantly Catholic southern and western parts of Germany into a federation of Rhenish states under French leadership, as in the days of 95

Napoleon, proved failures. Though the most ambitious French plans did not materialize, Germany was, nevertheless, reduced to a political and economic status with which she could never content herself. Under the stress of revolution and inflation Germany's primary objective for years was simply to "carry on", to continue her existence as a national unit. 

The disintegrating forces within her proved, more than once, so strong that French nationalism seemed near its final victory. But eventually the Germans regained their strength. 

They felt humiliated, but not demoralized. The German people and government worked toward one goal: to restore the fatherland to the position among the European powers that they considered due her. 

Franco-German dualism, a key problem of European prewar diplomacy, is one of the foremost issues that the World War and the peace treaties left unsolved. In spite of many sincere manifestations of good will between the peoples of France and Germany, progress towards reconciliation in the post-war decade was slight. France was unable and unwilling to give up the position she had acquired through the war. Germany was equally unable and unwilling to tolerate the conditions imposed upon her. So Franco-German relations continued to run along the old track. One of the main pillars of French pre-war policy had been the alliance with Russia, the "nightmare" of Prince Bismarck. In the early post-war period the Franco-Russian alliance was replaced by a system of alliances between France and the "successor" states in 

Eastern Europe: Poland and the Little Entente. Though the partners changed, the objective of French policy remained the same: Germany should be kept under control by double pressure from east and west. Short of a direct and thorough understanding with Germany-an understanding that appeared to be near several times during the Briand-Stresemann era. but that withered away again like a dream-the policy of blocking Germany from west and east was considered by the French the only possible way to prevent German domination over central and eastern Europe and to maintain the position of France as a first-rate power. It was one of Europe's tragedies that, just at a time when in Germany a new spirit was searching for new roads in Franco-German relations, the elements that were at the helm in France were unable to free themselves from the traditional concept of Franco-German relations. Thus Clemenceau's pronouncement that "there exist twenty million Germans too many" kept its ominous and discouraging meaning for Europe. When France seemed ready, in later years, to make concessions and to relax her tense suspicion against her eastern neighbour, German mentality had reached a stage that rendered chances for reconciliation slight. The motto: "too late" stood tragically over Franco-German relations. 96

The noblest forces in both nations have worked to reconcile the two peoples and to give a new turn to European history. 

But the rigid laws of behaviour prescribed by the struggle for hegemony, century-old fears and distrust planted deeply in the souls of the two nations, and interested by-standers who feared for their own security should age-old quarrels between the two peoples be settled, proved stronger than the idealists in both countries who dreamt of establishing a firm and lasting friendship between Germany and France. 

France and Germany were opponents not only in western, but also in eastern Europe. The aims of Germany's eastern 

European policy became apparent in the treaty of Brest- Litovsk which the victorious Germans imposed upon the 

Bolsheviks. Germany's subsequent defeat on the western front left her eastern ambitions unfulfilled. Through the defeat of Germany, the collapse of czarist Russia, and the destruction of the Austro-Hungarian empire an entirely new situation had arisen in eastern Europe. At the expense of 

Russia, Austria-Hungary and Germany, a number of small states were created. Other states which had fought on the 

Allied side were rewarded with shares of territory. Almost all eastern European states, however, were heavily burdened with difficult minority problems because of the redistribution of land by the peace treaties. Few represented ethnical and political entities. Most of them continued bitter feuds with at least one or two of their neighbors over an ethnical minority or a disputed borderline. The difficulties between Poland and Lithuania, between Poland and Czechoslovakia, between the members of the Little Entente on the one hand and Hungary and Bulgaria on the other-difficulties that at one time or another might easily have led to war during the post-war decades-are but the best known examples of the many unsettled problems that existed among the smaller states of eastern Europe. The natural distribution of the different ethnical groups and the geography of eastern Europe are such that the principle of nationalism, when applied in extremes under these complex circumstances, will, from inner necessity, lead to conflict and chaos. The domestic difficulties of the smaller eastern European countries and the conflicts among them were overshadowed by the contrasts that existed between them and their large neighbors. The tension between Poland and Germany over Upper Silesia and the Corridor, between Lithuania and Germany over Memel, between Poland and Soviet Russia over the Ukraine, between Czechoslovakia and Germany over the Sudeten region, between Rumania and Russia over Bessarabia, and between Yugoslavia and Italy over Fiume was the unavoidable outcome of the post-war settlements. 

During the first post-war decade it was France who succeeded in organizing the colorful pell-mell of races, nationali- 97

ties and language-groups of eastern Europe into a political system. Since states such as Poland, Rumania, Czechoslovakia or Yugoslavia were naturally distrustful of their large neighbors, France brought them easily into the orbit of French diplomacy through pacts of friendship and military assistance and through loans with political strings attached. 

French diplomats and bankers worked towards creating a solid belt of nations from the Balkans to the Baltic in order to block Germany or any other competitor in eastern Europe. 

Czechoslovakia was thought of as a spearhead directed into the side of Germany from the east. France prevented disputes among her allies, such as the Polish-Czech tension, from becoming acute. She even attempted to bring Hungary, Bulgaria and Austria into the French hegemonial system by various projects for economic reconstruction of the Danubian basin. For a short time, French gold ruled eastern Europe.

But French hegemony was soon to be challenged. The rivals to French aspirations in eastern Europe were Germany and Russia, both of which had suffered directly from the establishment of the new order, and Italy, whose active interest in the Balkans was intensified after the war and extended into the Danubian region. In view of the situation created by French post-war diplomacy it was quite natural for the young and weak German republic to draw closer to the one large country in the East with which she had no conflicting interests: Soviet Russia. The Soviet Union and the Weimar 

Republic were both interested in gaining time to strengthen their political and economic positions; there existed between them a far-reaching reciprocity of economic interests, and both countries stood in natural antagonism to the French eastern-European combination. The hard terms that imperial 

Germany had imposed upon the Soviets at Brest-Litovsk were forgotten when the German republic, several years later, concluded with the Soviet Union the Rapallo Treaties which marked the beginning of a decade of friendly political and economic relations between the two powers. England during all this time viewed French preponderance in eastern Europe and the Soviet-German rapprochement with suspicion. 

She threw her weight so as gradually to restore a normal balance in western Europe. But she carefully refrained from accepting definite commitments in the eastern European zones of tension. 

It is significant for the statesmanship at work at the Paris peace conference that strong dissatisfaction with the peace settlements was to be found not only among the vanquished, but also among the victorious nations. That Germany, Austria, Hungary, and Bulgaria would endeavour to bring about a revision of the peace treaties was self-evident. 

That certain issues among the other successor states, which in principle stood against revision, could not be satisfactorily 98

settled may be explained by the ethnical conditions prevailing in eastern Europe and the very nature of nationalism. But there remained one other large nation in Europe dissatisfied with the post-war order: Italy. Although one of the victors of the war and distrustful of Germany's endeavours in the Danubian region and bitterly opposing Anschluss, Austria's reunion with Germany, Italy appeared more and more frequently on the side of the revisionists. Dissappointed over her war spoils, she aspired towards becoming a leading power. But her way was blocked wherever she turned. In the Mediterranean, England, her old and trusted friend, kept close guard over the Empire life-line leading from Gibraltar via Malta to Suez and cutting straight through Italy's "own sea". In North Africa France refused to recognize Italian claims. Arabia had been divided up between England and 

France without Italy getting her share, a clear violation of the agreement of 1915 that brought Italy into the World War. In the Balkans and on the Danube, too, France and her allies, especially Yugoslavia, stood in Italy's way. D'Annunzio's conquest of Fiume from the Yugoslavs had almost brought on another war. The threads of Italian diplomacy were therefore spun at first in the direction of the small European powers that suffered most under the status quo: 

Hungary, Bulgaria and Austria, and later towards Germany. 

The contrasts that existed between the big powers thus became more and more intimately intertwined with the conflicts between and within the small states. Both Italy and 

Germany began to look favourably upon minority aspirations in the French-controlled successor states, in order to weaken them and to drive a wedge into the diplomatic structure that 

France was building all over Europe.

The power that, next to France, had reaped the most important territorial benefits from the war was England. The 

British Empire now stretched over one-fourth of the land surface of the earth, embracing one-quarter of the world's population. British European policy for centuries had been determined by the fact that England was not only a leading power in the European concert, but also the center of a world empire with far-flung possessions. The status of the 

Empire, in which marked centrifugal tendencies were felt after the war, as well as the fact that British interests on the continent seemed well guarded, turned England for a number of years largely into an interested spectator of the 

Continental diplomatic game. In western Europe Great 

Britain had accepted certain obligations under the Locarno 

Treaties to protect "England's frontiers on the Rhine". In the Mediterranean she was linked in friendship with Italy.

Whenever British diplomacy made an active move on the Continent, it was to safeguard the never-clearly-defined but yet concrete British Continental interests. England tried to 99

restore a certain balance among the Continental powers by bringing Germany back to the European discussion table without, however, drastically opposing French policy and jeopardizing Continental stability. British and French inter ests opposed each other in several parts of the world during the post-war period. In the Hither East, in Africa and in the 

Far East problems existed concerning which France and Great Britain did not see eye to eye. Though none of these problems was vital enough for the British to drop their friendship with France, they looked on with approval when Germany, who had ceased to be the dangerous naval and commercial rival of the pre-war days, began towards the middle of the twenties to recover her inner stability. Great 

Britain was interested in re-establishing friendly ties with her former enemy also for the reason that she watched with apprehension Russia's activities in central and southwestern Asia and could not wish for too great an intimacy between the two Continental powers.

Great Britain's interest in Continental balance and stability, the endeavours of France to cement her eastern European alliances and to induce Great Britain to guarantee the European status quo, Italy's movements to strengthen her influence in the Mediterranean and on the Danube, and Germany's efforts to stabilize herself internally and prepare herself to break through the inimical ring of states surrounding her these were the main objectives of European post-war diplomacy. Many minor motives were interwoven: border troubles, minority conflicts, alignments and realignments of the small states along political and economical lines, in which the leaders of the small countries exercised all their strength and skill to gain advantages at the cost of each other or at the expense of one of the large powers. But the grouping of the small powers, as in pre-war days, followed essentially the directions prescribed by the diplomacy of the big powers. Though the partners and alignments have changed, and are, in fact, constantly changing, the content of European diplomacy has remained the same. 

For more than a decade after the War the diplomatic activities of Europe were carried on under a veil that deprived them of much sharpness in appearance. This veil was provided by the machinery of the League of Nations. Regardless of the idealistic vision with which the League was conceived by some of its founders, it was not capable of changing the substance of international politics. For the diplomacy of France and her satellites the League of Nations was a convenient instrument to maintain the status created by the peace treaties, in other words to "keep Germany down".

For Stresemann and the diplomats of the Weimar Republic the League of Nations seemed the most suitable way gradually to recover some of Germany's lost influence in inter- 100

national affairs. For Great Britain the League, especially in later years, was an ideal vehicle to prevent European affairs from becoming rigid and to restore gradually a "healthy" balance. To the small nations the League appealed as a safety device, rendering high appropriations for national defense less necessary. Thus, Geneva, with its soothing atmosphere of unreality, served for a few years as a picturesque stage-setting for the duels of world diplomacy, and the lofty visions of "world peace", "collective security", and "international justice" provided a luring screen behind which were hidden the hard realities of power politics. was

Though the dream of world peace and world stability had greatly affected the minds of many people in many nations, it did not last long. The stability of the twenties was more obvious than real. Powerful political, economic and spiritual forces unnoticed then but by a few observers-were at work pressing developments forward. Events were in preparation that before long were brutally to tear to pieces the veil that covered caressingly the nakedness of political life. 

Events occurred during the War and the post-war decade that might have served as signs that a new age had dawned, that the security and relative peacefulness of the nineteenth century had irrevocably passed. Certain events might have served warning that the faith in world progress, in peaceful social adjustment and in international conciliation founded on unstable ground; they might have shaken the comfortable optimism that was settling down in the hearts of many men, a dangerous and intoxicating inheritance of nineteenth-century philosophy. But the symptoms of approaching storms were not recognized. The people did not not want storms; weary from the World War, they wished for peace, for money-making and merry-making. The stormsigns on the sky of world politics were interpreted as phenomena that necessarily follow a great war. The Russian and 

German revolutions, the fascist march on Rome, Italy's

Korfu venture, the German inflation with its far-reaching social and psychological consequences, the Hitler-coup of 1923, the political uneasiness in eastern Europe, the approaching world depression-all this was explained largely as "post-war maladjustment". The growth of the Soviet empire, communist propaganda in Europe and Asia, the political and commercial expansion of Japan, the unrest in the Far East, the tension in India and in the Hither Eastall this was registered as "interesting" by public opinion in most Western countries. It was found, perhaps, to be "deplorable" or "disturbing"; but the superficial myth of a peaceful and progressing world had penetrated so deeply into the mentality of the newspaper reader-and unfortunately not only the newspaper reader-that he lost all sense for realities and proportions. "Collective security" 101

was believed to govern international affairs at a time when the conflicts between the large nations were rapidly moving towards new and vehement clashes. "World cooperation" was the label for a world order in which competition between the national economies became more and more devastating. "Equality of large and small nations" was the slogan of an epoch in which the large nations worked feverishly to make the small countries their satellites. "Equality of races" was proclaimed as the ideal of an age in which race conflicts were to become deeper and more violent than they had ever been in modern history.

III 

At the threshold of the twentieth century, the national empires of Europe were facing each other in ever more compact organizations. But their spiritual and structural unity began, nonetheless, to show marked signs of disintegration. Disrupting forces, such as those at work in the international life of Europe, operated also within the single nations. The Western peoples were faced with a paramount question: Have the political and economic ideas that dominated the nineteenth century outlived themselves? Are the political and economic systems that were created on the basis of these ideas incapable of mastering the changed social realities? The European nations have given varying answers to this question during the two post-war decades. 

The rise to power of fascism in some countries and the growing influence of socialistic and communistic elements in others have but added to the international tension. 

The nineteenth century was an age of private capitalism and bourgeois liberalism. It was an epoch of industrialization and urbanization and of an unprecedented growth in population. When the nineteenth century began, the entrepreneur. the manufacturer, merchant and banker, had just liberated himself from the mercantilist restrictions of the absolute state. Though a broad and vigorous bourgeois class was on the rise, making its living in the towns from industry and trade, agriculture was still the firm basis of the national economies. Only in a few big cities in England and northwestern Europe did crowds of industrial workers begin to dwell in miserable quarters. When the nineteenth century drew to its close, large armies of an urban proletariat had grown up. Hundreds of thousands, finally millions of unemployed became a regular feature of economic life in the 102

great Western nations. The position of the rural population was shaken by the overwhelming weight of the industrial cities, by the introduction of industrial methods into farming and by strong oversea competition. The key industries, insurance and banking, were controlled by a few powerful combines. A numerous middle class, though far from homogeneous in itself, carried on a bitter two-sided struggle against the organized proletariat and the moneyed interests. 

With growing competition from non-European countries which were favoured through lower wages or higher rationalization, and with every depression, the conflicts between the economic classes became sharper. The system of economic freedom was less and less capable of providing a decent livelihood for all citizens, especially in those nations that were deprived by the peace treaties of a chance to expand. 

Under the influence of education and propaganda, on the other hand, the demands of the masses for a higher standard of living grew constantly more articulate. In several European countries it soon became apparent that private capitalism in its existing form was doomed. To prevent the collapse of the tottering economic system, the state started out, at first with hesitation, then more and more boldly, to become the regulator and master of economic life. In the later years of the German republic, for instance, economic developments compelled the government to assume control over practically all phases of the national economy. When labor unions and employers could not agree on hours and wages, the government at first mediated and finally dictated a labor peace in order to prevent a complete disruption of the national economic life.

Social conflicts were fought out on the political stage. 

A relentless struggle was being waged by the interest groups for a controlling share in the government. The more uncompromising the attitude of the interest groups became, the more difficult it was for the democratic governments to formulate and to enforce policies. To maintain a liberaldemocratic system of government, a uniform basis of will is needed. The growing economic tension in most European nations contributed much towards destroying it. Governments in Continental countries found it increasingly difficult to secure a firm majority behind a program that seemed economically feasible. The minorities in opposition ceased to obey the orders of unstable and fragile majorities. Governments founded on working-class majorities usually met with the irreconcilable hostility of the social minority that held control over the means of production and over the organs of propaganda, and that alone had expert knowledge in public affairs. In a number of countries the propertied interests finally sought refuge among anti-democratic groups. In short, at a time when circumstances made it inevitable that state 103

activities expand and that the state become the master over economic life, the processes of democracy became more and more unworkable in most European countries. The authority of the state vanished, and the body politic threatened to break apart under the pressure of divergent interests. The shadow of Soviet Russia began to loom as a dark menace to the conservative elements which considered themselves the guardians of European traditions and spiritual values. 

The task of reconstructing the state as an entity above the disintegrating pressure groups became a problem of supreme importance in all European nations. Few countries succeeded in solving it through liberal-democratic processes. 

The majority of European nations abandoned the principles of liberal-democratic government. Fundamental rights were curtailed. The control of the state was extended into all spheres of life. Parliamentary laws were replaced by executive decrees. Judicial review became meaningless; selfgovernment was abolished. The chief executive, as the bearer of sovereignty and symbol of national unity, and, under his command, the expert bureaucracy became the most important and finally the only important organs of government. During the years of transition from liberal-democratic to authoritarian-totalitarian government, the democratically-elected leaders were confronted with this alternative: either to carry into effect a concerted policy through undemocratic means or to let the affairs of the state drift into chaos and to deliver their nations to the growing anti-democratic forces. 

Wavering between these alternatives, most of Europe's democracies were eventually replaced by the fascist form of government.

Simultaneously with social and political disintegration a deep-reaching spiritual confusion spread through the peoples of the West. Neither development was the cause of the other; but both were intimately intertwined. The transformation of nineteenth-century Europe into twentieth-century Europe constitutes one process that embraces all spheres of life. Up to the World War, life to most Europeans seemed embedded in a comfortable plaster cast of secure and firm beliefs, based on rational convictions. The era of enlightenment had freed the human mind from the ties that had incorporated the individual in a God-guided society and human society in a God-willed cosmic order. The autonomous individual, guided by reason, was now considered the supreme achievement of creation. Religion was replaced by an unlimited faith in human reason and world-progress. 

The individualistic and rationalistic philosophy produced an atmosphere of comfort and security, but also a growing inner loneliness and emptiness in man. There was no longer anything absolute. Everything was open to question. A nagging scepticism grew behind the facade of security and 104

complacency and under the frail crust of a shallow materialism. When the sun was setting in a bloody red over the nineteenth century, a deep dissatisfaction with the reigning spirit and the established social order was taking root among the European nations. Few were the preachers who had predicted the dawn of a new age; and they remained unheard except for small groups of youth who rallied in a romantic spirit around the vision of an heroic age to come. The great political parties, which were the crucibles for the formation of the public will, were unable to absorb or to guide the new spirit. Whether conservative or progressive, reactionary, or "revolutionary", the old parties were children of the rationalist epoch, just as were the organized interest groups with which they were linked. They had no room and no understanding for the spiritual revolution that was preparing itself. They soon were to break to pieces under the onslaught of events.

The World War released the nascent forces. By inciting all mental and physical energies of the warring nations to a supreme effort, it awakened the urge for heroic and selfsacrificing action that slumbered in the hearts of youth. It killed in millions the belief in reason and progress. When the first totalitarian war ended, it was clear that the spiritual face of Europe could never again be the same. In all nations there sprung up numerous small groups, fanatically devoted to a new "mythos" that was rising out of the spiritual confusion. Recruited at first largely from dissatisfied warveterans, embittered members of the middle-class and idealistic youth, the small groups of fanatic fighters grew into powerful movements. There was no definite philosophy or program at first. But there was passion. There was no theory. But there was consuming thirst for action. Love, devotion, hatred again took their place in political life. The "mythos" for which the young movements lived was born on the battlefields of the great war. It rose out of the dim memories of a strong and glorious past that had been preserved during the nineteenth century through the traditions of romantic nationalism. The vision of one great nation, united behind one leader, like an army marching toward the goal of national greatness and the fulfillment of its destiny, was to be the content of the new religion. The "political soldier", who carries out the commands of his leader, was proclaimed as the citizen of the new age. The state was conceived as the all-embracing organism from which the individual derives his strength and the justification for his existence.

Italy was the first nation in Europe to be governed by a fascist movement. With the march on Rome of Mussolini's 

Blackshirts in 1922 a new era in Italian history began. "Nothing outside the state; nothing against the state; every- 105

thing for the state" (Mussolini) became the motto of the new régime. The all-embracing hierarchies of the totalitarian state are joined together in the culminating figure of the "Leader of fascism and Head of the government". As a member of the fascist youth associations, or of any of the semi-military, cultural or occupational institutions of the régime, every Italian citizen is an active part of the totalitarian organization of the nation. The political élite, comprised in the strictly centralized "order" of the Fascist Party, gives the pattern to national life. A vast hierarchy of syndicates and federations under the direction of the government regulates the economic affairs of the nation. Political opposition to the national high command, under the fascist vision of the "army-state", can be nothing but treason; strikes and lock-outs are national disobedience. "Discipline from above" has taken the place of "responsibility to those below".

The pattern of the "stato-armata", provided by fascist Italy, has since been adopted by many European nations. 

Not so much the desire to imitate Italy but the similarities in their spiritual and economic situations forced a number of European states to follow the lead of the Italians. Poland, Hungary, Austria, Yugoslavia, Rumania and several other nations founded their governments on principles similar to that of Italy's totalitarian corporative régime. The second of Europe's large nations to establish a leader-state was Germany. After Hitler's coming into power all physical and spiritual energies of the German nation were "coordinated” (gleichgeschaltet) under the central command of the "Fuehrer". "All Germans are National Socialists; the best National

Socialists are members of the party" (Hitler). In spite of certain differences between the fascist movements of different countries, the similarities in the basic social convictions, political forms and human ideals proved so strong as to create a spiritual affinity that, in turn, tended to facilitate political cooperation between the totalitarian states. 

IV 

Among the many uncertainties that becloud the European horizon, one looms darker than all the rest: the danger of another general European war. To many it appears as if the armistice of 1919 were ended and the national empires, which had hurled their soldiers against each other for four years, were about to resume the battle over issues left unsettled 106

twenty years ago because of general exhaustion. Europe seems in danger of falling apart into two blocks of nations, the one trying to maintain the status established at Versailles, the other endeavouring to bring about a change. England and France are the "saturated" nations of western Europe.

The settlements of 1919 left them as "have's" as far as territory, raw materials, markets and opportunities for growth and construction are concerned. The geography, the political and economic conditions of their empires, and the state of mind of their nations have made them defendants of the political status quo. Germany and Italy, on the other hand, are the two major dissatisfied powers in Europe. They are the "have-not's" among the European nations. Their unalterable course is expansion. It is prescribed to them by their geography, their national history, their economic and financial conditions and the very structure of their totalitarian states. Both nations are dominated by the same "dynamic" concept of history. Their avowed aim is empirebuilding. The Roman and the Germanic "Caesar-Imperator" are the ideals of their leading movements. The increase of national power that followed the rise of leader-states in Italy and Germany and the dynamic foreign policy adopted by the totalitarian states are among the major factors that work for a transformation of post-war Europe and thus accentuate the contrasts between the two camps of the "have's" and the "have-not's". When, after the proclamation of the Third 

Reich in 1933, Germany regained her position among Europe's strongest and most determined military powers, it was obvious that the artificial and static equilibrium established at Versailles could no longer be maintained. A fundamental reorientation of European and world politics-or warbecame inevitable.

In 1933 the victors of the World War still had the choice either of crushing National Socialist Germany before her military strength would become too formidable, or of coming to an understanding with her through compensations. The first opportunity, that of a "preventive war", was not seized because the former Allies were not united and neither of them, single handed. was ready for such drastic action. There was, furthermore, the acute danger of Japan seizing such an opportunity to bring about far-reaching changes in East 

Asia. Soon the chance of crushing Germany without a terrific war had vanished. The second course, that of reconciliation with Germany on the basis of a four-power pact, was not followed because none of the powers was willing, then, to bring the necessary sacrifices for a lasting arrangement with the Germans. Instead, French diplomacy, in accordance with its tradition, worked tenaciously to strengthen the "wall of steel" around the growing Germany. In western Europe, France tried to organize herself together with Great Britain 107

and Italy in the Stresa-front, which shortly collapsed, however, under the weight of Italy's Ethiopian campaign. In the east, the French attempted to cement their alliances with the smaller eastern European nations. And when the diplomats of the Third Reich, in a bold stroke, concluded a ten-year non-aggression and friendship pact with Poland, France countered by making a common defense pact with the Soviet Union.

A situation has thus developed that resembles in many respects the situation prior to 1914. As in 1914, two groups of nations face each other inimically, threatening to turn into warring blocks whenever the chances for shortlived compromises are exhausted and feverish rearmament activities have reached a peak. As in 1914 there live in the middle of 

Europe Clemenceau's "twenty (grown now to more than thirty) million Germans too many" as one of the fundamental problems of European balance. There exists, as in 

1914, the tendency towards political and economic hegemony of Prussia-Germany over central Europe. There is the same fear of pan-Germanism on the part of the other European nations lest German domination of central Europe lead to German hegemony over eastern Europe and eventually, if unchecked, to German supremacy on the entire Continent. 

Today, as before the World War, Germany and Russia face each other in bitter hostility, carrying on the century-old struggle between Slavdom and Germandom in eastern Europe. 

The post-war Franco-Soviet treaty was motivated by the same purpose as the pre-war alliance between the Czar and 

France: Teuton growth should meet the pincers from east and west. As in 1914 the great powers rival for influence among the smaller nations in eastern Europe and the Balkans. As in pre-war days, finally, Britain. linked in friendship with France, is active to prevent the thread from breaking between the two antagonistic groups of nations.

But in spite of many outward similarities between the diplomatic constellations of 1914 and of 1939, essential changes have occurred during the past quarter of a century. 

The European players of 1939 are no longer the players of 1914. The world around Europe of today is no longer the world of twenty-five years ago. It is expedient to outline some of these differences, because they tend to alter fundamentally the dynamics of European politics and they render the spectre of a general European war more ominous. 

One of the most noteworthy of the differences between 1914 and 1939 is the strength and position of Italy. Since the days of the fascist revolution, Mussolini's aim has been to restore Rome to her ancient glory. Under the slogan Mare 

Nostrum-"Our Sea"-Italy set to work to become the leading sea and land power in the Mediterranean region. Italy's growth began in the shadow of British friendship, a welcome 108

counterbalance for British policy against French hegemonial ambitions of the early post-war years. When Europe, later on, learned to her surprise that Mussolini's intent to establish a new Roman empire was not an empty boast, when Italy began to strengthen her position in Libya, when her diplomacy became active successfully active-in central Europe as well as in Arabia, when the Duce finally gave the command to conquer Ethiopia in defiance of fifty-one nations gathered in Geneva under British-French leadership, when, shortly after the Ethiopian conquest, Italy managed to get a foothold in Spain-then it was that Europe gradually realized that a new factor had appeared in world politics. 

With a strong military power in Sicily, on the island of Pantelleria and in Libya new problems have arisen for England's empire route through the Mediterranean and especially for the defense of Malta. With Italy firmly established on the 

Red Sea and near the Egyptian Sudan British interests in

Egypt and the Suez Canal require additional protection. 

Italian agitation among the Arabs has created new concern for Britain's position in the Middle East. Italy's influence in Spain exposes Gibraltar to new dangers. Just as important as for Great Britain is Italy's rise for France. Italian expansion in North Africa has increased French worries about Tunisia, Algiers, and Morocco. Mussolini's announcement that Italy's future lies in Africa has been interpreted in Paris as an open threat. Italian control over Spain and the islands of Majorca and Minorca endangers the life-line of the French empire. The transportation of colored troops from Africa to France in case of war in Europe, and the shipment of munitions from France to Morocco in case of war in North Africa may be blocked effectively by Italian submarines. The French Pyrenees and Alpine borders have acquired a new strategical importance for the defense of France. One million Italians living on French soil are a problem of new significance for France.

When Fascist Italy and National Socialist Germany, through the formation of the Rome-Berlin axis, formed a solid block of 120 million people-two nations in arms, stretching from the Baltic to North Africa-a momentous decision was reached in European politics. The Rome-Berlin axis renders a blockade against the two partners practically ineffective. It is a powerful diplomatic and military weapon to separate eastern Europe and western Europe. Several years ago, a clash between the two expanding empires on the Brenner Pass or in the Danubian basin still seemed probable. Europe appeared too small to house both a Roman and a Germanic empire. The National Socialist uprising in 

Austria, in which Chancellor Dollfuss was killed, brought an Italo-German conflict dangerously near. But the two fascist nations have succeeded in ironing out their differences 

109

and in welding their strength into one weapon. Their cooperation has already borne fruit. Italy supported German rearmament and the remilitarization of the Rhineland, and Germany aided Italy when the League of Nations tried to organize an anti-Italian boycott during the Ethiopian campaign. The two countries cooperated during the Czech crisis; they collaborate in Spain. Both leaders have shown great willingness to bring sacrifices in the interest of their friendship. Mussolini conceded Austria to Hitler and Hitler solemnly acknowledged the Brenner-frontier, hereby renouncing 

Southern Tyrol and Trieste. The fact that the two leaders of the Rome-Berlin axis have reached an agreement on the points that separated them in the past is an indication that they see problems ahead far greater and far more vital to their nations than the former issues of dissension. 

The second far-reaching difference between the situations of 1939 and 1914 lies in the inner transformation of Russia. 

Soviet Russia is one of the enigmas of world politics. When the French concluded their alliance with the Soviets, they seem to have been convinced that the military assistance of the U.S.S.R. would outweigh the disadvantages that the 

Soviet pact would bring France in her relations with her western and central European friends. The danger, on the other hand, that an industrialized Russia may someday overrun eastern Europe and, propelled forward by communist and pan-Slavic propaganda, establish herself as the dominating power in central Europe, appears not to have impressed the French statesmen who concluded the pact. The Soviet 

Union, geographically, spiritually and politically, is an Asiatic as well as a European power. Through the industrialization of her interior regions she has strengthened her Asiatic parts. Under the hammer and sickle the same process is taking place in northern Asia that is taking place everywhere in Asia. Russia's industrialization and the emancipation from feudal chains that began under the czars has been accelerated since the Bolshevist revolution. Lenin and 

Stalin have thrown wide open the gates through which 

Western ideas and tools have flooded the northern Asiatic plains. The presence in Europe of the giant Eurasian empire that is neither Eastern nor Western but a mystery of its own, that covers one-fifth of the land surface of the earth, that is immensely rich in raw materials and can boast a rapidly rising birthrate—the political presence of this colossus has thrown an ominous shadow of uncertainty over all of Europe. 

The French leaders were not long deceived: military cooperation between the Western democracies and Communist Russia is full of grave import for the social order of Europe. The Red Army, in case of a general European conflict, may carry the cry of the world revolution as the 110

armies of the French revolution once carried the cry égalité, liberté, fraternité all over the Continent. In a defeated fascist country as well as in an allied democracy it may fall on willing ears. Europe, exhausted by another long-drawn-out war, may yield to the tempting message from Moscow, or it may be plunged into chaotic civil war. 

By far more profound in their effects than any of the changes within Europe are the changes that have taken place outside of Europe since the World War. The Japanese empire in the making, the rising tide of Indian nationalism and the political storms brewing in the Hither East have a deep and ominous meaning for the destiny of Europe. The 

European nations are no longer alone in making European history. A general European war would not long be confined to Europe. The nations of Asia are ready to take advantage of any situation that the European nations offer. 

The cooperation between the fascist powers and the Empire of the Rising Sun is the first tangible result of the new world situation. Since Germany and Japan concluded the anti-comintern pact, to which Italy adhered later on, the Far Eastern problem is linked intimately together with the European problem. The Tokyo-Berlin-Rome axis. though avowedly designed only for the purpose of combating communism, is a weapon of great potential power in world politics. It is directed against the Soviets and against him who chooses them as his political friends. Hitler has proclaimed that "National Socialist Germany entertains no doubts but that even the greatest Japanese victory will not in the least touch the cultures of the white races, just as there is no doubt that the victory of Bolshevism would mean the end of the thousand-year-old culture of the West". The diplomatic cooperation between Germany, Italy and Japanwhether it is cemented together through secret military pacts or not appears unessential-has produced already important political results. Without German rearmament Italy's conquest of Ethiopia and Japan's conquest of China might not have been possible. Without Italy's benevolent neutrality and without Japan's keeping Russian, French and British attention engaged in the Far East, Germany might not have been able to carry out the Anschluss of Austria, the incorporation of the Sudeten region and the penetration of central Europe. There is an intimate political connection between the burning roofs of Madrid and the burning roofs of Shanghai. In case of an open conflict between the two groups of 

European powers the fires may spread not only over the whole of Europe but also over all of Asia. In such a war 

England may have to fight Japan in India, Malaya and possibly in Australia. The natives of India, Burma and 

Arabia may rise to throw off the European yoke. It is highly improbable that the United States of America would, over 111

a long period, stay out of a conflict in which Japan and England are opponents, especially if the fortune of war should not smile on British arms. The Latin American countries, too, might be drawn into the conflict, lining up possibly on both sides and carrying the war into the western hemisphere.

No further proof is needed; the situation of 1939 is no longer the situation of 1914-in spite of many outward similarities. If the European powers permit the present political situation on the Continent to deteriorate into a general European conflict, the second world war will be by far more terrible and in its consequences incomparably more disastrous for Europe than the first. Leading British statesmen have warned that it would neither be possible, nor necessary to raise the question as to who is the victor and who the vanquished. The physical destruction and mental confusion brought over Europe may be irreparable. The fear of it alone throws dark shadows into the minds of Europeans. The smallness of the continent, the density of the population, the multitude of nationalities, the existence of wide-spread metropolitan areas and of well-knit industrial systems-factors that have worked to the advantage of European civilization in the past-will be a curse on Europe in the age of totalitarian warfare, of poison gas and motorized artillery. Another world war, in combination with the steadily-declining birthrates in European countries, will lead Europe to the brink of ruin. After such a war, the nations of the West may be consumed by civil strife and revolutions, and peoples from the East will be at hand to take their place.

Can the catastrophe of a general European war be avoided? Yes, it can and must be avoided. But how, in view of the apparently unalterable determination of Germany and Italy to expand? There is, in the final anaylsis, only one way to preserve the peace of western Europe and to prevent a world war, and that is for Great Britain and France to permit Germany and Italy to develop their energies in a direction where they will not conflict with British and French empire interests. On the possibility of diverting and canalizing German and Italian expansion into regions where Great Britain and France are not compelled to defend vital holdings depends the peace among the Western nations. 

There are few areas in the world into which Germany 112

and Italy can expand without conflicting with well-established interests of the Western powers. The Americas which for centuries have absorbed the overflowing energies of Europe are practically closed. The opportunities for commercial expansion into Latin America are limited, political expansion is not possible without a conflict with the United States. 

Expansion into East Asia and the Pacific region-for instance, through Germany's claiming her pre-war colonies in China and in the South Seas-is not attractive enough to warrant a conflict with Japan. In southern Asia and 

Austral-Asia Great Britain will not permit a new nation to set foot. The African continent has already been divided up between the Western powers. The last free African country became a European colony in 1936. Although Germany has not ceased to demand a restitution of her African colonies, the discussion between the powers over a redistribution of colonies has never entered the practical stage. A redistribution of African possessions, especially in the Mediterranean area may become important. But by itself, it hardly will constitute a solid basis for European peace. In the Hither 

East, too, the most important positions are occupied by England and France who do not seem willing to cede them to others. In western Europe and in Scandinavia the political status quo cannot be changed without dangerously affecting the vital interests of France and Great Britain.

The only regions in the world that are not occupied or ear-marked for future occupation by one of the great western democracies or by Japan are certain areas of eastern 

Europe and of northern and western Asia, especially around the Black and Caspian Seas. Expansion toward the East is the only course open for the two fascist nations through which they might hope to satisfy their pressing needs without involving the western democracies and thus the entire world in war. If Germany and Italy should choose to expand in this direction, their major opponent will be the Soviet Union. A thousand years of German history lie back of the "Drang nach dem Osten". Many centuries have been filled with the bitter and tenacious struggle between the 

Slavic peoples pressing slowly westward into central Europe and the Germanic nations desperately trying to stem the tide and turn it back toward Asia. Seven centuries ago the banners of the Germanic knights waved far to the east and the crests of the Hansa merchants were seen on the towers of many cities as far east as Novgorod. Today Slavic characters are engraved and Slavic languages are spoken almost as far to the west as Trieste, Vienna and Berlin.

The traveller from East to West who reads Slavic inscriptions for the first time on the shores of the Pacific Ocean sees the last Slavic characters only a few hours before he reaches the German capital. 113

Hitler has pointed out in Mein Kampf that there are two roads for the Germans to build an empire. The one is that of oversea expansion. To proceed along this course, Germany needs a large fleet and England becomes her main rival and enemy. An important prerequisite for a successful 

German policy of oversea expansion is the benevolent neutrality of Russia, Germany's great eastern neighbour on land. 

Under the present circumstances, however, a policy of forceful oversea expansion would lead Germanv inevitably into a world war. The other course open to the Germans, according to Hitler, is that of expansion into the East. If she starts eastward. Germany will eventually, and inevitably, come into conflict with Russia, the leading Slavic power. In this case she needs, first of all, a strong army. A navy she needs merely to protect her coasts and to control the Baltic Sea from where she has to operate through Finland into northern Russia. The "Drang nach dem Osten", however, can be satisfied only if Germany succeeds in securing England's benevolent neutrality. In Hitler's opinion it was the fatal mistake of German pre-war policy to pursue both courses at the same time and to antagonize both England and Russia. 

If Hitler's analysis holds true, the most important prerequisite for a drive eastward of the fascist powers is the diplomatic preparation of the ground in western Europe. To separate western Europe politically from eastern Europe appears, then, to be one of the main tasks of the Rome- Berlin axis. Both Germany and Italy have gone out of their way to come to terms with Great Britain and France. In order to win English goodwill, Germany has concluded a naval treaty limiting her own sea forces to one-third the strength of the British forces. She has, after the Munich consultations, signed a pact with France declaring France's eastern frontier permanent. There are indications that, for the time being, she is willing not to press her colonial claims against France and England in exchange for a "free hand" in eastern Europe. Italy, on her part, has signed an agreement with Great Britain regulating their mutual relations in the Mediterranean and in East Africa and demarcating their zones of influence in the Hither East. It, furthermore, appears as if Franco-Italian tension that has arisen over the distribution of territories in the western Mediterranean, especially Tunisia, might find an amicable settlement. 

But regardless of all their efforts to win the support or, at least, the benevolent neutrality of the western democracies for an eastward expansion, the two fascist nations must be prepared for a possible two-front war. The objectives of their diplomats and soldiers are simple; geography has prescribed them. The task of the diplomat is to enable the soldier to fight a war on only one front; the task of the soldier is to be prepared for a war on two fronts. Only in September 114

did the diplomacy of the two fascist nations succeed in removing the major political obstacles in the way of a onefront eastern drive, the Franco-Soviet and the Franco-Czech pact. But not even after Munich is the atmosphere entirely cleared. Italian and German attempts to gain influence in 

Spain, along the French Pyrenees border, have, in that connection, a certain strategical significance since they compel France to defend herself on three fronts, should she choose to fight for Soviet Russia.

Equal in importance to the diplomatic preparation in western Europe is the preparation of the ground in eastern Europe for a final successful crusade of the fascist nations against the Soviets. Many smaller Slavic peoples, Poles, Latvians, Czechs, Slovaks, Ruthenes, Serbs, Croats, Slovenes,

Ukrainians, Bulgars and several others, as well as non-Slavic nations, Magyars, Rumanians and Finns, live between their great neighbors: Germany and Russia. To bring the small nations politically, strategically and economically into their orbit is a principal endeavour of the two great rivaling powers. It is one of the historical ironies of the peace treaties of 1919 that the dismemberment of the Habsburg monarchy and the establishment of independent Slavic states, largely intended to block Germanic aspirations towards the East, are working to the advantage of Germany. With the exception of the Czechs and Lithuanians, all Slavic nationalities of eastern Europe, though racially related to the Russians, had become more and more afraid of their big eastern neighbor for historical and ideological reasons, and even these two nations have, since Munich, come under German influence. Though anxious to maintain their independence and to pursue their own policy, most eastern European nationalities have, since the decline of French hegemony, come increasingly under the German-Italian aegis. Strong economic pressure brought to bear on the smaller countries by Germany, their most important customer and debtor, has done much to bring the project of a German-guided unit of central and eastern European peoples (Mittel-Europa) near realization. With Finland, Poland, Hungary, Bulgaria and Yugoslavia and now also Czechoslovakia-inclining toward the Rome-Berlin axis and Austria being a part of the German Reich, much has been done to replace the post-war order under which France ruled eastern Europe through the instrumentality of the now practically obsolete Little Entente. The only important obstacle that stood in the way of a Germancontrolled Mittel-Europa, stretching from the Rhine to the Russian border and to the mouth of the Danube, was the 

Czechoslovakia of Eduard Benes, adamant in her pro-Russian and pro-French policies. Like a spear-head she lay across eastern Europe, reaching into the very heart of Germany, in a key position geographically, because whoever has strate- 115

gical control over the Moldau basin has control over all of central Europe.

Even if the Berlin-Rome axis should succeed in "neutralizing" western Europe and in grouping around them the smaller eastern European nations, the difficulties and risks inherent in an eastward drive are very great. European history is full of ominous lessons. The Germanic knights pushed their spear heads towards the East-and were repulsed. The Swedes, advancing over the Finnish bridge, attempted repeatedly to gain a foothold in European Russia -and failed. Napoleon attacked the Russian empire; but the Grande Armée retreated in chaos from the ruins of sacred Moscow. In the Crimean War France and England attempted to press upon Russia from north and south; but they did not achieve their goal. In the World War Russia lost several of her western provinces. But her tremendous body remained intact. Neither the losses suffered in the 

Japanese war, nor the collapse of 1917, followed by several years of civil war and famine, neither Germany's attempt to subdue her at Brest-Litovsk, nor the endeavours of the allied powers to split several large European and Asiatic provinces away from the Soviets, caused the Russian empire to disintegrate. The Soviets were able to hold the vital points that the czars had conquered for Russia: her window to the west, St. Petersburg, most of her Black Sea provinces, her Asiatic possessions around the Caspian Sea and the only ice-free port on the Pacific, Vladivostok. The rapidly-advancing industrialization, especially in the interior provinces, the completion of the Trans-siberian railroad entirely through Russian territory and the progress in the development of a polar route from the Atlantic to the Pacific, add to the strength of the Soviet Empire. 

Two factors at the present time, however, make a war against the Soviets appear more promising for the nations of central Europe. One is the prospect of Japanese cooperation. A crusade against Moscow would crown the cooperative efforts of the Tokyo-Berlin-Rome axis. If an eastern European war against the Soviets should develop through the Baltic and Finland, through Poland and Rumania, across the Black Sea and possibly through Turkey and Iran, Soviet

Russia must be prepared also to fight Japan along her Manchurian and Mongolian borders. A second encouraging factor for the foes of Soviet Russia is the spirit of restlessness noticeable in several European and Asiatic Soviet republics. European nations, coming as "liberators" to the millions of Volga-Germans, Ukrainians, Armenians and Turkmans, may count on the cooperation of the anti-communist sections of the populations in these parts of the 

Soviet Union.

The opportunities for European nations to develop their 116

energies eastward from northern, central and southeastern Europe are colossal. The Black Sea basin contains everything in mineral and agricultural wealth that the fascist nations lack. The additional markets for which European industries are searching in vain can be found in abundance in White Russia, in the Ukraine, in Ukrelia and beyond the Caspian shores, once political control over these regions is established by European powers.

Can and will the western democracies permit the fascist nations to move eastward? The destiny of Europe and of 

Western civilization may depend upon the answer to this question. In both England and France opinion is very much divided on this most vital point. For several years British diplomacy attempted to dodge the issue behind the smoke screen of collective security and League principles. Only with Mr. Eden's departure from the Foreign Office in the spring of 1938 did Great Britain free herself from the Geneva vocabulary to return to a frankly realistic policy. 

Though the pro-League course represented by Anthony Eden was successful inasmuch as it gained popular support for Imperial policy inside and outside the Empire, especially in the United States, it threatened to entangle British diplomacy in the automatism of the League machinery that has already once led Britain to the edge of war. But even though the principles of collective security and indivisible peace have, at least temporarily, been thrown overboard by the Chamberlain-Halifax government, British policy seems by no means committed to any one definite course. It remains non-committal, in the spring of 1939, in spite of increasing evidence, since Munich, of an anti-Russian or, more precisely, neutral course as the least of many evils.

The major objection raised in France and Great Britain against a freehand policy for Germany and Italy in the East is that it would upset the European balance and give the two fascist nations hegemony on the Continent. It has been maintained that the World War would have been fought in vain by the Allies, should Germany be permitted to carry her flag to the shores of the Black Sea and perhaps further. 

It has been pointed out that control over Rumanian oil and grain would make Germany invincible. French statesmen, in particular, claim that to sacrifice both her Russian and Czechoslovak allies to the Teuton "lust for conquest" would render the position of France as a first-rate power untenable. They point out that after a successful drive toward the east the fascist powers may well turn west again, Italy to satisfy her Mediterranean ambitions in Tunisia and Morocco, and Germany to reannex Alsace-Lorraine.

Besides these considerations of Realpolitik, ethical, ideological and sentimental reflections have begun to play a certain part in determining the attitude of France and 117

England. Sections of public opinion in both countries claim that it would be unethical to sacrifice the eastern nations to the fascist powers. They condemn Neville Chamberlain's policy of conciliation with the fascist nations as "unrighteous." Thev urge a united anti-fascist front of the democracies (including Soviet Russia) just as the fascist nations have formed an anti-communist front. Many pacifists of yesterday have come to advocate a policy that would lead to an "ideological" war of the "democratic" and "peaceloving" nations against the "aggressors" and "dictators".

The idea of a concerted Western front against Soviet Russia, in which the two fascist nations do the fighting, has, on the other hand, also many powerful advocates in the western democracies, especially in England. Projects to dismember the Soviet Union and to open up her Black Sea and Central Asian possessions to European colonization are as old as the Soviet Empire itself. The following reasons are being advanced with increasing success by the defendants of an anti-Russian front. A united German-Italian-Japanese crusade against the Soviet Union undertaken with the tacit consent of the western powers would relieve western Europe of German and Italian pressure, and the European possessions in Asia of Japanese pressure for at least one or two decades. It would relieve the whole world of the danger of communism. It would relieve northern India, Afghanistan, Iran and other European influence zones in Asia of Russian pressure. A successful war against the Soviets would prevent 

Russia from continuing her process of industrialization and from becoming one of the strongest and most integrated world powers. It would remove the dangers of pan-Slavism from eastern Europe. A defeat of Soviet Russia would make it possible to adjust all eastern European border and minority difficulties at her expense and to settle men from western Europe's overpopulated areas as in the days of Catherine and Peter-on fertile eastern European land. A colonization of Russia, finally, would create luring chances for entrepreneurs and investors from all Western nations. The souzerain states to be created around the Black Sea, in 

Central Asia and in Siberia, though rich in mineral and agricultural wealth, would be in dire need of foreign capital which, however, neither Germany, nor Italy, nor Japan can supply. It has been suggested that satisfactory commercial guarantees concerning the opened-up territories should be made one of the conditions for British neutrality in case of an anti-Soviet war. It is noteworthy, in this connection, that the major Russian oil resources, for which the Shell interests have waged a long and tenacious struggle, lie in the path of a possible advance from the West. 

Concerning the danger of German-Italian hegemony on the Continent and the threats arising from it for Great 118

Britain and France, the proponents of a Western four-power arrangement with an anti-Russian tendency point out that this danger is not imminent in view of British and French military strength. They point to the probability that even after the victorious fascist powers have digested their acquisition, a resourceful British policy will always be able to align a sufficient number of European peoples against an over-ambitious Germany or Italy. A neutral Britain, after such a conflict, could dictate, as in 1905 at Portsmouth. a desirable peace to the exhausted belligerents. The advocates of such a course question whether German-Italian-Japanese cooperation could last beyond the days of an anti-Russian drive, and they emphasize the part that the United States of America has, by necessity, to play in the defense of western European and British Imperial integrity, since any further 

German and Japanese advances would menace her own security. The question must be raised in this connection, whether or not the traditional "European balance" has lost or, at least, changed its meaning in the twentieth century due to the rise of non-European powers such as the U.S.A. and Japan and due to the colossal possibilities inherent in the industrialization of Russia. 

With regard to the dangers, furthermore, that may arise for British and French interests in the Hither East from an increase in strength in the fascist nations, the proponents of the so-called "pro-fascist" course in England point out that a conflict can be avoided as long as Germany and Italy respect British and French interests by keeping their activities sufficiently far to the north. With Germany keeping north of an imaginary line drawn from the Brenner to the Caucasus, and Italy holding to the south of it, there is ample room for the two partners of the axis to build their modern Roman and Germanic empires without conflicting with wellestablished French and British rights. As counter arguments, finally, against the ideological objections to such a policy it is being maintained that the trend of world developments is inevitably toward large continental units and that the smaller nations of eastern Europe will have to group themselves into the framework of a united Europe guided by the four large powers of western Europe, if Europe is to survive. It is emphasized that some of the dictatorial restrictions of the fascist countries may gradually be relaxed, after the economic and spiritual tensions are eased by successful expansion. If the crusade against the U.S.S.R., the "living Antichrist" would get under way, it is argued, finally, that the Christian churches would have an opportunity not only to reestablish themselves in Russia, but also to recapture some of their lost positions in Europe, especially in Germany.

Whatever solution the governments of the two western 

European democracies choose for Europe-a general Euro- 

119

pean war leading to a second world war, or toleration and canalization of German-Italian expansion toward the East -there will always live in the heart of Europe, as one of its most burning problems, more than eighty million Germans. The existence of the German people, striving, driving and pulsating in the center of the old and war-torn Continent, may be uncomfortable to some of its neighbours, but it cannot be ignored. To ignore it, or to attempt to press this powerful dynamic reality into the narrow space between Rhine and Oder, is a dangerous course for European diplomacy to take. If it is mismanaged, the German problem can develop into a curse for the entire West. If its energies are directed and wisely guided around the dangerous cliffs of a maladjusted twentieth-century Europe by a European diplomacy worthy of its name, the German people will group itself into the concert of European powers. For the first time in modern history, the United States of Europe, based on an understanding between the four leading powers:

Great Britain, France, Germany, and Italy, and on satisfactory guarantees for the smaller European nations, may become a real possibility. 120

Chapter V 

And America?

Americans have come to look with dismay and disdain at the Machiavellian play of power politics that the old world presents. They see in the imperialist struggles between the European nations an out-dated form of barbarism. They consider fascism and national socialism a return to the Dark Ages. The spectacle of Europe preparing to devour itself through fratricidal war fills them with horror. In the Far 

East they angrily look on while Japan carves piece after piece out of China. Disillusioned about the way in which the world moves, most Americans cling to the hope that at least in their own country and in the western hemisphere they will succeed in building up a civilization according to their ideals. With the immense territory and natural wealth at her disposal, they hope that America will be able to solve her social and political problems in her own way, that is with peaceful means.

In approaching the practical problems of the day, however, and in trying to uphold their ideals in a world of political and economic realities, Americans find themselves in one of the most confused stages of their history. Concerning national politics, economic or international problems, there are everywhere the same uncertainties, the same disagreements, the same contradictions. There is a strong aversion to fascism or any form of regimentation. But there is also a widespread underlying feeling that more discipline in public life and stricter state regulation is inescapable if the problems of the present epoch are to be mastered. 

Americans have a strong attachment to the "American way”, the tradition according to which the newsboy works himself up to be a millionaire. But they have also a secret fear that this stage in American history and all that it represents is rapidly becoming a thing of the past. Americans have a genuine contempt for all the "isms" of the old world. But they cannot fail to observe similar movements and trends growing in the United States. Freedom of speech and assembly are among the sacred institutions of American political life. But Americans begin to ask themselves how these rights 121

can be upheld if radical movements and organizations that use these very rights to undermine the American system of government gain in influence. Concerning world affairs, the majority of the American people seem to feel that the United States and, in fact, the entire western hemisphere should withdraw from the self-destructive squabbles of Europe and Asia and live out its own existence in peace and security. But regardless of the genuine desire for peace on the part of the people, the conviction is growing that America is unable to isolate herself and to stay out of another world conflict, should it come. Many Americans, on the contrary, advocate that the United States should help to defeat the "evil" forces of aggression and dictatorship in the world by joining in on the "right" side in another war. 

Some even predict that the United States will be compelled, through the sheer weight of her physical power, to accept a position of leadership in world affairs whether she wants to or not. The dangers, however, that mobilization for a totalitarian war creates for the democratic institutions of their own country do not escape politically-minded Americans. Though the American mind, on the whole, considers the new forms of social and political life that have developed in Russia and Central Europe during the past two decades as steps backward or in the wrong direction, it sees in them a challenge for spiritual leadership and it is sincere enough to recognize that democracy, liberalism, and all social achievements in which America has led the Western world during the past century and a half, are on trial not only in Europe and Asia, but also in the western hemisphere itself. 

The uncertainties and contradictions, of which presentday American life is full, makes it impossible to give an exact picture of the spiritual and material forces that are molding America. But a brief look at American history and a comparison with European developments may enable the analyst to draw certain fundamental conclusions about the America of today, the opportunities and difficulties with which she is confronted and the part that she plays and is to play in world politics.

The history of America from the days of the early colonial settlers to the World War is one continuous pageant of powerful expansion. When Europe became too crowded, the most restless, the most ambitious, the most daring and the most downtrodden of European men started out to seek their fortunes in other lands. On the wide and fertile shores of 

America they raised the flags of their countries; they occupied land in the name of their kings; they built towns and fortresses and established many new commonwealths; and they pushed the fringe of Western civilization always further and further westward. The European invaders found a race of proud and forceful people in the new lands. With them 122

they traded or battled, as circumstances required. South of the Rio Grande, where the Indians were numerous and had a developed civilization, the Spanish conquerors put them to work as serfs and peons. In the north, where the natives were thinly-scattered and averse to acquiring the habits of the white man, the English colonists pushed them back into the interior of the continent. Where no other solution was at hand, the Indians were exterminated. In the same century in which the British conquered the interior of India and Burma and wide parts of Africa, the Americans conquered the North American continent. Step by step, the frontier was advanced until there was one single empire from the shores of the Atlantic to the Pacific. When the area of the United States was rounded out and the age of the frontier was drawing to its close, Americans began to make powerful thrusts beyond the borders of their country. Alaska was acquired. Strategical positions in the Pacific Ocean were occupied. From the South Seas to the Arctic a wide circle was drawn: Samoa, the Philippines, Guam, Hawaii, and the Aleutian Islands came under the stars and stripes. 

The frontier was being pushed from California on to the shores of East Asia.

At the same time the United States advanced southward into the Caribbean Sea and into Latin America. Though the English and Hispanic communities of the new world had remained culturally and economically closer to their home countries than they were to each other, even after they had broken away from Europe politically, there developed early among them a number of factors which worked towards welding the heterogeneous parts of the New World together into one family of nations: pan-Americanism. The pan- 

American movement had its origin in the days when, under Simon Bolivar, the Spanish colonies revolted against Napoleonic Spain. After the congress of Panama and the pronouncement of the Monroe Doctrine, however, little progress toward American unity was made throughout the nineteenth century. The dreams that were dreamt in Latin America about a unified American continent, opposed in its liberal idealism to the reactionary practices of the Old World, had to remain in the background behind the urgent realities of nation-building. The most important nation in the western hemisphere, the United States, was preoccupied at home and took little interest in the movement. Only towards the end of the nineteenth century did pan-Americanism receive a new impetus. After rounding out its territory and terminating the frontier, after building up a powerful industrial machine eager for raw materials and markets, and after reconstructing the nation following the Civil War, the United States became interested in Latin America. The romantic era of pan-Americanism under Latin American leadership ended. 123

The practical-commercial era under the leadership of the 

United States began.

The expansion of the United States coincided with a period of inner colonization and building. The one complemented the other, spiritually and economically. The historical forms under which America grew inwardly and expanded differ in many respects from those of European expansion. 

But the forceful impulse with which American expansion was carried forward was the same that moved the peoples of Europe. The life rhythm of America and of Europe is the life rhythm of the West. The nineteenth century was an epoch of frantic activity and of boundless optimism in Europe and America. The paramount problem confronting the young 

American states after their political separation from Europe was that of nation-building. The colonies were to become national states; colonials and immigrants were to become citizens of new nations. In Europe the national states were evolving gradually out of the dynastic states. In America the task of building up national states was more difficult and, at the same time, more simple than in Europe: there were no obstacles of dynastic or traditional character; there were no century-old hatreds and jealousies to break forth in minority conflicts and to harden the disputes of imperialistic rivalry. But there were, on the other hand, no national traditions making for spiritual unity. The peoples of America could not look back upon many centuries of colourful history and folklore. The men and women who came to the 

American shores had little in common except the desire for freedom-or the urge to make a better living than was possible at home.

Whereas the number of European immigrants into Latin America and into those parts of North America that remained under the British crown was comparatively small, the United States absorbed wave after wave of European "invaders". The most courageous and the strongest, those who needed fresh air to breathe, the most upright and those most loyal to their faiths, the weakest and the most wicked, the most cunning and the ones least capable of making a decent living in Europe these all came to America. Adventurers and shopkeepers, farmers and stranded poets. Irish,

Italians and Germans-by the hundreds of thousands they were dumped on the American shores, pilgrims for freedom and opportunity. There was hardly one too good or one too bad for the new country, which waited with open arms, luring and promising, hungry for men who knew how to handle a rifle, a plough or anything else. The epic of Ellis 

Island is a part of the epic of America. For a long time immigration could not satisfy the demand for cheap labour that existed in the new world. To fulfill this need, a commodity was imported from Africa: black men. Life in the 124

colonies and in the young states was hard and strong and unsentimental. The fittest survived. To become an American meant to become a builder, a pioneer. 

The human raw stuffs imported from all parts of Europe were mixed and amalgamated in the greatest human melting pot known to history. Native-American, African and Asian ingredients were added to the European substances. Several times the gigantic caldron threatened to explode. Once, three-quarters of a century ago, a dangerous cleavage broke open. It led to the first mass war in modern history. Many hundreds of thousands died. But the Union was preserved. 

Again and again the temperature rose to the boiling point. 

The entrance of the United States into the World War was one of the latest important steps in American nation-building. 

Though new materials were constantly thrown into the crucible, the process of melting went on uninterruptedly.

A new substance was to be compounded: the American. Is it surprising that America has thus become a country of the oddest contrasts and, at the same time, a country of the oddest uniformity? All psychological tensions originating in the national souls of the old world have been transplanted into the new world. The market life of Naples, the ideals of the Swabian peasant, the songs of the Swedish woods, the folklore of Ireland and Scotland, all have been thrown into the melting pot. The Anglo-Saxon elements of the north, and the Spanish-Portuguese civilization of Latin America furnished the major motifs for the American melody. In the new composition, "The American", all elements live on, often buried deeply under the surface, often unrecognizable except for distant longings that once in a while quicken in a remote corner of the American soul.

Is there a genuine American national culture? Many 

Americans have asked this question. An answer to so fundamental a question must necessarily be based upon assumptions. But once given, it contains also the answers to all important questions concerning the political and economic destiny of America. The national culture of the United 

States cannot be defined any more accurately than that of Italy or Germany or England. The sum-total of the institutions, views, traditions, forms, habits and artistic expressions that America has developed and continues to develop, is the American culture. It is the life-expression of the American soul. The New York skyscraper is as much a part of it as the cowboy song of the Southwest. That many elements in

American national culture can easily be traced back to European roots, that American national culture is as inevitably and inseparably a part of the culture of the West as any of the European national cultures, that American culture has its sectional variations-these are no arguments against the existence of an independent and integrated national culture. 125

Multiformity can, in itself, be a vital element in a national culture. What matters alone, is the will of a community to have a culture of its own, and its power to create and to uphold its own institutions and traditions. The cultural problem thus becomes a political problem in the widest sense. 

The creation of a national culture is the problem of the spiritual integration of a people into one body. 

The special position of American national culture is often beclouded by the fact that it is compared and contrasted with French or German national culture and with European culture as a whole. Though the American people may as a nation have developed certain characteristics that set them apart from all European nations, the value of any abstract cultural antithesis, Europe versus America, possibly along the lines of the popular antithesis of Roman and Greek culture, seems highly questionable. Western civilization is a great tree, of which all national civilizations of the West, French, German, American, are branches. Through the merging of the life substances of the West: Christianity, the Greek-Roman classics and Germanic tribal culture, the forms of growth were outlined for all Western nations. No 

Western nation can outgrow these forms, as great as the political distance between the "old" and the "new" world may be. Every Western nation is limited by the common inheritance of the Occident.

When America grew up, the West was about to enter into an advanced stage of its evolution. It was the age of rationalism, of industrial civilization and urban growth. 

Spring and summer, the Gothic and the Reformation, some of the great formative stages of Western culture were past. 

If Americans ask themselves why they did not produce a 

Shakespeare, a Rembrandt, a Beethoven, the answer may be that the time of the Rembrandts and Shakespeares had passed-irrevocably-in the lifespan of the West, when America grew to maturity. Americans, in the nineteenth century, their first centurv, produced a John D. Rockefeller, an Abraham Lincoln, a Henry Ford. In Europe it was the age of Bismarck, of Cecil Rhodes, of Krupp, no longer the age of Dante or Goethe. 

America could develop as she did during the past century and gather momentum only because she was permanently and rapidly expanding. When the pastures were used up, sheep and shepherd moved on to new ones. American life during the formative stages of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries was by no means without form and discipline, as it is often maintained. But, with the exception of certain areas in New England and Virginia, strongly embedded in English style-feeling, the forms and disciplines of early American life were imposed by hard realities, by urgent practical necessities, not by homogeneous traditions and an 126

inner unity of style and taste. The discipline of the pioneer and settler life was imposed neither by tradition nor by the state, but by individual ambitions, by the fear of perishing and by the hope of becoming rich. Government was reduced to a minimum. It was the classical age of "laissez-faire". 

The laissez-faire philosophy has experienced its most perfect application in nineteenth-century America. The opening up of the continent and the evolution of American industry are two of the most spectacular achievements of nineteenthcentury individualism. The features of dozens of American cities, on the other hand, built without feeling for form or style, without any attempt to plan and coordinate, without civic spirit in the deeper sense, constitute one of the sorriest outgrowths of the same individualism running wild. America was the land of the unlimited opportunities, the country of luring horizons. The American spirit was a spirit of unbounded optimism. The American faith was one of neverending progress and prosperity. 

II 

The World War marked an important historical turning point for America. Regardless of the short and unreal postwar decade of "bigger and better" things, of the "highest standard of living," and of "everlasting prosperity," during which Americanism turned into a grotesque cartoon of itself, the World War terminated the nineteenth century also for 

America. Not until the early thirties-decisive years for both the old and the new world-did the great distance that separates the present from the past century become evident. 

Most of the new problems confronting twentieth-century America are essentially the same as those confronting the other Western nations. They assume a special American profile, due to America's particular spiritual, political and economic conditions, and America is trying to find, and may succeed in finding, solutions different from those of European nations: American solutions. But it must be repeated: the

Western world is, in spite of the deep cleavages in its political and spiritual structure, too intimately knit together to permit one country to escape any of the fundamental problems common to the whole Occident.

The new age is determined for the United States, to a large extent, by one basic factor; the natural growth and expansion that characterized the entire nineteenth century up to the World War has, at least temporarily, come to a 127

standstill. The population ceases to increase; industries and markets cease to grow; room for expansion seems to be no longer available; "security" takes the place of "wealth" and "power" as highest aims in the minds of the people. The transition from a dynamic and expanding to a static society is more sharply pronounced in the United States than in European countries chiefly because of two factors: the closing of the frontier and the termination of immigration. 

Pioneering and immigration had given American expansion its particular élan. Now, that they have ceased to exist, the transition is more clearly noticeable than in Europe, though the basic trends are prevalent throughout the entire Occident. 

The retarding of the growth of population in the Western nations is one of the most significant trends of the present age. The alarming decline in the birth rates of the United 

States, of England, France and most other Occidental nations will not become fully obvious for one or two more decades, since the high birth rate of the second half of the past century and the decreasing mortality rate cooperate to produce the picture of "growing" nations. But this growth is not a healthy natural growth. It is symptomatic of an ageing nation, and the absolute decline will become visible in its full and terrifying reality as soon as the last numerous age groups, born before the turn of the century, approach their fifties and sixties. For the United States the declining birth rate has one especially disquieting aspect: it tends to change the ethnical composition of the American nation. The Anglo- 

Saxon, Scandinavian and German elements, which numerically, politically, and economically held strong preponderance over all other racial groups, are in danger of losing it, since their brith rate is declining more rapidly than that of other groups. With eastern European and other elements on the increase, the social and political profile of the United States is bound gradually to undergo certain changes. The possibility of correcting the declining birth rate through a liberal immigration policy no longer exists. Even if the United 

States should choose to open her gates again, emigration from western and central Europe would remain restricted to a small number of political refugees. It is noteworthy, though perhaps not surprising, that the United States is the only great Western power that has not yet taken official measures to counteract or, at least, to slow down the decline of the birth rate. For Americans, free of expanding and inimical neighbours, the possible catastrophes resulting from a continuous decline in the birth rate appear. on the surface, less menacing than to Europeans or Australians, whose consistent decline is in ominous contrast to the equally consistent growth of the neighbouring Asiatic nations. 

Americans feel the tendencies of transition from the period of inner growth to a static society most painfully in 128

the economic field. In the preceding age a gigantic machinery of production was built up, ready to supply the growing demands of a growing country, of a quickly-developing community of nations. The proper functioning of this machine was dependent upon growth and expansion. The very laws of industrial capitalism require a continuous dynamic growth to keep the existing economic mechanism going. Once the opportunities for growth cease to exist, the system, if not transformed or artificially stimulated, cannot operate otherwise than in a state of continual maladjustment. "Depression," with many millions of men out of work, becomes the normal state of the national economy. Since the process of natural growth has stopped, artificial stimulation of the economic system by pump-priming, by "creating additional purchasing power" through relief and public works expenditures has become one of the most important and one of the most permanent tasks of government in America as elsewhere. The transformation of the economic system to make it independent from its prerequisites of growth and expansion-in other words: economic planning for consumption in a stationary society-or, as the only alternative, the creation of new opportunities for expansion, is the most pressing decision before the American nation.

The economic maladjustments and increasingly sharp social conflicts that ravage urban industrial mass society put liberal democratic government on trial. The social evolution is producing results upon the machinery of government in the United States similar to those observed in European countries. A trend towards an increase in governmental functions, towards centralization, towards an expert bureaucracy and the restriction of individual liberties is as noticeable in America as it is in Europe. The social war between the organized interest groups makes elective government and legislation through democratic channels more difficult. With ten or more million people unemployed (hundreds of thousands of whom have a college education!)-a situation that is accepted almost as a normal aspect of national life-not only is the economic system of a nation on trial, but also its political integration into one harmoniously functioning bodypolitic is threatened. The crisis of liberal-democratic government is for America, however, of much greater importance than for Europe. "Democracy" has become, like the flag and the Constitution, almost a symbolic expression of national unity to Americans. To be undemocratic is to be unpatriotic and un-American. The task of preserving democracy, therefore, to Americans, is identical to the very task of upholding the honor and integrity of the nation. To fulfill this task in 

America, however, is more difficult than, for instance, in England, since America does not have a political aristocracy well enough versed in the art of government to carry on 129

under a democratic veil a government that for all practical purposes is undemocratic. 

The new reality of a mass-society tending to become static, and all the depressing repercussions of this process, were at first almost incomprehensible to the American mind which was more deeply imbued with the nineteenth-century philosophy of natural growth and human progress than that of any other Western nation. Since it can no longer be ignored that the era of individual and uncontrolled growth is ended, the American nation finds itself compelled to rebuild its social philosophy. The present mental confusion explains itself largely as a stage of transition in the process of spiritual adjustment to new social realities. The epoch of unlimited optimism and enterprising individualism is succeeded by an epoch of growing scepticism and disillusionment. The spiritual profile of America is too complex and the marks engraved by the "bigger and better" era are too lasting to permit a sweeping generalization. But uncertainty, hesitation and discouragement, characteristic of the post-war decade in Europe, are also rapidly becoming a pronounced feature of the American mind, especially in the eastern cities. All the realities of the new society: regimentation of life through the routines of mechanization; standardization of education, of fashion, of taste, entertainment and recreation; the existence of metropolitan areas with millions of inhabitants pressed together in streets one like the other; millions of unemployed as a regular feature of society; increasing concentration of economic and political power in the hands of a few powerful organizations: corporations, trusts, political parties, bureaucracies, or labour unions; the increasing bitterness in the collective struggle of the interest groups for a larger share of the national income; and the unprecedented flourishing, under these circumstances, of social parasitesall these realities point away from the individualistic philosophy of life under which America has grown up. The rationalistic philosophy of the last two centuries prepared the road for the absolute sovereignty of the individual. It laid the basis for modern science, inventions and engineering, and it made possible the materialistic tendencies of the industrial age. America, in her best and worst extremes, is, so to speak, the masterpiece of this spirit. But having thus made its contribution and having separated the "civilized" individual from all dark ties of superstitution or religion, rationalism has led man to the edge of the Nothing. Here he finds himself wavering between the plunge into intellectual nihilism,—a plunge which millions have made in Chicago or New York (in spite or, perhaps, because of the improvements in education)-and a return to something firm, stable, strong enough to hold on to and to believe in. A community may exist, but it cannot live, it cannot grow without a center 130

of ideals that lends direction to its vitality. "Freedom for something," not merely "freedom from something" constitutes the decisive element of creative living for individuals as well as nations. Youth in the United States, in order to carry on and to overcome the difficulties of the coming decades, must find solid spiritual paths—or the country will drift into intellectual and finally into political anarchy. 

Periods of uncertainty and confusion are fertile periods for all kinds of "isms", creeds and panaceas that make inroads into the mind of a nation. Modern masses, constantly exposed to the powerful propaganda weapons of radio, cinema and press, and equipped with a half-education that enables them to understand without comprehending and digesting critically, are apt to fall victim especially easily to clever and unscrupulous propaganda. Americans have thus far preserved themselves remarkably free from any new ideology or "ism". The communist and socialist parties have gained little influence. A fascist movement has not made its appearance in national politics. Nevertheless, it must not be overlooked that radical thought is being represented increasingly by organized interest groups, such as certain labour unions and farm organizations. Fascism, wherever it made its appearance in Europe, originated as a defensive movement against the disintegrating activities of radical organizations. 

One of its greatest assets in the eyes of millions of middle class people, who were otherwise politically inert, was that it came "to restore order," to prevent strikes and lockouts and to uphold the dignity and traditions of the nation against all corrupting foreign influences from within and without. 

In this connection the words of the late Senator Long from 

Louisiana must not be forgotten, "Fascism, if it ever comes in the United States, will come with an anti-fascist program." 

America has developed one "ism" of her own, as opposed to all creeds imported from abroad: Americanism. Americanism is a large container, capacious and flexible enough to hold all emotions, wishes, resentments and prejudices of a multiform nation. If an aggravation of the social crisis should make a reintegration of the body-politic an urgent necessity, Americanism appears to be the only ideology with the inherent power to become the focus for a spiritual and political rejuvenation of America. Though it is not strictly defined in content or just because of this, it is capable of exerting a strong appeal upon the political instincts of a young, disillusioned and searching generation. Organizations that have the will and power to be the torchbearers of a movement of "One-hundred-percent Americanism," should the opportunity arise, are well underway in all parts of the 

United States. 

In 1933 the American government and people embarked 131

on an experiment with the purpose of solving the economic and social problems of the nation within the framework of liberal-democratic government. Though largely concerned with specific economic problems, the New Deal is more than an attempt to rescue America from the acute dangers of a grave economic crisis. It is an attempt to rebuild the material and psychological basis upon which the national economy operates. "Government with a soul, and a nation with a social conscience", in the words of President Roosevelt, are among the supreme aims of his administration. With the 

New Deal, America has passed from an individualist into a social democracy. The reconciliation of conflicting group interests, of capital and labour, of big and small entrepreneurs, of producers and consumers, of city and country, and of regions, protection for the economically weak, relief for the needv, and economic security for the insecure-all these have definitely become tasks of government, to be achieved through comprehensive economic and social planning. The outstanding feature of the New Deal, in contrast to the new deals of continental Europe, is that these aims are to be attained democratically, that is, in accordance with the principles of the Constitution of 1789 (elective government, separation of powers, civil liberties and federalism), and in accordance with the spirit of a liberal democracy. Although, throughout six years of the New Deal, the power of the national government, especially of the chief executive and his professional bureaucracy, has greatly increased, the fundamental plan of public life as laid down by the Constitution has not been touched. The fate of democracy in the United 

States hangs on the success of President Roosevelt, and whoever follows him, in giving economic security to the citizen without taking freedom from him, and in finding a synthesis between the traditions of the American nation and new and pressing social realities.

As great and lasting as many economic and spiritual achievements of the New Deal may be, there is but little doubt that many dangers inherent in the unbalanced economic situation have been averted only temporarily. Vast spending programs are an important medicine in the New Deal cure. A rapidly-growing public debt, however, as wholesome as it may have been in the days of growth and expansion before the war, may constitute a stultifying burden under the conditions of a static society in future years. There is no way back for America to the old system and spirit that reigned prior to 1933. Whether the New Deal and Rooseveltian liberalism will solve the major problems of adjusting the political and economic organization of the nation to the new social realities, or whether they only postpone the crisis, can be seen only several years hence. Looking toward the 132

future, however, the American scene cannot be observed as an isolated phenomenon; some of the decisions most important for America's inner development will fall in international affairs.

III 

What is the position of the United States in world affairs?

What are the objectives of American foreign policy and the problems that it encounters? Throughout the nineteenth century the United States could remain faithful to the admonition that George Washington gave to the young nation as a part of his political testament: No entangling alliances!

There was no need, there was not even a temptation for the United States to enter into an alliance with a European power. Europe was far away and the combinations on the 

European chessboard seemed unreal and of little vital interest to Americans. The opportunities that the Western hemisphere offered could be seized without active participation in European power politics. Even the opportunity to expand into the Pacific Ocean offered itself to the United States without, immediately, forcing upon her serious political ties. When the nineteenth century drew to its close-in the July days of 1914 the United States stood before the world like a young giant, alone, apart from the others, but strong enough to be reckoned with by all as a serious factor in world politics. 

The entrance into the World War was the first conscious weighty step that the United States, as a big power, undertook in world politics. By throwing her lot in with Great 

Britain, France, Russia, Italy and Japan, she contributed decisively to the defeat of Germany and the destruction of the Habsburg empire. Though she refused to sign the Versailles 

Treaty and join the League of Nations, her participation in the war and her influence during the peace conference were instrumental in creating the political and economic status of the post-war world. The United States herself came out of the war with greatly increased strength in relation to the other large powers. Financially, she had become the most powerful world-creditor. Psychologically, the war had demonstrated to all nations the immense importance of American raw materials, of American man-power and of the American industrial machine. Politically, the United States gained recognition of her claim for naval parity with Great Britain, the strongest naval power of the world. She achieved a replacement of the old British-Japanese alliance with a tri- 133

lateral arrangement. She strengthened her commercial and political influence over Latin America. The war. finally, solidified the basis for her own national existence and created a powerful stimulus for increased agricultural and industrial production at home. Though the United States did not reap any territorial spoils from the World War as did the other victors, though she tried to withdraw from the scene of world politics after the war as rapidly as she had entered, though she was later on not even able to collect the sums that she had lent abroad, the war decade meant a great absolute and relative increase in political, economic and psychological strength for the United States.

In spite of the increase in national power that the United States derived from the World War, the American people was disillusioned rather than encouraged by its first big step into world politics. Many Americans believed that the World 

War was to be "the war to end war". The illusion of universal peace penetrated their minds deeply. They seemed to have forgotten that "peace", in itself, is no aim in world politics; like war, it is only a means to achieve certain ends. 

Though the idealistic hope of many Americans for universal and everlasting peace coincides with the material interest of the United States in world stability to build up her international trade, this idealism cannot be explained exclusively by this coincidence. It is a result of a century of rationalism in political thinking, although it is by no means in accordance with the actual history of the New World, which was one of self-help and forceful expansion. It is a part of the general longing for security and stability that has been taking increasingly the place of a more aggressive vision of expansion. 

Nowhere in the world was there so strong a belief in the League of Nations, in the value of anti-war pacts and collective security, as in the United States. No nation seemed so genuinely surprised, when, beginning with 1931, the beautiful dream of a universal league to preserve world peace faded away. And yet, paradoxically enough, the United

States is the only one of all big powers in the world that has been at no time a member of the League of Nations. 

She even refused to adhere to the Permanent Court of International Justice, on whose bench have sat some of her most prominent international lawyers. 

The American temper may, thus, tend strongly toward isolation. But no great power can isolate itself from world affairs under modern conditions. Due to her size, wealth and geographical position, the United States possesses among the powers relatively the most freedom from interdependence. 

But there exist certain conditions and possibilities outside as well as inside her borders that make it impossible even for the United States to remain aloof from the international scene. The more intimately the world political system is knit 134

together and the greater the power, the more difficult it will be for it to remain isolated, that is, passive in the diplomatic and military struggles of other powers, without jeopardizing its very position as a great power. 

Whether or not the United States will be compelled in the near future to adopt an activistic foreign policy that will in the end bring her into war depends, of course, to a large extent upon the turn that developments in Europe and Asia take. If British policy succeeds in solving the European and 

Far Eastern problems by canalizing German-Italian energies toward the Ukraine and Japanese expansion towards Siberia without a general war, there is no reason why the United States should alter her present diplomatic course which is to "wait and see," as far as Europe and Asia are concerned, and to strengthen her position as an unrivaled political and economic leader in the western hemisphere. The problem of an active policy towards Europe and Eastern Asia arises for the United States only if England should fail in her diplomatic task. In this respect it seems hardly exaggerated to say that the elements for the diplomatic course of America are largely determined in London. 

One of the major factors that link American and British policy together at the present moment is the balance of power in the Pacific. The United States cannot, for selfish reasons, afford to see Great Britain weakened or driven out of the Pacific. Since the end of the World War a parallelism of interests and an interdependence in the Pacific region has existed between the two Anglo-Saxon nations; American foreign policy cannot disentangle itself from this position without a drastic alteration of its course. Since, under the present circumstances, a Pacific conflict would have inevitable repercussions in Europe and vice versa-the United States has a paramount interest also in the position of Great Britain in Europe. The British Empire has been called America's first line of defense. Though there are no direct American interests to defend on the European continent, the United States cannot tolerate a decisive British defeat in Europe. 

The British Empire can count firmly on American support of an economic nature and, should the necessity arise, of military nature, if its efforts to stay out of a European or Far 

Eastern conflict should fail.

Another important factor that tends to reduce the freedom of action of American foreign policy concerns Latin America. In spite of all efforts to cement the nations of the 

Western hemisphere together into one great "family," and in spite of certain undisputable successes of the good-neighbour policy, enacted by the Roosevelt administration in 1933 as a new phase in pan-Americanism, there exist certain realities in the political situation of Latin America that may confront the United States with the necessity of making very far- 135

reaching decisions. Many Latin American states still enjoy strong economic and cultural ties with European nations. 

In spite of all manifestations of inter-American goodwill and solidarity there lives on in many Latin American states the fear of the "peligro Yankee," and an animosity towards

North American imperialism. The Lima conference of 1938 

has, again, demonstrated this clearly. The United States and several European nations, especially Great Britain, Germany, France and Italy, are rivals for favours in Latin American trade. Also Japan has demonstrated her interest in Latin 

American commerce. What lies nearer for the Latin American states, in view of their economic and political weakness and in view of their burning pride in national independence, than to derive benefits from the rivalries of the great powers and, whenever possible, to play one against the other? Foreign capital has experienced some severe reverses in Latin America due to this policy. Mexico's expropriation of foreign oil and farm holdings was the most drastic. Similar, though less far-reaching, measures have been undertaken or are being contemplated in several other Latin American countries, among which are Bolivia, Chile and Argentina. The world political situation is unusually favourable for Latin 

America to free itself from foreign commercial chains. If the United States and England, as the major sufferers under the Latin American policy of economic independence, should embark on a policy of retaliation-both countries, in fact, have indicated a readiness to retaliate the chances for the Latin American nations to survive the retaliatory measures are better than ever, because they may find customers for their products and friends in Germany, Italy, and Japan. 

The slogan "Keep America out of war," heard so frequently not long ago in the United States, has been substituted by the more realistic slogan "Keep war out of America." Behind this slogan lies hidden the great vision of an

American continent, united under the leadership of the U.S.A. 

The avowed sympathies of several Latin American nations for the Spanish rebels and the support granted by others to the Loyalists indicate, indeed, a manner in which peace within the Western hemisphere might be disturbed, if a general European war should break out and if pan- American solidarity is not enforced by a strong and watchful policy on the part of the United States. Latin America, furthermore, is in a state of rapid evolution, whose outcome is unpredictable. The stagnating tendencies that are evident in the industrialized and urbanized nations of Europe and 

North America appear to be absent in Latin America.

Nationalist movements with socialistic tendencies have risen in several Latin American nations. Though based on the caesaristic traditions of Spanish-American history, the young dictatorships resemble, in many aspects, Europe's 136

totalitarian states. The social and political emancipation of the Indians in many Latin American states, especially in Mexico, progressing slowly and almost unnoticed abroad, may be counted among the most weighty trends in presentday world politics. It may bring about political realities of such potency and intensity that the nationalist socialism of a Cardenas in Mexico and the integralism of the Brazilian "green shirts" will appear as a mere prelude to a new phase in Latin American history. If the United States wants to maintain her position of leadership in the Americas and keep the Western hemisphere united, she may thus find herself compelled to concentrate great energies upon Latin America and to bring great sacrifices. President Roosevelt's rearmament program, announced shortly before the departure of the American delegation for Lima, is an indication that the connection between pan-American solidarity and American naval strength is fully realized in Washington.

Among the forces preventing American isolation from world affairs and from a possible world conflict the inner economic and psychological uncertainties of the United States are most prominent. As genuine as the desire for peace on the part of the majority of the American people may be, the question must nevertheless be raised: will not the maladjustments of the national economy, which confront America in spite of five years of New Deal, make an active policy and even war appear as a not altogether unwelcome means of bringing at least temporary relief from many ills? War, and the preparation for war, would undoubtedly bring a strong boom; it would also permit a supreme coordination of the forces of production that would be difficult to achieve in peace time. It would justify a psychological and political coordination of the nation that is deemed necessary by influential liberals and conservatives alike. War, in itself, would not remedy the ills of a stagnating capitalist society. But the temptations it presents to numerous groups of societyby no means to "wicked" munition makers alone may be so strong that no abstract love of peace could prevent the country from being drawn into it. The process of national growth has been interrupted ever since the last territorial conquest of the United States was digested, and the commercial expansion has been stopped by shrinking oversea markets. National expansion can be resumed on a large scale only through war or through an imperialist policy that, though it may not actually resort to war, is nonetheless ready at any time to exchange the weapons of diplomacy for guns. It is impossible to predict whether or not the 

United States will yield for purely inner reasons to the temptations of war when the situation arises. There can be little doubt, however, that the neutrality legislation of 193637, which is being applied in the Spanish civil war, but not 137

in the Japanese-Chinese war, is of very little or no value if strong forces at home and abroad are operating, for economic or political reasons, to involve the United States. 

Should a general war in Europe and Asia become unavoidable, it appears almost certain that the United States would be drawn into it. Among the forces working for American participation in a general war there is one that cannot be overrated in an epoch in which the weapons of propaganda have achieved an unprecedented power over the masses. In the United States more successful propaganda concerning international affairs is carried on by private groups and agencies others than the national government-than in any other large country. Indisputably one of the most successful propagandistic drives is the concerted effort of foreign interests, linked with influential groups in the United States, to prepare the American people to be on the "right" side, should American support be needed in a European conflict. "To make the world safe against aggressors,' 

99 to enforce "collective security," or to "uphold democratic principles against the dictators," are some of the slogans systematically driven into the American mind. The very real dangers of a totalitarian war to democratic institutions are usually discarded by the prophets of an "ideological" war who would like to see the United States repeat its policy of 1917. It must be restated in unmistakable words: if the European democracies succeed in settling their difficulties with the fascist nations through a four-power arrangement and in pushing Japanese expansion in a direction less harmful to them, American support will not be needed. America can devote herself to her American tasks. If they fail in this endeavour, they will hardly fail in enlisting American support for a "just" war. The anti-Chamberlain propaganda that is carried on in America by patriotic Englishmen can, therefore, never be damaging to British interests. If Chamberlain's policy of "appeasing the dictators" can be carried to its logical conclusion in Siberia and in the Ukraine, there will also eventually be found means to "appease" American liberalism. If Chamberlain fails, the battalions under the 

Stars and Stripes will be ready to defend "democracy," that is the British, French and Soviet empires not only in Latin America, but also on the Rhine and on the Yangtse Kiang. 

IV 

The United States has been called the land of unlimited opportunities. Many opportunities have been missed; forests have been cut down, and ugly, unhealthy cities have been 138

built. But trees grow again and cities can be rebuilt. America is still the land of unlimited opportunities. The opportunities of America are no longer the opportunities of the individual, to become phantastically rich overnight and to build fairy castles in ghastly imitation of European royalty; the unlimited opportunities before America are the opportunities before the national community to build, or to rebuild, a powerful commonwealth in which social justice prevails, and in which, nonetheless, the spiritual integrity of the individual is respected. The opportunities are unlimited: the land is still wide; it is fertile and rich in minerals; no envious neighbour threatens. Outside the United States twenty

Latin American countries with all their resources and manpower lie wide open to provide material for the building of an all-American community of nations. The problem more decisive than anything else, more decisive than unemployment statistics and budget figures, more decisive than battleships and flood control and foreign trade, more decisive even than independent courts and states rights-the most decisive problem of all problems facing America is a spiritual one: the spiritual rejuvenation of the American people, the prevention of a slow drifting into spiritual anarchy. Spiritual anarchy, whether it be one of indifference or mass hysteria, is always followed by political anarchy-or dictatorship, of which Americans have a panicky fear. A free country cannot exist without inner discipline. The period of transition through which the Western nations are passing and that will last for many years to come needs more than anything else political and spiritual discipline. When discipline is lacking from within it will have to be supplied by a militant dictatorship from without or chaos will reign. Strong unawakened forces seem to be still slumbering in the American soul. 

Out West, where men have not been used up by the haste of the big city, where man's integrity has not been sickened by the poisoning routine of metropolitan life, calling for perverted thrills and stimulants for counter poison-out on the prairies, where "America" is really alive, where "Americanism" is an ethical reality and democracy more than a convenience for governing, there slumber spiritual forces that need to be awakened and formed. It is form, in its deepest sense, more than anything else that America seems to be in need of for a successful future. An aristocracy, based on form and a strong feeling of responsibility, is not contrary to true democracy; only with firm and responsible leadership can democracy survive under the great trials of the twentieth century. If a general European war can be avoided during the coming decade, the United States will be able to concentrate all efforts on her inner rebuilding and on the building up of a pan-American commonwealth. 

Even without international complications these will be very 139

difficult tasks. If the spectre of another great European war should become reality, it is possible that the European nations come out of it in so weak and confused a condition that the spiritual as well as political leadership of the Western world would fall automatically into the hands of the American empire. It would be towards Washington, no longer towards London, that Canada and Australia would look for protection. The United States, then, may be the only power strong enough to counterbalance in western Europe the weight of a Slavic Soviet empire reaching from the Kamchatka to Central Europe. She may be the only power able to hold her own with Japan in the Pacific. It would be America's frontier, not England's, that would have to be defended on the Rhine. Are Americans conscious of the opportunities that the coming decades offer? Are Americans conscious that their country is still the country of unlimited opportunities? 140

Summary and Conclusions Towards a New World Order 

Two things have become increasingly clear throughout the past decade. On the one hand, the idea of a universal political and economic order, once proclaimed the panacea against nationalist decomposition, has broken down. All attempts to establish a political or economic organization covering the entire globe have failed. The conflicts between the national states and between the great world areas prove too strong in our time to permit the realization of the dreams that have inspired the idealists among the defenders of the League of Nations, of collective security and of a world trade system. The extreme nationalism, on the other hand, in which the philosophical and economic evolution of the nineteenth century culminated, has brought many nations dangerously near economic collapse or catastrophic international conflicts. Between the hazy and bloodless universalism of a unified world-state and the sharp edges of an uncompromising political and economic nationalism, our generation seeks its path, groping for a saving synthesis.

It appears as if the realities of the present themselves were pointing a way towards a solution of many of the world's difficulties. More precisely every year, the profiles of a few great world regions take shape, each of which, though not self-sufficient in a technical sense, forms a political and economic unit. Blocks of nations, cemented together through the superior will of one people, through cultural or racial ties or economic interdependencies, are gradually forming themselves. 

There is, first of all, East Asia! Sad as they may be for some, the realities that have evolved on the Asiatic shores of the Pacific during the recent past must be clearly recognized. A continued self-deception on the part of Western nations about the Far-Eastern situation, motivated by the wishful thinking of a frustrated imperialism or by humanitarian sentimentality, may well produce catastrophic results. 

A new and independent East Asia (that is, independent from European and American domination) is emerging rapidly under Japanese initiative. Though a major battle between 

Japan and Soviet Russia still looms up over the unanswered question concerning the ultimate leader of East Asia, the fate 141

of the Occident in China appears definitely to be sealed.

Westerners, henceforth, cannot be but tolerated guests within the borders of the East Asian Empire controlled by Japan. 

Only one way seems to remain open to protect some of the more important Western interests in East Asia: the further to the north the energies of a conquering and colonizing Japan will be concentrated, the longer will it be possible for Western powers to hold on to their possessions in southeastern and southern Asia. In other words, an independent 

Siberia, open to Japanese economic penetration, may be at least a temporary relief for French Indo-China, the Philippines, and Malaya.

Over the formation of an East Asian block the deeper issues behind the events in Asia, which reach far beyond the borders of Japanese imperial power, must not be forgotten. All over Asia the awakening nations of the East and the empires of the West are locked in a deadly struggle. 

The profile of Asiatic nationalism becomes more clear-cut every year. Every battle in China, every riot in Bombay, every clash of arms in Palestine sharpens its features, and beyond the borders of nationality, of culture and religion, a deep instinct for Asiatic solidarity activates the minds of Asiatic leaders-if for no other purpose than to form a front against the common opponent: European-American imperialism, the white man from the West. Since the new spirit rejuvenates the old bodies of the Asiatic nations, the meaning of time and space is changing for Asiatic men. Decades now leave marks on the face of Asia which, during the feudal ages, only centuries could carve. Deserts and seas become small when planes and motorships take the place of camel caravans and sampans.

The emancipation of the nations of Asia is inevitable. 

Europe cannot prevent it. The leaders of the West, however, can do one thing: they can steer the tremendous energies that are seething in India, in Iran, and in Arabia before the very doors of Europe. If a conflict between the Empire of the Rising Sun and Western nations can be avoided, and

Japanese energies, for some years to come, be wisely guided northward, away from specific Western interest zones, British efforts to work out India's problems through a gradual process of democratization and self-determination within the framework of the British Empire may, perhaps, succeed. 

Also in the Hither East, as tense as the situation is, the die has not yet been cast, and the chances for a peaceful solution seem not yet completely spoiled, provided, of course, a general world conflict can be avoided and the Western nations that have possessions in the Hither East realize their opportunity. Calm will not be reestablished in western Asia, as long as the dream of a united independent Arab federation is not fulfilled. This fulfillment, however, may come in two 142

ways: in cooperation with Occidental nations, or against them! Arab national dreams and Iranian political ambitions can be realized without serious damage to Western interests. 

The latter, on the contrary, can only profit in the long run from an undisturbed social and political emancipation of the middle-eastern nations. Iran, more so than any other single nation, seems destined by its geographical and ethnical conditions, and the determination of its ruler, to play a leading and decisive part in the transformation of western Asia. If the rise of the Asian nations can be accomplished by world diplomacy without a world war, southern and western Asia may someday become a world region linked through friendly ties both to Europe and to East Asia, and future generations may look back upon the fourth decade of the twentieth century as the dawn of a fertile and constructive period in the history of Occident and Orient. Only through a peaceful development of these forces does it appear possible, under the circumstances, to preserve, even in a somewhat reduced form, the vast combine of military, financial and trade relations and loyalties, that stretches over all continents and that is called the British Empire.

The second world region that seems to evolve out of the turmoil of the post-war period is America. The vision of a united Western hemisphere, conceived in the days of Simon Bolivar, has survived in spite of the cultural and social heterogeneity of the two Americas and in spite of the strong ties that bind individual nations of the new to the old world. 

The recent past has seen increased efforts on the part of several American nations, especially the United States, to overcome the still considerable difficulties that lie in the way of real pan-American solidarity. Many barriers have been removed between the United States and Latin America as well as between the United States and Canada. Canada, in fact, is in many ways closer to her great American neighbour than she is to her distant motherland. Though the United 

States must still make great economic, diplomatic, and military efforts before she will be accepted as the protector of the Americas and before “Pan-America” will be a solid reality, it might well be, in view of the gigantic forces that lie hidden in the American body-politic, that pan-Americanism has arrived at the threshold of a great and fertile period. 

If it will be accomplished, "Pan-America" will be one of the strongest blocks of nations, a reality to be reckoned with on the Atlantic as well as on the Pacific.

There exists besides East Asia, rapidly growing and expanding as a political entity, and the Americas, now in the process of unification, a third great world area charged with strong political and economic energies, and potentially capable, if certain conditions are fulfilled, of forming a politicoeconomic world region: Europe with its vast colonial hinter- 143

land, Africa. Too small, regardless of its rich and fertile possessions in Africa and elsewhere, to house and nourish the many energetic and ambitious nations that compose its population, Europe is being torn by internal strife. She devours herself, while in Asia and America schemes of unprecedented magnitude that were but visions a few decades ago, become reality. In Continental politics Europe has thought for centuries, and still does think, in terms of thousands of meters, whereas in Asia and America hundreds of thousands of square miles are the units of the statesman's and the builder's calculations.

If Europe is to survive amidst the rising giants of America and Asia, she must find her way towards unity. A united 

Europe has been a dream of many centuries, dreamt by her noblest minds. Can it ever become reality? It can and must become reality, even in this last hour. There are four great nations in Europe that decide the fate of the Continent and its wide dependencies: Great Britain, France, Germany, and

Italy. Democratic or authoritarian, Romanic or Germanic, Catholic or Protestant-these differences among the European peoples do not matter in view of what is at stake: the fate of Europe and of European civilization. Cooperation between the four great powers of Europe is the most urgent command of the hour. There exist, at present, only two real obstacles-grave but not insuperable on the road towards a four-power pact and towards lasting European solidarity: 

Germany's and Italy's need and desire for additional territories or influence zones. As soon as Germany and Italy are satisfied ("appeased," in the technical language of contemporary diplomacy), Europe can become the center of a politico-economic world-region, stretching from the Arctic to the Cape, and from the Atlantic far to the East. 

The only way to solve both the German and Italian problems without a disastrous European conflict seems to lie in a logical and rational continuation of the policy initiated through the four-power discussions of Munich: the two authoritarian nations must, with the sanction of France and Britain, develop their energies eastward. The liberation of the Ukrainian, Turkman and other minorities from the Soviet yoke will permit a rearrangement of eastern European lands and an economic stabilization of both Italy and Germany. In view of the opportunities that offer themselves to the European nations on Europe's eastern "frontier", territorial adjustments in Africa and in the Mediterranean, especially between France and Italy, that have been the subject of much heated controversy lately, appear to be of secondary importance. They can be brought about through diplomatic bargains. Odessa, Constantinople and Baku are as vital to all great European powers as Tunis and Djibouti. 144

Eastern Europe and Central Asia are the last regions of the world open to European initiative.

It may not be possible to solve the problems of the two great European "have nots" without a war against the Bolshevist colossus and those nations of the Hither East that choose the Soviet Union as their friend. But after the territorial readjustments in eastern Europe and in the eastern 

Mediterranean are achieved, and the Bolshevist shadow has disappeared from Europe and Asia, there is no reason why the Russian nation should not resume the fruitful part that it has played for centuries at the conference tables of Europe. 

At Munich the four powers of Europe met in the historical September days of 1938. This first meeting of the leaders of Europe took place, it may be true, under an immediate threat of war. But it did take place. At Munich it was demonstrated to the world that a four-power arrangement between Britain, France, Italy, and Germany is possible. A 

European four-power pact, advocated for many years by European statesmen, can be made the solid and yet flexible foundation upon which the government of Europe rests. 

Neither Anglo-American friendship nor Italo-German cooperation with Japan would hereby be endangered. On the contrary, these diplomatic instruments complement and balance each other. A European four-power pact is the only way to avoid not only a general European war, but a world war into which Asia as well as America would be drawn. 

A European four-power pact, opening up to Germany and 

Italy, the great Have-Nots, the lands of northwestern Asia and Asia Minor, is the only guarantee for the preservation of the British Empire that is hooked to all world regions from East to West. If Neville Chamberlain's efforts are crowned with success, he will go down in history as one of Britain's greatest premiers, as one of the world's most courageous and most far-sighted statesmen. If his policy fails, disaster looms up ahead, and the name of Chamberlain may be associated henceforth with one of the last and most tragic of great Europeans. Ours is an age of uncertainty and fear. Ours is an age of grave and momentous decisions.
