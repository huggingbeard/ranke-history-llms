AT BROWN HE Rhode Island Alpha of Phi Beta Kappa has used, during the century of its existence, keys of a quite remarkable variety of designs. Some were handsome, some eccentric; and it has seemed worth while to record them all before it is too late. Even now the investigation has proved difficult, and the story is by no means as clear-cut as could be wished, particularly in the matter of dates. The Chapter's collection of keys includes excellent specimens of two early types and of one recent one, but lacks several of the intermediate forms. Inquiries addressed to the families or descendants of an older member reveal sometimes that he had no key, sometimes that it is lost; when it is available, it is sometimes a modern replacement, and often the date of purchase is not known.

In spite of all, however, the story which follows is substantially correct.

The original Brown key is of red gold, the body measuring one inch horizontally and 61/64 inch vertically. It has an [ 1 ]

upper stem surmounted by a swivel ring, and a lower stem terminating in an iron ferule designed for watch winding. 

The obverse displays centrally the letters of the Society, BK, in the upper left quarter six five-pointed stars in various arrangements, and in the lower right the hand pointing diagonally toward the stars. The reverse carries at the top the member's name in a broad ribbon or scarf, centrally the "SP" in an incompletely monogrammed script, and beneath it "December 5th 1776." The edges of the key on both sides have a simple border of tiny triangles. 

The general effect of the design is similar to that of the original medal of the Alpha of Virginia. 

The obtaining of keys and supplying them to members was authorized by early votes of the Chapter. It was voted at the meeting of July 31, 1830 "... that Professor Goddard be a committee to inquire respecting the making of the medals of the society and to report on the same." No subsequent report is recorded, but on the 25th of August, 1830, it was "Voted, that Prof. Caswell be a committee to procure medals for the members of the society; and also a medal for the use of the President of the society," and again, on September 6, 1832, the treasurer was authorized to purchase "two dozzen Medals similar to those now worn.'

There is no record of the source from which the pattern of the key was derived, but its actual origin can be conjectured with some confidence. When the Rhode Island 

Alpha was founded through the efforts of President Wayland, in 1830, there were five active branches of the Society. Harvard still used its silver medal, without a lower stem for watch winding or decorative purposes. The Bowdoin Chapter, instituted in 1825, also used a silver medal, [2]

no doubt deriving it from Harvard. The Brown key is unlike these. The Yale Chapter has in its archives the key of 

David Powers of the Class of 1837. It is a key, not a medal, in yellow gold; the body is of the same size as the original 

Brown key; and it displays six stars on the face or obverse, the sixth no doubt standing for Brown. It has, however, an elaborate border of conventional sprays within rulings, and its lower stem, wholly of gold, is very short and "rudimentary." It is farther, in 1837, from the original simplicity of the William and Mary medal than is the Brown key of 1830.

The Union Chapter received its charter from Yale in 1817 and probably derived the design of its key from the pattern at that time followed in New Haven. It also is a key, not a medal, in red gold. The key of Isaac Ambler of the 

Class of 1823 is of the same size as the Yale and Brown keys, but unlike the Yale key it has a full-sized lower stem, with an iron tip for actual watch winding. It has a simple design, like the original of William and Mary. In the upper left corner of the obverse side it has four¹ loosely grouped stars, not carefully engraved but lightly sketched in outline.

The Brown key of 1830, though not exactly like this Union key in such details as the decorative border, resembles it closely in its red-gold material, the general spacing of the design, the form of the stars, and the iron ferule on the lower stem. Brown, then, it would seem, in spite of receiving its charter from Harvard and its Laws from Harvard and Yale, elected to pattern its key upon that of Union.

It is a curious and interesting fact that though the Brown Chapter's emblem was from the beginning a key and not a medal, the two terms are used interchangeably in the early records of the Chapter. "Medals" are mentioned in the

For thirty-five years the Brown key was to retain approximately the original size of its body² and the general proportions of its stems. But in less than a decade there began the series of modifications in the design which provide the justification of this monograph. The reasons for the changes are obscure, but not all can be ascribed to the imaginative versatility of the engravers. Conjectures on the subject will be hazarded from time to time as the history of them proceeds.

The fully developed second type is represented by the perfectly preserved key of John Barstow Willard of the Class of 1842. The legends on the obverse and reverse are the same as in the Lyon key. But the architectural motif has been developed by replacing the leafy sprays at the tops of the columns by a substantial arch of brick or stone. 

The columns have become Corinthian, and the ribbon on the reverse (by now fully transformed into a scarf) is draped over a horizontal bar suspended by a slender cord from the center of the arch. The hand now points, not up to the starry constellation, but straight at the star of Brown in the lower corner.

This design as a whole is unique, so far as is known, and, [6]

AT BROWN 

however inappropriate and unjustified, is a beautiful piece of workmanship. In its three types it seems to have remained in use nearly a decade, for the key of Frederic Denison of the Class of 1847, elected in 1846, carries its essential engraving.¹ As its form was developed gradually, it is unlikely that it had a specific source or inspiration. It is not impossible, however, that local sentiments and associations had an influence upon it, as they certainly did upon the type of key which was next to appear. 

Rhode Island has always had a strong sense of local pride and independence, and Brown University was imbued with the local feeling. This clearly appeared in the picturesque history of the Federal Adelphi, a “purely Rhode Island" institution, founded when Harvard refused Brown a chapter of Phi Beta Kappa in 1790 and destroyed when our present chapter was instituted. The key of Edward Padelford Taft of the Class of 1854, elected in 1853, on its obverse shows several notable changes. The Society's letters, losing the geometrical decoration which had persisted in Types II and III, have acquired a foliate character, apparently suggested by the scrolls of Type III. The "A" has been removed from its resting place on the state seal to the upper left-hand corner. 

As leafy as the B K, it is particularly distinguished by a human figure firmly mounted astride the prolonged and upward-curving crossbar. Fully garbed, even to a hat, the neophyte here proudly proclaims that he has "arrived," and that his is the Alpha. The stelligerent shell, displaced by the "A" and now conventionalized to a circular disc, appears in the lower left-hand corner as though extruded from the abbreviated foliate scroll. The date of the founding of the Society, "December 5th 1776," has been transferred from the reverse to a central place between the bottom scrolls.

On the reverse there is a further attempt to add a personal touch to the engraving. Gone is the eagle, gone the space-filling lower scrolls. The scarf bearing the member's name remains, but as a mere band or sling, while its draped ends have evolved into a pair of heavy curtains looped back at the two sides near the bottom. In the lower corners disclosed by the parted draperies appear on the one side the books of the exulting rider of the “A," and on the other the instruments of his craft, inkwell, quill pen, stylus, and parchment. In the center the "SP" now first abandons script for a modified Old English lettering—a form which [II]

was to be retained, except for one brief interval, until 1914 -and is enclosed within two sprays, one of oak leaves and one of grass or oat blades. Across the key, finally, and below the central symbols, stretches the legend "BROWN UNI-

The type represented by Professor Clarke's key is the last of the old school. Three years later a wholly redesigned key of smaller size appears, corresponding roughly in dimensions to the standard No. 3 key of today, as the former keys corresponded roughly with No. 1. But the radical change to a smaller and less native key was evidently for some time unacceptable to those Rhode Islanders with a strong sense of the past. In consequence, we find, during a decade or more, sporadic reappearances of keys of the older designs, usually not quite true to type, perhaps incorporating details from two types or quite as likely copied with some exactness from a key of an intermediate form which has not been recovered by this study. Some of these survivals may be listed briefly. 

The key of Alvin Fowler Pease of the Class of 1875, he reports, was "copied from a borrowed key." Its general effect is of Type III, but on the seashell there are thirteen stars, the number used in Type V, and it further resembles [ 14 ]

By 1866 the new and smaller key which marks the abandonment of free fancy and extraneous Rhode Island elements in the design had been adopted. The severer pattern is found in the keys of Henry Crocker, 1867, elected in 1866, and Joseph Jackson, 1868, the latter of which is here reproduced.

The body of the key measures three-fourths of an inch [ 15 ]

vertically and slightly less horizontally. There is no swivel in the upper stem. On each side there is a simple border of crosshatching. The seven¹ stars are grouped in two horizontal rows in a rectangular panel in the upper left corner of the obverse face. Beneath the slightly decorated & B K appears the word "BROWN," a return to the principle of local designation. The index hand has no decorative background, and the remaining surface is plain. The reverse carries in the upper left corner the year of the founding of the Society, 1776, and in the upper right corner the corresponding date for the Chapter, 1830. In the upper center are placed the "SP," again in a modified Old English, and again, somewhat as in Type IV a, surrounded by two slender leafy sprays. Beneath, on a short ribbon, is engraved the member's name; and below all, the date of his election.

The Brown key today, nevertheless, retains certain individual peculiarities, one of which goes back to 1838. It still displays the name of the College on the obverse. On the reverse it still carries at the top the two dates "1776" and "1830," at the bottom the member's name and his college class, and beneath it the date of his election. 

Of the various types of key the second was perhaps the most delicately beautiful in workmanship, the third and fourth about equally outré, and the sixth the longest to prevail. There is, one may venture to add, a feeling on on the part of some Brown men that though the standard key is superior to the sixth type of Brown key in authenticity, it is of an uglier design.
