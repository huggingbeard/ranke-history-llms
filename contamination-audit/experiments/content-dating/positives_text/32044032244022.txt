I am at last able to respond to your request that I set down my comment and criticism on Professor Warner's Survey of Criminal Statistics.] My task has been simplified somewhat by the fact that Commissioner Rutledge and Dr. Upson have already submitted a statement to you on behalf of the Committee on Uniform Crime Records. This makes it unnecessary for me to summarize the views of the committee, to which I subscribe. It remains for me therefore only to add a few words on points not covered by the committee's statement. 

On page 11, Professor Warner states that only 13 cities annually report the more serious of the offenses known to the police. If Professor 

Warner's report had been submitted a year ago, the number of cities including such data in their reports would have been even smaller, because several of these cities undertook to present reports of known offenses entirely at the instance of the International Association of Chiefs of Police. As of the present date, the number of police departments including statistics of offenses known to the police in the annual departmental reports is materially larger than the figure which Professor Warner gives and will be still further augmented during the next few months as the annual reports for 1929 come from the press. 986.8 SMI On pages 28 ff., Professor Warner lays down the proposition that national criminal statistics may preferably be entrusted to the Bureau of the Census. In this connection, Professor Warner states that "the peculiar fitness of the statistical department of the government to handle criminal statistics is clearly recognized in foreign countries. For example, in England criminal statistics are collected by the Home Office, which has nothing to do with criminal prosecutions." He also cites Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, but no others. This statement concerning foreign practice is inaccurate, incomplete, and utterly false in its implications.

unknown 95-41-20 -2- So far as England is concerned, Professor Warner is in error when he says that the Home Office "has nothing to do with criminal prosecutions."

I have a considerable familiarity with Home Office practice from first hand study, but on this point, I think it only necessary to quote from the recent work on "The Home Office" by Sir Edward Troup, who was Permanent Under Secretary of State in the Home Office from 1908 to 1922. The author sums up the situation as to prosecutions in the following words (pages 75, 77-78): "The Home Secretary has important powers in directing prosecutions, some of them arising from the prerogative, others given by statute . "The Director of Public Prosecutions and his assistants are appointed by the Home Secretary, and the rules made by the Attorney General as to the Director's duties are subject to his approval jointly with the Lord Chancellor. These rules require the Director to prosecute in all cases of murder (an arrangement introduced by Sir William Harcourt) and in any other cases which the Home Secretary may direct him to undertake, as he has done for instance in cases arising under the Moneylenders Act of 1900. In ordinary course the Director initiates and carries on prosecutions either on his own responsibility or by direction of the Attorney General, but his connection with the Home Office is a close one and many other matters arise, especially in capital cases, in which he comes to the Home Office for consultation or direction. He makes an annual return of his prosecutions to the Home Secretary for submission to Parliament: "In cases of a more ordinary character which do not require the Director's intervention but are left to the Police to pro- · ceed either summarily or by way of indictment, the Home Secretary sometimes gives general guidance to the police as to the prosecutions they should undertake; and instances might be given of test cases begun or carried to appeal at his request for the purpose of securing a decision on some doubtful point of law of general importance." 11 It is absurd to pretend that the Home Office is an English counterpart of our Bureau of the Census. In so far as any parallel may be drawn, the Home Office is more closely related to the United States Department of Justice than to any other federal agency. Specifically, the Home Office supervises not merely prosecutions but also police administration, and conducts, under the general control of the home secretary, the entire business of prison management. 866492

-3- So it would appear that of the four examples of foreign practice listed by Professor Warner only three are valid. Professor 

Warner does not, however, mention the criminal statistics of France, Belgium, Austria, and Germany which are collected and compiled by the several ministries of justice; nor does he mention the judicial statistics of Scotland which are compiled by the prison commissioners for Scotland; nor, finally, does he cite the very interesting history of criminal statistics in Italy, where criminal statistics were originally compiled by the director general of statistics, (who was responsible to the ministry of agriculture, industry, and commerce), but where the responsibility for this work has more recently been transferred to the statistics office in the ministry of justice. It is submitted that the foregoing examples of foreign practice in Britain and Western Europe are more significant than those of three British dominions which Professor Warner cites.

It is my own belief, which amounts almost to a conviction, that the future of national statistics of crime during the next generation or so lies solely with the Department of Justice. This belief is derived from three facts: 

1. The demonstrated reluctance of the police to report police statistics to the Census Bureau when the latter attempted to collect them four or five years ago. 

2. The demonstrated willingness of the police to cooperate with the Department of Justice as evidenced by a.

The activity of the International Association of Police in securing the establishment of the Division of Identification and Information in that department. b. The impressive manner in which the police departments throughout the country have participated in the business of submitting identification records to that division during the past five years.

C. 

The action of the International Association of 

Chiefs of Police in formally requesting the Department of Justice to assume responsibility for collecting police statistics, (This action was taken by the Association at its last national convention in Atlanta, June 4, 1929).

The fact that the Division of Identification and Information has at its disposal the assistance and services of trained Department of Justice agents-in-charge and their subordinates who are now most useful in assisting local police departments with their identification records and who could be used in similar fashion to provide the personal supervision which is essential to the securing of uniform and accurate crime returns.

Professor Warner lays great stress upon the fact that the English compilation of "crimes known to the police" were for many years considered inaccurate, and draws the conclusion that the United States must pass through a similar experience. This, however, is error, be cause: 

1. The statistical branch of the Home Office made no serious effort to improve the quality of these police returns until recent years. 

Thus, it was not until 1928 that any extensive rules for scoring offenses were laid down. 

2. The work of the Committee on Uniform Crime Records has been directed towards anticipating the difficulties encountered by foreign record systems, with the result that a complete manual of crime reporting has recently been prepared. The crime returns now being compiled by the 

Committee on Uniform Crime Records are in nearly all cases prepared by the police in accordance with this manual. 

Professor Warner's only solution for the problem presented by the reluctance of police departments to report crimes takes the form of a proposal for state legislation which will compel the police to submit such reports to a state bureau. 

The system of voluntary nationwide crime reporting which the Committee on Uniform Crime Records is now operating will probably never in itself become complete for the entire United States. But the Committee contemplates supplementing this voluntary system by state legislation which is consistent with it. This plan is based upon the practige of the Census Bureau in collecting vital statistics, Professor Warner's recommendation, on the other hand, will probably not secure either uniformity or completeness Complete returns for certain states might be secured, but certainly not for the entire country, because this would involve convincing 48 legislative bodies of the necessity for such action, Similarly, uniformity will require unified supervision and control, especially during the formative stages of national crime statistics. 

In the matter of accuracy, our experience during the past three months in collecting uniform crime reports has proved most illuminating. 

We are carrying on a considerable correspondence with police departments

which have requested us to assist them in improving their record systems. In a number of cases amended returns have been filed by police chiefs who, upon becoming more familiar with our practice and procedure, recognized that their original returns were incorrect. 

Others have called our attention to specific inaccuracies in their returns owing to incomplete or defective record systems and almost without exception have assured us that these defects will be corrected. 

It may be doubted whether equal results could be secured from a conpulsory reporting system, Within the next few days, I shall be able to send you our monthly bulletin for March, which will include statistics of known offenses for over 500 American cities. These will be supplemented from time to time by statistics of persons charged by the police and by figures indicating the proportion of each class of cases which have been cleared by arrest. Within the next two months, the state of Ohio will be reporting known offenses for the entire state, including the rural districts. Utah will probably follow a similar course in the near future and we expect to add several of the populous eastern states before the year is up. If we had a field force of men familiar with police practice, such as the Department of Justice possesses, we could within a short time embrace over 90 per cent of the United States in a crimes registration area. As it is, more than 75 per cent of the larger cities are represented and over 50 per cent of all cities of 10,000 to 100,000 population, as well as a good proportion of the smaller communities which have been invited to participate. 

In the light of these facts, I cannot subscribe to the altogether inadequate proposals made by Professor Warner. 

If these proposals are approved by the National Commission and receive general acceptance, the net result may be to destroy the body of police statistics now coming into existence. I hope, therefore, that the report will be amended in such fashion as to assist the work already under way, and I am sure that the National Commission will wish to correct the inaccuracies of fact and of implication which I have cited above. 

I had not intended to write such a lengthy letter, but now that it is written, I trust that you will not find it unduly burdensome as to length and that you will communicate it to the members of the Commission when that body meets to consider this question. Sincerely, 

(Signed) BRUCE SMITH.
