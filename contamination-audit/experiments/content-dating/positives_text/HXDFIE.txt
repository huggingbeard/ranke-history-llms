HIS poem was wrillen originally for Old Home Day. It was published in the annual report of the New Hampshire Old Home Week Association for 1921. The simple verse is intended to preserve one of those legends and lales of our earlier history which too often have vanished beneath the green turf and while headstones of our cemeteries with the ashes of the forgollen dead. The rugged Yankee of the period after the Revolutionary War, during which the Industrial Revolution occurred, was true to the innale principles of democracy. His honor did not rest on questions of expediency bul was a maller between himself and God. 

When the rough fragments of mountainous soil, avoided by the proprietors of earlier lowns who moved inward from the more desirable lands of the Merrimack and Connecticul valleys, were gathered into the incorporated town of Wilmot it was so named after Dr. James Wilmot of London, one of many to whom the authorship of the Junius letters had been attributed. 

Dr. Wilmol was a minor figure in that strange group which fought for democracy in the contest between parliament and king. The name of John 

Wilkes appears in the histories, Horne Tooke is less known. Dr. Wilmot died in January of 1807 and the sturdy democratic proprietors gave his name to their town when it was incorporaled In June following. 

The illustrations are pleasing to the author despite their very obvious crudity. He had heard nothing of Zeke the awkward, clumsy friend of his earliest school days till he found him again amid those hills they knew as boys. With the simplest of malerials-a piece of wood and a sharpened iron-he has caught something of the spirit of the scenes described in the verse. No apology is offered, however, for poem or pictures, designed as are both only for a few uncritical friends.
