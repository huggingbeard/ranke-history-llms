8-13-63 GIFT JAFFE New York State Bar Association SECTION ON ANTI-TRUST LAW HON. CHARLES SAWYER, Secretary of Commerce, Chairman of the President's Committee on Business and Government Relations, 

Commerce Building, Washington, D. C.

DEAR MR. SECRETARY: 

July 10, 1950.

In response to your request, the Anti-Trust Law Section of the New York State Bar Association, through a Special Committee, has been considering how it might be of assistance to your Committee in its important work. Your task, we take it, is the examination of the facts and analysis of the issues as to the relationship of business to the Anti-Trust Laws, and their presentation, with your conclusions, to the public and to the Congress. We are confident that you can do so much by introducing a sense of proportion into this difficult and acrimonious field, that we are indeed anxious to be of any assistance we can.

It may be useful, therefore, if, as lawyers, we present certain facts to you as we have observed them, and some of the issues they raise under the Anti-Trust Laws as they have framed themselves from our experience. We shall state the problems rather than draw conclusions, for, as citizens, we may well differ as to how these issues should be resolved.

Though we have not even touched on numerous matters of interest and difficulty in this field that are encountered daily by lawyers and businessmen, our resulting Memorandum has grown to such Gargantuan proportions that we have hesitated to submit it. It would be unreasonable to expect you to do more than dip into it on any particular points that may interest you. However, if nothing more, it may pique your curiosity if we now in brief trace the general course of our exploration.

GENESIS 

i. "Public" and "Private” Businesses

Our point of departure has been recognition that the approach of the law to "public" businesses and its approach to "private" businesses are traditionally at opposite poles, yet under present interpretation of the Anti-Trust Laws these approaches have been confused. 

Towards public businesses the approach is regimentation. With their accepted inherent characteristics of "monopoly," the public 1030488

imposes obligations of affirmative action upon those who engage in such "public" businesses. They must serve all, with adequate facilities, without discrimination, at a reasonable price and for so long as the public needs their service. This the public more and more backs up with a meticulous regimentation of their conduct by regulatory bodies so as to get from these businesses what it feels it requires. In turn the public confers on these businesses certain correlative rights and security. 

On the other hand, to the "private" businesses the fundamental approach is "freedom." We expect to get from them the benefit of increasing productivity and consequently the possibility of a rising general standard of living and results in the production and exchange of products and services of a character and on terms ordinarily reasonably satisfactory to both buyer and seller. We expect these results ordinarily to flow reasonably automatically from the play of initiative and dynamic urge of self-interest in both buyer and seller and the spur and rein of competitive influences on the minds of both. We know this free play in "private" businesses involves inevitable wastes and duplication of efforts and facilities. We know it will never work perfectly. There will often be inefficiencies, losses and hardships. But we are disposed to take all this as part of the price we are prepared to pay for freedom and its dynamic urges. 

While these fundamental basic differences in the approach of the law to "public" and "private" businesses are clear enough historically in the common law, and in the main in the statutes, we have raised the question in our Memorandum whether some of the confusion which is complained of in the Anti-Trust Laws and their application is not due to confusing these two approaches in some statutory provisions or at any rate in their actual administration. May we be converting their "don'ts" into what is in effect an administrative supervision and affirmative meticulous direction of the conduct of concerns in "private" businesses? May this be going to the point that the dynamic energy and initiative which we must fundamentally rely on to get our results in "private" businesses may be lost in uncertainty or in regimentation? Can we have our cake and eat it too? 

2. The Extensive Combination of Individuals and Their Property in a Single Business Unit

Again, it has seemed to us important to have in mind throughout our discussion the dramatic change in our ways of doing business wrought by the technology of the industrial system. In wide areas of our economic life the business unit, whether buyer or seller, is not and cannot again be the individual. It is and must be a combination of individuals and their property, often thousands and even hundreds of thousands of them, in which the "owner" can no longer be the "manager." ii

As a consequence, the business units to which the prohibitions of the Anti-Trust Laws against combinations in restraint of trade apply, are often in themselves combinations-and lawful ones-of thousands of individuals, whether buyers or sellers, who, in a more primitive economy, would have been competitors of each other. At times, in apprehension of the very word "combination," do we let a natural nostalgic yearning for the conditions of the past colour our thinking in the application of the Anti-Trust Laws to the present? 

SPECIFIC PROBLEMS CONSIDERED 

Aside from these two general considerations, we have divided our discussion of the Anti-Trust Laws and its problems, in the accompanying Memorandum, into four parts: (1) The action of a single economic unit acting independently; (2) Joint action of business units; (3) Conscious similarity of action by economic units; and (4) Certain miscellaneous matters. 

1. The Problems of a Single Economic Unit Acting Independently 

The rough and tumble of the competitive system cannot be expected to operate painlessly on those who engage in "private" businesses. But no concern in pursuit of its self-interest can with impunity use below-the-belt ways of doing business which shock the sense of fairness of the ordinary man-and that sense is probably more easily shocked now than sixty years ago. Such attempts of a concern to preempt a field by ruthlessly destroying or buying up other concerns, as prompted the passage of the Sherman Law, are of course forbidden. From our observation, as we develop in our Memorandum, there is general acceptance and observance of these basic fundamentals of the Anti-Trust Laws. The confusion in the mind of the public and of business does not lie here. (a) Limitation on the use of traditional competitive methods The difficulty, as to this aspect of the law, comes, as we try to develop, when a method of doing business attacked as "unfair competition" or otherwise forbidden by the Anti-Trust Acts, is one which springs naturally from competitive influences and has not hitherto been regarded as having anything morally wrong about it. 

To cite a few examples: (1) a buyer, if he can get it, pays a lower price to a particular seller than other buyers; (2) a seller, to move his product, sells at a lower price to one buyer than he is able to get from another; (3) a buyer, in order to get a lower price, gives all his business to a particular seller; (4) a seller, to assure himself of an outlet for his products or services for a period, arranges in iii

advance to fill all the requirements of a particular buyer for that period; (5) a concern without a complete distribution service of its own, to be assured of devotion to its interest, arranges for the services in an area of an "agency" or some "distributor" quasi-agency or "representation" relationship. What is the practical effect of the decisions and statutory provisions questioning the use of these and a host of other such ordinary methods of doing business? 

Is the objective to split the processes of production and distribution into successive stages and attempt to so supervise "private" businesses that all competitors in each stage shall start their race from the same mark? Or is it that the use of such methods is only forbidden when in practice they substantially interfere with the general results we expect to obtain from the operation of "private" businesses? Or, as a practical matter, has the propriety of a wide range of such ordinary competitive methods been now so plunged into uncertainty that for practical purposes they must be treated as prohibited?

Included in the explorations of our Memorandum in this same connection is the shift of the decisions under the Anti-Trust Laws from the position that the owner of a patent could use the monopoly it conferred in pretty much any way it would promote his business, to the present and prospective increasingly drastic but uncertain limitations on that use -and certain issues this trend raises. 

(b) Growth and Size 

We next turn to an examination of the situation created by the instinctive apprehension which many feel as to "bigness." Suppose there is no intent to preempt an industry by ruthless and unlawful means. In that event, what, if any, limitations based on mere size do the Anti-Trust Laws impose on the freedom of a single concern to grow by initiative and ordinary, decent ways of doing business? 

Do otherwise lawful methods of doing business become unlawful for it because of its size or because of a fear that size might make more effective the accomplishment of an evil, unlawful intent, if it came to be entertained?

It has seemed to us that a sober survey of the facts is important. 

Our own observations hardly indicate that in fact "bigness" relieves the mind of management from the rein and the spur of competitive influences in the conduct of a business. It has also seemed to us worthwhile to explore whether in these areas where bigness prevails there may in fact be highly effective competitive influences from potential competition and from inter-industry competition. Again it has seemed important to explore the handicaps in operation from large as well as from small size and also the existence of intangible psychological factors which may operate effectively as restraints on the misuse of the assumed "power" of "bigness" and may in fact make it particularly vulnerable to the competition of smaller competitors. Does or does not the record show that a "big" concern iv

is free, through the existence of its assumed "power," from the ordinary vicissitudes of "private" businesses of having at times to carry on its operations with little or no profit or at a loss? Any concern appears able to make a profit in good times-and from our observation frequently it is a small one that makes the highest. 

Again we inquired as to how far "bigness" is the resultant of new technologies of production and distribution. If that is true in wide economic areas, then our old aspiration of opportunity for every individual American to enter and carry on a business of his own may no longer be practicable in those areas. From that standpoint, if the business has to be done with an artificial combination of the property and efforts of numerous individuals in an artificial entity, with the managers no longer the owners, is it material whether in a particular industry there are a dozen or fifty such units? In such areas may we not have to rely on the opportunity, which now exists, more widely than at any time in our history, for almost any individual to acquire some participating property interest in any large industry? 

For the development of the individual in such areas may we not have to rely, as the moral equivalent of "running his own business," on the stiff personal competition he encounters in a highly effective professional managerial group?

We also explored certain possible or suggested tests for limitation on size-if it is to be imposed. 

2. Problems of Joint Action Among Competitors 

The application of the fundamental objectives of this aspect of the Anti-Trust Laws, from our observation, creates no difficulty. A group of competitors can no more in the name of "freedom" be allowed to gang up to preempt the field of an industry, to drive out or keep out competitors by ruthless methods or to force prices up or down to oppressively high or oppressively low levels, than a single concern can be allowed to set out to do so. This prohibition in protection of the public interest is readily understood and, from our observation, generally accepted and observed. 

The real difficulty begins, as we try to develop, when the interpretation or administration of the Anti-Trust Laws goes beyond this simple concept and attempts to restrict joint action of competitors as something inherently unlawful. We have examined the conflict over sixty years, with frequently changing fortunes, of those who would, like Adam Smith, regard even social intercourse between competitors as dangerous to the public interest, and those who see broad fields in which joint action by competitors in our present economic system is in the public and private interest and in certain of them, as in agriculture, essential to it. The conflict has resulted in a constantly changing balance between fear and freedom-our instinctive fears of "combination" and our instinctive aspirations for the largest practicable measure of freedom. It is a conflict in which businessmen and their lawyers have been so frequently caught

"offside" by changes in the approach of courts and administrators that the sense of confusion and frustration is at least understandable. 

It is in this area of our examination that we have also encountered certain unfortunate but inherent characteristics of our competitive system. It is an unwelcome paradox that the freest play of competition under certain circumstances may and does work as oppressively to buyers or to sellers as the tightest of monopolies and perhaps more so because it is so almost automatic, whereas with a monopoly it would be a matter for single decision. It is a commonplace that what occurs in a competitive market is the resultant of competition among buyers and competition among sellers. A radical and persistent imbalance between available capacity and effective demand will throw the market clear out of gear by slowing up competition on one side of the market and feverishly accelerating it on the other. The resultant from such imbalance will be as oppressive and destructive as the resultant of balanced activity in competition will be reasonable and beneficial.

We are inclined to duck this unpleasant characteristic of a system which we all prize highly. It is true that in prolonged and general crises such as the great depression and the recent war we hastily improvise an N.R.A. or an O.P.A. to put floors and ceilings on its operation. But this characteristic is often apt to be at work in one industry or another and sometimes is chronic. From our observation much of the controversy over “joint action" has stemmed from defensive efforts among buyers or sellers to mitigate the oppressive effects of this characteristic and the warping trends on customs of the trade that result from it. We have felt that it should be explored rather than ignored. The public should be aware of its implications. Congress has dealt with it as to agriculture, labor, communications and transportation by in effect taking the attempts to deal with it by joint action out of the Anti-Trust Laws. If you can find an intelligent way to deal with it, within the Anti-Trust Laws, it should be dealt with. To deal with it, one way or another, seems fundamental to the healthy working of our competitive system. 

3. Problems of Conscious Similarity of Action Among Competitors 

This is a matter we next explore in our Memorandum. From our observation there is perhaps nothing in the interpretation and administration of the Anti-Trust Laws that has created such a sense of confusion and mental exasperation among businessmen as the proposition that there is something inherently unlawful in competitors buying or selling at the same price at the same time and place if they are aware that they are doing it. To them, as indeed to the older school of economists, it is ordinarily merely a normal phenomena in a highly competitive market for standardized products. 

To forbid it seems to businessmen particularly anomalous in view of the tendency in the interpretation and administration of the law to require a seller to sell to all his customers at the same time and place vi

at the same price. Is he going to be able to sell if this single price is higher than that of the single prices of his competitors? Are his competitors required to give him the advantage of a single price lower than their single prices? If this is to be the law, they ask, how is it to be administered to secure the desired result? Who is to prescribe the niches in the range of prices which each must occupy to avoid similarity? How could they be kept secret? How, they ask, are the pressures of competition for substantial similarity to be met? 

We have made some comments on the history of this recurring proposed interpretation of the Acts and on the conceptions of competition from which it arises. 

4. Miscellaneous Matters 

(a) Overlaps and conflicts

We take up certain areas of overlap in administration and conflicts in policy which are confusing and do not seem to make sense. 

We discuss instances of this in our Memorandum. (b) Observance of the Anti-Trust laws and penalties As to their fundamental objectives and as to where their violation would result in some substantial injury to the public interest, it is our observation that the Anti-Trust Laws are well observed. This perhaps accounts for the fact that juries have been slow to convict and courts to impose jail sentences on conviction in Anti-Trust cases. Shifting interpretations of the laws and their at times conflicting policies and attempts to mitigate the oppressiye results of competition out of balance, often produce a flood of litigation in the "fringe" cases-which might well give a different impression to the public as to observance. We have believed this situation worth exploring. 

As to penalties, the subject matter of these laws is such that we question the wisdom and justice of criminal procedure to enforce them unless conscious illegality and substantial public injury are made specific elements of the offense. Also whether punitive trebledamages should be provided for in private litigation except where conscious illegality and intent to injure are found. In the twilight zones of interpretations and of nice balance of advantage as between imposing limitations on freedom and retaining the dynamism of freedom with which Anti-Trust litigation ordinarily deals, it may not be inappropriate for us to say, as lawyers, that equity procedure and simple damage actions are all that seem to us needed or appropriate. We develop the situation in our Memorandum. (c) Statutory changes

In conclusion, we do not in our Memorandum outline suggested statutory changes. We do stress that before determining the advisability of changes it is necessary to explore the facts and reach conclusions on the issues outlined above. In particular, on one vii

fundamental issue-should the statutes, and their interpretation and their administration, be flexible or rigid? Should we seek to preserve the freedoms of competitive enterprise with their dangers?

Or must we put our instinctive fears ahead of our instinct for freedom and adopt the course of increasingly meticulous restraint and regimentation?

The New York State Bar Association Statement 

We desire to express to you at the outset our confidence that your Committee can render a prime service with respect to the Anti-Trust Laws in bringing to their consideration a fresh approach. 

First, we look to you for a sense of proportion-difficult to preserve in this matter.

Second, as a necessary condition for that, we look to you for a freedom from the tyranny of generalized words such as "monopoly", "competition", etc., which, hurled as epithets of sin or salvation, too often inhibit a sober examination of the facts and constructive thought.

Third, we hope from you an insistence on getting behind the varying and oversimplified pictures of business ordinarily presented by the academicians or the advocates or the business men. How do the wheels in fact go round? How do the minds of the multitude of actors on the scene actually work in producing what actually goes on as the resultant of their complex interplay?

Fourth, from such an approach we may reasonably expect to get a useful appraisal of where we stand under the Anti-Trust Laws, how we came to get there, and what, if any, changes it would be sensible to make in their provisions or procedures. At a minimum, you should be able to give to the public a picture which would clear away dead wood and confusion, quiet some hysterias and enable the public to deal with a difficult situation with a proper sense of proportion. The maximum possibilities of your usefulness are very, very great.

On the other hand, what can we do that may be of use to you in response to your invitation?

The members of the Anti-Trust Law Section have had certain practical opportunities to observe the situation from many angles. 1

Almost all of us have participated in anti-trust litigation, many both for and against the Government, or have close associates who have done so. Some of us have even breathed the rarified air of the academicians. What is more to the point, we have, one or another of us, had the opportunity to observe from the inside the actual business processes of almost the complete range of industries. And, what is particularly important, to observe the actual workings of the mind of those running large, medium and small business units. If we are competent as lawyers we have brought to that observation a relentless determination to analyze the facts and get at the issues. Our Special 

Committee has drawn freely on this reservoir of observation and endeavored to reflect it. 

Perhaps if we give you some of the result of this observation it may be of use to you. As citizens we may, and probably do, differ among ourselves in conclusions as to just what course the Congress should pursue. But as lawyers we will present certain facts to you, as we have observed them, and the issues they raise as they frame themselves in our minds. We approach the matter as analysts, not advocates. It is for your Committee, the Congress and the public to draw the conclusions from them.

I. CERTAIN GENERAL OBSERVATIONS A. GENESIS OF FEDERAL TRADE REGULATION 

Not until after nearly a century of our national life did we frame the Interstate Commerce Act and the Sherman Act by which we undertook Federal regulation of our business processes. When we did so undertake it we resorted in these Acts to the application to Interstate and Foreign Commerce of certain principles stemming from a long common-law and legislative experience in England and the States.

Those Acts expressed two fundamentally different approaches growing out of the differences in the situations with which they dealt and the objectives to which they were directed. 

1. The Affirmative Obligations of So-Called "Public" Businesses and Their Rationale The approach to "public" businesses is an affirmative one. Broadly it imposes certain affirmative obligations on those who engage in certain types of business. The original Interstate Commerce Act and the regulatory complex into which it has developed are but the expression of obligations the law was accustomed to impose on the so-called "public" businesses. Broadly, these obligations are to serve all, without discrimination, at a reasonable price, with adequate facilities, to continue the performance of that service once undertaken, and to comply with specific determinations by public authority 2

within constitutional limitations, if it makes them, as to just how they are to perform these oblgations. 

The genesis of this approach is in the fact that there are certain types of business whose service is essential to the community, where there is apt to be serious inequality of bargaining power between those who carry on the business and those who are served by it, and where there cannot be ready resort by the public from one concern to another if service is refused or is inadequate or is on unreasonable terms. In this sphere of business-and it is a wide one-it is accepted that a considerable measure of what we loosely describe as "monopoly" will be present and at times complete; as for example the telephone.

For these types of business Government sets up certain affirmative standards of conduct as to how those engaging in such a business shall conduct it, and, by the collective processes of public authority, makes determinations as to what conduct will satisfy those standards. 

2. The Negative Obligations of So-Called "Private Businesses" 

In contrast to the affirmative approach of the regulation on "public" businesses, which tells concerns so engaged what they must do, is the negative approach of the Sherman Law and its preceding and succeeding structures of case law and legislation, which tells concerns engaged in interstate commerce what they must not do. 

For example, this branch of the law does not tell concerns that, once engaged in a business, they must continue to render the services which they have provided. It does not command them to buy from or sell to all who desire to deal with them. It does not command them to buy or sell at a reasonable price, or to afford adequate facilities in the conduct of the business. Instead, this branch of the law merely forbids their carrying on their business activities by certain methods or by engaging in certain transactions, which, but for these prohibitions, they otherwise might or might not-choose to use or engage in.

B. BASIC PHILOSOPHY AND OBJECTIVES OF ANTI-TRUST LAWS 

The genesis of these "thou shalt nots" of the Sherman Anti Trust Law, particularly as to conspiracy and combination, was perhaps the same fear of the power of combined action which was expressed in the general common law doctrine of "conspiracy", which forbade as an unlawful conspiracy concerted conduct by a group of individuals toward one of their fellows which it might well be lawful for each to pursue individually.

There seems also to have been a fear that courses of conduct, if permitted to be pursued by business concerns, in "attempting to monopolize" or "in monopolizing" would put them in a position individually, or in combination, to create adverse conditions and results, analogous to those we guard against in "public" businesses by

prescribing the affirmative duties mentioned above. Also there was a fear that the use of certain methods in business would be "unfair" to others engaged in the same business or to particular members of the public with whom they dealt. 

It is these instinctive fears that have found expression in the familiar prohibitions of the common law and statutes forbidding action to combine, conspire or contract "in restraint of trade" or "to monopolize or attempt to monopolize."

What does the Community seek to secure by imposing these limitations on freedom of action in business? Isn't it the primary objective, that by compelling the observance of these prohibitions, the interplay of the initiative and self-interest of each concern in business will operate sufficiently to satisfy the private and public interest? There will ordinarily be rivalry among buyers to secure the services or commodities they need; among sellers to secure outlets for the commodities they are producing or the services they are offering. 

This will ordinarily secure a flow of products and services on terms which, by and large, will be reasonably acceptable to buyers and sellers. If there is need for additional or better products and services and attractive opportunity for profit, ordinarily new concerns will enter the business to take advantage of the opportunity for profit. 

This will so generally be the situation that as to "private" businesses the Government does not prescribe either general standards of required affirmative action or specific application of those standards such as it does as to "public" businesses. As to "private businesses", it felt historically, at any rate, it could content itself with the "don'ts." 

We shall have occasion to inquire in subsequent sections whether much of the present confusion and uncertainty as to the application of the Anti-Trust Laws does not stem from the injection of restrictions on the play of competition by recent resort to the regimenting approach of the law of "public" businesses.

C. THE EFFECT OF CERTAIN DRAMATIC CHANGES IN ECONOMIC ORGANIZATION 

In an extremely primitive economic society with small local units, individuals may normally each produce and offer his commodities and services in the market, to individual consumers each seeking to secure such commodities and services for his own use. In such a situation it might be felt any union of any buyers or of any sellers in such a market would constitute a restraint on what otherwise would be the freedom of trade. Further it might be felt that any deal between a buyer and seller in advance of the customary offer in the market place, by taking off that market a part of what would normally flow into it, or any acquisition by this method or in the market by an individual of products to be held for resale in the thus narrowed market and on better terms, would also be a restraint on what might then have been regarded as a normal flow of trade. And there have been statutes and interpretations of them which were

based on, and which attempted to preserve this simplest of economic patterns. We still have some traces in our present thinking of a nostalgic yearning to preserve or restore this pattern. 

But we have come a long way from an economic society in which each individual offers the product or service of his own effort to the individual consumer who has occasion to use them. Instead we find the extensive amalgamation of the efforts of a host of individuals under a single employer or in cooperative effort; and we find the pooling of property of often a multitude of individuals in the artificial entity of the corporation. This means that an effective combination of the productive efforts of a considerable and often vast number of individuals in a single producing and marketing unit is now a fundamental characteristic of our economic society. 

And so of services,-hundreds of thousands of individuals with services to offer may make, through their combination, a single offer. 

On the other side of the market some of these same producing or service combinations of individuals are themselves "buyers"- so that there is often an enormous buying power in such combinations and in cooperative organizations of individual buyers, operating as a single unit. Instead of a middleman--the wholesaler-who merely buys and re-sells, being conceived as restraining the free flow of trade as between the producer and the consumer, he is now felt to be a useful conduit for the two-way flow of that trade. These middlemen also are now in themselves substantial and often even enormous combinations of the activities and property of large numbers of individuals. 

D. INTERPRETATION AND APPLICATION OF THE SHERMAN LAW STARTS WITH THE ASSUMPTION THAT EXTENSIVE COMBINATION OF THE AC- TIVITIES AND PROPERTY OF INDIVIDUALS IS NEC- ESSARY AND LAWFUL AND NOT "IN RESTRAINT OF TRADE" 

At the time of the passage of the Sherman Law, as a result of this complex development of 19th century economics, we started off with the accepted widespread phenomena of many individuals combining their effort and property in production, in service and in marketing; and also of combining their activities in buying products and services to satisfy their needs. Many individuals, who in a simpler economic system would have been competitors of each other in buying or selling, were thus now customarily combined in single economic units, large and small.

This was wholly foreign to the simple conditions of individual activity in which the first concepts of "restraint of trade" and "monopolization" had had their genesis and application. We had been living for a century in the atmosphere of the industrial revolution. That atmosphere and its technology had found its reflection

in the laissez-faire philosophy. It had broken down the old simple economic system and restraints on freedom of action by individuals to act in a group, appropriate to the older system, and had also broken down certain recognized types of group activity in the guild system. 

In this burst of new economic activity in the 19th century in the field of "private" business, the free play of self-interest by individuals, alone or combined in economic units, had come to be regarded as the normal way to achieve the common economic good. 

As a result when instances of ruthlessness, actual or feared, of the play of that self-interest in methods of doing business or in group action prompted the passage of the Sherman Law, there was no thought of a root-and-branch extirpation from the economic system of extensive combination of the effort, property and activities of individuals. 

Obviously that could not have been done without destroying the system itself. It was and has continued to be rather a question of, first, in what areas were the "don'ts" of the Sherman Law applicable, and, second, what is the extent of these limitations on freedom of action by individuals or by such combinations of individuals? Extensive combination is assumed as a matter of course. 

E. DEVELOPMENT AND SHIFTING BOUNDARIES OF THE LAW 

It was inevitable that the passage of the Sherman Law, with its language as broad as some of our basic constitutional provisions, was only the beginning of the legislative process. To be sure the Act had as its background prohibitions of the common law as to restraint of trade and monopolization-but those were themselves general statements subject to inevitable particularization and amplification by the administrative and judicial processes. Federal administrative agencies and the Federal courts took over the exercise of this function where the common law courts left off. 

The Sherman Law constituted restraints on what, at any rate so far as the Federal law was concerned, had been freedom of action. 

At times there has been a tendency for the Courts to choose limited definitions and application of the general terms in which the law was expressed, in view of its being in derogation of freedom. At other times, in view of the fact that the prohibitions have been thought themselves to have been designed in the long run to preserve a greater freedom in our economic life, the courts have chosen to adopt broad and drastic applications.

Congress itself has, from time to time, in effect reversed or moderated some determinations of the courts which it thought undesirable, as for example in the matter of transportation. Or it has imposed some new specific prohibition on freedom of competitive action of its own devising, as in the Robinson-Patman Act. Or at times it has washed the Federal hands of a matter by in effect turning it back for the States to handle, as in resale price maintenance and insurance.

Quite apart from statutory extensions or contractions, the pendulum of construction of the broad language of the statute by those administering the law and by the courts over the period of 60 years has swung back and forth. Insensibly influenced by public opinion or by their own changing membership they have radically changed their own determinations.

The economic system to which we attempt to apply the concepts of the Anti-Trust Laws is now infinitely complex and infinitely varied in different fields of its activities. It has changed in character over the many years since the common law began to deal with it and also in the years in which there has been occasion to apply the statutory language and these concepts. As a result the boundaries of the application of these laws restraining the freedom of action of concerns and individuals in trade can not be expected to remain fixed or at any time exact.

It is our observation that it is over these boundary lines that litigation rages-with that bitterness which seems to be peculiar to "boundary disputes." It is, perhaps, primarily due to the shifting of the boundaries by the courts that at times so many persons engaged in business are caught "offside." As we shall develop later, it is our observation from the experience above indicated that where any substantial injury to the public interest would be involved, the laws are generally well observed.

Specific issues raised by the Anti-Trust Laws, in their original and amended form, and by this development of their construction and application by administrative and judicial action, we will proceed to treat in four parts: 

1. The action of a single economic unit acting independently; 

2. Joint action of business units; 

3. Conscious similarity of action by economic units; and 

4. Certain miscellaneous matters. 

II. PROBLEMS OF A SINGLE ECONOMIC UNIT ACTING INDEPENDENTLY 

We shall consider now what the law says to a given economic unit by way of prohibition on the freedom of its independent action in its self-interest in the maintenance, development, growth and scope of its business.

We are differentiating here between what such a unit may do itself as a matter of its internal policy, and the joint action which it may be forbidden to take with other units which are engaged to a greater or less extent in buying or selling in the same market and also from so-called "conscious parallelism", which we shall later consider. 

This general question would seem to have been much in the minds

of the framers of the Act, at a time when there were complaints that certain units were securing spectacular growth at the expense of other units by intrinsically unfair and morally obnoxious methods and by the aggregation into a single unit of the properties of rival concerns by the device of the so-called "trust" or otherwise. The use of such methods and the growth of such concerns by such measures was early the subject of litigation. 

A. PROHIBITION OF ATTEMPTS TO MONOPOLIZE AND THE USE OF INHERENTLY OBJECTIONABLE METHODS OF COMPETITION 

Growth and obtaining of business at the expense of rivals by at any rate the cruder of "unfair" methods was early determined to be within the prohibitions of the law-certainly where they were used with an intent to "attempt to monopolize." The "chamber of horrors" in the Cash Register case afforded illustrations of the kind of thing to which it has never been seriously claimed the free play of self-interest is allowed to extend. In the bitterness of the competitive struggle, characteristic expressions in internal correspondence, revealed by litigation, certainly did not minimize the objectonable character of methods used! Perhaps there was even at times some element of boasting as to "nailing up the hides of competitors so that all could see and take warning.' 

There never seems to have been and there is not now any serious question as to the general principle involved in such situations. 

Deliberate attempts of a concern to preempt the field by ruthless competitive methods used to put concerns out of business or soften them up for absorption or to exclude new concerns as incident to an attempt at "monopolization", such as prompted the passage of the 

Sherman Law, are of course forbidden. From our observation, there is general acceptance and general observance of this fundamental of the Anti-Trust Laws. Such uncertainties as arise in their application to this basic objective are merely such as are bound to arise in the enforcement of any law. They could only be removed by eliminating the fallibilities of the working of the human mind in its recollection and appraisal of facts. 

B. LIMITATION ON FREEDOM TO USE TRADITIONALLY ACCEPTABLE COMPETITIVE BUSINESS METHODS 

1. In General

The prevailing confusion in the mind of the public and businessmen as to application of the Anti-Trust Laws as to what a single concern may or may not do is not with respect to these basic objectives. Instead, most of the issues that now arise as to limitations by the Anti-Trust Laws on the conduct of a single concern stem

from novel conceptions in the field of competitive business. They are as to whether limitations are imposed on the freedom to use methods of doing business which spring naturally from competitive influences, which have not been generally regarded as having anything morally wrong about them and have long been used by competitive concerns, large and small. They are as to situations where the determination as to how the balance tips between the advantages to the public of retaining the free play of competition, and the disadvantages from this free play to particular competitors or to members of the public, has not been clear, either to businessmen, the administrative agencies or to the courts.

Such questions have arisen primarily under the Federal Trade Commission Act, the Clayton Act, the Robinson-Patman Act and under some recent interpretations of the Sherman Law by the Courts. 

In 1914, in setting up the Federal Trade Commission to deal with "unfair competition," Congress seemed to recognize that the possible extension of the application of the term "unfair competition" into new fields might well be a matter for an administrative agency to feel its way in. The Federal Trade Commission Act provided for an examination and prohibition of individual competitive methods which appeared to it to be "unfair," but without the imposition of penalties for their use prior to the issuance of its order. The wisdom of this is emphasized by the broad discretion in this field that the courts, reversing an earlier view, now hold is vested in the Commission. 

There are certain areas under this Act where the resulting uncertainty as to competitive methods that exists is not too serious. 

For example, what in selling is merely lawful "puff", to which the public has long been enured and perhaps can take care of itself by discounting, and what is "unfair competition" by statements which actually mislead. Opinions may differ as to whether or not, as to such matters, the Commission attempts to be too meticulous and precise. 

2. Limitations on the Use of Customary Terms on Which Buyer and Seller Contract a. In general

The serious confusion that now arises under these Acts is, rather, in limitations on the terms on which buyer and seller may contract. 

For example, it has been ordinary business practice (1) for a buyer, if he can get it, to obtain a lower price from a particular seller than other buyers are obtaining; (2) for a seller, in order to move his product, to sell at a lower price to one buyer than he may succeed in getting from another; (3) for a buyer, in order to get a lower price, to give all his business to a particular seller;

(4) for a seller, to assure himself of an outlet for his products or services over a period, to arrange in advance to fill all the requirements of a particular buyer for that period; (5) for a concern without a complete distribution service of its own, to assure itself of devotion to its interest by arranging for services in a particular area by an "agency" or some quasiagency "distributor" or "representation" relationship.

Of course, if a concern violates the basic objective of the Anti- Trust Law, as defined above, by ruthlessly monopolizing a field and uses these customary practices as part of its means to that end, the whole scheme is obviously unlawful-but that illegality does not appear to stem from anything inherently objectionable in these practices. 

The provisions of the Clayton Act, as amended by the Robinson- Patman Act, and interpreted by the Administrators and the Courts, however, now raise a question as to the use of all these ordinary business practices when there is no such unlawful plan and intent. b. The provisos as to effect on competition 

To be sure, certain of the particular prohibitions of the statute, which raise the question, purport to be qualified by provisos that they are forbidden only under the circumstances set forth in the provisos. But for these qualifications the provisions of the statutes would clearly import into the field of "private" business, where the free play of competition is relied on to work out satisfactory results, the regimented obligation of the "public" businesses to contract with all alike on the same terms. Such a limitation on the use of normal competitive methods would create in competitive "private" businesses a rigidity akin to that in "public" businesses.

With these general prohibitions qualified as they are by provisos, there is bound to be some factual uncertainty as to the application of the qualifications. That uncertainty and confusion appears to have been accentuated by diverse, changing and what many regard as highly dialectical conceptions of the administrators and the Courts as to what is intended to be meant by the provisos. 

For example, what is meant by the proviso "when the effect may be substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly"? 

Is this test to be whether the use of these ordinary competitive methods would actually interfere substantially with our getting in a particular industry the general results, outlined above, which we can reasonably expect from the workings of the competitive system in one of the "private" businesses—a test based on the business realities of the situation-such as Brandeis was accustomed to contend for and such as the ordinary businessman is reasonably able to gauge? 

Or is the test to be-and the Supreme Court seems to have been tending that way-some speculative possibility of some effect on some undefined conception of "competition"? Or is it enough if the quantitative amount of business to which the terms of particular 10

contracts apply, which are the expression of the use of ordinary competitive methods, is "substantial"-as seems to have been the test applied by a sharply divided court in the recent Standard Oil of 

California "requirements contract" case?

In the section of the Clayton Act, as amended, where the proviso goes further and covers the situation where the effect may be to "injure or destroy or prevent competition with any person who either grants or knowingly receives the benefit of such discrimination or with customers of either of them", there is again uncertainty but the emphasis seems on protecting a particular "competitor" from the effects of the free play of competition rather than some general adverse effect on the public. 

It is a fair question for your Committee to consider whether some of the language of the provisos themselves and the intellectual refinements now being applied to their interpretation, with the resulting uncertainty, which we observe, as to how far, if at all, a concern is free to use these ordinary competitive methods, may not be the equivalent in practical effect of wiping out the provisos altogether -and in effect applying the regimentation of "public" businesses. If so, does your Committee regard this as desirable?

Whether they can or cannot use these and similar everyday methods of competition is of course a very practical question in the day-to-day life of business concerns, large and small, buyers and sellers, all over the United States. c. Restraint on freedom of competition at one stage of the competitive process in the endeavor to equalize competitive conditions at another

By the very presence of the provisos, the law seems to the businessman to recognize that there is nothing inherently unlawful in these ordinary business practices constantly used in our competitive system. But paradoxically, as it seems to the same businessman, he is told that he cannot engage in this normal play of "competition" if it "substantially lessens" or "injures" "competition.'

One key to the paradox may be that Congress has observed that often the marketing processes in the production and distribution of products roughly divide into stages at each of which there may be a substantially different group of competitors. Recognizing this, Congress seems to have conceived that it is desirable that competitors in each group in the various stages should start their rivalry in their competitive race from the same mark. One buyer, by being able to buy at a less price than others, who are his competitors in reselling, might have a head start in the race. Therefore, in an effort to equalize the competitive race in the subsequent stages, Congress has felt it desirable to place limitations on the normal play of competition in an earlier stage.

The logical conclusion might be that the only effective way to place all competitors on the same mark, as to the price at which they have purchased, in their competition in the subsequent stage would be to 11

require all sellers to sell to such competitors at the subsequent stage and all such competitors to buy at the same price. Neither the statute nor the decisions yet go that far. But if that is the ultimate objective, a requirement that each seller should have a single price to all of its customers, who are in turn competitors among themselves, certainly tends strongly to accomplish it in the case of standardized products.

If a producer is unable to take advantage of the play of competition to get the best price he can from each customer and to lower his price to get business from particular customers, and his competitors are in the same situation, then it would seem that no one of them is apt to be able to get much business if he makes his single price higher than the single price of his competitors. Nor would it seem probable that any one of them which tried to attract business to itself by making its single price lower than that of the single price of its competitors would for long be able to enjoy that advantage. The competitive pressures toward uniformity are strong enough in any event, but a prohibition against a seller or a buyer from using the competitive method of making or obtaining a difference in price certainly accentuates this tendency. d. Certain illustrative questions 

The same philosophy is apparently back of the limitations on the use of terms in contracts, other than price, which have hitherto been the commonplace of competitive businesses. Parenthetically, it may be noted that the factors which would have to be dealt with, if real equality of competitors at each stage were to be effected, would be legion.

From our observation wherever there is such an attempt to limit competition at one stage in order to relieve competitors at that or another stage from the effects of the free play of competition at that stage, difficult questions and uncertainties are pretty much bound to result.

We have mentioned above certain familiar methods of competition as to which these questions arise. But an infinite number of more specific questions will immediately occur to any business man or lawyer who, in day to day operations, is trying to follow the thread of the lawful through the veritable labyrinth that has now grown up in this field. 

For example, as to pricing: 

1. May a seller safely differentiate in price between his customers on the basis of differing functions or locations? If the differentiation is on the basis of meeting competition, what evidence must he have of the competitor's pricing to the particular customer? 

2. How may prices be quoted by the seller with multiple plants or warehouses or subsidiaries? An identical price at all points? Differing prices, necessarily differentiating between 12

customers? Differing prices by a manufacturing subsidiary and a distributing subsidiary? 

3. How does a seller furnish or pay for services and facilities on proportionately equal terms? When only a few customers can qualify? Or when he desires to localize sales efforts to a certain territory? 

For example as to selection of customers: 

1. May a seller still unreservedly limit his sales to one customer or to a select few per territory? To what, if any, degree is there a trend toward imposing the obligations of "public" businesses of serving all that apply? 

2. If there are restrictions based on what the reasons are for selection, do they limit selection on the basis of the customer's willingness to handle the full line of the seller's product, or the customer's pricing policies with respect to "loss leaders" and otherwise? Or avoidance of competition for markets in which the seller desires to handle his product direct to the consumer? 

3. May the seller similarly drop customers for any reasons?

For price cutting? For refusal to handle more than a few selfselling items? For competition in an area in which the seller desires to sell direct?

For example, as to control of customers by the terms of contract: 

1. How far may a seller who relies on his customers for the distribution of his products at the next stage exercise control by contract over such customers to ensure that they aggressively and intelligently merchandise his goods? Carry the proper stocks? Push all items? Hold sales to move slow items? 

Price on an economically sound basis? 

2. How far is a seller forbidden to restrict by contract resales with the aim of protecting himself and his product from the result of what he regards as improper or unsound business practices in such resale? Where the product is potentially or inherently dangerous? Where fraudulent and unlawful practices on the part of the buyer are discovered? 

3. What is the seller to do when the F.T.C. condemns resale price maintenance in some circumstances, yet-as in the pending Indiana case seems to require it in others? 

e. The Basing Point Controversy 

The history of the "Basing Point" controversy illustrates the difficulties that may arise when the Anti-Trust Acts or their interpretation by its administrators or the Courts interfere with the play of normal competitive practices.

The issue as to the Basing Point method of doing business, as it has finally developed, is whether a concern, reaching its determination solely in view of its own self-interest, may systematically engage in 13

offering its products in different areas at a delivered price which takes into account the lesser transportation costs of the product of its competitors. By reason of such pricing, it inevitably receives what is described as a lower "mill net" return on parts of its business than it does on other parts. 

It had been generally supposed after the earlier Cement case in 1925, that the use of this method was to be regarded as the natural and unobjectionable result of the free play of competition. This was particularly so in view of the repeated rejection by Congress of requests from the F.T.C. that this method be prohibited by statute. 

The Federal Trade Commission, however, finally embodied the limitation it had been advocating in its decision in the Conduit case. 

The C.C.A., bowing to what it regarded as controlling language in an opinion of the Supreme Court reversing its decision in the Second Cement case, sustained the order of the F.T.C. As the Supreme 

Court subsequently divided equally on the appeal, the order stood and became the law-just as the reverse would have become the law, if the C.C.A. had not yielded its earlier views. 

This anomalous situation led to a widespread change in business practice as a result of what many deemed a new and undesirable limitation upon the free play of competition. It was their view that this, by narrowing the area in which a concern with a plant in only one location can compete, tends to confine competition to regional rather than national areas and puts the small operations at a competitive disadvantage.

On the other hand it has been argued that to permit the free play of competition causes burdensome economic waste through crosshauling and that while buyers over a wider area may pay the same price under this method, those with an advantage of location ought to be able to secure a lower price than the others. In reply it is said that these arguments might be applicable in the field of regulated "public" businesses, with their accepted element of monopoly. But are they applicable to the field of "private" competitive businesses? 

In that field it is ordinarily supposed that we accept such economic wastes as a duplication of facilities, advertising and sales effort, and cross-hauling as part of the price which we have to pay for the advantages of the free play of competitive initiative and a price well worth paying.

Congress took a hand in the dispute. Still standing by its previous rejections of the F.T.C.'s request for the statutory adoption of the 

F.T.C. position, Congress passed the "Basing Point" bill to restore the situation. But the Executive interposed a veto. 

Meanwhile in the stormy controversy which had arisen the F.T.C. had made certain propitiatory explanations of its decision and of its future administrative course with respect to the same. On the veto, a "watch dog" committee was set up in the Senate to keep an eye on how the administrative body carried out its modified administrative approach. 14

Whatever the merits of the controversy, the situation of uncertainty which still persists on a matter that vitally affects the day-to-day carrying on of their business by industries and concerns large and small all over the country can hardly be satisfactory to any of the parties to that controversy. It certainly illustrates the complexities attendant upon attempts by public authority to restrict the free play of competition in the competitive "private" businesses, and the attempt to apply to their regulation the philosophy of regimentation which is applicable to the "public" businesses. 

f. Miller-Tydings Act 

Another illustration is the situation as to the restriction on freedom of contract between seller and buyer with respect to resale price maintenance. The courts, after some vacillation, determined that it was a forbidden restraint of trade for a seller to contract with a buyer as to the prices at which the products it sold should be resold. 

Freedom of contract of the seller was thus limited where the effect of such contracts was to limit the freedom of contract of the buyer in his dealings at a subsequent stage of distribution. 

Here again there was bitter controversy as to this limitation on the free play of competition with respect to customary business methods. Congress determined that, in some situations at any rate, it was preferable to leave the seller free to contract with the buyer for some limitation by the latter on the play of competition at the retail level in order to prevent what was argued to be the "competitive abuse" of "loss leaders" with resulting injury to the good-will and business at the producing level. By the Miller- 

Tydings Act, Congress in a rather broad area, in effect, washed its Federal hands of the matter and left it to the State legislatures to determine whether and where the accustomed freedom of contract in this matter should be restored.

It is to be noted that this result is the converse of the result reached on the issues referred to above, where the tendency is to restrict freedom of competition in the initial stages in order to endeavor to secure equality at a subsequent stage. Conflict of opinion still exists in this field, and an examination of it by your Committee should be useful. g. Patents and secret processes

There is no question that a patent license cannot be used as a convenient cloak for concerns to "gang up" together in an agreement the terms of which constitute an unlawful restraint of trade. That was early decided and is not now in controversy. 

Further, the construction of the Sherman Law by the Courts as to whether a single concern might use its patent monopoly in aid of contracts to promote its business in products not covered by its patent has swung to the opposite pole from the earlier decisions. 

It is now clear that the patent monopoly can no longer be used by the holder of a patent as a contractual adjunct in efforts by its owner 15

to sell products not within the scope of the patent monopoly. This change has been accepted. The adjustment of business practice to it has been made. 

But there are a host of questions raised under the Anti-Trust Acts by administrative action or opinions as to possible limitations on what has been the customary freedom of the owner of a patent to contract with respect to his rights under the patent itself, the settlement of disputes over his claims and the acquisition of rights under the patents of others.

How far can the owner of a patent divide up rights under it? 

When may a patent owner safely settle interferences with competitors in return for licenses to avoid the hazards of litigation? 

What are the limitations, if any, on acquiring patents to forestall similar acquisition by competitors? Or to acquire them with competitors on the basis of joint research? 

Is there any obligation either to use the patent or to license it? If a concern begins to license it, can it unreservedly pick and choose among licensees? May it impose restrictions or limitations upon the licensee's prices, production or markets? Require reciprocal licenses?

License in convenient packages? Vary the rates and other terms among licensees? Adopt the policy of cross-licensing in the same industry, or in different industries? On an agreed royalty sharing basis? On a basis open to a limited few or to all? 

There are similar problems involved in the acquisition, use and licensing of trade-marks and copyrights, secret processes, and in contracts as to technical "know-how."

In our approach to dealing with patents in such situations we inevitably get into the cross-currents of two public policies: 

First: the present trend of Public Authority, we have mentioned above, to be impatient of any situation which appears to give an advantage to any competitor and a resulting tendency to impose restraints on freedom of contract in order to equalize competitive conditions for competitors at the same or different stages of the processes of production and distribution. 

Second: the policy to create incentive for invention and discovery by placing in the inventor, for a period, the determination of by whom and how the competitive superiority, given by the invention, shall be enjoyed. Any invention, if it is of commercial value, gives to the user or users a competitive advantage in a field of business, which may go to the length of giving it a "monopoly"-limited, of course by the extent of the superiority over prior art.

Your Committee may find that in their effort to promote the first policy of the equalization, by regulation, of the situations of competitors, administrators of the Anti-Trust Law and the Courts have shown a tendency to use the Anti-Trust Laws to circumscribe the second policy by in effect amending the patent laws. Disregarding the merits as to the desirability of such amendment, you may find 16

that such amendment by this process is necessarily productive of the confusion and uncertainty in the minds of businessmen which it is our observation now exists in this field. 

An additional example of the uncertainty that has prevailed is reflected by the sweeping contentions made in the recent Hazeltine case. If accepted they would have outlawed in the name of the 

Anti-Trust Laws the use by a patent owner of a widespread and traditionally convenient mechanism for measuring the compensation for a license to use his inventions. These contentions were not accepted by the majority of the Supreme Court, but they were supported by a vigorous dissent. 

Incidentally you may feel it a question whether the effective deprivation of rights under a patent is a penalty that Courts can properly impose for a violation of the Anti-Trust Acts, without action by Congress adding it to the enumeration of penalties under those laws. 

3. Issues Emerging 

To summarize our observations as to the issues presented to you in this field:

There is no controversy as to the basic prohibition of the Anti- Trust Laws against an attempt by a single concern to set out to "monopolize" an industry, in the ordinary sense of the word, and of its use of methods to do so which the ordinary man would regard as inherently ruthless and wrong. From our observation this fundamental of the Anti-Trust Laws is observed. 

The difficult questions are as to limitations on freedom to use ordinary competitive business methods-not inherently unlawful. 

The motivation of such limitations appears to be primarily a desire to equalize the situation of competitors in various stages of marketing -limiting the play of competition at one point with a view to securing an effect at another stage in the processes of "private" businesses. 

The question immediately arises, how far can we go in this direction without abandoning our policy in such "private" businesses of relying on the free play of competition to secure our results and turning instead to the opposite principle of "public" businesses of imposed affirmative duties and regimentation of operation? To what extent can we mix our drinks?

This problem, as we observe it, is becoming more difficult by the tendency away from earlier pragmatic interpretation of the Clayton Act provisos as to the application of their limitations. An interpretation that prohibitions against the free use of ordinary competitive methods, not inherently unlawful, apply only when it can be said under the circumstances in a particular industry their use so interferes with the beneficial general operation of the competitive system in that industry as to work a substantial injury to the public interest, might not, as a practical matter, be too difficult to administer or for a businessman to understand. Courts and juries in tort cases every day apply the test of "reasonable care" "under the particular cir- 17

cumstances." Everyone has to apply it as best he may in his daily conduct.

But, instead, the tendency, as we observe it, is toward interpretations which are either so drastic that this ordinary play of competition is forbidden, even though it is doing no harm to the public, or which are, to a paralyzing degree, uncertain. 

If there are to continue to be limitations on the use of ordinary competitive methods-and this seems probable-is it practicable to work them out satisfactorily by any general statutory provisions in the absence of some such flexible standard which enables the widely varying circumstances and needs of different industries and stages of industry to be taken into account?

To attempt to crystallize such differences in situations in the provisions of statutes, themselves, would from our observation be wholly impracticable.

The F.T.C. in the twenties made a vigorous attempt to deal with the inherent difficulties of the situation by working out with different industries the particular limitations on the use of competitive practices which the circumstances of a particular industry seemed to call for. But its action was in no way binding on its companion enforcement agency, the Department of Justice. The results of an effort along similar lines by the N.R.A. were not regarded as happy. 

Unless we are prepared to accept the rigidity of limitations on the free play of competition and more and more meticulous regimentation, mitigated only by sporadic corrective action by Congress, toward which present statutory provisions, administrative action and decisions have been tending, some solution for greater flexibility in the application of any such limitations will have to be found. 

C. LIMITATIONS ON FREEDOM TO GROW BY ENTERPRISE AND GOOD MANAGEMENT 

1. In General

There is, of course, the "basic" limitation on growth, referred to above, where a concern sets as its goal the ruthless exclusion of all others from a field. It is not that sort of situation as to which uncertainty and confusion exists. Instead, there is now before the courts, and very much in the mind of Congress and the public the issue of whether freedom of growth in a business enterprise is limited under the Anti-Trust Laws by some test of mere size? 

Opinion on this point has trended back and forth as the waves of apprehension over "bigness" in our economic life have crested and subsided. At one time we seem to view with equanimity and even pride what seems to us to be an efficient and great industrial concern. 

At another time size alone grows to be viewed with grave alarm and as calling for drastic action.

For a long time it was supposed that unless size were obtained or retained by an inherently illegal means or by an actual abuse of 18

overpowering strength in the competitive field, growth in size was not a violation of the law. It was supposed that a concern might engage in actively enlarging its market and the scope of its business, and go ahead by its efficiency, foresight, technical improvement, accumulation of its resources, and ability to attract additional resources, as far as these efforts could take it-and we had language from the Supreme Court which would seem to justify that concept. 

Now the Aluminum case looks the other way-even though a concern has exercised only that type of business energy and sound judgment which act by act is beyond reproach in seizing upon opportunities for the development of the size and scope of its business, apparently there may come a time when the courts will say to it: "You have carried on your proper effort to compete efficiently and decently too successfully. You now are able to get the public to give to you a percentage of their patronage which is such that, at some moment in your course, you became a criminal. You should not have entered all the doors of honest business opportunity that opened to you."

This is an issue which your Committee will undoubtedly have occasion to consider. 

2. Mere Size Not Unlawful in "Public" Businesses

In the regulated field of public businesses, where it may be intolerably inconvenient to have more than a single enterprise, it would seem clear that any limitations upon growth and size should be in the hands of the regulatory body. That seems to have been the philosophy of Congress by removing such matters from the control of the courts, as by the Transportation and Communication Acts, and placing the decision as to where the public interest lies in the hands of a regulatory body. 

3. Mere Size Not Unlawful in Labor and Agricultural Combinations and Buyers Cooperatives

Again for practical purposes, control over mere size of a labor unit contracting for the services of its members has been removed from the Anti-Trust Laws by statute and decisions, and is at present uncontrolled. The same also seems to be true as to the size of an agricultural products cooperative unit. 

There may be a question as to a limitation on size of a buyers' cooperative unit, but, aside possibly from the Associated Press case, it does not appear to have been raised or to be in the public mind. 

4. "Bigness" in Other Industrial Fields

As a practical matter, therefore, the issue now revolves around the situation in the remaining fields of production, service and distribution in "private" businesses. It seems to us highly important that that issue be examined by your Committee to see whether, as a practical matter, there is occasion for the enunciation of any rule 19

against mere growth in size in these fields; what, if any, that limitation should be; and, if any, how it should be administered. 

It is important, from the standpoint of the public, that there be a sober presentation of the facts and of the pros and cons on this issue, so that we may proceed with our economic life on a workable basis and without the constant disturbance of litigation and of extreme fears engendered in either the public or in the businessmen who are carrying on that life. What are some of the considerations that it might be useful to advert to in making this presentation? 

5. What do We Expect to Get Out of Freedom of Enterprise in 

"Private" Businesses, and Does "Bigness" Prevent Our Getting it? a. In General

In this connection we suggest some of our observations as matters for your consideration:

What is it we can legimately expect to get out of the healthy working of the competitive system in "private" businesses as distinguished from "public" businesses? (a) a display of initiative in reductions in cost and improvement of service and product and the providing of the desired supply to the buyers; (b) a resulting range of prices, by and large, reasonably satisfactory to seller and buyer a range that would ordinarily be likely to yield a very attractive profit to the exceptionally efficient and enterprising, some profit to the well-managed, and no profit or a loss to the inefficient. (As we shall later develop, in situations of acute contraction in demand in relation to capacity, the range is apt to drop down to or below the costs of even efficient sellers; in times of acute expansion of demand in relation to capacity, it is apt to rise to yield a high profit to even inefficient sellers.); and (c) the entry of new individuals or new groups of individuals into that business if they see a demand for additional or better products or services and consequent opportunities for initiative, energy and capital to secure profitable business. Of course, if we rely on freedom to secure these results, we know they will never be perfectly secured. They depend on the action and personal characteristics of a multitude of individuals engaged in this play of self-interest as individuals or as the working parts of combinations of individuals in cooperatives and corporations. 

We know that these individuals will be of varying intelligence, ambition, energy and ability, acting under widely varying circumstances over which they often have little or no control. The results will 20

never be the uniformly admirable results which some would turn to regimentation in the hope of securing. But is there any industry where the situation is substantially as discussed above, in which can it be said that the mere size of any unit or units engaged in it has the indicia or effect of undue restraint or "monopolization"?

It seems to us to be a desirable question for your Committee to examine, therefore, as to whether mere growth in size of a unit or units in any "private" business is now, as a practical matter, actually destroying or can be expected to destroy such a pattern. b. The influence of certain secondary competitive factors 

Further, with regard to mere "bigness" of a seller, there are certain secondary competitive factors which are very real in their effect on the actions and determinations of any such large concern in a particular industry and consequently on whether the public is in fact prevented by such size from getting the legitimately expected benefits of the competitive system. 

(1) The Potential Competitor 

First, there is the "potential competitor". Often it is not too far in the background to exert what is a practical and immediate competitive influence on the action of any such large concern. Any big concern knows that the organization of technology, research and information, coupled with managerial ability and resources, of almost any of the larger concerns in other industries is such that it can extend into a new field of business successfully and is likely to—if it is made attractive competitively to do so by the concerns in that industry not keeping up to concert pitch in satisfying their customers. 

(2) Marginal Overlapping and the Substitute Product 

Again, our observation of the actual workings of wide ranges of industry is that the actual play of competitive forces with respect to a particular industry is not confined to the activities of those directly engaged in buying and selling its particular product. The overlapping of use of almost every product with that of another, while often only marginal, we believe your examination will reveal, may be and quite generally is, an important competitive factor. 

Coal may react in such a marginal field to competition from oil, and cotton to rayon and nylon. Such reactions are particularly evident and far-reaching where, for practical purposes, there has to be but one price for a product, no matter to what use it is put. There the pressure of competition in the marginal fields may well influence and even limit the price of a product in areas of its use in which the other products could not be substituted. Inter-industry rivalry is as effectively "competition" so far as benefit and protections to the public are concerned as intra-industry rivalry. 21

(3) Competition for the Consumer Dollar 

Of course, there is always the very real competitive spur as between concerns engaged in industries that do not overlap, of competition for the consumer dollar. A marginal part of the business of every industry is dependent on its greater or less success in that competition. 

(4) Operating handicaps of Size 

You may find it an important element in your appraisal of the situation that while mere size has certain advantages, experience indicates that it is not without its inherent disadvantages. After all, a corporation is not a physical entity. In the complexity and scope of very large operations, all it has to depend on are the actions and abilities of the human beings who constitute its directing and working parts. At some point the law of diminishing returns is apt to set in in such large organizations. If its managers trust to mere size, resources and momentum, is it not again and again demonstrated that size in itself becomes paralyzing and the large concern of yesterday is at the mercy of smaller and growing competitors?

In large measure then is not size, so far as it presents a problem, apt to be its own corrective? (5) High price-low volume versus low price large-volume Another point which, from our close-up of the workings of industries, seems to be worth your examination, is the suggestion, sometimes made, that the vice attributed to monopolies of contenting themselves with relatively high prices, and consequent lower volume and higher costs, is a characteristic of "bigness" in the "private" businesses.

Does an examination of the facts bear this out, or is just the contrary the truth? With its large overhead and organization and extensive facilities, is not the pressure even stronger on the large concern to seek its profit in volume, with relatively lower prices and reduction in costs?

Further, it would seem to be the situation in any industry that no matter what might otherwise be the tendency of the other units, if one unit large or small adopts the large-volume, low-price, low-cost policy, the others are forced to conform to it. 

(6) Effect of Small Number of Units 

Another suggestion is that mere size destroys the competitive pattern where there are only a small number of concerns engaged in a given industry. Here again, the "proof of the pudding is in the eating." In these industries is the pattern outlined above in fact absent? Is there in fact enterprise and improvement in service and 22

in product, and a range of price reasonably satisfactory to buyer and seller? There may be, it is true, a greater tendency toward stability in such industries. In the competitive system is the socalled "jiggle" of market price of any substantial advantage to anyone but the speculator? There may be more cushioning in the range of prices on the downward side when demand contracts, and a less erratic rapid upward spiral when demand expands dramatically.

Perhaps the large size of units in an industry does have such a stabilizing effect, just as wages in industries in which there are large labor units, may not rise or fall so spectacularly with extreme changes in demand as they do in unorganized industries, as for example, personal service.

If new units appear to be slow to enter such industries, is it because scale, technological conditions, markets and the character of the product or service already being rendered make it inherently unattractive for venture capital to embark in a new unit? 

It is perhaps not surprising that the courts, so far, have been slow in yielding to atomization arguments based simply on the large size and small number of concerns in a particular industry. They appear to have recognized that the competitive system is fundamentally at work in such industries, that they are subject not only to the intrabut also the inter-industry competitive influences which have been mentioned above and by and large are giving the results to the public which we look for from the competitive system. 

(7) Size and Integration 

The question of the effect of mere size is also raised where the large unit integrates in its operations various stages of business activity which have been or sometimes are customarily carried on by different economic units. Of course, there is nothing in "integration" which is new or foreign to our economic life. Perhaps it finds its most extreme illustrations in fairly primitive forms of economic life where the processes, from the extraction or growing of the raw material down to the consumable product were carried on by the same family or economic unit.

The dairyman may buy from other producers all of his livestock and all the feed which they turn into milk; on the other hand he may breed his stock and raise their feed in an integrated operation. 

The question of how far integration will be carried is bound to vary with the nature of the particular industry and the genius of particular concerns. Where the economics are in favor of it, competition may compel it. For example, in the oil industry, in the old days of the 

Standard Oil Trust, production, refining and retailing were carried on by different concerns. It was the rather strong business tradition of the old Standard company to confine itself to the refining and wholesale marketing stage. But with the advent of some concerns whose business was based on production and who also proceeded to refine and then market their products right through to the gasoline 23

pump, their competition forced a change in the characteristic pattern of the business.

With regard to integration, here, as in other matters, it seems to be of the essence of the competitive system that there will be, from time to time, changes in the business structure. At times these changes in structure come in industries where the scale and technology are such that large aggregations of capital and manpower in particular units seem indicated by their economics and where the number of such concerns is relatively small. But, sweeping changes in practice, particularly as to the integration of different stages of production and marketing, can come very swiftly under particular pressures in industries, such as the textile, where there is a very considerable number of units and the proportion of business done by even the largest unit is a very minor one.

In all such changes, as generally in the operation of the competitive system, there are individual hardships and losses which, to those who sustain them, seem like individual "injuries". The objections to and hardships to others from integration may be present where the integration is carried on by a small concern as well as by a large one-but the present issue in law and in the courts, seems to be tied in with size.

If there are to be limitations on integration, are they to be imposed merely by reason of size, on economic units in industries where the pattern of results is that to be expected from a competitive industry as outlined above? If any such limitations are to be imposed, what should be the tests for their application? (8) Size and diversified business Another issue which is being raised at the moment is whether the Anti-Trust Laws impose limitations on the variety of business activities in which a single economic unit can engage. Historically there seems to have been no limitation on the number and variety of private businesses which an individual or a group of individuals, acting through the corporate form, could engage in. 

Here again, the practical issue now raised before the courts and the public is not as to whether a limitation should be imposed. generally on one economic unit being engaged in more than one variety of business, but whether, by reason of size, such a limitation of freedom of action should be imposed on the very large. Of course if that concern, in any branch of its activities, is violating any of the "don'ts" of the Anti-Trust Laws, it is responsible for that violation. 

It can be enjoined from a continuance of that violation. The same would be true if it engaged in a connected violation by the interplay of two or more of its activities.

But the bald question which here seems to be presented is whether, because of its size, a large unit should be forbidden to engage in more than one type of business, or forbidden to supply the needs of one of its activities with the product of another. Inherently, is the 24

situation different from that of a farmer supplying his needs as a dairyman from his activities as a raiser of corn? It may or may not give him a competitive advantage over a dairyman who buys his feed. A change from the purchase of his supply to growing it may not be comfortable for the previous supplier. The truth is the adjustments in a free economy are not without inherent hardships. 

Can they be eradicated except by a regimentation which appears to have some disadvantages of its own? In the case of a large economic unit, the transaction should not be obscured in our minds by the fact that different subsidiary artificial corporate entities are used for different activities or by the fact that there are separate operating and accounting systems for different divisions and departments of a single concern.

Here again, it is suggested for your Committee's consideration that it would be extremely useful to the public to have the results of your examination, so as to see whether mere size in situations of this description warrants a restriction on freedom. Are such concerns, engaged in a variety of businesses, in fact conducting them in a way that is consistent with the competitive pattern and getting the type of result which we have suggested above as the test as to whether there is or is not ground, on balance, for new limitations on freedom? 

If, in the view of your Committee, there is the necessity for such limitation, then how can it be practically expressed-so that a concern may know when it is opening one door too many of honest business opportunity and is engaging in a crime? (9) The effect of size on freedom of opportunity to engage in business One real or supposed effect of size which very much troubles the public mind and evidently gets into the thinking of the Congress and the Courts, is whether mere size of economic units in many industries is closing the door on traditional American individual opportunity and enterprise perhaps also whether the aggregation of personnel and resources in such large units is consistent with our political system. 

Looking at the economic side, of course, it is true that if there are a small number of large units in an industry there is less room for a large number of small units. But how far does this limit the actual opportunity for economic and social development of the individual American? (a) Participation in investment

From the standpoint of the actual opportunity for the American individual to make an investment of his property and savings in a wide variety of businesses, it has probably never been so great. He can, on the open market, commit what he has-in large or small amounts to participation in almost any field of large business enterprises through stock ownership, and share in the vicissitudes of such industries. Of course this does not carry with it the training and experience of running a business. 25

But in these large-scale businesses, which are the necessary result of our technological and economic system and the amount of capital they require, there is coming to be a separation between ownership and management. It is only in the professional, the agricultural, certain service and certain distributive phases of our economic life and now and then in a new industry that a man can count on having what we have traditionally regarded as the enriching and developing experience of paddling his own economic canoe. But in those fields there still and are likely to remain so far as we can see many millions of such opportunities. 

In those industries in which there are very large units, for technological or other economic reasons, the scale of operations to get the desired modern economic results is such that while perhaps there might be some larger number of units, there could not be any such substantially larger number, under our present technological and economic conditions in those industries, as to be important from this standpoint of individual opportunity to run such a business. (b) Field of opportunity for managerial positions In the large scale industries there is developing a wide and open field for responsibility and initiative in management, whether at the center or on the periphery. As the scale of the business increases, if it is to succeed, there has to be a degree of decentralization in management and operation which affords a multitude of positions in which the individual can get the training and development of both responsible decision and opportunity for initiative and leadership. As a result has there not been in the last fifty years a development of a professional spirit and rivalry in management at all levels which may be a psychological equivalent for the developing effects of an owner-operator situation?

Further, from our observation, the competitive spirit and rivalry in this professional managerial class often furnish to the large units in large-scale industry at least as much of the dynamic urge toward betterment of product and service, reduction of costs and keenness to procure volume, as we look for in a small-scale industry through the existence of a large number of individually operated and owned units. 

It must be remembered that in this field it is the able, the skillful and the energetic who are sought for as managers. Perhaps this and the large resources at their command account for what seems to have been the tremendous economic development of this country and the improvement in product and services and in lowering of costs, which has taken place coincident with a change from the owneroperator-management of relatively small units, to the operation by professional managers of relatively large single units in our present large-scale industries.

It would be interesting and important for the public to have a realistic appraisal of this situation by your Committee, and it might 26

well have a bearing on the issue of whether or not mere size in largescale industry is in itself depriving Americans of opportunity for the economic and individual development vital to the country's needs. 

When we approach this matter, as almost all of us do, with a certain sense of apprehension as to size, are we thinking too much in the terms of the economic and social life of the preindustrial system, rather than in the light of the inevitable consequences of having adopted that system?

Perhaps we are too glib in saying that mere size in large-scale industries is threatening to close the door on American opportunity. 

Certainly the results so far, important in peace and vitally important in a cold war and in any shooting one, of the effect of size seems to indicate that individual Americans are functioning with their traditional initiative and vigor in the radical changes wrought in our economy during the last fifty years. (c) Political and social consequences As to the political and social consequences of size, as distinguished from economic, it is our observation that an increasing sense of trusteeship and decency in public relations and in employee relations has accompanied the growth of large-scale operations. Can it fairly be said that there has been any increasing tendency to use economic size in the private businesses for political purposes? Has not the tendency been the other way over the last fifty years? c. Possible limitations on mere size 

If your committee should be of opinion that there is occasion for some limitations on freedom of growth on the basis of mere size what should the test of any such limitation be? (1) Percentage of "control"

There have been suggestions in the cases that an arbitrary test of a concern being able to secure a certain percentage of the patronage of buyers would be desirable. There are two things about this proposal which, from our observation, may be significant: 

The first is a matter of semantics. The courts, and even the public, are apt to use a word of double meaning, such as "control", argumentatively in a way which is not, from our observation, realistic. 

If a concern is able to induce the public to make 40, 60, 80 or 90 percent of its purchases from that concern, the concern is loosely said to "control" 40, 60, 80 or 90 percent of the business. Then taking "control" in its sense of having "exclusive power over” it is argued that such a percentage of the business so controlled means "monopolization". As a matter of business fact, the extent of public choice of the products of one concern at one time by no means assures 27

any such extent of choice or any arbitrary power to compel that extent of choice in the future.

This may very well have been in the mind of the Court when, in the Quaker Oats case, it held that the fact that the company might be expected to secure 60% of the purchases of breakfast cereals by the public, created in no real sense any "monopolistic control" either of the industry as a whole or that percentage of its market. The rationale of the decision seemed to be that the taste of the public in breakfast foods cannot be "controlled" by the mere size of a purveyor. The only way it can expect to continue to succeed in being able to induce the public to buy that much is by continuing to make its products, price-wise and quality-wise, that attractive to the purchasers. 

What are the facts of the situation? Will your examination show that historically and under present conditions a concern can in fact treat any large percentage of the business it has been able to secure as its private domain and those operating it as freed from the rein and spur of competitive influences? Or will such examination show that in fact in such situations such larger percentages grow and shrink under those influences; that large concerns are apt to be able to hold on to them only by their eyeteeth and by constant competitive effort to improve product and lower cost; that if they relax in these efforts and seek to coast on acquired good will and customer acceptance they are apt to be easy pickings for far smaller up-andcoming concerns. Our opportunities for observation of the business facts in a wide variety of industries indicates grounds for a serious question of the easy assumption that a mere high percentage of customer preference is the equivalent of "control" of that business in any sense significant under the Anti-Trust Laws. Any such assumption is certainly a far cry from the ordinary day to day workings of the mind and strenuous efforts of the professional managers of large enterprises in "private" businesses, as we observe them. They certainly would expect a rude jolt and a likelihood of finding themselves out of a job if they acted on it. 

If a limitation on growth based on mere size were imposed and any such "percentage" test were adopted it could hardly be regarded as applicable across the board. The factual situations in different industries are too different. (2) "Power" There runs through the discussions of a limitation on the basis of mere size the instinctive apprehension roused among free peoples by the very word "power" and our instinctive association of it with size. May a limitation on mere size as a practical matter be based on "power". By "power" we mean here not the actual grasping after it and oppressive use of it, which is of course itself unlawful, as pointed out above, but just the potentiality of its misuse, which inspires our fears. Before we yield too readily to those fears there are perhaps some things we should consider.

TIL 28

(a) "Freedom" and "Security" The price we pay for freedom is always some danger of its misuse. "Security" against a violation of the law whether of the Anti-Trust Laws or murder, rape, theft and fraud could only be secured by locking up all those having the potential "power" to commit them. 

Few of us would be left at large. The potential "power" to violate the Anti-Trust Laws is not confined to large concerns. Very small ones have and do violate them singly or in combinations which may have as much "power" as the largest of units. Can we say that there is so much more intrinsic likelihood of their successful violation by the large that we should lock them up or execute them in advance of such a violation? Are we distrustful of ourselves and of the will or courage of our Courts or juries to deal with an alleged violation merely because of the large size of the defendant? Perhaps experience is rather that in any such prosecutions the large concern is apt to go into court with two strikes against it. It is hardly by taking counsel of its fears that America has obtained the benefits it has from the competitive "private" businesses. 

(b) The Tyranny of the Corporate Fiction 

In these discussions of "bigness" do we lose sight of the actual nature of a large concern? Does our imagination trick us into thinking of such an artificial creation of the fictions of the law as in itself some actual impersonal super being? Instead of course the fact is that such a concern is merely a collection of a multitude of owners and a number of managers-all human beings like the rest of us and subject to the same workings of the mind as to obedience to the law and running the business. (c) Gulliver and the Liliputians 

Is there a reason why mere size may not be too impressive? Those running a large concern in an industry are not entirely unlike 

Gulliver among the Liliputians. Gulliver did wake up to find himself bound by tiny but effective cords. He found his body smarting to slings and arrows loosed by those so much less impressive in bulk. 

Large concerns are themselves painfully aware, from our observation, how small a percentage of the supply can determine a downward course of the market and hold it there against the Gullivers. As an illustration-in the '30s the "dumping" of two cargoes of Russian gasoline at a Lake port forced down the prevailing price in a number of populous States to irrationally low figures. (d) Psychological limitations on the "Big"

Another very practical consideration, which we have observed, that perhaps should be taken account of in appraising apprehensions with regard to mere size in this : 29

Very large concerns in these days are rarely, if ever, psychologically capable of taking "the public be damned" attitude attributed, rightly or wrongly, to many of their predecessors in the last century. 

Standards of conduct toward competitors and toward their own customers, imposed by public opinion, bind about their limbs, which look so muscular and powerful, with intangible but perhaps more effective restraints than statutes can impose. Certainly as a practical matter and perhaps as a matter of law the very circumstances of their size are apt to make unavailable to them certain ordinary competitive practices highly effective for a small concern but unsuited to a large one because of breadth of customer relations or because the use by them might appear or actually be oppressive. 

We suggest that the realities of the situation should be looked at to determine whether in fact in our industrial life mere size does actually threaten an abusive exercise of such power as may flow from it and whether present experience indicates that there is much more than a theoretical possibility of abuse from mere size. On analysis, will you conclude that the "horrible examples" which are sometimes cited are characteristic? Or are they sporadic? Or on impartial examination do many of them prove not to be so "horrible" after all? 

(3) Factual Approach 

If there were found to be occasion for some limitation to be imposed based on mere size, would there be a better test for its application in a particular industry than to determine, from an examination of the business facts, such as Brandeis was accustomed to make and advocate, whether such mere size in fact works a substantial injury to the public interest by actually preventing the public from receiving the legitimate benefits of the competitive system, as outlined above, in that particular industry? 

6. Growth by Acquisition 

We have considered the prohibitions of the Act against a single unit promoting its business by the employment of certain business practices which are thus made unlawful. We have considered whether there are limitations on the freedom of such a unit to grow by otherwise lawful means through its mere attainment of size. Is such a unit foreclosed from growing by the consolidation with or acquisition of the property and good will of a rival in its particular business?

The first issue, here, is whether the resulting elimination of a "competitor" unit is in itself a forbidden combination in restraint of trade. In the American Tobacco case in the lower court (in the 

Second Circuit), Judge Lacombe felt obliged by language, if not 30

decision, of the Supreme Court, to hold that any such method of growth was per se a prohibited restraint. 

It has

This view and the arguments of the Department of Justice in its support, were expressly rejected by the Supreme Court. never since been accepted by a majority of that Court. 

The Congress, in the Clayton Act, provided what on its face looked like a fairly flat limitation on a particular method of acquisition of the business and property of a competitor, namely, by the purchase and holding of its stock. But the Supreme Court adopted a liberal interpretation of this statute. Business growth by such means is not in itself unlawful. The elimination of a "competitor" unit is not in itself an unlawful restraint. The whole picture in the industry must be looked at to see whether there is intended or there is in fact an effect on other competitors and the public which would bring the transaction within the prohibitions of the statute against "monopolization" and "attempts to monopolize".

There is a current effort in Congress to "close the gap", by extending the provisions of the Clayton Act to the acquisition of assets of a competitor. If the Supreme Court should apply to this amplified provision the same construction which it has heretofore applied to the stock acquisition provision, which it is supposed to supplement, there would seem to be involved no radical change from the effect of the present provisions of the Sherman Act as to acquisition of the assets or stock of a competitor. Whether this would or would not be a desirable result seems an important issue for your Committee to consider and express an opinion.

With regard to acquisitions of the stock or assets of a competitor there have arisen under the Sherman Law, interpreted as indicated above, "fringe" cases. In these there have been, and are likely to be, differences of opinion as to the facts and inferences from the facts. They also point up the practical question as to what are fundamental indicia as to whether a particular acquisition constitutes a "monopolization", actual or attempted.

Of course, if the competitor is being bludgeoned into going out of business by unfair methods, the whole transaction would be unlawful by reason of the character of those methods. The limitation on the acquisitions, if any, would seem to be upon the concern which remains in business and seeks to acquire the assets. There has as yet been no suggestion that there is any obligation on those who engage in "private" businesses to continue their investment or activities. 

If the acquisition is being made as a step toward acquiring the entire business of an industry, it has the indicia of "monopolization." 

But what if it will merely provide additional facilities or good will desirable for the business development of the acquiring concern? 

At present this would not seem to be forbidden unless it would destroy that pattern characteristic of a competitive industry, referred 31

to above. The issues and discussions above with reference to the effect of mere size seem pertinent here. 

But it is obvious that any substantial increase in size through acquisition by an already large unit will and should be closely scrutinized. Is it really merely a normal development of the business of the acquiring concern that will not threaten the competitive pattern of the industry?

In spite of the fact that it is well settled law and business practice that the acquisition of the business of a competitor unit is not per se forbidden, there is perhaps still in the public mind an issue as to whether it should not be so forbidden-irrespective of any showing of actual or prospective injury to the public. It seems to us it would be helpful for your Committee to consider that issue, elucidate for the public the various considerations, pro and con, and give some expression of its conclusions.

III. PROBLEMS OF JOINT ACTION OF BUSINESS UNITS UNDER THE ANTI-TRUST LAWS A. THE CONTRAST IN BASIC APPROACH 

We have dealt so far with limitations imposed by the Anti-Trust Laws on the freedom of action by single economic units, large or small, acting independently, and have outlined certain of the issues as to such limitations which are now very much in the public or 

Congressional mind.

We turn now to the vital range of issues as to joint economic action by two or more economic units in matters which they deem to be of common interest. This of course applies equally to units which are competitors in buying, as to those which are competitors in selling. 

In the various aspects of this field there have been wide variances and fluctuations in the points of view of the public, the courts and the Congress. There have been two main schools of thought among judges and among administrators of the Anti-Trust Laws as to their interpretation and practical application. There is what might be called the school of Peckham, Harlan, McReynolds and Daugherty, which, over a period of years, tended to take the flat position that joint action by competitors was inherently inconsistent with the competitive system and was flatly prohibited. They in effect read into the language of the Sherman Law the words "joint action by competitors" in lieu of the words of art, "restraint of trade". Broadly they took the view that any agreed-on common course of action among competitors in their business was an unlawful "restraint", whether among buyers or among sellers.

The most extreme exponents of this branch of thought in the courts and in the administration of the application of the laws were 32

probably McReynolds (both as Attorney General and as a Justice of the Supreme Court), and Daugherty, as Attorney General. It has been said of the "extremists" that their conception of competition was that of a "fight to the death with knives in a dark room.' 

On the other side of the picture were White, Holmes, Brandeis, Hughes and Roberts. This school of thought has expressed the point of view that there are wide areas of circumstances in which joint action by competitors, whether of buyers or sellers, was not only consistent with the operation of the competitive system in an industry, but useful to its successful workings and consistent with and promotive of the public interest. 

The pendulum of interpretation and administration has swung back and forth over the period of fifty years. We will not attempt to align those who are now members of the court or engaged in the administration of the laws with the historic approaches outlined above. But it is clear that during the last fifteen years the pendulum has swung far in the direction of prohibition of any joint action whatever.

Congress has from time to time felt it necessary to mitigate the rigors of the extreme construction by providing for exemption from such an application of the Anti-Trust Laws in various areas, where, in its view, such restriction had gone or was likely to go so far as to be impractical, from the standpoint of both private and public interest. This has been particularly so as to combinations of employees in contracting for services, in the field of agriculture and agricultural products, in communications and in transportation. 

We shall first discuss certain of the less controversial situations. We shall then discuss our observations of certain inherent characteristics of the workings of competition which make the questions involved in the restriction of joint action both of extreme difficulty and of prime importance. As to this it has seemed to us that there should be far more understanding by the public of the issues involved and of the balance of pro and con considerations than has been evident. 

It is our belief that your Committee should be able, by presenting a fundamental analysis, to aid materially in this situation, and perhaps to suggest practical measures to deal with it. 

B. SPECIFIC APPLICATIONS 

1.

Joint action of some or all competitors of an industry in technical development There are no cases which expressly forbid the undertaking of joint effort by competitors in technical matters which concern their industry, whether engineering, accounting or marketing techniques.

There is a school of thought, stemming from Adam Smith's famous aphorism, which seems to regard any occasion for competitors to meet as in itself suspicious and dangerous and such thinking has been 33

reflected in governmental attitudes toward certain joint research projects. But apart from that, joint action, such as that above, while likely to alter to some extent the business conduct of each competitor by removing, for example, the competitive pressure on each to do his own techcnological research and development, if properly conducted, should not be regarded as questionable. 

From the standpoint of the small concern, in contra-distinction to the large, the effect of any such restraint on freedom of such joint action would be particularly adverse. Large economic units can of themselves more readily assume the financial burdens of such an activity. Small concerns can to advantage pool their endeavors. 

While the results, being available to all those who are acting jointly, are not likely to effect the competitive position of each as against the other, they may be quite vitally important in a broader or more vital field of competition with other industries making substitute products.

Further, such action would seem to tend definitely toward the public interest by procuring for the public better products or services at lower cost. 

2. Joint action by a group of competitors who might lawfully be consolidated or whose business might lawfully be acquired by one of their number It may well be that a group of competitors, particularly small concerns, may find it in the interest of economy and in their interindustry competition, to treat their businesses as one for other purposes besides technological research and development. For example, they may desire to market their products with one marketing organization and a single determination as to whether to sell or not to sell and on what terms. Or, conversely, a group of concerns on the buying side may desire to have a common purchasing agent to seek out supplies, test the market and bargain for them.

At times it has been argued that the mere fact of the joint character of this action by competitors rendered it unlawful even though the same group might consolidate their business or one of their number might lawfully acquire the assets and business of all others.

The lower court in the Appalachian Coals case expressed that view. 

The Supreme Court reversed that decision on that among other grounds. The Appalachian Coals case does not seem to have been. subsequently overruled as to this part of its holding. But there still seems to be a very real issue on this point in the minds of those who are administering the law and perhaps of Congress and the courts. 

For that reason it would seem to us highly desirable for your Committee to consider this issue and express its views. Here again the ability for small-scale competitors to act jointly in certain phases of their business may be important in making it possible for them to compete economically and to preserve their identity and initiative in the other phases. 34

3. Joint collection and dissemination of marketing information by competitors, whether buyers or sellers

Probably no one other than Attorney General Daugherty has ever gone the full length of holding that such joint action was in itself unlawful, though Justice McReynolds and a one-time majority of the Supreme Court seemed to regard it as the kind of thing which genuine competitors just would not do. However, the minority of the Court in the Lumber case became the majority in the Cement and Maple Flooring cases, and Justice Stone emphasized the importance to the useful workings of the competitive system of competitors having full information as to current market factors.

Justice Brandeis had expressed, in his dissent in the Lumber case, the great importance, as he saw it, that small concerns should have freedom of joint action of this nature. Even the somewhat blatent exhortation and advice of the particular trade executive in that case as to how the information should be used did not distract his attention from what he regarded as the essential importance of knowledge of the market.

There have been some specific limitations by the decisions on the time and manner of collection and exchange of current market information, but there has been only an indirect challenge of the principles laid down by Justice Stone. The broad language of the 

Supreme Court in the Socony-Vacuum case, however, and in some others, may indicate that there is still an issue and a vitally important issue for your consideration here.

There in sweeping language the court declared that any joint action by competitors which may affect market price is per se forbidden. On the other hand, one of the arguments which Justice 

Stone had used in supporting freedom of the joint action of competitors in the collection and dissemination of market information was that it would tend to affect price, namely, by stabilizing it. 

Whether or not the Anti-Trust Laws should restrict this freedom of action is again an issue which your Committee may well desire to examine. 

4. Joint action by sellers or by buyers to force market price above or below a normal competitive range by the exercise of their collective power There never seems to have been, and is not now, any issue that even under the most liberal interpretation of the "rule of reason" such joint action constitutes an unreasonable restraint of trade and is therefore unlawful in areas to which the Sherman Law applies. 

Nor has there been any question that indirect joint methods when intended and likely to produce the same result, as by limitation on or allocation of production or markets on the part of the sellers, or limitation or allocation of buying power on the part of the buyers, are equally unreasonable restraints of trade. 35

The same would appear to be true of joint action on either side of the market as to particular components of price if calculated to have the similar result of forcing market prices to levels below or above those of a normal competitive range; also of joint effort to force changes in customary methods of buying or selling, and practices in connection with the same, if they have the same intent and probable effect. 

5. Joint action by buyers or sellers to protect against or ameliorate the effects of a range of prices markedly above or markedly below the normal competitive range; and such joint action to change customs and practices in the trade, whether among buyers or sellers, or in contracts between them, which are no longer appropriate or might be deemed to constitute "competitive abuses."

It is to be noted that the purpose and also the effects of such efforts, if they do not go beyond the purpose, is directly opposite to the clearly unlawful action forcing abnormally high or abnormally low prices by joint action.

It is in this field that there has been the widest diversity of opinion among judges, among administrators of the law, in the Congress, and among the public. The pendulum of interpretation has swung back and forth. The majority of the Supreme Court has become a minority, and then a majority, and then again a minority and then again a majority on certain of these issues. 

The administrators of the law have at times sought to apply the law in such a way as to prevent all such action. At times they have encouraged certain phases of it. 

Congress has at times acted specifically to permit such action, and the public has been of varying mind. That Perhaps the most fundamental and farreaching of the issues before your Committee are connected with these situations. An examination and elucidation by your Committee of their pros and cons would seem to be an essential contribution you can make. consideration will require of you a full analysis and a square facing up to certain fundamental characteristics of the workings of the competitive system.

Certain of those characteristics, which we will proceed to analyze, unhappily, are by no means pleasant ones. From our observation, we are all of us too much inclined to take the easy way and to ignore the existence of the disagreeable except in times of major national crises. Any conclusions arrived at as to these issues, to carry conviction, must, however, evidence, as we see it, a recognition of these facts and of a balanced judgment as a result of their survey. 

We are, therefore, now at the heart of certain of the major controversies which have raged over the Sherman Law, and of major problems which arise in connection with their application, observance and enforcement.

36

C. THE COMPETITIVE SYSTEM `HAS INHERENT CHARACTERISTICS WHICH, UNDER CERTAIN CIR- CUMSTANCES, WILL CAUSE ITS OPERATION TO PRODUCE EFFECTS WE FEAR FROM MONOPOLI- ZATION AND CAUSE ITS OTHERWISE BENEFICIAL OPERATION TO BECOME DESTRUCTIVE OF THE PUBLIC INTEREST 

We have outlined above the beneficial effects which we expect the operation of the competitive system to secure. It is because of these beneficial results that the public has strongly preferred the freedom in "private" businesses to the imposed regulatory regimentation which appears to be a necessary incident to "public" businesses. 

Constantly improving techniques and products, progressive lowering of costs, and a range of prices that, with a margin of profit, bears some reasonable relation to the cost of production, are the things we want out of the competitive system. They would seem to afford, as we have suggested, satisfactory tests as to whether or not it is functioning beneficially to the public. 

But observation and a survey of our economic history immediately indicates that these beneficial effects are the result of the successful dual functioning of competitive influences on the market. There must be competitive influences operating on the buyers; there must be competitive influences operating on the sellers, for the results we desire from the competitive system to be obtained. 

The reason we object to a monopoly in buying, or in selling, is not because as a result there will be no competition in the market. There may be plenty of competition in the market where there is a monopoly in buying power-but it will be the hyper-active competition among sellers. Or, in converse, if there is a monopoly on the other side of the market and production and selling is channeled through a single hand, then again there may still be active and hyper-active competition in the market, but it will all be among the buyers in satisfying their needs. In either case the price would be apt to have no reasonable relation to cost of production. Instead it would tend to be as low or high a price as could be forced by monopoly on one side and the panic play of competition on the other side of the market. 

It is a commonplace that for practical purposes we have this undesirable situation whenever available capacity and effective demand get sharply out of balance. Then the competitive system loses its balance. If the balance is off on the scarcity side, then the competitive pressure is on the buyers to secure products for their needs. If the balance is the other way, then the competitive pressure on sellers to use their capacity and dispose of their products, becomes hyper-active. Either the buyers or the sellers, as the case may be, lie back while the competitors on the other side of the market bid up the price among themselves to higher and higher levels, or bid it down among themselves to lower and lower levels, neither 37

level having any reasonable relation to cost of production and ordinary margin of profit.

At almost all times there are some industries which are experiencing the results of this characteristic of the competitive system. Sharp interferences with production or accelerations of demand subject the buyers to abnormal competitive pressure. On the other side, in the chronically depressed industries, capacity has outrun and remains chronically far in excess of available demand. Sellers are chronically under an abnormal degree of competitive pressure. In both situations any reasonable degree of equality of bargaining power is lost. The disastrous results are painful enough to the underdog in either situation. The general public, however, is apt to ignore these situations-many of them reasonably rapidly self-corrective--and to forget the existence of this uncomfortable characteristic of the competitive system. Ordinarily we accept the anomolous results as temporary and a price we can well pay for the benefits of freedom. 

But there come situations when the public, as a whole, is jolted into the consciousness of this characteristic and of the havoc it is capable of playing with our accustomed patterns of industry and life. 

We must all remember having gone through two periods in the last twenty years in which the devastating nature of this inherent characteristic of the competitive system was dramatically evident. 

First, illustrating one aspect of how imbalance works, was the Great Depression. During that period the available capacity in industries very generally went completely out of balance with the available demand, and remained so over a period of years. As a result, in market after market, competition among buyers slowed up to not much more than a pulse-beat. On the other hand the producers and sellers, under abnormal competitive pressure, each engaged in the vain effort to secure an amount of business which would enable each to run something like full and keep down overhead and were offering lower and power prices, and seeking to force down costs by lower and lower wages. Prices tended to fall to levels where no funds or credit would be available for research, development or capital improvements. Ironically, instead of being checked by the unlimited play of competition, this spiral was in fact accentuated by it the activity of that competition being all on one side of the market. There was a contraction of our economic life to a point where the enormous economic waste of unemployment rose to appalling figures and seemed to be becoming chronic. 

On the other hand, we had the period beginning with the great war in Europe where there was a converse situation,-where available capacity to meet civilian needs was held down and was relatively completely outrun by available demand. Then we had the experience of competition among sellers in turn slowing down to not much more than a pulse-beat, while prices tended to be bid up by buyers, to ranges which had no relation to normal competitive levels. 

A formal, actual monopoly among the buyers would hardly have had the temerity to force prices down to levels as low as those which 388

unbalanced competition carried them in the Great Depression. In fact any actual monopolistic concern on the buying side would presumably have had more intelligence than to do so. On the other hand, no monopoly of sellers would ever have had the temerity, or probably the unintelligence, to force prices up to the astronomical levels which they would have attained but for certain public action that was taken during the war period. 

This same inherent characteristic of the competitive system shows itself not merely in the terms of ultimate price, but it is apt to be illustrated by the adoption of methods of business in competition which, in the situation of a normal balanced competition would not be regarded as sensible or decent. On the selling side there is a strong tendency to obtain business by secret rebates, deceitful practices, terms of contracts which are unconscionable and unintelligent as between buyer and seller, give-away programs, and a whole crop of such devices.

Further, it is characteristic of the competitive system that the majority in these situations is, for practical purposes, forced to follow the example of the minority-and a very small minority-in the use and adoption of such practices.

Also, no matter how outworn a custom of the trade may be, or how fundamentally unfair it may be as between different classes of purchasers, while a very small unit or number of units can force a departure from reasonable or sensible practices, only a very great majority of the industry as a whole can take any effective steps to improve such customary methods of doing business. 

D. METHODS OF DEALING WITH UNBALANCED COMPETITION AND ITS RESULTS 

We have all too recently been through the practical experience of the existence and results of the characteristics of the competitive system in "private" businesses just outlined, to have any question of either their reality or the disaster that can flow from them. 

They pose a practical question with which government and business have had to deal to a greater or less extent through the whole of the existence of the Anti-Trust Laws, and which will have to be dealt with in the future. 

What are the attempts which have been made to deal with it? 

What light do they throw on the conclusions your Committee should reach and recommendations you should make? 

1. Joint efforts by competitors

For anyone who has had occasion to go through the files of concerns in a highly competitive industry over a long period of years, as some of us have, an interesting correlation is revealed. They will conclude that by ascertaining the dates when there had developed an acute imbalance between available capacity and effective 39

demand, with the results on the market indicated above, examination of the files would be apt to show that coincidently there would be an eruption of intra-office memoranda of the various concerns as to the necessity of "getting together with their competitors", finding some way of bringing the use of their respective capacities into some reasonable balance with the available demand, or "of setting some floor on prices" which had some relationship to cost of production, or otherwise taking some other defensive joint action to minimize the disastrous results of the breaking down on one side or the other of the market of a reasonably balanced competition. 

It is at such times that there appears the classic suggestion,subsequently appearing in litigation-that "jail may be preferable to bankruptcy". Coincidently the files or the personal recollections of the actors in the situation will show efforts to get together with competitors, usually abortive, "to do something about it". And the same files may well show formal or informal engagements to participate in such joint action-which the production and accounting records of the concern will usually show, under the terrific pressure of this unbalanced competition, to have been honored more in the breach than in the observance. 

It is only in the chronically depressed industries that there would be apt to be disclosed in the files any chronic or habitual efforts along these lines.

On the other side of the market-among buyers-when the competition is all among them in their bids against each other, there may be "buyers' strikes", organized or unorganized, or informal arrangements among the purchasing agents as to price to be offered, or allocations of their buying, which they may feel to be protective. 

There is also the tendency to deal with this situation by attempted self-denying ordinances among competitors that they will not engage in certain methods of obtaining business which are within the range of what the Federal Trade Commission and the Supreme Court at various times have described as "competitive abuses"-undesirable from the standpoint of the "sound functioning" of the competitive system.

Further, in less extreme situations, but still influenced by the same characteristic of the competitive system, there have been joint attempts to revise the settled customs of the trade to bring them in line with changes in the business facts of an industry. 

For example, there may have grown up a classification of products as to standards of size and quality, which have become impracticable to change because of both producer and customer habits-unless there is a substantial uniformity of action by all in that change. 

Again, there may have been customer differentials in the pricing of different sizes and shapes of the same product which again have become unrealistic in changed conditions or with changed techniques, and which from the standpoint of neither customer nor producer are for practical purposes susceptible of change unless that change is made throughout the industry. 40

Again, there may be customary differentials in price in the sale of products to different stages of distribution to allow for services performed at an intermediate stage. Those ingrained customs are difficult, both from customer and seller standpoint, to change without joint action.

As to such trade practices then, there are, first, attempts made to prevent their deterioration in character as a result of all being under the pressures of unbalanced competition and being as a practical matter compelled to follow the foolish and unsound action of a single competitor or minority of competitors; and, second, other efforts to accomplish affirmative improvements in practice which cannot be made unless there is general affirmative action. 

The alternative to joint action by competitors, if any attempt is to be made to deal with the destructive results of these characteristics of competition, appears to be some participation in the matter by public authority. That might range anywhere from a mere requirement of information as to such voluntary joint action of competitors to some public authority, to actual prescription by public authority of what individual units must do or must not do in these situations. 

2. Action of the courts, administrative bodies and the Congress in dealing with efforts to prevent or ameliorate the undesirable effects of certain characteristic difficulties in the functioning of the competitive system

As to the Sherman Law there is, as has been indicated, an extreme view under which all such activities by competitors are anathema. 

It would give effect to the aphorism of Adam Smith to which we have referred, that competitors cannot meet even socially without the danger of injury to the public by their tampering with the competitive system.

There is an opposite view: that in our complex modern economic life the matter is not so simple; that within the framework of the competitive system there may be activities of competitors which, instead of destroying it, may help us secure the real benefits we expect from it. Efforts which are merely directed toward bringing prices which are so high or so low as to have no reasonable relation to the normal competitive price when the effects of the duality of competition are reasonably in balance, is just the opposite in intent and purpose from monopolistic efforts to force prices up or down to such unreasonable and undesirable levels. 

The matter early came before the Supreme Court through the situation among the carriers. The development of the railroads had been perhaps over-rapid. From the very nature of that business, rates between common points had to be the same unless a lower rate were concealed by rebates. Rate wars, under the stress of one sided competition among the sellers, emphasized by a capacity for transportation which available demand would not absorb, produced a fantastic situation disastrous to all but a favored few shippers. Joint 41

consideration of rates and traffic problems appeared to be a necessary defensive measure against what were felt to be the disastrous results of the unbalanced competitive situation then prevailing. 

In a five-to-four decision the issue was clearly drawn in the Supreme Court, between a majority which appeared to believe that any joint action as to their business by competitors was an unlawful restraint of trade, led by Justice Peckham, and the minority, led by the then Justice White, who took the position that unless the rates and practices proposed by the joint action of the competitors were unreasonable, there was no injury to the public, and consequently there was no unreasonable restraint of competition and therefore no unlawful restraint of trade.

The extreme position which Judge Lacombe in the American Tobacco case felt constrained to take by reason of majority opinion of the Supreme Court, came before the Supreme Court in the Standard Oil and Tobacco cases. The members of the old minority now became the majority. They rejected that position and instead enunciated the "rule of reason". The members of the former majority became a vigorously dissenting minority. 

As a result of this opinion, Judge Lacombe and the Circuit Court, in the Hamburg-American case, held that the "Conference system", in arriving at joint decisions among competitors as to the conduct of their business, which had grown out of the terrific rate wars occasioned by overcapacity in the shipping industry, was lawful,-so long as the results, in service and rates to the public, did not inflict an injury on the public through being inadequate or unreasonable as a result of the agreement. The Government's appeal to the 

Supreme Court in this case was dismissed as moot because of the 

First World War. 

Congress, however, took a hand in the matter. As a result of a lengthy investigation of the workings and characteristics of the shipping industry, it provided that if such agreements were filed with the Maritime Commission and were not found by it to be against the public interest, they were not subject to questions under the Anti-Trust Laws.

In rail transportation, with the conferring of express power of regulation of rates upon the Interstate Commerce Commission, the carriers followed the custom of the initiation of such rates by joint action. It was effective in the absence of contrary determinations by the Interstate Commerce Commission. 

Express joint agreements by carriers as to a wide variety of matters of common interest in the conduct of their respective businesses, were expressly authorized by the Transportation Act of 1920 when filed with the Commission and found to be consistent with the public interest.

Meanwhile in other fields Congress withdrew combinations of competitors contracting for their personal services from the purview of the Anti-Trust Laws,-perhaps by reason of the conception of inequality of bargaining power. Again, the devastating effects of the 42

workings of the competitive system, where there was over-capacity and over-supply of agricultural products, were such that joint action of competitors appears to have been deemed a proper way in the agricultural industries to attempt to deal with the characteristic destructive effects of the competitive system under such situations. 

As to industry in general, the enunciation of the "rule of reason" in the Standard Oil and Tobacco cases by the Supreme Court, and the chronically disastrous results in many industries during a period of imbalance between available capacity and demand through the years following the panic of 1907, through 1915, had a molding effect. 

Perhaps this accounted for the wave of enthusiasm in both business and the public alike for joint effort among competitors to deal defensively with what were regarded as "competitive abuses" arising largely from the imbalance of competition in industry. 

The watchword for a time, taken up even by the administrators of the Anti-Trust Laws, became "cooperation" among business units to meet their common problems, accentuated by the then economic situation. It was regarded as not inconsistent with the sound workings of the competitive system but rather a proper adjunct to it. The trade association received an impetus as a means of effective joint action.

With the advent of World War I, the economic pendulum swung the other way, and the hyper-activity in competition came to be on the buyers' side rather than on the sellers. For the effective marshaling of the country's resources during the war, however, it was essential that there should be a high degree of joint action among competitors. "War Service Committees", at the instance of the 

Government, were organized in almost all industries. The effect was probably, on the whole, to increase product and to somewhat hold down runaway prices.

With the reaction from the War demand, there was a catastrophic shift for a time to a buyers' market in a wide range of industries. 

Prices plummetted. Joint efforts, so far as they were made by the trade associations and the remnants of the industry organizations inspired by the Government and set up during the war, were ineffective. Ironically enough, it was at a time when there was more economic justification for joint effort than there had been for years, that both the Supreme Court and the Administration returned to the earlier drastic limitations upon joint effort among competitors. 

Resisted though it was by Brandeis and Holmes, the Supreme Court, in the Lumber case, took the extreme positions that have been referred to above against even the collection and exchange of market information by competitors. President Harding pilloried trade associations as a prime cause of the depression. Attorney General 

Daugherty took his extreme position with regard to any joint activities of competitors. He began prosecutions to destroy the old business mechanism of so-called "price lists"-used alike when the prices were above or below cost. He took the position that similarity of price, even in industries with standardized products, was in itself 43

proof of price agreement and conspiracy to raise prices to unreasonable levels.

Again the pendulum swung back from this extreme position. In the Window Glass case the Supreme Court recognized that in a depressed branch of an industry, which was economically going to the wall through technological development, combinations of competitors directly affecting their production were but reasonable defensive measures and lawful. 

The school of thought of Holmes and Brandeis was again ascendant in the Supreme Court. It recognized that there might be wide ranges of joint action among competitors which promoted stability and so made the competitive system function more soundly and beneficially for the public. It rejected the Daugherty doctrine that mere similarity of price in standardized articles was in itself evidence of an agreement, and accepted the dictum of the older economists that it might just as well be the evidence of the active and normal workings of competition. 

At this time in the Federal Trade Commission there was not only acquiescence in but direct stimulation of joint action by competitors in eliminating trade practices which were deemed unlawful and might be actively proceeded against by the Commission. Also, it encouraged joint action as to those practices as to which the Commission felt it had no power to enforce a legal prohibition but as to which it was willing to accept such action of the industry as desirable and in the interests of the sound functioning of the competitive system. 

In the Supreme Court, Justice Brandeis, in the Chicago Board of Trade case, elaborated his doctrine that under the rule of reason any particular restraint had to be viewed in the light of all the circumstances and characteristics of the particular industry and its probable effect. It held to be reasonable and lawful a restraint which in effect constituted an agreement on what prices should be between open market sessions.

In a time of acute scarcity occasioned by a coal strike, an agreement by producers that they would not charge more than a maximum price -which was below the figure to which the hyper-active competition among buyers would otherwise have raised the price-was publicly hailed in a report to Congress as a step in the public interest which had saved the public millions of dollars. 

One step there was, however, and a highly important one-toward a drastic limitation upon the application of the "rule of reason" to joint action of competitors and toward the rejection of the test of whether a particular step could be said to be actually injurious to the public.

In the 1921 depression there had been joint effort by producers in the pottery industry to set a floor on prices by an agreement in the industry on the minimum prices which would be charged. Like most such attempted efforts it had not been effectively observed. 

The Circuit Court of Appeals, in the Second Circuit, following what 

44

had been regarded in that Circuit as the law since the Hamburg- American decision ten years before, held that under the "rule of reason" the agreement on a minimum price that was not unreasonable was not an unlawful restraint of trade. That decision, in the Trenton

Potteries case, the Supreme Court reversed. Its decision has continued to stand as representing the interpretation of the Sherman Law as to any agreement on any specific price, maximum, actual or minimum, which was agreed by joint action to be paid by the buyer or offered by the seller. In its opinion, the Court stressed as a reason for this determination what it thought would be the administrative burden on the courts of determining what was a reasonable price and the effect of changes that might occur in circumstances from time to time. But for the time being this decision was not interpreted by the court itself as going beyond a definitely agreed on specific price figure.

There emerged at this time from a chronically depressed industry, that of coal mining, what was then regarded as an important case. 

That industry had seemed incapable of pulling itself up from the successive bankruptcies of large numbers of its units and circumstances under which wages, living conditions of its labor force, and methods of mining, both from the standpoint of efficiency and conservation, were chronically bad. An effort was made by joint action among a large group of competitors to deal with this situation -where the available capacity, almost constantly in excess of effective demand, was producing its characteristic result. 

In Appalachian Coals, the Court held that these joint efforts to deal with what were described as competitive abuses in the industry, through the creation of a single channel for their sales, could not in itself be said to be unlawful, even though the hoped-for and to-beexpected consequence was that it would result in a higher, more stable and fairer price level as between buyers and sellers. Perhaps the 

Court went further in expressing the fundamental conception of what might be described as the White-Brandeis-Holmes-Hughes-Roberts point of view than in any other case, saying: "Voluntary action to rescue and preserve these (competitive) opportunities, and thus to aid in relieving a depressed industry and in reviving commerce by placing competition upon a sounder basis, may be more efficacious than an attempt to provide remedies through legal process. The fact that the correction of abuses may tend to stabilize a business, or to produce fairer price levels, does not mean that the abuses should go uncorrected or that cooperative endeavor to correct them necessarily constitutes an unreasonable restraint of trade." (288 U.S. 344, at p. 374.)

The crisis of the Great Depression led the Executive, the Congress and substantially our whole economic organization into a sweeping application of the philosophy of dealing with the destructive effects of the competitive system, when out of balance, by placing some limits on the play of competition during such a period. 45

In 1928, a "small business" group had emphasized to President Hoover the need that small business competitors had of taking voluntary joint action to deal with competitive abuses in accord with the philosophy soon to be expressed by the Supreme Court itself. 

With the onset of the Depression "spread the work" became a general slogan to distribute the inadequate demand throughout the competing units in an industry. To bring available capacity into something approaching balance with effective demand, as well as to get rid of a deep-rooted but socially undesirable custom, there was a movement, with informal Governmental encouragement, in one large industry to cut out the "graveyard shift" for women and minors; in another, to accomplish the same fundamental result and lessen the waste of a natural resource, by production limitations. 

The crisis deepened. Our whole economic system seemed to be moving toward dead center. At the beginning of 1933 an informal group drawn from small and large business in a variety of the more important industries met to emphasize to the Chairman of the House Judiciary Committee the importance of voluntary joint action among competitors within the framework of the Sherman Law. After some days of comparison among themselves of the situation in various industries they concluded that the situation was beyond the power of voluntary joint action to deal with. They proposed to the Presidentelect that such joint efforts be made under Government leadership, and with Government sanction, for such limitations on the play of competition as might be arrived at. The President-elect thought initially that voluntary action under a liberal interpretation of the Anti-Trust Laws would be preferable and would suffice. Then came the depths of the crisis, the closing of the banks, and the prompt emergence of the National Industrial Recovery Act. The President, perhaps because of what he described as the 10% of "chiselers", concluded that voluntary joint efforts were not enough without the backing of authority.

There followed a dramatic, if confused effort, to check the downward spiral by creating confidence that the bottom had been reached. 

Floors were put under wages and prices. Available capacity was sought to be brought into something approaching balance with demand by imposing ceilings on hours of labor and operation of equipment. Further, practically every industry embarked on what even the enthusiasts would now describe as an orgy of efforts to outlaw every method of competition which they had found unsound or painful. All but the simpler "Codes" ran into situations where the attempted limitations as to particular practices went so far beyond practical acceptance by those in industries that in broad areas they became as unenforced and unenforceable as prohibition laws. 

None of all these measures, proposed by industry, could be given effect by the National Recovery Administration unless the President affirmatively found them not to be monopolistic. Action in so finding "Codes" marked the extreme application by Administrative authorities of the coincident philosophy of the Supreme Court in the 46

Appalachian Coals case of the propriety of defensive action to deal with the characteristic workings of the competitive system when it gets gravely out of balance. 

With the turn of the economic tide in 1935, the earlier sense of imminent need for these measures wore away. There was a general sense of relief when the Supreme Court, in the reductio ad absurdum of the "Chicken" case, struck down the law primarily on the basis of narrowed rulings as to the scope of interstate commerce and delegation of legislative powers to Administrative bodies-as to which it proceeded in short order to reverse itself. 

With the competitive system generally returning toward a more normal balance, there was a sharp reversal of attitude in the Federal Trade Commission, the Department of Justice and the Supreme Court as to voluntary joint action. Administrative bodies and the 

Courts swung back toward the earlier drastic approach to any joint action by competitors attempting to deal defensively with the results of imbalance in the competitive process. Codes of Fair Trade Practices, formerly promoted by the Trade Commission, were revoked or emasculated. Prosecutions were initiated against the vestigial carryovers of joint efforts from the 1920's and the N.R.A. days. 

The Supreme Court, in the Socony-Vacuum case, in sweeping language, seemed to reject actual injury to the public or private interest as a test and to prohibit any joint action which might "affect price" and it is difficult to see what joint action taken by either buyers or sellers might not be deemed to affect price. The Court departed from the philosophy of Appalachian Coals that an industry need not necessarily wait for action by Goyernment authority to relieve itself from competitive abuses and that voluntary action might be a preferable method. It appeared to remit such problems wholly to the legislature.

While the pendulum cannot be said to have yet swung back to the full extreme of the doctrine of Peckham, McReynolds and Daugherty, it appears to be still swinging in that direction. 

But practical problems have arisen where Congress has deemed extreme applications of this doctrine to require modification. the long chronically depressed coal industry, before the shift to a selling market in the last war, it provided an interminably complex system of price floors to limit the one-sided play of competition in an industry with over-capacity. Since the war, it has by special legislation relieved the railroad and insurance industries, wisely or unwisely, from new drastic interpretations of the law. It has continued to sanction efforts by State authorities to so balance available capacity with demand in the oil industry as to mitigate what were deemed to be destructive effects on natural resources and prices from unbalanced competition. It has deemed the need of dealing with the effects of such competition in the world markets to be such that it has sanctioned sugar and wheat agreements limiting the play of such competition. 47

Ironically, no sooner had the reaction from measures deemed necessary to meet the buyers' market of the Great Depression been complete, than the competitive system was again thrown completely out of balance-with similar inevitable results, but on the opposite side of the market. With the dislocation of the second World War and the resulting period of shortage of available capacity in relation to demand, prices started to skyrocket. To meet this oppressive effect of competition the Government imposed limitations on the results of the free play of competition among buyers by imposing ceilings on prices and by the allocation of materials and products in relative short supply. In purpose and to a considerable extent in its machinery, it was N.R.A. in reverse. Like the N.R.A. and most prohibition laws it produced its areas of non-observance and black markets. As the situation eased and the activity in the dual play of competition among buyers and among sellers regained its balance, the O.P.A. became equally unpopular. Only in the rental field, where the imbalance persisted, has it been deemed necessary in wide areas to continue to limit the play of competition among "buyers" in their bidding up of prices. 

3. Issues presented as to how to deal with oppressive effects of persistent imbalance in competition This review of the approach of the Administrative bodies, of the Courts and of the Congress to the problem of dealing with the oppressive results of the competitive system when gravely and persistently out of balance, seems to indicate two things: 

First, the full play of competition is not an economic or social panacea for all our ills at all times. 

Second, there is posed the practical question of how to deal with this inherent characteristic of the competitive system in circumstances varying from its merely creating negligible difficulties in normal situations in an industry, to its becoming a veritable mechanism of disaster in times of national crisis. 

Under the "rule of reason" as originally enunciated, joint efforts of competitors to deal with such a situation appeared, as in Canada, to be left to the determination of whether they were "reasonable" or in fact worked an injury to the public interest. The standard was perhaps from the standpoint of courts and juries as indefinite as that which they are charged with administering daily in negligence cases in applying the standard of "reasonable care." It leaves a great deal to the feel of the situation under the particular facts and the common sense of the ordinary man. Perhaps that might be regarded as a recommendation. We submit the following as important questions for consideration by your Committee: 48

1. How did this "rule of reason" work in the past? 

2. In the view of your Committee, did or did not the competitive system by and large then produce for the public the economic progress and benefits which we can legitimately expect of it? 

3. Is it always true-as it was assumed to be in the two great crises in the last twenty years-that the joint voluntary action of competitors cannot be sufficiently inclusive or cohesive to deal adequately with the oppressive working of the competitive system when it gets seriously out of balance? 

4. Is there any scope for the application of any "rule of reason" to joint action of competitors, if any such action by them as may affect price is per se forbidden? 

5. Is it desirable for Congress to continue to deal piecemeal, as it has done, with situations where applications of the Anti- Trust Laws by administrative bodies or the Courts seem too rigid, impractical and against the public interest? When it does so, should it entrust, as it has tended to do, the administration of the matter to agencies which have an affirmative function to build up and promote the healthy working of the industry in question? 

6. Should Congress wait, as heretofore, until a major crisis is well along, before it attempts to set up machinery to deal with the oppressive business dislocations which appear to be inevitable when the competitive system gets seriously out of balance? 

7. Should it make some special provisions to prevent the wastes in natural resources which it is claimed are incident to such situations? 

8. Should Congress continue to take account of what it regards as oppressive prospective dislocations in world trade arising out of imbalance of competition in certain products having a world market and participate in measures limiting the play of competition in such situations?

The foregoing are issues raised by the present statutes and decisions. They are vital questions in our Anti-Trust policy. Your 

Committee could render a marked service by a presentation of your analysis of them, of the pros and cons in a discussion of them, and its conclusions and recommendations with respect to them. 49

IV. PROBLEMS OF CONSCIOUS SIMILARITY OF ACTION AMONG COMPETITORS A. IMPLICATIONS OF THE PHENOMENA OF UNIFORMITY OF PRICE AND TRADE PRACTICES 

No issues are more in the mind of businessmen, Administrative bodies, the Courts and Congress than those which center round uniformity of price and trade practices. 

Is such substantial similarity at the same time or place the indicia of and sufficient to establish illegality through a doctrine of "tacit conspiracy" or "conscious parallelism?"

If the phenomena of such similarity is sufficient to establish it, that similarity is so prevalent in times of normal balanced competition that practically the whole of our competitive system must at such times be pervaded by illegality. Is it only in the periods of crisis that the competitive system functions "lawfully", when competition is out of balance and we experience the wild bidding up or suicidal bidding down of prices by the activity of competition all being on one side or the other of the market and when prices and practices are apt to show their greatest divergence?

If this is so, it might be a fair question for your Committee, in view of the enormous strides in improvement in product and lowering of costs and prices which have been and are being made under this system with its all prevading "illegality", whether such "illegality" is of actual injury to the public interest. 

On the other hand, is there some truth in the view of those who dispute these inferences of illegality from similarity? Are these frantic efforts to prohibit it and eradicate it from our economic system rather the efforts of the Mrs. Partingtons to sweep back with their brooms the natural flow of the tides of competition? 

It is a clean-cut basic issue of fact. It is one, in our opinion, your 

Committee should examine and elucidate for the public. From our observation there is no contention that creates greater uncertainty and sense of helpless insecurity among businessmen than that conscious similarity is in itself unlawful. There is nothing that creates more of a question in the mind of the public, whether justified or unjustified, than the prevalence of the phenomena of similarity. 

It is our observation that such is the strength of the tide among administrators and the courts against any agreement whatever among competitors, that it seems to be threatening by the use of this doctrine to flood over into fields in which in fact there is no such agreementinto fields where it had been supposed that each competitor could go his own way and, as he saw fit, buy and sell goods above or below, or, as he ordinarily does, at the price prevailing in the market, give the cash discounts prevailing in the trade or not, as he saw fit, and otherwise follow, as he usually does, the custom prevailing in a trade. 50

Such a tendency, as we have observed it, has shown itself in two ways. First, by in effect holding that evidence as to mere similarity of behavior is sufficient in itself to establish the existence of an agreement to take such action; and, Second, by substituting, as sufficient to constitute a violation of the Act, such mere similarity of behavior in the market for the actual existence of an agreement or combination or conspiracy in any ordinary sense of those terms. 

B. SIMILARITY AS CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE OF AGREEMENT ON PRICE OR TRADE PRACTICES 

Early in the twenties, as has been pointed out, it was claimed by the Justice Department, under Attorney General Daugherty, that uniformity of price or of methods of quotation among competitors in the same market or of trade practices at the same time and place, was in itself sufficient evidence of an agreement to use and to maintain such prices or methods or practices. 

At that time, this contention was rejected. The Supreme Court pointed out that the fact that competitors are doing the same thing in the market at the same time, and in particular as to the price of standardized articles, may equally point to the fair and active play of competition. The ordinary rule as to circumstantial evidence that when the same fact may equally point to contradictory hypotheses it cannot fairly be used as evidence of either, seemed then to be firmly established in the law and founded on common sense. 

Many lawyers now feel that there is a tendency to permit administrative agencies, in practice, to substitute for the ordinary requirement of other evidence as to what is actually the fact, mere speculation as to which of two possible contradictory inferences from the circumstantial evidence is in fact the correct one. 

C. DOCTRINE OF “PARALLELISM” 

Perhaps because the enforcement agencies hesitate to press too far the inference of actual agreement from mere similarity of behavior of competitors, a novel doctrine of "conscious parallelism" is advanced. 

That doctrine seems to be that if there is similarity in the behavior of competitors as to their practices in the market and the competitors are aware of this fact, as they are pretty much bound to be, then that similarity may be of itself unlawful, though any one competitor could have bought or sold at the particular price or followed the particular method of conducting his business. 

Objections urged against this doctrine are; First, that it is asking the court to legislate on matters on which Congress has been silent; 

Second, that the gravamen of the Sherman Law offense, as in the common law doctrine of conspiracy, lies in the creation of artificial power by the very agreement of a group to act together; and, Third, that it is an attempt to require of competitors artificial dissimilarities 51

in action and so remold the ordinary human being into the image of some abstract, competitive economic man. 

If this doctrine of a competitive obligation of dissimilarity should ever be accepted by the courts and applied to prices and practices, and decrees entered upon it, it would be interesting to see how the courts would determine in such a decree which seller was thereafter to charge the lowest price and which to charge on up in range to the highest price, which buyer was to be accorded the privilege of buying at the lowest price, and which buyer would be compelled to buy at the price up to the highest price; and so also as to cash and trade discounts and other customary trade practices. The matter could not be left to "competition", for the experience of the freest markets shows that prices ordinarily work back to prevailing uniformity at the same time, at the same place, between periods of basic price changes; and the same as to customary trade practices. 

D. DIVERSE CONCEPTIONS OF THE COMPETITIVE PROCESS 

Much of this controversy seems to stem from differing conceptions as to the very nature of the competitive process. Some seem to think that competing sellers normally engage in a race to sell at the lowest price and buyers in a race to buy at the highest price and that otherwise "competition" is not influencing the price at which each buys or sells. To others, this is an Alice-in-the-Looking Glass conception. To them, each buyer in competition normally seeks to satisfy his needs at the lowest price he can and, if possible, at a lower price than his competitors; but at times, particularly of short supply, he will bid a price above that bid by his competitors. Each buyer, however, in the price he actually offers is going to be influenced by what he thinks his competitor will be doing and by what he thinks the people on the other side of the market are going to do. The net result is that he will probably be prepared to buy at the prices generally prevailing in the market at the time. And the same way with the sellers. Each would rather get more for his product or services, but he cannot ordinarily expect any buyer to pay more than the prevailing market price. He certainly does not desire, if he can avoid it, to sell at lower than that prevailing price, though when the market gets out of balance, at times, in order to try to move more of his product, he does so. The prevailing market price tends to change with the frequent or infrequent changes in some substantial factors of costs or supply or demand. And the same as to trade practices, with the tyranny of the "customary" being very strong. 

To those who urge this new concept of violation of the Anti-Trust Laws through mere "conscious similarity" in behavior, the interaction of what goes on in the market place in influencing what each competitor does seems artificial or, at any rate, highly suspect. In actual fact, however, a buyer or a seller, in determining his own conduct, is bound to be primarily influenced by what competitors are 522

doing or he thinks them likely to do in the market. Probably in no field of human activity is what is done by each so much a sensitive composite of interacting psychologies as in a competitive market. 

Strong pressures for resultant similarities of behavior seem inherent in the system itself.

It is true that in the old days of special prices and special concessions, any particular buyer or any particular seller might be induced to buy or sell in particular transactions at some different price even over long periods, if the fact could be kept secret. At present, under the statutory prohibition directed against discriminating among his customers, the seller almost perforce sells to all his customers at the same price, at the same time, in the same market. 

His inability to make his price higher than the prevailing market price is thus accentuated. His unwillingness to drop his price below that general market price inevitably becomes greater-with resultant increased competitive pressure toward uniformity. 

Thus the old generalization of the economists as to the naturalness of uniformity in competitive prices, by reason of competitive forces, is reinforced by the limitation which the law itself has now placed on the method of competition of charging a different price to different customers at the same time and in the same market. It would be ironical if the seller or the buyer in the present situation should now almost automatically become suspect of an offense because of the obligation that the law now imposes upon him in limitation of the methods of his competition.

There is a phrase "duty of a competitor to compete"-bandied about loosely in this connection which it seems to us it would be useful for your Committee to examine. What is any such supposed "duty to compete"? In "public" businesses, it is true that any one who embarks on them subjects himself to regimented affirmative duties. But is it not of the essence of "private" businesses that we rely fundamentally on the free play of self-interest of units engaged in such business to produce, on the whole, satisfactory economic results. For one reason or another, or in one set of circumstances or another, we think it necessary to impose the limitations of "don'ts" on their freedom of action-sometimes very drastic "don'ts".

Beyond these limitations on action do we not and must we not rely on the free play of each unit's own conception of that self-interest? 

We hope that this conception will usually be intelligent and one that recognizes that benefit to the concern itself and to the public are pretty well intertwined. But we know that it won't always be intelligent and that if it isn't, any such unit is apt to go out of business. But is there any affirmative duty to improve facilities or product, or to try to get a larger or smaller or a present proportion of the going market or to offer a lower, higher or the same price as other units? If we are going to impose affirmative "duties" on the management of individual enterprises, as we do in "public" businesses, then do we not get away from the fundamental conceptions of freedom in the competitive "private" businesses. If we did, would 53

we not also then get into the whole complex realm of correlative affirmative rights of a concern in one of the regimented "public" businesses; the right of the exercise of "eminent domain"; the right to obtain a price which will yield, if the traffic will bear it, a fair return on investment and the other incidents of regimented "public" businesses?

Instead, should we not, within the limitations of the "don'ts" imposed in the public interest, let the units of a "private" industry individually and collectively each work out its own salvation and sink or swim? So far as the contribution to economic progress of the nation goes, has not this worked out satisfactorily? It may well be that examination would show that over a period of fifty years the individual investors in "private" industry as a whole might have done much better for themselves by putting what they had in savings banks. But they have had the incentive of exceptional profits, if their units were exceptionally well managed. They have had the satisfactions of the exercise of their abilities and of taking chances. 

They have breathed the atmosphere of reasonably free enterpriseusually invigorating though sometimes lethal. The competitive system flourishes through the incentives and opportunities it offers and the freedom of units each to govern its own business activities. Is not any attempt to regiment that system by formulating and imposing some supposed affirmative "duty to compete" on each unit, foreign to the fundamental conception of freedom of such units to live or die, succeed or fail, be efficient or inefficient in its own way. 

Can you legislate "efficiency" or a "competitive spirit"?

E. THE ISSUES 

The foregoing seems to resolve itself into these issues for your consideration and elucidation to the public: 

1. Under normal competitive conditions, is conscious similarity of price and practices among competitors, buyers or sellers, in the same market at the same time a to-be-expected frequent result of the operations of the competitive system? 

2. If so, is it a result forbidden by the Anti-Trust Laws? 

3. If so, what method can be devised for doing business under the competitive system for avoiding that result? 

4. Can it be prevented except by the regimentation by public authority of the marketing decisions of each and every business unit doing business at the same time in the same market? 

5. If that were the situation, would we then have a competitive system? 54

V. CERTAIN SPECIAL PROBLEMS A. CONFLICTS IN ADMINISTRATIVE POLICY AND OVERLAPS IN ADMINISTRATION 1. Foreign Trade 

We believe that your Committee will find important issues requiring your consideration with respect to the application and administration of the Anti-Trust Laws as to foreign trade. There are certain inherent difficulties in the application of what have come to be drastic limitations on freedom of action in the domestic field to the different conditions both as to economic situations and as to local law in the foreign fields. 

a. The Webb-Pomerene Act 

Congress early took note of this situation by its passage of the Webb-Pomerene Act. A serious problem of overlapping and concurrent jurisdiction of two agencies of government over the same conduct exists with respect to export trade associations organized pursuant to this Act. 

The Webb-Pomerene Act exempts from the Anti-Trust Laws certain conduct of associations organized to engage solely in export trade. In order to enjoy the benefit of this exemption, these associations are required to file with the Federal Trade Commission complete information about the association and its activities, and the Commission is given power to investigate and to make recommendations for the readjustment of the business of the association if it concludes that the law has been violated. If the association fails to comply with such recommendations, then the Commission is required to refer its findings and recommendations to the Attorney General for such action as he may deem appropriate. 

In the important decision of the Supreme Court in U. S. Alkali 

Export Association v. United States, decided in 1945, the facts were that the United States had instituted proceedings under the Sherman Act against an export trade association organized in conformity with the provisions of the Webb-Pomerene Act. The Association urged in defense that the relationship of the Sherman Act and the Webb- Pomerene Act was such that the authority of the United States to proceed against such associations under the Sherman Act was suspended until the Federal Trade Commission had proceeded against the Association by way of investigation, recommendation and report to the Attorney General. The Supreme Court rejected this contention and held that the United States was authorized to proceed against the Association under the Sherman Act without reference to the powers of the Commission under the Webb-Pomerene Act. 

The result of this decision is that both the Federal Trade Commission and the Attorney General possess concurrent jurisdiction over these associations. The further result is that any association that 55

proposes to engage in any activity may not rely upon the fact that the Federal Trade Commission in the exercise of its supervisory powers over these associations approves the proposed activity wholeheartedly. The Attorney General may take a different view and proceedings taken in reliance upon the approval of one agency of the government would be subject to prosecution by another agency of the same government. That situation exists today, and it presents a problem that we believe we can properly say, as lawyers, should be corrected.

In this situation, the Government should put its house in order and clarify the relationship between the Sherman Act and the Webb- Pomerene Act. It should be made plain that, as provided under the 

Webb-Pomerene Act, the Federal Trade Commission is to be the agent that shall exercise supervisory powers over these associations, and that proceedings may not be instituted by the Attorney General against an association, or in which an association is in any way involved, until the Federal Trade Commission has first exercised its powers. b. "Point Four" of our International Assistance Program

Apart from Webb-Pomerene Act problems, American concerns that carry on business abroad are confronted with considerable uncertainty as to what limits are placed upon their freedom of action with respect to operating through wholly-owned foreign subsidiaries, through partly owned foreign subsidiaries, through foreign concerns which are from one aspect independent contractors and yet from another aspect have a quasi-agency relationship and as to contractual arrangements which they may make as to patents, "knowhow" and technical assistance. These uncertainties stem in part from obscurities in the statutes and in part from their administrative and judicial interpretations.

Such uncertainties may be paralyzing on the initiation of business activities, particularly in a distant field. It is not "good business" to engage in activities which may produce litigation even though it is believed that such litigation, if it came, should not and would not end unfavorably.

In normal times it may not be too important whether or not Americans in their foreign business in fact are being restrained by such uncertainty beyond what the law in fact may require. But under the present conditions in the world, your Committee may well feel it is of vital consequence to endeavor to clear it away, so that the national policies we have adopted with a view to aiding in the reconstruction and development of the economies of the free peoples of the world may have the maximum of lawful aid from American business.

As your Committee knows, efforts are now being made, under "Point Four", to maximize the participation of American business in this task, and to secure the application of American capital and 56

American "know-how" to foreign fields, so that their increased productivity can form the basis not only for their own increased strength, but also as a basis of exchange for the products of our own economy. 

In determining their response to these efforts to secure their participation, how are American concerns to answer to themselves such questions as the following: (1) In spite of differences in economic situations, customs and local law in the foreign field, do the provisions of the statutes (aside from Section 2a of the Robinson-Patman Act) 

as to limitations in differences in pricing to customers and its effect on competition in different stages in production and distribution, as to tying clauses and stock acquisitions, and as to restrictions on the terms of patent licenses, apply in the same way to the actions of American concern in its carrying on of business in such foreign field and its dealings with foreign concerns in those areas, as they do to their actions in the domestic field? (2) Is it possible, in exporting "know-how" or investing money in a foreign company which is not a subsidiary, for an American concern to provide for any ancillary restraints protecting the foreign company from the competition of the American company, or vice versa? Or for a limited time, until the foreign company is able to take care of itself? Are interlocking directorships permissible in such situations, or management contracts? Are any such limitations applicable even where foreign laws and other conditions make impossible for an American concern the normal parent-wholly owned subsidiary relationship? (3) If the American concerns in a position to conduct the business of granting the needed "know-how" and investing the needed capital abroad are large companies, then, in view of the present trend toward imposing limitations on freedom of action on the basis of mere size, may such large companies safely enter into a series of license and investment agreements with foreign companies or are they to be met with a charge of unlawfully dominating the flow of foreign inventions and investments? 

If they do not undertake such activities, are there small companies which can? Must large companies so limit their activities of this nature as to go far toward preventing the accomplishment of the objectives of "Point Four"?

Under the flexible approach of the White, Holmes, Brandeis, Hughes and Roberts school of thought, the answer to all these questions could be approached by looking at all the economic and factual considerations which 'enter into the situation. The factors of differences in economic climate and legal systems, and of any overriding considerations of our National policy in the present condition of the world, could be taken into account. Insofar as any "restraints" were 57

involved in such actions, the test would be whether they were "undue and unreasonable" and "substantially injurious to the public interest." 

But if no allowance is to be made for such factors in the application of our Anti-Trust Laws to the foreign field, the uncertainties, with their attendant drastic penalties for misjudgment, may well deter them.

May not your Committee be able at the least to work out some modus vivendi which, even though it does not result in completely reconciling conflicting views of policy of different departments of the Government, may aid the national policy in going forward? Or is it the situation that in the present "cold war", as proved to be the case in the late "shooting war", some special legislation to resolve conflicting Departmental views is necessary to permit flexibilities and cut some of the hard knots in which our tendency toward perfectionism and our instinctive fears may tend to tie up our activities, even in times of crisis? 

2. Overlaps and Conflicts between Federal Trade Commission and Department of Justice in Conspiracy Cases 

An important problem seems to us to exist in the overlapping of the jurisdiction of the Federal Trade Commission and of the Department of Justice in the field of conspiracy cases. This overlapping grows out of a line of Supreme Court decisions which have held that certain types of conduct are violative of both the Sherman Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act. 

From our observation this dual power over the same conduct has resulted in conflicting views of public policy, needless duplication of effort and oppressive burdens of expense and harrassment on parties subjected to investigation and proceedings by both agencies. 

The outstanding example of proceedings being brought by both agencies against the same parties is the proceedings brought against the cement industry. The proceeding brought by the Federal Trade 

Commission against the industry terminated in the decision of the Supreme Court in 1948. Yet while that case was pending before the 

Circuit Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, the Attorney 

General filed a civil complaint in the District Court in Denver, Colorado, against substantially the same parties and based on substantially the same charges. That complaint is still pending. Further, in that civil case the Attorney General attempted to require the District Court to adopt the findings of fact made by the Federal Trade Commission in the earlier case as findings of the District Court and made binding upon that Court in the new case. The District Court, however, declined to do this. 

Liaison between two agencies at times does not seem effective to deal with such an overlap, although real efforts are being made in this respect. In the example cited, the second proceeding was instituted and carried on in defiance of liaison. Further, the two agencies may, as in this case, reflect different viewpoints of public policy. 58

We are not finding fault with the fact that there may be a difference in view in and among agencies of Government as to public policy. However, such differences should be resolved before proceedings are instituted against private parties. It is unseemly that two agencies of the Government should be in the position of competing for the enforcement of conflicting views of public policy. The private parties ought not to be made the subject of such competition. 

An intolerable situation may result if parties must conduct their affairs upon orders of two different courts as a result of proceedings under different jurisdictions.

Situations such as these, we believe, will appear to your Committee to need permanent correction. It is time that one or the other of these agencies should be permanently selected for enforcement where there is an overlap in jurisdictions.. 

B. OBSERVANCE OF THE ANTI-TRUST LAWS AND PENALTIES UNDER THEM 1. Observance 

As we have indicated above it is our observation that the Anti- Trust Laws as to their basic objectives and in aspects which involve willful and substantial injury to the public interest, are well observed. 

To be sure there have been a vast number of proceedings and a substantial number of criminal prosecutions. But in the latter, Courts in the sixty years of their administration of these laws have rarely found violations which seemed to them to warrant prison sentences. 

In perhaps the most famous of those cases-the Trenton Potteries case after the judgment had been sustained by the Supreme Court it was felt that the circumstances had not in fact warranted prison sentences and they were remitted. 

From our observation, the periodic floods of Anti-Trust litigation do not stem primarily from any lack of deterrent penalties or from willful disregard of the statutes or the public interest. They stem rather from certain causes inherent in the nature of such legislation and in our methods of interpreting and administering it. Congress saw fit, and as lawyers we think wisely, to phrase the fundamental policy of the nation in the regulatory economic provisions of the Sherman Law in the broadest of language. As an inevitable result, as in the case of similarly broadly phrased provisions of our Constitution, they have been as we have pointed out subject to changing interpretations and administration to meet changing current conceptions of regulatory needs. Consequently businessmen have periodically had to adjust the way in which they carry on their business to what is the equivalent of a succession of legislative acts. 

But these changes, as we have developed, come about not by specific published Acts of Congress, but by the uncertain and often somewhat obscure process of change in judicial interpretation and the application of the law to new situations. Businessmen are not clairvoyant 59

as to what such decisions are going to be. Indeed, often in the most farreaching ones the judges themselves are in conflict as to the proper interpretation of the statutes or their application to the particular facts. As a result, businessmen periodically find themselves in the position of having been engaged in a long course of crime through conduct which previous judicial decision and administrative attitude had indicated was lawful.

We will not repeat the examples we have given of these changes and oscillations. We have also pointed out to what an extent the area of what is questionable has been enlarged to include competitive methods, formerly thought normal, and cooperative action, at times thought useful and proper in meeting what were regarded as "competitive abuses" when competition was out of balance. 

There is another consideration to be taken account of in drawing conclusions from the volume of Anti-Trust litigation. In appraising what has been the actual conduct involved in such litigation, it is our observation that it cannot safely be assumed, whatever its outcome, that the charges outlined in indictments and complaints and the provisions of consent decrees accurately reflect undisputed or established facts as to such conduct. The pleader naturally is apt to pursue a somewhat shotgun method of alleging everything that he might like or hope to prove. The defendant who enters a plea of nolo contendere or even of guilty, or settles civil litigation by a consent decree, may well do so not because he in his own mind admits the scope or accuracy of those charges or the appropriateness of the provisions of the consent decree to his own past conduct. It may be he does so because, with the law as it has developed, he realizes that he is in fact vulnerable on some, perhaps, minor aspect of them. Or he may realize that the circumstances on which the charge is based may be so ambiguous that, under the present tendencies of the Courts in interpreting such evidence, they may be interpreted, as he would think, improperly against him. Or it may be simply a situation where he has no desire to pursue a line of conduct charged and prefers to accept a prohibition of it in a consent decree rather than take up the time-consuming and expensive burden of Sherman Law litigation in endeavoring to establish his own contentions as to the actual facts. 

Even in convictions after trial the courts are so liberal in permitting variances from the exact charges of the indictment that it takes a careful examination of the evidence in any given case to determine how far it supported the exact charge in the indictment. Of course there is a hard core of cases where the charge as laid in the indictment or complaint is completely or substantially supported, but the extent of that hard core cannot readily be determined and may easily be overestimated. 

2. Criminal Penalties 

Present penalties are not out of line with the general pattern of our criminal law. Your Committee may well want to consider, however, whether the present provisions for criminal prosecution and 60

punishment are appropriate for the wide range in the nature, certainty and seriousness of matters within the purview of the Anti-Trust Acts as now amended and interpreted. Should not conscious illegality and some actual injury to the public or private interest from the restraint or combination be made necessary elements in the proof of a violation where criminal procedure is made applicable? As lawyers, we feel we may say that we regard this as highly desirable to prevent anomalous situations. Enforcement by order of the administrative agency or decree of the court in equity, backed as they are by the strong but flexible powers of a court in contempt proceedings, are adequate for those situations where there is no element of moral obliquity or substantial injury. 

3. Private Actions for Treble Damages 

A serious problem exists in the field of private treble damage actions that seems to us to call for your consideration of the statutory provisions under which, in all private actions, damages are automatically trebled.

Actual damages are designed to make an injured party whole. 

Treble damages are punitive. They are punishment that in the antitrust field may be more severe, in terms of money, than the fines imposed in criminal proceedings. A flood of treble damage actions typically follows a final victory of the United States in a proceeding brought by it. The result is that punitive damages are added to the penalties and remedies already secured by the United States. This 

Committee is advised that, in the case of the moving picture industry, treble damage actions are now pending in which the recovery sought is in excess of $200,000,000. The problem presented is obviously not a small one. The financial burden of defense is itself a substantial one.

The statutory provisions that result in the automatic trebling of damages in all cases result in injustice in all instances in which the defendant's conduct was in no way deserving of the assessment of punitive damages. A change of decisional law may make conduct illegal which was legal when undertaken. Conduct may have been undertaken in good faith and in reliance on legal advice that may later prove to have been erroneous. It may be conduct in a field in which there are no guiding court decisions and in which legal advice is contradictory.

This Committee suggests that consideration be given to the amendment of the treble damage provisions of the Anti-Trust Laws so that treble damages be awarded only in cases in which a court has first made a specific finding that the defendant has knowingly and wilfully violated the law and had a conscious purpose to injure the plaintiff. In such cases it is proper to award punitive damages. In all cases in which the record will not support such a finding, the award should be limited to actual damages. The party plaintiff should of course be made whole, but there is then no reason why the defendant in such situations should be subject to such drastic 61

punishment. This would lessen the incitement to strike suits and also lessen the present reluctance of juries to find verdicts which at present automatically result in a punishment under circumstances which they may well feel make it unjust. 

C. STATUTORY CHANGES 

Analysis, consideration and conclusions as to the issues outlined above seem to us necessary first step before your Committee or Congress can determine whether there is any occasion for statutory changes and what those changes should be. Perhaps there is one other matter necessary for any such determination. We will proceed to outline it. 

1. Certainty and rigidity versus uncertainty and flexibility in the Anti-Trust Acts There has always been an insistent cry for certainty as to the scope and meaning of the Anti-Trust Acts. Businessmen naturally complain of their general language and shifting interpretations by the courts and the inability of counsel to forecast such shifts and consequent uncertainty as to what their concerns can or cannot lawfully do in the conduct of their business. 

As to broad ranges of conduct easily recognizable by any decent citizen as vicious and oppressive of the public or competitors, there is and always has been certainty for practical purposes. A catalogue of the specific forms such conduct might take would be Homeric. But it would hardly help the businessman much, if it could be compiled for it is no longer regarded as "good business", if it ever was, to engage in that type of conduct, and he can recognize it without any such catalogue. 

On the other hand the situations as to which there is and has been controversy, the swing of the pendulum and consequent uncertainty, are difficult to define. The answer so often depends on the facts of a particular industry and particular circumstances in that industry. 

Only by taking extreme and drastic positions would there seem to be any possibility of securing certainty in such areas. 

A statute might categorically forbid any joint action by competitors. 

It might categorically forbid any buyer or seller to offer to buy or sell at a price which he knew any other buyer or seller was offering to buy or sell at. It might categorically forbid any business unit from engaging in more than one type of business or in more than one stage of that business. It might categorically forbid any business unit from employing more than X dollars of capital or from furnishing to customers more than X percentage of the dollar or physical volume of the market for the type of product or service it was engaged in producing or rendering. 

The price of such certainty would be a rigidity heretofore wholly foreign to private business. But even the statutory formulation and adoption of such extreme doctrines and such strait-jacket provisions as those suggested above, would leave some areas for uncertainty. 62

There is a price too for flexibility. If there is to be flexibility to the limitations imposed on freedom of action and use of competitive methods, so that freedom is not to be curtailed beyond what is necessary under the particular circumstances of the particular industry to secure from that industry by and large the results which we can reasonably expect under our competitive system, then the price is a considerable degree of uncertainty.

The "rule of reason", like the rule of "reasonable care" in negligence, was easy to state in general terms, but its application, the limitations of language and of the human mind and judgment being what they are, necessarily left broad margins of uncertainty. 

Perhaps some more graphic or appropriate language than "undue or unreasonable restraint" might be used to secure flexibility; perhaps a fresh start in phraseology might be useful to escape from the toils of highly technical decisions in which we are now enmeshed. But no new language that can be devised can be expected to escape uncertainty in this field of the law unless we are to accept the rigid inflexibility of the regimented public businesses. 

Before it determines on any particular statutory change in provisions or language, the task for your Committee would seem to be to make this choice between certainty and rigidity and uncertainty and flexibility.

Conclusion 

With regard to the issues which we raise for your consideration, we are not insensible to the complete sincerity and earnestness of purpose of those who have entertained different views upon them, and particularly those who are directly charged with the administration of these laws. Such differences spring naturally from the complexity of the problems involved. Their resolution, if they are to be wisely resolved, must depend on good will and efforts at mutual understanding. We feel that your Committee, chosen as it is from representative agencies of the Government, with varying interests and responsibilities in this field, will be able to make a constructive analysis and recommendations which will be of the highest value to the Congress and to the Public.
