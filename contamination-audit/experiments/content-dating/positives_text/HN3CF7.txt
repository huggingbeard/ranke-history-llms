FOREWORD TO FIRST EDITION 

Contrary to the expectations of the author and his friends, the most enthusiastic encomiums upon The Problem of Poverty emanate from wage-workers and members of labor unions. The evidence is cumulative in support of the belief that there is a general disposi tion to underestimate both the number and the intelligence of those who disapprove of the present domination by those organizations, but who are bludgeoned into silence and compelled to co-operate with the ma jority of their fellow artisans.

This suppressed minority within the unions seems actually to long for a spokesman who can give expression to their thoughts and vent to their emotions. 

In this, as in other respects, politicians have failed them. All those who hope to attain office at the hands of the electorate assume that antagonism to the demands of the labor unions means political suicide. 

Hence, irrespective of what the consequences to the country may be, they generally agree with the unions. 

They confine their professions of faith to that which they feel certain the majority of their auditors would like to believe. It is for this very reason that the politi cians shy at The Problem of Poverty, while many people who have been wage-earners all their lives read it with relish, even with avidity. They find in it, clearly expressed, that which they themselves know to be facts and conclusions which they themselves more or less consciously entertained but never dared voice.

I am mindful of the aversion of the average reader to anything that smatters of scientific exposition of economic subjects unspiced by the garlic of partisan polemics. Hence I have endeavored to reduce The 

Problem of Poverty to the minimum in bulk, lest the prospective reader should be deterred by the dimensions of the volume. But, in doing so, I find that the discussion of some of the subjects treated became so brief as to seem vague to the minds of some people. 

This is especially true of the law of wages. It is, therefore, as a supplement to the aforementioned volume that the following monograph is prepared and given to the public. This would seem especially appropriate, for two reasons, viz.: In the first place, I am aware of no publication on this subject designed for other than professional students of political economy; in the second place, the law of wages is the most fundamental as well as the least understood of the economic forces disturbing the peace of the nation, even the world, at the present time. It is certain that only through a clearer comprehension of its dominating influence and a more faithful obedience to its edicts will economic adjustment be possible.

THE LAW OF WAGES 

Is there, or is there not, any economic law governing wages? Is there any limit below which wages cannot fall, or above which they cannot rise, without passing the point of diminishing returns? If so, where is that point? How is it found or determined?

I am justified in posing these questions on account of some objections made to my previous statement of the law of wages and prices. I there laid down the rule that the fairness of wages cannot be measured by the prosperity of individual industries. It must be measured by the prosperity of the country as a whole. 

Assuming that each laborer is entitled to compensation equal to the proportion he contributes to the aggregate products of the country, the aggregate of the wages cannot exceed the aggregate value of the products. If this be conceded, it must also be conceded that if one receives as wages a sum which is proportionately greater than the value of what he contributes to the sum total of all the products, that excess is pilfered from others to whom it rightly belongs. 

This pilfering, I contended, is primarily the cause of unemployment for so many. 

Is there any weak spot in this reasoning? If there be, it has never been pointed out. Why, then, the objection to it? Obviously, because so many are in a position where they cannot afford to accept the conclusions which the rigor of the logic forces upon them. 

To do so would compel the scrapping of many sociological schemes which for decades have been the fetishes of a large and respectable percentage of our population.

It would necessitate realignment on many political fronts.

In this lies the bewildering importance of the doctrine of the law of wages, and in this importance do I find the justification for elaborating on the basic principles involved.

Most of the economic sophistry of the day has found adherence because of the inability of so many to see money only as the medium of exchange and not in and of itself the thing of value. Its symbolic function is so generally mistaken for the reality by all of us. For this reason let us, during this discussion, for a moment forget money and resort to direct barter.

Let us postulate a primitive settlement by civilized people in a new country, in the midst of the wilderness, and trace events as the country grows: 

How Civilization Starts 

When the colonists arrive, some of them get busy clearing ground to raise food or material for clothing. One has a spot especially fitted for corn, another has land better adapted for cotton, another for hemp, another for sheep, and so on. Others get busy erecting fortifications for protection against enemies, others are police officers with duty to maintain order; others become shoemakers, blacksmiths, carpenters, tailors, and so on. Each proceeds to do that for which he is best adapted, has the best facilities, or for which there is the greatest need in the community. 

As there is no money in the settlement, how do these people get paid? Each receives some of the commodities which the others produce in exchange for that which he himself contributes. Figuratively, all

that is produced is put into one heap, and from that heap each is given his share. One thing is certain, the community cannot give out any more than it has produced! Here, then, we arrive at the axiom that the aggregate of the compensation cannot exceed the aggregate of the products. Converted into terms of money, this would mean that the total amount of wages paid can never exceed the aggregate value of the products of the labor for which those wages were earned.

The corollary to this theorem becomes self-evident, viz.: If some receive more than their share, others must necessarily receive less than their share. The sum of the parts cannot be greater than the whole. 

But are all really entitled to an equal share of the aggregate? If a man has been lazy and accomplished no more in one week than others accomplish in a day, would he be entitled to share equally with the others who have been much more industrious? Certainly not. Equal sharing in such cases would soon destroy the discipline of the settlement. If one, through training and study, or extraordinary ability or efforts, was more useful to the community than anybody else, and in that manner contributed more to the aggregate production than others, is it not wise and just that the community let him receive compensation in proportion to what he contributes? Certainly.

Nature's Basic Laws 

When the foundation of the world was laid, it was decreed that actions which tended to exhilarate the functions of life should be attended with pleasant consequences, and actions which tended to depress life

should be attended with disagreeable consequences.

Only under such a system, as Spencer observes, could sentient existence evolve. In other words, nature offers its reward for right action and punishment for wrong action. No race or nation that does not give effect to that basic law of creation can possibly survive. 

The length of its survival depends upon the strictness with which that law is enforced. So we arrive at the secondary premise, that the wages of each should be in proportion to what each contributes in time, exertion, and efficiency to the sum total out of which wages are paid.

But if, instead of payment in the form of commodities from the common stock, the community should estimate the value of the total and issue scrip to each in the form of certificates showing how much he is entitled to receive, the question of what is just and right will not thereby be changed. Even if the members of the community should start trading in this scrip, that would not change the amount to which each would be entitled for his services. The measure of the value of services would still be the units of time, exertion, intelligence, and skill each contributes to the aggregate production. It is when money is introduced as a medium for the exchange of commodities that the average mind loses its bearings and sense of propor tion, and no longer can see the relationship between the value of individual labor and the value of the totality of the products of labor. He can no longer see that to give to one more than he has contributed, is to steal from somebody else.

Suppose, now, that in our primitive community, after a more complete division of labor has developed, 10

the carpenters, for instance, should form an organization and agree to demand higher wages, what would happen? To begin with, people would do more of their own carpenter work, and thus to some extent reduce the demand for carpenters. In the second place, more men would leave the less remunerative trades and commence to work as carpenters. In other words, where there is no interference with the law of supply and demand, labor always tends to leave areas or trades of low wages and drift to areas or trades of higher wages, thus equalizing the demand by increasing it where wages are too low and decreasing it where they are proportionately too high. The same principle applies to prices. 

Nature has thus provided its own stabilizer. The relative value of the various forms of labor adjusts itself automatically where the ebb and flow of supply and demand are left untrammeled. But assume that the carpenters' union, for instance, by force or intimidation, maintained a wage scale twice what it would be under free competition, could anybody intelligently argue that thereby the community had become more prosperous? Hardly, although many very strange assertions are made in defense of such course. Obviously, the extra compensation would have to come out of the rest of the community. To the extent the carpenters are thereby enriched, the others would be impoverished. The common stock of products has not been augmented. It has only been unequally divided. 

The result would be that many who need carpenters would get along without them. There would be less for carpenters to do, so that as a class they would earn little, if anything, more than under a lower wage scale. 11

Assuming that the same number continue to follow the trade, there would be a good deal of unemploy ment among them. Their defense would be: "We work only half the time under the new scale of wages, but we earn as much as if we worked full time." That signifies that half their efficiency, half their manpower, is wasted-is lost to the country. That waste has to be made good by the sweat of the rest of the community. By working half the time, they force the community to give them as much as others receive for full time; while prices are raised correspondingly. 

Artificial Wage Scales Hurt Labor 

So, in the same manner, from every artificial wage scale there is loss to every community-a loss in production, a loss in man-power, an unjust division of the products of labor, an unjust division of the responsibilities of citizenship. These injustices manifest themselves in physical discomforts and in spiritual unrest and discord. "True," say the professional agitators and politicians, "but the wages of everybody must be raised alike, then everybody will be richer and everybody's purchasing power will be increased." Absurd as this may be, it is, nevertheless, re-echoed throughout the length and breadth of this country.

Let us, for the purpose of a simpler illustration of the natural forces, keep our minds on the primitive community. Money is only a draft against the prod ucts of labor. Will those products increase by simply changing the figures on the draft? Will there be more commodities available to divide simply by the expedient of increasing the money measurement of what 12

each is to have? If there is not more to divide, how can each get more by such a scheme? 

Some impatient reader will insist that it is an insult to the intelligence of humanity to argue this question, but I have daily observed that both laborers and business men stumble over it. Even those high in the councils of the nation stub their toes on it at times. 

The reader will, therefore, pardon me for endeavoring to make fundamentals so plain that the dullest can understand.

The proposition may be reasserted, that a general rise in wages will result in a corresponding general rise in the cost of commodities, so that the wages would buy no more-the share of each in the common stock would be no greater, while an artificial or forced rise in wages in some trades will be a charge against other laborers in one form or another. 

Back to Basic Principles

The concatenation of cause and effect in a large and complex society is too difficult for the human intellect to follow in all its ramifications. This has afforded the opportunity for so much political sophistry to masquerade as either wisdom or charity, or both. There is only one safe way to go, and that is back to basic principles, and in the light of those principles pass judgment on political policies. If these cannot, after being stripped of their trappings, stand that test, they are damned, for the truth is not in them. 

No person, unwilling to shut his eyes to fundamental principles, should have any difficulty in discovering that as regulators of wages or prices trade unions 13

or monopolistic corporations are as injurious to the economic life of the country as are other organizations engaged in the restraint of competition.

When a person charges more for his commodity or his labor than fair competition in a free market would yield, he extracts more from the community fund than he is entitled to. This violation of nature's system of distributing wealth is penalized by lowering the purchasing power of the rest of the community. Thus, if through monopolization the price of a commodity is increased the market for that commodity is thereby restricted. The immediate profit on what is sold may be greater, but the quantity sold will be smaller. This reduction of the sale reduces the demand for labor to produce. Conversely, when the price to the consumer is lower, the consumer's ability to buy more is augmented. It must be evident that the demand for labor ebbs and flows with the amount of the sales of the products of labor. This is the reason why monopolization tends to create unemployment.

In an unmonopolized market the price will fluctuate, but the motive for the fluctuation is to find stability at a point where production yields a fair compensation, that is, a compensation which is in proportion to the intrinsic value of the services rendered the community. 

If the price is proportionately too high, more goods of the kind involved will be produced or substitutes will be offered till the price comes down; if too low, the supply will be decreased till the price goes up and the right level is reached.

What applies to monopolies of commodities applies equally to monopolies of labor. Workers in any industry may put themselves into the breadline by charging 14

so much or producing so little that people cannot afford to employ them. The more they charge the more do they restrict the demand for their products. 

After brick masons raised their wages to twenty dollars per day and materially reduced the tempo of the work, most of them were glad to get a job at four dollars per day trundling a wheelbarrow pouring concrete where bricks were formerly used. If a man has ten thousand dollars to spend for a new home he will furnish employment for twice as many men and get twice as good a house if labor is paid five dollars per day than he would if it were paid ten dollars per day. 

In the latter case he might not even think it worth while to acquire a new home.

The mischievousness of industrial and commercial monopolization is universally acknowledged, and our statutes consequently denounce combinations for such purposes as crimes against society. This being so, it is unnecessary to discuss that subject any further at this time. But monopolization of labor has been considered justified, and labor unions have been accorded special privileges. In their combinations to restrain competition they are protected by law. This is the social error which needs airing. It is the principal cause of unemployment.

The social sin of the labor unions consists in establishing arbitrary rates of wages for some workers without regard to the proportionate value of the social service rendered. The Department of Labor reports that "labor" in the United States consists of over 

20,000,000 "gainfully employed persons," other than farmers, servants, professional people, and entrepre neurs. Of these, the American Federation of Labor 15

controls twelve per cent. The C. I. O. and Company

Unions and other unions constitute another ten per cent, making a total of some twenty-two per cent union members. But if we should count farmers and servants, not to mention professional people, as workers, the possible number of union members amounts to only about ten per cent of those "gainfully employed." 

Through the activities of about half of these organizations, that is to say, through about five per cent of those who labor for a living, the laborers have been divided into horizontal strata, with each stratum forced to remain upon a price level different from that of the others. These strata are so far apart that they cannot readily do business with each other. The members of the building trades, for instance, in many cities, have fixed the standard at from ten to twenty dollars per day below which they will not permit anybody to work at any of those trades. But the majority even of urban wage workers can get only four dollars per day-many much less, the great majority very much less.

Why More Cannot Own Homes 

Now, to illustrate, it must be obvious that a fourdollar man cannot hire a twenty-dollar man to build him a house, nor can he afford to live in a house built at that expense. He cannot well afford to pay rent in a house built at labor cost of more than fifty per cent over his own earnings. The result is that the fourdollar man is without adequate home and the higherpaid man is without a job. The occupants of the intermediary strata, who directly or indirectly depend upon the building trade, are thereby also thrown out of 16

employment. In the cities, eighty per cent of the members of the building trade unions are sometimes unemployed and a large percentage of these are on relief. They would not deign to work for less than standard wages. 

Rather let the low-paid man with a job support them. 

At the very lowest stratum is the farmer. He receives so little for his services that he cannot, except in rare instances, do business with either of those above him. He constitutes one-third of the population. 

He especially suffers through both wage fixing and price fixing.

What has been said about the building trade applies in principle to all other organizations which, by sabotage and racketeering, prevent work except on their own terms. The effect of such artificial wages upon the farmer, whose rehabilitation everybody professes to realize is necessary for the restoration of prosperity, has been sufficiently described in The Problem of Poverty to render further dissertation on that subject unnecessary at this place.

There is no more justification for the enforced disparity in the earnings of the various classes of urban laborers than there is for the forcibly established disparity in earnings between the farmer and the indus trial laborer. The horizontal stratification enforced prevents everywhere ready interchange of services and products.

Were the subject within the scope of this treatise it would be easy to prove that if capital should absorb all the benefits of labor it would strangle itself. It is equally easy to demonstrate that if wage-workers should confiscate all the products of labor and allow nothing to capital, they would starve themselves. 17

Here, as everywhere, right action is followed by agreeable consequences and wrong action by disagreeable ones. A just division is that which results in the greatest prosperity to both, and that is the division based upon proportionate merit. 

The two trades that are suffering the most from lack of patronage these days are railroading and building. These have the highest wage scale and the least to do. The latter is demonstrably a consequence of the former. One after the other of our railroads finds itself administered by the courts because it cannot pay its bills and its equipments are going to ruin for lack of funds to replace the senescent parts, such as rails, locomotives, and cars. As the direct result of the disproportionately high wages, it is not only the railroad employees themselves, but the coal miners, the iron miners, the steel mill workers, and locomotive and car manufacturers who are unemployed. But the average laborer cannot see that lower wages for the railroader would mean more work and more wages for everybody. 

As a mere illustration of this situation may be mentioned that for some twenty years prior to 1930 the Class I railroads of the country used to purchase an average of some 1200 locomotives annually. Since then their annual average has been only about four or five. Of the 52,500 locomotives owned by those roads, considerably more than fifty per cent are over twenty years old, worn, and out of date. Referring to this situation, President Hudson of the Baldwin Locomotive Company stated that "all the locomotive plants in the country will need to operate on full time for fifteen or twenty years to catch up with normal replacement." 18

This refers only to locomotives. The railroads are equally much in arrears with replacements of cars and rails. For very many years in the future hundreds of thousands of men could be employed in the shops, steel mills, and mines to put the railroad properties in sound and up-to-date condition. 

Why have not these men been put to work ere this? One reason is that the railroad employees receive, out of the earnings of the road, much more than they are entitled to when we measure the value of their services by what other employments requir ing no less skill and intelligence yield. They receive very much more than their legitimate share of the total products of the country available for wages. During 

1934, when wages were about ten per cent below the standard rate, the railroads paid out to their employees the sum of $1,544,219,621. If this sum were reduced by half a billion dollars, the employees would still be paid more than laborers throughout the country earn on an average today. The sum saved could be used, and would be used, to set locomotive manufacturers, car manufacturers, steel mill crews, and miners to work. If these in turn would reduce their demands some, the railroads would be enabled to purchase so much the more and put still more men to work. 

This illustrates how the disparity in earningsthe refusal of some to share the products of labor in proportion to the intrinsic value of services rendered -causes the present widespread unemployment.

But nature is relentless and is bound to have her way. Therefore, those who do work are forced to share, both directly and indirectly, with those who do not. Meanwhile, much of our man-power, for 19

which work and jobs are patiently waiting, is going to waste in idleness. As a result, we have less wealth to divide than we would have if everybody were busy producing something.

The government research experts reported in the fall of 1934 that there was then immediate demand for at least 1,750,000 new homes, and that this number increases by senescence at the rate of at least 350,000 per annum. At the same time it is demonstrable that if all our present available man-power were brought to bear on house building, we could construct only about 600,000 dwellings each year. At that rate it would take very many years to catch up to the present shortage.

We are not suffering from overproduction. There is more need of labor today than there ever has been. 

There are more jobs looking for men than there are men looking for jobs. Why, then, this stagnation?

Public Anxious To Buy 

There is something far more substantial than that lack of confidence which our financial interests would have us believe is the root of the evil. There can be no doubt that the heavy penalty placed upon real estate improvements in the form of property taxes is a factor in preventing the industry from resuming the activity which the country needs. But even in the absence of such burdens, the disparity between the wages and prices in the building trade and the earnings of the great majority of the public is sufficient to hold the industry back effectually. People cannot afford to build, because the cost of a proper dwelling is out of proportion to their own earnings. Nevertheless, these 20

solidly unionized trades block industry and refuse to go to work unless they receive several times more than their just share of what the industries of the country yield. 

More than forty per cent of the homes of the country have no bathtubs, 7,000,000 homes do not contain as much as a kitchen sink, and more than 10,000,000 have neither hot nor cold running water. Why?

Because the earnings of the occupants of those homes are far less than the wages demanded by those who produce those conveniences-they do not receive their share of the total products of labor. 

The above figures relative to homes do not, of course, take into consideration farmers. The latter are not generally expected to attain a standard of living where bathtubs and running water in the homes are within the aspirations of the occupants. 

The coal miners are at the present time demanding a thirty-hour week and one dollar per hour in pay as their share, while the farmer works at least eighty-five hours per week at ten cents per hour and generally much less. The significance of this is that if the two could deal with each other directly, and without taking transportation and other intermediaries into consideration, the farmer would have to work at least three hundred hours to produce what would be necessary to pay the coal miner for what costs the latter only thirty hours of work. He must work ten hours to earn as much as the coal miner earns in one hour. 

That is approximately the rate at which the farmer pays for coal when he ships his wheat and his corn and his cotton over the railroads. 

To put this disparity in a different form: The city pays the coal miner one dollar per hour for furnishing 21

coal, but pays the farmer ten cents per hour or less for furnishing food. Some may say that the disparity is greater, some that it is less. That is not of importance here. I am only illustrating the operation of a principle which both the coal miner and the farmer generally overlook. Neither seems to be able to see that his present predicaments arise from this very disparity in the division of the products of their mutual efforts. 

Those who know better try to remedy the maladjustment with artificially administered confidence, or to hide it by a barrage of mere blah. 

Not only do the labor organizations—that is, the part of them that is effective-appropriate to themselves out of the common fund what belongs to those less paid, but all forms of price fixing by private interests or government commissions are harmful because they lower the production of the wealth out of which wages must be paid. In a modern country, industry has become so complex that each person is but a cog, or a bolt, or a wheel in one great machine. When one fails to do his assigned part, the entire machine fails to function, or does so only inefficiently. 

A lockout does not affect only those in the strike; it affects, directly or indirectly, every member of society and reduces the common fund out of which all must draw their compensation. The transportation unions can shut down practically every industry in the coun try. Their salaries are not due to superior merit or higher intrinsic value of services rendered. These salaries represent what under other conditions would be classed as blackmail of a community placed at their mercy believing that the money comes not out of the pockets of the public but out of the pockets of a few financiers. 22

The business of common carriers partakes today but slightly more of the nature of public service than many of the productive industries. To the extraction of coal and iron are bound most of the other industries, directly or indirectly. The general effect might not be so immediate, because of the stock of supply on hand, but a lockout in those basic industries for any length of time would ultimately close down business generally.

Labor Should Have More Goods 

After all arguments against artificial wage scales or prices have been rehearsed, and we feel at last it has become clear that wages and prices are subject to economic laws which cannot be flouted with impunity, somebody will be sure to yank us back to where we started and talk about a "more abundant life." They will say that higher wages increase the purchasing power of labor.

So thoroughly has this sophistry permeated the mentality of the nation, that the average man seems to have lost all faculty for thinking in other terms. I have often observed that after all premises upon which such a conclusion originally rested have been swept away, the conclusion itself still remains.

So very many prominent journalists, publicists, and orators generally take it for granted that artificial wage scales enhance the purchasing power of the nation, that it may be pardonable again to review, though from a different angle, the fallacies upon which the doctrine rests. This, however, involves a repetition of principles which have already been fully stated. 23

Political leaders who talk about raising the purchas ing power aim to levy an assessment upon all the people for the benefit of a class. The advocates of artificial wage scales admit the increase means correspondingly higher cost of production. In other words, the increase in wages must come out of the general consumer. But, it is argued, what is thus collected from the public and given to a small class of wage-workers or stockholders is by the latter returned to the public in the form of increased consumption. There is no claim, and indeed none could honestly be made, that more is returned than is abstracted. If we were disposed to follow the funds in detail, we could show that less is returned because of the loss in the form of higher insurance on, and increased cost of marketing of, the higher priced commodities. 

As subjects for psychological study these economists are most interesting. They can see that if what is taken from the public is distributed equally among the people it would accomplish no good and would entail some waste. But when the funds so collected are given to only one class of citizens, they can see only the benefit to that class. The cost thereof to the public is too widely scattered to enable them to observe it in detail. So they console themselves with the hope that the cost to the individual is so small that it will not be felt. Any rise in retail prices restricts the market and diminishes the demand for the products. This diminishes the demand for labor to produce. 

Thus it may safely be asserted that one who proclaims that he can increase the wealth of the nation and give its citizens "a more abundant life" by merely augmenting industrial wages is either a simpleton or a fraud. 24

What Is a Fair Wage? 

My contention further is that the difference in compensation for services must be based upon difference in value to society. Too many factors enter into the element of intrinsic worth to enable the human intellect to determine the question of adequacy. However, the forces of nature will find the proper equation where supply and demand are allowed to operate unobstructed in their function of establishing and maintaining the equilibrium. The objection to fixed wage scales and prices is that they are artificial, forced, and in contravention of natural laws.

Nor is there any objection to the segregation of humanity into classes or castes, as long as the same takes place according to natural laws and in conformity to innate instincts and emotions. Only under such classification can each unfold the best there is in him and function to the best advantage of individual and racial growth and the attainment of the highest culture. But here again it is nature that must decide and not dull human reason.

I hope that by this time it has been made clear that monopolization of the labor market is just as mischievous as is monopolization of the commodity mar ket. Artificial price fixing in the labor market is as disastrous to business generally as is monopolization of the commodity market, and, as already noted, that the latter is destructive of social well-being is conceded.

This situation which confronts us today, however, is not new to this world of ours. Humanity has faced the same problem time and time again in every country 25

-in a little different form each time, perhaps, but in principle the same. We have some 6000 years of known human experience to look back upon and to learn from. 

Civilization has always started on the farm, as it did here. In response to the subdivision of labor, commerce develops and cities come into existence. The social intercourse offered in these more populous centers and the more varied opportunities for men of genius or wit to advance in the competition for place and position, attract men from the countryside. Capital accumulates and is expended on public and private works. Wages increase apace, while labor becomes less strenuous than in the fields. The disparity between compensations and prices accorded rural and urban populations grows steadily wider, and the exodus from the country is steadily stimulated by an easier life in the city.

As the countryside empties itself into the cities, the rulers, whether autocrats or democrats, soon feel themselves dependent upon the favors of the mass of the people and, out of fear of them, doles or relief work starts. The more the proletariat receives the more it demands. Bread and circuses are first supplied by politicians, out of private purses. Then next comes demand upon the State by the idle. Increased taxes and confiscations follow until the well-to-do are exterminated, and nothing remains in the city to feed the hungry. The countryside is next raided until agriculture is destroyed. When that is accomplished, the cities starve until their power ceases. That gives the farmer a new chance. Agriculture slowly revives, cities again grow up, the population flocks to the cities where work is easier and wages are higher. And so 26

the old drama is restaged in exactly the same manner as before.

What History Teaches 

The above, according to Sir William Mathews Flinders-Petrie, are the processes which were operating in Egypt through the ages, as far back as 4000 years before the Christian era. This was the experience of 

Assyria, Greece, and Rome. It has probably been the experience of every other race and country. Will it be the experience of America?

Rome is the typical exposition of both the evolution and the devolution of race and may be used as a pattern of the web which history has woven and must of necessity weave in every country.

The grandeur of Rome, the magnificent buildings and ruins of huge structures, temples, and triumphal arches, which tourists admire today, were the result of relief work carried on with public funds wrung from the taxpayers. These structures represent the rulers' fear of the proletariat, whose indolence and insolence increased pari passu with the free supply of more bread and more circuses. The expeditions of conquest were undertaken very largely for the purpose of bringing home loot to feed the maws of the city mobs and keep them from looting the wealthy or wellto-do classes. But it proved futile. It served only to whet their appetites and give them courage to make further demands. As the proletariat waxed bolder, work became easier and wages higher, which increased the malady by drawing more people from the country to the city, until there were none but slaves left to till the soil. 27

To keep the urban hordes from destroying and to silence the continually mounting demands, taxes were constantly increased until the wealthy and the wellto-do had been exterminated. With the confiscation of private wealth by taxation, thrift, enterprise, initiative were all discouraged until these virtues ceased to operate as active forces within the nation. As a consequence, the wealth of the entire community progressively shrank, until dire penury became the lot of all classes. The decriers of wealth found they had only succeeded in reducing everybody to want, and nobody was left who could hire laborers or purchase their products.

When nothing more could be squeezed out of the cities, the rulers raided the farms to feed the city proletariat, just as today the farms of Russia are raided to feed the industrial proletariat. That destroyed the farmer. The result was starvation for the cities, until they lost their strength to loot. This eased up on the countryside and agriculture was given a chance. In the course of the centuries, farming returned. Agricultural communities living on and off the soil came into existence. Subdivisions of labor and the concomitant commerce, with its cities, returned. The old drama would have been enacted anew in Italy during the last two decades had not Mussolini stepped in and put the proletariat in its place. 

How Long Will the United States Survive? 

From what we know of the past, we may deduce the rule that the persistence of a civilization depends how long the proletariat can be kept from gainupon 28

ing ascendancy in the cities. When it does, the civilization has passed its zenith and is approaching its end. 

When we hear and read more about Rome than about the other races that have come and gone, it is not because its experience was different, but because the records of its transactions, political and private, are better preserved. We know that the Romans repeatedly dealt with the same problems that confront us. Their land questions were the same, so were their moratoriums, inflation schemes, interest cancellations, debt reductions, taxing systems, et cetera. The speeches we hear from political rostrums of America today were delivered during similar times in Rome between 250 B. C. and 500 A. D. Yet, knowing where the ancients made their mistakes, we proceed, nevertheless, to follow in their footsteps, even as the son, in spite of the warnings of the father, insists upon making the same errors as his ancestor. But the rule still holds good, that a person's intelligence must be measured by his ability to profit by the experience of others. 

In reviewing the errors of Rome, Ferrero points out very clearly that the original mistake, the fatal one, was made in trying to satisfy the urban proletariat. "The result of that policy was," he asserts, "that life was artificially made easier and more comfortable in the cities, and harder and more difficult in the country, whereas the natural trend of circumstances would have produced the opposite effect. The evil treated in so ridiculous a way became worse. The exodus of the peasants into the city increased and brought a corresponding increase in the demand on the public purse for the amelioration of the conditions of the city life. 

The intensification of the evil was met by an increase 29

in the doses of the very remedy which aggravated ituseless expenditures in the cities, and ruinous taxes on agriculture. Matters went from worse to worse, until the system reached the limit of its elasticity, and the whole social fabric collapsed in a social catastrophe." 

There was only one way to deal successfully with the situation, says this learned analyst, and that was "that life in the cities should have been allowed to become unbearable to a certain number of citizens, so that they may be tempted to exchange it for life and work in the fields. But," he continues, "the Roman 

State could not bring itself to follow its natural course with the view of easing the urban proletariat, it took public works in hand in every direction, regardless of their utility. It distributed victuals free or at half price. It multiplied philanthropic institutions and engaged the wealthy families to initiate and assist it." * ...

With a change of names, this would be a very exact description of the outstanding features of what is going on in the United States.

As before stated, the errors of Rome were only the errors of her predecessors repeated. Up to the present, and as far as we have gone, the history of the ancients has reproduced itself in every detail in this country. 

With six thousand years of experiments in philanthropy and mollycoddling before us, we persist in making every blunder that was made by our predecessors. We never have been, and are not now, willing to let the laws of nature equalize earnings and living conditions according to merit; we are never willing to *The quotations from Ferrero are, with the permission of the publisher, taken from "Ancient Rome and Modern America," by Guglielmo Ferrero, published by G. P. Putnam's Sons, New York and London. 30

allow the law of supply and demand to establish the equilibrium between city and country. 

Lessons We Should Learn 

The sin of the Romans-the error which brought in its train their undoing—was their effort to nullify the law of competition, or, as it is usually called, the law of the survival of the fittest. Nature demands that individuals of certain anti-social, or at least unsocial, proclivities be eliminated. Against this the 

Roman politicians protested. Blind or indifferent to the system and methods of the law of evolution, they undertook to retain what nature was determined to reject. The result was that the whole race came under the interdict and was rejected. We may pardon the Romans on the theory that they acted through ignorance; but should the judgment upon us, who know better, be as mild?

Let us pursue these reflections a little further. 

During a period covering several decades in the immediate past, wages in some closely unionized industries in the cities have been steadily forced up. As a result of this artificial increase, cost of living in the cities has risen steadily. Other earnings have kept pace, because of the natural tendency to adjust all remuneration to what seems proportionate in value in each locality. The charges of professional men, accountants, day laborers, etc., were augmented by this tendency. The result was exodus from the country to the city, which produced increased unemployment in the cities and lack of laborers in the fields. 

With general high wages to those employed, people would rather go idle than work for lower wages than 31

others receive. Nor could one who needed to have work done, without disgracing himself in the eyes of the public, offer a man less than standard city wages. If he could not afford to do that, he would go without. 

Nor could men who had seen people receive from five to twenty dollars a day in the city, go back to the farm, whether to work for themselves or others. In either event the compensation would be far below the standard of the city.

At the present writing, we have a great many mil lion people on relief-nearly all in the cities-in spite of the fact that money, measured in "astronomical figures," has been poured out by federal, state, and municipal governments to create employment. And the situation is growing worse. Under the present system, it must inevitably continue to grow worse. 

Even if under artificial stimuli a temporary improvement should come, our reserves are being exhausted by tax levies, and only about fifty per cent of what is poured into the pump to prime it can be accounted for. 

As this is written, I have before me an article from the pen of a popular publicist, who sells a great deal of sobstuff to periodicals and newspapers. He is calling the nation to arms against low wages. Without trying to find the basic law governing wages and prices, or even suspecting that there may be such a law, he demands that minimum wage scales be fixed by statute and all wages generally increased.

To prove this necessity he catalogues a long list of employments where starvation wages are paid. He shows how employees in eating houses often, if not generally in many places, receive no other compensation than their meals; how house servants receive 32

only a few dollars per week; how competent stenographers receive twelve dollars per week; how women doing piece work sewing part time earn only two or three dollars per week.

Minimum Wage Laws 

All of this is undeniably tragic, to be sure, but what is the cause? Lack of minimum wage law, he proclaims, and seems amazed that nobody has thought of it be fore. It has never occurred to him that these people receive too little because others receive too much of the nation's products. It has certainly never dawned upon his intellect that there is a limit to that total which is available. It has never occurred to him that when some receive more than their share, others suffer, or that some must starve because others refuse to work, and refuse to let others work. It has never occurred to him that the people whose fate he laments receive so little because their employers are unable to pay more, and that it is a case of low wages, or nothing. There are hundreds of thousands of families who would like to employ house servants, but cannot afford it all because they have to pay too much for something else. For the same reason there are other hundreds of thousands of families who can afford such luxury only when the servants can be obtained at negligible cost.

Thus, without the slightest regard for the laws which operate in and govern the distribution of wealth, bales of irresponsible literature are turned loose on the public to give most naïve advice as to what ought to be done to regain prosperity. 

The physicians have at last learned that disease is 33

a maladjustment brought on by failure of "internal relations to adjust themselves to changing external relations," to quote Herbert Spencer, and that nature is ever striving to make the adjustment and restore the desired equilibrium. They have also discovered that their art must not counteract the laws of nature, but must be confined to facilitate their operation. To help nature is the physician's slogan today. 

The statesman has yet to learn that lesson. At least a great many of them need to learn it. They must learn that to heal economic maladjustment they must accept the economic laws as the pronunciamentos of the highest wisdom. They must step in line to support those laws by removing all human obstructions to their effective execution. These gentlemen will still discover that those laws are as ineluctable as the law of gravitation and cannot be violated with im punity. But, by the time they learn that, it is probable that we shall have a new crop of statesmen who also will imagine they are wiser than all their forebears. 

They will refuse to learn anything from those six thousand years of experimenting and experience which precede them.

The unemployment from which we suffer is the result of artificial wages and easier work in the cities than in the country, in the factories than in the field. 

The remedies we apply simply aggravate the evilexactly as in the Roman Empire. As was the case in the cities of the ancient world, so here today—there is only one remedy that will cure. This remedy is, as 

Ferrero insisted, to equalize the earnings so that life in the cities becomes so disagreeable for some people that they will have to find their way back to the farm. 34

This is the method of equalization provided by nature, but to the majority of our people it is that ugly face of reality from which the average person staggers back in dismay. In his hysteric anguish he will shout that the course suggested by Ferrero means revolution. 

If this be the correct estimate of the situation, it shows that we have already traveled so far in the wrong direction as to lose control of the government, and that we are sliding irresistibly down hill toward the brink of the abyss. If we are too weak today to handle the situation as it should be handled, we shall be weaker tomorrow, and the next day still weaker, for we are giving shelter to a vulture that is constantly eating further into our vitals. 

Americans Need Courage

But, granted that our government is too weak to discharge its sovereign functions, is it not better to have the revolution now than later? This nation has still a great deal of reserve moral strength. Its strong men and women have not yet been exterminated. We still have in a measure our master classes intact. These can yet be depended upon to take the lead in rebuilding. But, by the course we now pursue, they will in time be exterminated, even as in the races of antiquity. 

This is the inevitable end if we keep doping ourselves with sedatives and political nostrums, waiting for miracles to happen.

The question put plainly is whether American democracy is strong enough to grapple with these problems. The present time is ripe for such undertaking. 

There is no reason to hope that the future will present a more auspicious opportunity. Social justice would 35

raise the exchange value of farm products and make life in the rural districts more pleasant, while life in the city for many should become progressively less so. 

Thus would the direction of the migratory current be reversed. Even at this time, were the wages in the industries graded down to the rural level, there would be no unemployment for those who desire work. 

This operation would not be so painful as it may at first seem to timid people. As soon as the depression set in, the natural tendency in industries was to reduce wages in order to reduce the cost of commodities to meet the depreciated buying ability of the consumer. But the natural tendencies of trade are exactly what so many governments consider themselves especially delegated to stop. Politics is the only field in which the operators consider it their first duty to overthrow the laws of nature. And so, from Washington came a demand upon business to maintain the old 1929 scale of wages and prices at any hazard. Close the shop rather than lower the prices became the official battle-cry. It has been the official slogan ever since. 

It sounds so generous to the proletariat that it misleads them into thinking the proponents are their special patrons. When argument, inspired propaganda, and official command at last failed to have the necessary effect, the NRA came into being to prevent the operation of the laws of nature from making the required adjustment in wages and prices.

Had it not been for Washington's interference with the operations of the laws of nature in our economic life, the desired adjustment between city and country would have taken place naturally without revolu tionary pains or anything which approached them. 36

From to 1933, inclusive, there was a fairly strong migration from city to country. In 1934, after public relief got under way, that current again turned in the direction of the city. Already in the fall of 1934, the farmers were shorthanded. In the spring of 1935, they had still greater difficulty in finding men willing to work in the field. We then missed our best chance for adjustment between the shop and the farm, but we still have an opportunity to swing the evolution of this country into safe channels and hold it there. 

But it takes men to do that, and a willingness on the part of a fair percentage of the public to face the facts.

JOHN G. HOLLAND The Next Step 

What will the next step be?

The relationship between capital and labor-between the man, on the one hand, who advances the wages to the laborer until the products of the laborer's toil can be marketed, and the man, on the other hand, who performs the work does not come within the scope of this essay. In passing it may, however, be observed that when capital is unemployed, so is labor; when the demand for capital increases, so does the demand for labor; when the earnings of one are up, so are the earnings of the other; and whatever hurts 37

capital hurts labor, and vice versa. Some capitalists know it, but few laborers do. The economic problem involved is to establish the equilibrium between the two by giving the laws of nature free opportunity to maintain the balance by means of the free use of the moving equilibriant of supply and demand. Only thus can each get what is his due, and no more and no less. 

When at times it looks to the casual observer as if the producers do not receive the full benefit of the exhilaration of work supplied by technological improvements, it is due mainly to four factors, viz.: 

1. The expenditure of energy on new industries and enterprises necessary to the increasing population, which could not have come into existence in the absence of such improvements; 

2. The mounting cost of marketing, which seldom is less than 100 per cent over cost of production, and in most instances absorbs from 70 per cent to 80 per cent of the retail price of the products. In so many lines of industry the cost of marketing has increased faster than the cost of production has decreased; 

3. The ravages wrought by industrial controversies including strikes and lockouts, by speculation and lavish or wasteful living on the part of the rich, including many employers; 

4. The devastations caused by the financial robber barons, who produce nothing and live by preying on the industrious and the thrifty. 38

Did the Creator Make a Great Blunder?

Just how, under proletarian domination, the best stock of a race disappears, is not certain. We know that the families that furnish the leaders vanish and so do the gentry to which they belong. We know that a large number of them have been killed, as in Italy, France, and Russia. It is probable that many others cease to propagate. Hence, we have already a fair start down that same incline.

To quote Spencer again: "The production of the highest type of man can go on only pari passu with the highest type of society." Here it becomes natural to ask: "What is the type of society which produces the highest type of men?" In answer to this question nature clearly points to that system which by competition tends to eliminate the least fit. But it is that very system, inaugurated at the beginning of time, which our "liberals" and "progressives" and "humanitarians" believe was the Creator's blunder.

The evidence is overwhelming, even conclusive, that only under certain now well-known and understood conditions will the highest class of humanity procreate. It is also certain that even the very continuance of the race depends upon whether procreation of the best types of the citizenry proceeds as fast or faster than that of the lower types. If the latter procreate faster for any length of time, the race is certainly doomed. It may, therefore, be safe to assert where social conditions have become so perverted that the best types of men are being eliminated, and the weaker types are allowed to thrive, that type of 39

society which is designed to produce the highest type of men has ceased to exist. If we now assume that the aim of the Creator is to produce the perfect man, the method which "progressive" politicians have adopted certainly goes counter to the scheme of creation. 

The disparity in wages enforced by trade unions is probably the least of the evils minority groups may inflict upon the public. Under their domination we have already approached a point where the efficiency of certain workers is in inverse ratio to their wages. 

With utter contempt for the rights of the rest of humanity, they often strive to give a minimum of service for the compensation received and to make a job as costly as possible. Under such a system the least efficient or the least faithful stands as good chance to be employed as do the most efficient. They think thereby to overrule the law of natural selection. Hence 

I say that a government to survive must insist upon a free market for labor, commodities, and capital. 

There is at present in all so-called democratic countries a very distinct drift away from a government for equals to a government for a class. Special privileges are being handed out by democracies. It is immaterial whether these privileges be granted to the majority or only to a minority of the population. In either case the principle of equality before the law is being violated; a natural law is thus being broken; and punishment indubitably must follow. For many years we have been according special concessions to those whom the politicians with agonizing unction address as the "underprivileged." The effect of such legislation has of necessity been the very opposite of the one intended. 

For instance, we have undertaken to protect impecu 40

nious tenants against the just demands of their landlords. The result has been increase in rents for the laboring class. Losses are charged up to cost of operations. Under the present regulations of the relationship of landlord and tenant, all laborers who live in rented houses are forced to pay rent for the dishonest and the destructive tenants. The delinquencies of the few are charged up against the many. 

The Curse of Politics 

An outstanding example of special privileges is the exemption of labor unions and other groups from the operations of statutes denouncing monopolistic and other forms of restraint of competition. We have even relieved them from obligations to carry out the terms of any contract which they make collectively with their employers. The result has been increased unemploy ment, increased cost of commodities, increased tax burdens, lower per capita production, and a lower standard of living. 

Proletarian rule may aim at "the greatest good for the greatest number," but the effect of special privileges, even though these be reserved for the majority of the population, fail of their purpose and are disastrous because they are in contravention of cosmic forces. To a nation and its industries a proletarian rule is, in fact, more debilitating than a rule by an aristocratic class, because it tends to destroy all sense of industrial responsibility and cultural leadership. That is the reason why proletarian rule very soon ends in chaos first and then in dictatorship.

It is the competition of politicians for place and position which ultimately lands the proletariat in the sad- 41

dle. As aspirants for office they have been promised equality through a change in the form of government. 

So they destroy one form and set up another. They overthrow autocracy and set up democracy. But the promised equality does not make its appearance. They overthrow democracy and set up some other form of autocracy in the same vain hope. Changes are constantly made to attain the unattainable, until politics degenerates into nothing but unabashed fights for the emoluments of office and the opportunity for loot. 

The good of the public and the future of the race, no one any longer thinks of.

When government degenerates into individual means of oppression, all authority capable of maintaining order, soon disappears. Then follow long periods of time when the country is overrun and ravaged by bandits and brigands. The criminal element rides unrestrained, and industry and culture are both destroyed. Then communities band themselves together around some strong man for self-protection, and the smaller communities unite with others for greater strength and safety. Thus begin new governments.

Soon we see cities exercise complete sovereign functions either as oligarchies, or under a king, a doge, or a duke-if nothing better, under robber barons. In the course of the centuries, they become, for further protection, united under one mutual sovereign. 

And so, we commence to speculate as to what is likely to happen in this country when our government grows so weak that it can be intimidated by bands within its own borders.

But to finite human sense, the great tragedy of the breakdown of government consists in the destruction 42

of that part of the population that constitutes the generators and propagators of culture and employment. 

It requires many centuries for any race to produce strong and honest employers. It is done by the slow process of exudation, and unless the atmosphere is conducive to its growth, this class cannot exist. Where the conditions are favorable, it maintains order and promotes science, literature, and art, creating cultural values which, though distinct for each race, become the inheritance of all future races forever. 

It takes very little to start a country on the downward grade. Remove from any race the two or three per cent of the population which constitute the strong and creative employers, and the race will start on the road back to the jungle whence it came. When that happens it seems to mortal sense as if everything which has been gained is again lost. But it may not be so. If the successors have learned a lesson from their predecessors, there will be a modicum of gain. It is possible that each decaying civilization may serve to fertilize the ground for a better culture to come. 

But why go through more unnecessary experimentation and suffering to learn this lesson? Let us learn it now and profit therefrom. 43
