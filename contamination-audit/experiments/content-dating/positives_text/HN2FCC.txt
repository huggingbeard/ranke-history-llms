CHAPTER I THE PSYCHOLOGICAL BASIS OF THE COURSE THIS COURSE IS BASED UPON FUNDAMENTAL PSYCHOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES OF HOW THE PUPIL LEARNS 

Teaching consists essentially in making it possible for young people to learn effectively. Good teaching is nothing more, indeed, than providing the mental atmosphere as well as the physical environment in which the learning process goes on, and employing every possible agency to stimulate the active participation of the pupil in learning. Furthermore, the most effective environment is one in which the needed materials are made available to the pupil and where the atmosphere created by the teacher is one which draws out the heartiest participation on the part of the pupil. In short, "How do pupils learn?" is the fundamental psychological question which will orient the entire work of the teacher. It is, indeed, the pivot about which the content and organization of the course swing. 

It is important, therefore, that the teacher of the social studies be thoroughly acquainted with the facts of learning and also with the most widely accepted hypotheses concerning the organization of material.

Fortunately, a vast amount of careful investigation has been carried on during the past three decades, and much critical speculation, concerning the conditions which favor child growth. The result of this voluminous study of childhood is a considerable body of well-grounded knowledge concerning learning and teaching. Good teaching in the social studies, therefore, should make use of the following psychological principles. 3

The first principle: The pupil learns only by active assimilation 

There is one general principle that lies at the basis of all others, - that is, that a pupil learns only by assimilating the experiences which he encounters. This he can do only when he actively participates in them. Therefore the slogans of the new school are "activities" and "learning by doing." It is the active pupil, not the passive one, whom we desire in the social studies. 

Formerly the school was considered as a place in which the pupil sat with arms folded, eyes front, memorizing textbooks, putting up a hand if he wanted to move. The keynotes of the older formal school were "Pay attention!" "Order!” and “Learn by heart!" Not "What do you think?" but "What does the book say?" Instead of guided growth in ability to reason, the schoolroom seethed with restraint and repression. 

The active school is replacing the listening school. The classroom is being transformed into a laboratory, an open forum, a studio for self-expression. Correspondingly, the central concepts of the new education are individual experimentation, independent investigation, critical discussion, and creative self-expression. 

On the intellectual side, thorough active assimilation is the central psychological note. The pupil learns only as he assimilates new meanings, new facts, and new principles into his previous experiences.

Hence the new course in the social studies is essentially a stream of activities, a succession of things for the pupil to do. 

There is widespread reading from books, magazines, and newspapers; the vigorous interplay of minds in open forum and debate; the original preparation of briefs, outlines, summaries, and criticisms; the invention of new graphic methods of portraying the important facts and principles of how people live together. 

The new courses have discontinued the practices of the older ones in the memorization of encyclopedic classification of the facts on raw materials, products, resources, long lists of location facts and historical events, etc., for it was found that such practices

merely left in the mind of the pupil a cluttered up hodgepodge of isolated facts.

Therefore our new social-studies course is constructed on the principle of learning through activity. The Reading Books definitely practice pupils in the use of important meanings, concepts, and generalizations, while the Workbooks assure thorough assimilation through the carrying out of a wealth of individual and group activities. Opportunities for learning are not left to chance, they are definitely planned for in advance. 

There is an important corollary to this principle of active assimilation, namely, that growth in understanding and tolerance emerges only from constant practice in thinking, reasoning, and generalizing.

No task confronting the teacher of the social studies is more important than that of providing pupils with a continuous stream of opportunities to think, to draw generalizations from facts, and to apply them to the study of the problems of contemporary life. 

The task is not only of paramount importance, it is also one of great psychological difficulty. What is necessary in order to organize activities and materials in the social studies in such a way that they shall stimulate thinking? First and foremost, the arrangement of materials in thought-provoking form. The course must be organized around issues, problems, and unanswered questions. 

The pupil must be confronted constantly with alternatives. He must not only absorb facts, he must be constantly practiced in making decisions with facts. The ability to think critically about modern social problems will grow only through practice in thinking about modern social problems. The formation of attitudes as well as of meanings is an active matter. One develops an attitude of prejudice for or against immigrants, patriotic societies, the chamber of commerce, or the labor union, for example, only as he reacts toward them. One gets his attitudes toward people by living with them, and toward problems by studying them. It is impossible to conceive that an attitude can be built up merely by passive acquiescence.

The second principle: The situations of the school must be real and dramatic

The implications of the foregoing principle are clear: The work of the social studies must be shot through with as much firsthand experience as is possible. The pupil from the kindergarten through the college must deal as concretely as possible with the environment immediately surrounding him, the environment to which he is accustomed and which he can check directly. 

Only by much first-hand experience excursion, observation, vivid discussion - will he achieve a true understanding of the social world. 

In the study of the local community and region in which the pupils live, the application of the principle is direct and simple. 

In the development of an understanding of the world outside that region, however, the teacher confronts a grave difficulty. For most children first-hand observation and criticism of all the modes of living in the United States and in the rest of the modern world is impossible. For an understanding of all those modes of living which are far distant from the immediate environment of the pupil, the teacher and her class must rely upon pictorial material and dramatic word descriptions. The pupil will gain an appreciation of remote regions only through reading and discussion. The growth of understanding will be expedited or impeded in terms of the clarity or vividness of the description. Hence the imperative necessity of making materials of the social-studies course as dramatic, as vivid, and as dynamically interesting as possible. 

The third principle: Learning proceeds through the gradual accumulation of experience The pupil's understanding of the world in which he lives grows bit by bit as, day after day, he adds to his experience of it. No psychological generalization is more important than that one. 

The new education conceives learning in school, like the onward

movement of life itself, as an inductive, cumulative process. As we encounter new situations, new personalities, or new problems, we grope our way haltingly and tentatively into an understanding of them. Each new experience with them modifies the attitudes which we had previously assumed toward them, infuses new shades of meaning, or elaborates or reconstructs concepts which we had of them. Steadily the stream of experiences which make up the educative process shapes and reshapes understanding. 

We call this way of learning inductive in contradistinction to the basic deductive principle of the older methods. In the formal presentation of history, geography, and related social studies, growth was assumed to proceed by processes of deduction. Principles and generalizations were learned by heart and illustrations of these were drawn after the learning. The deductive methods assumed that increased power of generalizing would come from the learning of increasingly difficult generalizations. This was a fallacious assumption. The inductive methods, on the other hand, assume that young people will grow in the power of generalization only as they steadily accumulate a wider and wider background of meaning, and practice continually in drawing more and more complicated generalizations. 

This new course in the social studies, therefore, is carefully graduated in learning difficulty. If learning is to proceed effectively on the cumulative basis, the course must consist of a carefully graduated series of experiences. Learning must not be defeated at the outset by plunging the pupil into a mass of incomprehensible problems. He must first encounter experiences that are relatively simple, containing many familiar elements and abounding in content like that common to his previous experiences. As the study proceeds, however, subsequent activities will lead him step by step into a more abstract and mature investigation. It is an important corollary, therefore, of the principle of cumulative learning that the activities of the curriculum must be carefully graduated in difficulty.

The fourth principle: Every avenue of learning should be employed

This principle emerges directly from the principle of cumulative learning. If pupils are to understand the world in which they are living, then the course in the social studies must provide the pupil with a great wealth of illustration. 

To this end every avenue of learning should be utilized. 

Not only must there be much reading of vivid material, descriptions of modes of living, much reading of dramatic episodes, such as is found in autobiography and biography, books of travel, exploration, thrilling fiction, and historical romance, — but other learning devices should be utilized. The use of moving pictures and other pictorial illustration will contribute concrete imagery to the development of understanding. Paralleling the vivid word portrayals of social and economic life there will be a wealth of problem material evoked by the conditions met in the study. 

There will be much interplay of young minds in vigorous open forum. There will be much practice in dealing with graphic and statistical presentations of the quantitative aspects of modern life. The course will employ a great amount of individual research in library and museum, and in the concrete activities of the community. Not only will the assimilative capacity grow, the creative capacities as well will be drawn out through the writing of original compositions, the incorporation of much artistic expression, and in the invention of graphic illustrations. We repeat: every avenue of learning, every mode of assimilating the meanings of modern life, and every agency of expression will be utilized by the new social studies.

The fifth principle: Maximum growth in understanding 

To guarantee that learning is truly cumulative, that the pupil is constantly growing in understanding, in tolerance, and in critical judgment, the activities in which he participates must

be carefully organized. They must be planned so that the pupil will encounter frequently and in varied settings examples of the major concepts and themes, an understanding of which is to be developed. The curriculum must not only consist of stimulating experiences; those experiences must be selected which give greatest promise of illustrating the important meanings and generalizations. Through the study of these situations the pupil's grasp of meaning expands. He becomes increasingly mature. His power of reflective thinking, of critical judgment, grows through exact methods of using these meanings and generalizations together. 

Activities will be definitely arranged in the curriculum, therefore, on the principle of planned recurrence in varied settings. 

The sixth principle: Systematic and economical practice on the skills

The social studies contain some materials which, indeed, need to be practiced by systematic verbatim repetition. There is, for example, a body of location facts, some of which are used so frequently by most people that the school should be responsible for their thorough mastery. Precise imagery must be developed, for instance, of the location of the chief cities and ports, the key deposits of coal, iron, and other important natural resources, lanes of ocean trade, strategic railroad lines, important industrial and agricultural regions, etc. These facts of location are to be learned by systematic repetition in economical practice devices. 

Similarly this new course in the social studies provides definite exercises in the reading and making of maps and the building up of exact ideas of chronology through time-line exercises. 

The seventh principle: Learnings develop simultaneously 

Learnings are never single; they are always many-sided. Even in learning to add 7 and 8, for example, a child is not only forming a specific addition association; he is at the same time learning in many other ways. He is developing habits of intellectual work;

TEACHER'S GUIDE 

attitudes are being formed toward the number system, toward the society which produced it, toward the teacher who is teaching it, toward the school for insisting that it be learned, etc. If true in arithmetical computation, how much more applicable is the principle in the case of the activities and experiences of the social studies. Every increment in learning in the entire course is a complicated integrated matter. Take, for example, the reading of dramatic episodes and the consequent acquiring of meanings, generalizations, and points of view. Every such experience is not only an intellectual matter, it is thoroughly shot through with emotion, with the attitudes of approval, disapproval, enthusiasm, and so forth. In such situations understanding may even be determined by ancestral prejudices or group affiliations, by personal likes or dislikes. Many illustrations are at hand to support the principle that even the most minute unit in the social-studies course must engage the pupil in a wide variety of learnings. 

The eighth principle: The intensive study of a few things 

Thorough mastery of a problem comes only from prolonged study of it. Most of the problems and modes of living in modern civilization are exceedingly complicated and difficult to understand. Because of the rapid multiplication of printing facilities there is available a tremendous mass of written illustration of contemporary life, much more than can be incorporated in any curriculum. The teacher, therefore, is compelled to select the examples which he would have his pupils study. The examples which constitute the nucleus of this new course have been chosen on the basis of careful analyses of adult needs, of scientific studies of learning, and of the judgment of authorities in history, geography, government, economics, etc. 

In the organization of the course, therefore, another important principle of learning is utilized. This principle postulates that understanding grows more effectively through the intensive study of a few matters than through the superficial study of many

matters. Take map locations, for example: in place of the learning of thousands of map locations, the new courses substitute the thorough mastery of the locations of a few crucially important ports, mineral deposits, railroad and other transportation lines, rivers, etc. Likewise, instead of studying in encyclopedic fashion all the industries of the United States, we concentrate our study upon the key industries - coal, iron and steel, machine agriculture, etc.

This principle finds one of its most important applications in connection with growth in the mastery of meanings and concepts. 

Here too the problem of what to teach is of central importance. 

Specialists in the social sciences are cognizant of no less than millions of meanings and thousands of generalizations. An understanding of modern life, however, depends upon the grasp of a few hundred of the central concepts. It is the task of the teacher, therefore, to discover those concepts and meanings which are of greatest importance, to assemble concrete illustrations of them, and to practice young people in thinking about them and with. them. Witness the importance of such concepts as the rise and spread of industrialism, the changing standards of living, the importance of trade in shaping civilization, the migration of peoples, etc. These are illustrations of the basic meanings and concepts, a wealth of interpretation for which must be built up through the work of the social studies. 

The ninth principle: Attention centered upon one thing at a time

This new course in the social studies is organized in a series of definite units of work. Young people deal with abstract matters more clearly if they can concentrate their attention upon one principle or one theme at a time. Accordingly, in this general course the pupil is confronted at any one time by only one important aspect of life, by one central movement or theme, by one fundamental concept or generalization. In each unit of work,

the units varying in length from one day to two or three weeks, the pupils' minds are concentrated on one task. 

Likewise in each problem the pupils' attention is centered on one topic. Note, for example, in An Introduction to American Civilization and in the corresponding Pupil's Workbook that one chapter and its parallel problem are devoted to the intensive study of 

America's railroads. Another chapter deals with America's workers and their work. Each chapter assembles in close relationship only those facts, generalizations, and historical materials which are needed for a clear understanding of that particular topic. Thus attention is concentrated, learning is efficient, and mind-wandering and confusion are avoided. 

The tenth principle: Courses should be organized upon "understanding units" To achieve the general aim of the course-tolerant understanding the activities and materials are organized in what might be called "understanding units." The older courses in geography, history, and civics divided the work into geographic, political, or economic units. Geography, for example, was studied by states, by political units; more recently by economic and geographic regions. This made it inevitable that the facts of the political units of the work were studied in isolation from one another. Similarly, history courses were primarily political history, the story of the development of government. Industrial or commercial history was frequently organized as a separate course; the same was true of industrial or commercial geography. 

In the new courses, however, only one question has been asked to determine the organization of units of work, namely, What facts, generalizations, and historical materials do young people need to know to understand the topic under consideration? 

Hence the course is broken up into understanding units. 

Consider, for example, the organization of the first book of the series, An Introduction to American Civilization, and its corre-

sponding Workbook. In them American civilization is visualized as a whole, and the factors which produced it are studied. Instead, therefore, of breaking the United States up into geographic units or into states and studying each one separately, these books organize the pupils' work around "the four chief factors of American civilization”: (1) our fortunate location in a stimulating and productive climate, (2) our vast size and varied natural resources, (3) our many people, and (4) the scientific knowledge which brought about the Industrial Revolution. Activities of many kinds are organized in the endeavor to bring pupils naturally into an understanding of these chief factors. The study of each factor is an understanding unit. Each one includes historical, economic, geographic, and social material, all of which is assembled in natural learning relationships. The teacher will make use of any material that he needs to aid pupils in understanding. Any material, we repeat. If, for example, the understanding of a given topic can be made clearer by a brief historical sketch, let us interject that historical sketch at the opportune moment. 

For instance, in this new course in the social studies the concept of the rôle of modern mechanical power is clarified by sketching how man learned to invent engines and how he made power before the days of those inventions. Similarly, to aid the pupil in understanding how machines have transformed the selfsufficient agricultural modes of living into our interdependent industrial civilization, a short introductory history of the Industrial Revolution is interjected.

These, then, are the basic principles of learning upon which the new course has been organized.

CHAPTER II : THE FRAMEWORK OF THE COURSE 

The materials of the first semester's work are presented in three books the Reading Book (An Introduction to American Civilization), the accompanying Pupil's Workbook, and the Teacher's Guide. The three books have been constructed together; each unit of any one has a corresponding unit in each of the others. 

The content of each book is based upon prolonged investigations of what to teach and how to organize the materials of the social studies. The conclusion appears to be justified that every unit of this first semester course is absolutely necessary to an understanding of American economic life. Each of these three books, therefore, has been written to fill a pressing need. 

The Reading Book constitutes a carefully selected and organized body of information concerning the factors that have brought about American civilization. 

The Workbook contains a selected but comprehensive list of suggested pupil activities, tests, and other learning exercises. 

The Teacher's Guide assembles the established psychological principles upon which teaching in the social studies must be based, together with a wealth of definite suggestions for conducting the course. The teachers should have each of these three books, for each has a unique function. 

The Reading Book is a comprehensive and organized introduction to the study of American economic life The teacher should bear in mind constantly that An Introduction to American Civilization has been constructed to center the pupil's study upon the important aspects of American life. 14

Therefore throughout his study the pupil should be constantly oriented with respect to the whole of American civilization and the factors which produced it. To guarantee that, this course has been definitely organized about the chief trends or historical movements and the chief themes of contemporary life. It is of the utmost importance that the teacher understand clearly the manner in which these themes have controlled the choice of content and the organization of material. 

The course has been organized around the following basic economic themes of American civilization 1. The tendency of the natural physical environment 

topog- raphy, climate, etc. to determine the character of man's economic and social activities. 

2. Man's increasing control over nature, including the improvement in the means of producing goods, of transportation, and of communication. 

3. The rapid rise of industrialism in Europe and North America and its gradual adoption in the other continents. 

4. The transformation in industrial civilization from small selfsufficient communities of peoples into a fragile world society of interdependent people. 

5. The trend toward specialization of labor and standardization of machine manufacture under the Industrial Revolution. 

6. The rapid rise in the economic standard of living produced by the Industrial Revolution. 

7. The tendency toward great concentration in the organization and control of business. 

8. The development of mass production of goods by means of division of labor, standardization of machine manufacture, and increasing accumulation of corporate capital. 

9. The wasteful exploitation of our natural resources forests, coal, oil, and gas. - the soil, 10. The trend toward shorter hours of work, higher wages, and better conditions of work, brought about in part by the organization of labor.

11. The startling increase in population in the United States with the development of the Industrial Revolution. 

12. The rapidly increasing concentration of people in towns and cities, especially since 1850. 

13. The rapid immigration from Europe into the United States, especially since 1800. 

14. The development of a complicated nation-wide and world-wide system of exchange of goods.

These are the fundamental themes which have characterized the development of economic life in the United States. Not one can be omitted from a comprehensive course which undertakes to introduce young people to an understanding of our ways of living.

Note that through them all the one central idea is that of change change in the way in which people obtain food; change in the way they produce goods; change in the way they travel, transport things, and communicate; change in their methods of trade; change in their standards of living; change in their dependence on other people. All these changes are summed up in the title of the first chapter of the Reading Book and in the first sentence of the last chapter, "We live in a new and changing civilization."

Each unit of the course illustrates one or more central themes To guarantee clarity of understanding, the course has been arranged in nine units of work (see Contents of the Reading Book). Each of these units of work consists of readings and pupil activities definitely arranged to illustrate one or more of the basic themes. For example, Unit III, "An Introductory Study of Power," illustrates the following themes: (1) man's increasing control over nature, (2) the rise of industrialism, and (3) the exploitation of our natural resources. Indirectly, of course, Unit III provides material which is used in later units in illustrating other themes of the course; for example, such themes as

(1) the change from self-sufficiency to interdependence, (2) the trend toward specialization and standardization, (3) the rise of the standard of living, (4) the development of mass production, (5) the concentration of people in cities, etc.

Each unit of work employs historical as well as current material Note, furthermore, that in illustrating fundamental themes of American life, two kinds of material are used in each of the units of work: first, historical; second, contemporary. The themes themselves, therefore, are twofold in nature. Throughout the course our work is organized on the principle of really using history to explain current life. Children will come to understand economic life today only by seeing the chief movements of American life in clear historical perspective. Scan the Reading Book and note how this principle is applied in each of the chief units. 

It is employed even in Chapters I and II by means of sharp contrasts. It is used definitely in Unit III ("An Introductory 

Study of Power") and in every following unit.

Learning in the social studies necessarily consists in part of acquiring information. The maximum growth of meaning, therefore, becomes one of the chief goals of teaching in the social studies. Through reading, excursion, and open-forum discussion the pupils are constantly adding to their information, that is, to their store of meanings. To guarantee that this accumulation of meanings shall be as rich and as vivid as possible the Reading Book presents a wealth of dramatic episodes, narratives, bar graphs, time lines, and cartoons. Thus thousands, indeed,

TEACHER'S GUIDE 

hundreds of thousands, of meanings are gradually and surely built into the experience of the pupil. 

With such a wealth of meanings developing, however, both teachers and pupils need to have "cue meanings," or catch phrases, that is, concepts, which sum up succinctly many meanings; for example, "standard of living," "changing civilization," "industrial country," "mass production," and "specialization of labor." To each of these cue meanings of broad scope we give the convenient name concept. Each of these is a summarizing term an abbreviated symbol - which stands for a great wealth of meaning and with which we do our thinking. When a person uses intelligently the phrase standard of living it represents scores, perhaps hundreds, of specific meanings in his mind. 

The concept standard of living is merely a brief symbol standing for many meanings like these: 

Amount of food available; same for clothing, shelter, etc. 

Kinds of food available; same for clothing, shelter, etc. 

Wages received 

Length of working day 

Leisure time available to most people 

Comforts and luxuries 

Etc. 

Of course each of these terms also stands for other still more specific meanings. The meaning food, for example, can also be regarded as a concept, the understanding of which depends upon many specific meanings like wheat, flour, bread, cereal, cattle, meat, beef, steak, etc. The development of meaning, therefore, consists in the building up of a rich experience through which delicate nuances of understanding are distinguished. 

We emphasize, therefore, the rôle of concepts in the teacher's organization of his course. To guarantee the maximum understanding of the factors of American civilization, the teacher must have in mind a clearly arranged outline. The intellectual basis of this outline is the central concepts, an understanding of which is

to be developed by the material of the course. Because the complete list of specific meanings would be so long and complicated as to be unwieldy, we have organized the discussion of the teaching of units of work about the cue concepts. The list of concepts is short, averaging not more than ten to twenty for any one unit. 

The teacher would do well to have these cue concepts definitely in mind in organizing the discussion of each problem and of each unit of work in the course.

Each unit of work also illustrates several basic generalizations The teacher will also find frequent reference to a third means of organizing the course, namely, basic generalizations. 

In the past too much of the work of pupils in the social studies has consisted in memorizing specific facts. In the preceding section we laid great emphasis, it is true, upon the importance of building up a mastery of facts, meanings, and concepts. Understanding of modes of living and problems of American life, however, will never be guaranteed by the mere learning of isolated facts. It is the relationship between facts that is of greatest importance. As an instance take the important problem of understanding the development of cities in the United States. 

It will not be sufficient for pupils merely to learn a multitude of facts about cities and their growth, such as population figures, and facts of manufacturing, trade, transportation, and communication. Really to understand basic aspects of the life of the city, one must know such relationships between these facts as the following: 

1. Cities grow up near important mineral deposits. 

2. Cities grow where transportation facilities are favorable. 

3. Cities grow at good trading points. 

4. A good trading point must have good transportation routes, must be near markets, and must have a rich hinterland. 

5. To become a center of manufacturing a city must be near sources of power, receive raw materials easily, and have available markets for its goods.

TEACHER'S GUIDE 

These are only a few of the many generalizations that can be made to sum up the essentials needed for cities to grow. Really to understand why cities grow, one must have deduced for himself many generalizations like the foregoing. Note that a generalization is merely a statement of relationship between two or more concepts. Take the foregoing examples: The first generalization is a statement of relationship between the concept cities and the concept important mineral deposits. The fourth is a statement of relationship between the concepts (1) good trading point, (2) good transportation routes, (3) nearness to markets, and (4) rich hinterland.

In each of the chapters of this Teacher's Guide which apply to specific units of the Pupil's Workbook, therefore, the teacher will find a succinct list of the most important generalizations which have been illustrated in the various problems. If pupils are to be tested for mastery of the material in the course, the test should concentrate upon understanding the chief themes, concepts, and generalizations, and understanding will be indicated only when a pupil can illustrate clearly each of these. 

The teacher should understand, therefore, that themes, concepts, and generalizations are used merely as the basic intellectual skeleton of the course. (See Chapter III of this Guide for the outline of activities.) They provide a succinct outline of American civilization. Since the goal of the whole course is an understanding of economic life in the United States, the use of such an abbreviated outline will facilitate the work of teaching. Nothing is more important, therefore, than that the teacher concentrate his attention upon these fundamentals. All the intellectual activities of the pupil should be organized with the understanding of these as the chief outcomes to be sought.

Important note concerning the phrasing of generalizations The teacher will observe that the generalizations have been written for teachers rather than for pupils. We have felt that these ideas were so important that they should be phrased in concise, adult language. Therefore we would caution the teacher that these generalizations are not to be learned verbatim. Pupils should practice the skill of drawing generalizations like these from the study of the chapters of the Reading Book and of the problems of the Workbook; but of course they will use their own less mature vocabulary.

The Workbook suggests an orderly plan of activities Experience invariably teaches the value of a planned program of work. Skillful teachers have learned that only by careful planning can the desired outcomes be achieved in the social studies. 

The lack of a plan is almost sure to guarantee a chaotic organization in the minds of the pupils. 

Believing, therefore, that the teacher will welcome an outline of the activities that have been found by experimentation to be most valuable, we have assembled them in systematic and orderly fashion in the Pupil's Workbook. These have not been set up in the expectation that every class or every pupil shall engage in each activity suggested. The aim of the authors is to provide the teacher and his pupils with a varied and stimulating list of things to do from which the teacher, the class, and the individual pupils can choose, or which they can use as suggestions to be supplemented or superseded by others.

An important caution: The Workbook and the Teacher's Guide are merely collections of suggested activities These suggested activities are not to be slavishly followed by the teacher. He should feel free to adapt the program and activities to the needs and abilities of his particular class. The preparation of the Workbook and of the Teacher's Guide has not been undertaken with the thought of prescribing rigidly in advance the content of the course, the sequence of lessons, or even the order in which units of work are to be taken up. On the contrary, the teacher should regard the Reading Book, the Workbook, and the Teacher's Guide merely as a wealth of optional suggestions, as a comprehensive list of activities from which to develop a vital and stimulating semester's work.

The course should by all means spring out of the real needs of pupils and should develop hand in hand with their emerging wants and abilities. Let the pupils feel that it is their course. At every point in the course the pupil's initiative and originality should be encouraged. Get them to recognize the importance to them of studying the factors which have produced the civilization in which they live. Get them to regard the Reading Book as merely a voluminous outline moderately lengthy and dramatic in itself, but after all, merely an outline, around which they are to assemble much new material. Each unit of work as presented in the Reading Book is merely an orderly summary of the chief meanings, concepts, and generalizations which educated minds need in order to consider intelligently modes of living in the United States. The study of each problem is oriented about a few important central questions Note that the semester's course is divided into thirty suggested problems, each of which corresponds to a chapter in the Reading Book. To guide the study of each of these problems, two or three central questions are stated. The work of the pupil is immediately stimulated by the task of finding his own answers to these questions. For answering these questions the corresponding chapter of the Reading Book supplies the basic information. This chapter presents historical and current discussion of the problem contained in the Workbook. The pupil is encouraged first to read the chapter rapidly, that is, to skim to discover the broad sweeping themes, the chief concepts and generalizations. Thus his reading is guided by the central questions with which he has been confronted. Sometimes his reading is checked by the task of selecting the three main ideas of the problem.

The objective tests in the Workbook stimulate individual learning and group discussion Note that in each problem of the Workbook are included several objective tests. These tests have been designed with great care to bring out the chief concepts and generalizations necessary to an understanding of the problem. They serve to check the pupil's mastery of the content of the chapters. The tests focus the pupil's attention definitely upon important facts and principles. They have been so designed as to give the pupil opportunities to use his facts in new settings. Furthermore, they are objective, economical to score, interesting to pupils, and because of the form in which they are presented they make it possible for the pupil to spend most of his time in thinking rather than in writing. 

An important suggestion at this point: the tests should be used primarily as a means of stimulating vigorous class discussion, as well as of checking the pupil's mastery of the content of the chapter. They serve the latter purpose excellently, for they have been designed to canvass systematically all the important principles and concepts of the problem. Nevertheless, their most important function is to provide a definite basis for class discussion. By all means have class discussion immediately of pupils' responses to these tests. Experience has shown that the true-false test, the completion test, and the matching test provide excellent opportunities for the defense of pupils' points of view, and for clarifying and applying their knowledge of important concepts and principles.

Should the tests be corrected regularly and scores totaled? 

Again, of course, this is left to the discretion of the teacher, but there are proved advantages to doing so, at least occasionally. 

First, definite stimulus is given to thoroughness in learning; second, the record of the pupil's score provides him with a stimulus for further achievement; third, the pupil's confidence grows in his understanding of the problems of American civilization; fourth,

his sense of personal responsibility develops, that is, he grows in skill in managing his own affairs. 

A variety of methods may be employed in scoring tests. First, pupils may score their own papers. Second, the pupils may exchange papers with one another, each pupil correcting a classmate's paper and totaling the score. By either of these two methods the correct answer should be read by the teacher. Third, the teacher may correct and score the papers. 

There are arguments in favor of all these methods. In the first and second, the pupils are interested and are critically engaged, and there is a direct appeal to their sense of fairness. The third method is suggested as an occasional procedure, particularly where a teacher is obliged to give school marks in the social studies, for less injustice will be done when scores are based on the results of objective tests.

After the scoring has been done and each pupil is in possession of his own paper, an opportunity should be given the pupils to ask questions and discuss any obscure points on the test. 

No teacher should expect all pupils to make perfect scores on each of the tests. The tests of every problem contain items of varying difficulty, for they have been designed to cover the maximum range of a pupil's ability in the seventh grade.

A popular method of assigning scores is on a "per cent correct" basis. However, a better way of scoring the work of this course is by the "points correct" device. These point scores can be included in the Pupil's Workbook and in the teacher's record book, and the half-yearly average drawn up in per cents in one operation at the end of the semester. 

It is recommended that occasionally the teacher list in order (from high to low) the scores made in the objective tests in a given problem, indicating the middle score and the high and low fourths. By this means pupils are enabled to get their class rankings. However, a too frequent use of this device is likely to discourage slow pupils.

The use of time lines

The study of history has no more important purpose than that of building up in the minds of pupils a clear grasp of the sequential development of events. This need for mastery of historical movements and trends has been one of the chief purposes which led the authors to include historical material in each of the units of work in the Reading Book. The Workbook, therefore, suggests the frequent use of time lines as an important aid in helping pupils to achieve a clear grasp of historical developments. 

The social studies provide three ways in which time lines may be used: First, the almost daily location of events and movements on blackboard time lines. Teacher and pupil should get the habit of illustrating chronological development by a quick, spontaneous graphic representation on the blackboard. Second (suggested in the Workbook), the occasional location of a sequence of important events dealing with a single movement, for example, that of the invention of machines, or the development of land transportation, or of instantaneous communication. Definite provision is made for pupil activity of this type in practically every unit of work. 

Third, in addition to the two foregoing applications of the time line, an important activity is proposed in the construction of the term time line. This consists of the location of a few important events on a permanent time line to be started early in the course by each pupil. This time line will present at any stage of the course the key events in the historical development of those aspects of civilization which have been studied up to that point. 

The recurring use of time lines will serve in two ways, therefore, to orient the pupil with respect to chronological development: first, by relating in definite time sequence the most important events of a particular movement; and second, by relating one movement to another. For example, not only will the pupil come to visualize the chronological development of the invention of engines and machines, or of the invention of new means of transportation, but he will also grow in his understanding of the rela-

tion between these various movements. He will see the development of city populations, for instance, as a movement that had a direct relation to the invention of new modes of transportation and communication, to agricultural machines, to the increase in scientific knowledge, etc. Hence the proved importance of using time lines.

The use of map exercises The pupil must be oriented with respect to both time and space. 

As the time line helps to orient him with respect to time, so the map exercises help to orient him in space. 

The Reading Book provides a wealth of maps, each one specially designed to give the pupil his bearings with respect to the location facts of the topic under discussion. The corresponding problem of the Workbook provides the pupil with corresponding map exercises. Through these exercises he will be aided in mastering the needed map locations. Map exercises as definitely designed as these should never be omitted. Since there are many more map locations than can or should be learned in school, in each exercise only those map locations have been incorporated which have been proved by careful scientific study to be of greatest importance for the understanding of American civilization. The practice of the pupil should be concentrated upon those few location facts that are of proved social importance. 

Furthermore, since the understanding of many important problems of current life will be defeated by lack of orientation as to location, it is of the greatest importance that definite mastery of the essential location facts be guaranteed. The teacher will, of course, supplement the individual maps in the Reading Book and the planned exercises in the Workbook with the constant use of globes, wall maps, and other special maps in the classroom.

The use of open forums

No outcome from the work of the social studies is more important than that brought about through open-forum discussion. 

The Workbook suggests for every problem in the book a vigorous open forum.

The teacher will recognize three important outcomes to be gained from the frequent use of group discussion. 

First, there is the practice to be gained by each pupil in alert and active thinking on his feet. This is a skill used by all people throughout life, and the social-studies class provides a rare opportunity for its maximum development. 

Second, there is the development of attitudes of far-reaching importance. No one of these is more important than that of tolerant understanding. The development of tolerance, indeed, grows only through practice in thinking with people. Only through the frequent interplay of mind on mind can grow attitudes of open-mindedness or of willingness to listen, of suspended judgment, of recognizing the inevitable difference in the points of view among people, of seeing that no one person is always right, and of coming to believe that many problems can be solved by cool and critical discussion. Tolerance has a twofold aspect: first, open-mindedness; second, critical-mindedness. "Not a mind so open that it is drafty," not a mind "open at both ends," but a mind that, while receptive to the ideas and convictions of others, scrutinizes all ideas and convictions with ruthless rigor.

In this connection note the suggested use in the Workbook of slogans or catch phrases to help orient open-forum discussions. 

For example, "Light not heat!" "What are the facts?" "What is the authority for your statement?" "Are your sources of information valid?" Throughout the open-forum discussion, pupils should be encouraged to use these slogans.

aspect of the suggested pupil activities. A considerable number of pupils in every class will find time, in the study of most of the problems of the course, for independent reading and investigation. Especially in the management of large classes teachers will find these suggested topics for individual research of definite help. The making of special reports is an important device for the stimulation of open-forum discussion.

Pupils are brought to define their knowledge more clearly by the invention of original graphic devices, such as cartoons and pictographs, by the writing of original descriptions, narratives, stories, summaries, by informal and spontaneous dramatization, by the collection of news items and of other material on current events, etc.

They will serve in an important way to stimulate interest in the chief problems of the day, an interest which may well continue throughout life if founded in these younger years.

If properly cultivated, this habit of reading critical books and magazines would lead to permanent concern in the problems and issues of modern life.

CHAPTER IV FACTS IN THE SOCIAL STUDIES 

All too frequently in the past pupils have been forced to memorize a large number of facts. In parrotlike fashion they read and recited verbatim the facts from the textbook. Facts were seldom encountered by pupils in thought-provoking situations. Facts were learned in isolation, with little attempt to show relations among them. Facts were presented in an abstract setting; they were not incorporated in a great variety of concrete illustrations. 

Facts were prosaic and uninteresting; they did not arouse the intellectual curiosity of children. Little wonder that pupils memorized a host of unimportant specific facts that were soon forgotten; little wonder that they failed to master a few central facts needed to understand modes of living in the modern world. 

In the social studies the teacher confronts many questions concerning the use of facts. What facts should pupils learn for permanent use? What facts does a well-informed person need to know? How does one get at the facts about a social situation?

What are the best sources of facts? To these and similar questions the teacher must formulate tentative answers.

4. WHAT IS THE NATURE OF FACTS IN THE SOCIAL STUDIES? 

There are three sources of difficulty in solving social problems: the complexity of the situation, the unreliability of sources of information, and the knowledge and attitudes of the observer. 

For convenient reference, we are enumerating certain characteristics of facts in the social studies: 

1. Facts in the social studies are "true" relatively, not absolutely. 

Regarding certain matters we can approximate the "truth" more closely than in others. For example, we can attach more certainty of truth to the statement "The coal production of the United States in 1929 was 609,000,000 short tons" than to the statement "Until 1900 the American people showed little interest in empire-building." When facts are qualified we are more likely to achieve the truth. Therefore, in describing situations pupils should learn to use qualifying words and phrases like the following probably true, probably false; always, usually, sometimes, seldom, never; all, most, many, few, none. They should specify as exactly as possible the time during which the statement holds in 1620, in 1775, in 1920; between 1750 and 1850; during the last ten years, for hundreds of years, since the discovery of America. They should designate as precisely as possible to what the statement applies: the Eastern Hemisphere, Europe, England, the eastern United States, the Mississippi River Valley. They

FACTS IN THE SOCIAL STUDIES 

47 should describe exactly the person or the groups of people involved: Benjamin Franklin, the white race, the Indians, Americans, German business men, Russian fur-traders. Thus will pupils grow in the habit of precise statement of fact. 

2. Facts in the social studies are frequently changing. Old facts are revised; new facts are acquired. Year after year statistics of industry, of commerce, of population, are changing. As time goes on, the people in agricultural communities modify their attitude toward machine manufacturing. The unemployment situation in the United States assumes new aspects from year to year. We live in a changing civilization. The implication for the social studies is direct: statements of fact and opinion must be tentative. Pupils must be open-minded to new information on both past and present events. 

3. Facts in the social studies are often difficult to ascertain. In some countries there are no statistics of population which are exact. There are no complete statements of the general economic condition of other countries. These are "facts" difficult to secure. 

Likewise, many historical facts are hard to verify, - such, for example, as those concerning modes of living in the Americas centuries before the coming of the Europeans. Historians have unearthed here and there bits of evidence which have been brought together into more complete descriptions, but many of these descriptions rest upon incomplete evidence. Of this fact pupils in the social studies should be aware. 

4. The facts of a situation are sometimes obscured by the bias of the reporter. Historians sometimes fall into error. Writing of the battle of Waterloo, English historians attributed the defeat of 

Napoleon to the English general, Wellington; Prussian historians, to the Prussian military leader, Blücher; French historians had still another version of the encounter. 

Impress upon the pupil the fact that the trained investigator as well as the layman faces many difficulties, and, to form as impartial a judgment as possible, the following six conditions must be observed by him:

1. The social student must be familiar with the background of the problem. 

2. He must consider all points of view and examine them critically and impartially. 

3. He designates the sources of information in terms of their reliability and their bias. 

4. He recognizes the origin of attitudes and information, both of himself and of other people. 

5. Whenever possible he takes objective evidence, especially that which is statistical. 

6. Upon the results of objective evidence he formulates his judgment of the matter.¹

Have pupils study a situation from many points of view other than their own. Have them examine and minimize their own prejudices and biases. Stimulate the habit of impartial thinking.

CHAPTER VIII II. AN INTRODUCTORY STUDY OF POWER 

The pupil should recognize that Problems VI to X form a single unit of work

Five problems relating to power (VI to X) are studied in this unit of work. These continue the study of the factors that account for the relatively high standard of living of the American people. In previous units have been considered the influence of (1) the location of the United States, (2) its large size and, briefly, its resources, and (3) its diverse population. Now we return to a more extended study of the second factor, the resources of the United States, confining our attention to those natural resources coal, oil, and water that have been used to propel engines. No single factor underlying the development of our new civilization is of greater importance than this one of mechanical power. Hence it receives much attention early in the course. 

Two topics are covered in this unit of work: first, in Problems VI and VII, how man has learned to make power to aid him in doing work; second, in Problems VIII to X, how the recent invention of engines has resulted in the widespread exploitation of coal, oil, and water power. Remind the pupil that coal and oil are fuel, potential power, -and that flowing water is also potential power.

What facts should the pupil memorize?

Beginning with Unit III the material in each of the following chapters of the Reading Book becomes more voluminous. In order to provide a vivid understanding of the problems considered in the remaining chapters, much detailed information must be given. 

This necessity of providing a large volume of reading matter raises an important question: Which facts in the social studies shall be memorized?

Certainly only a small number of the most important factsstatistics, names of persons, dates, movements, and the likeshould be learned "by heart." For example, in Unit III the names of a few conspicuous inventors like James Watt, Oliver

Evans, C. A. Parsons, etc. should be remembered, and a few important dates should be so well fixed in mind that the pupil will be oriented in his later consideration of modern problems. 

But only a few of the most important names and dates should be so emphasized.

Because of the importance of this matter we summarize our principles:

First, only a few of the most important facts-statistics, events, persons, dates, etc. - should be thoroughly fixed in memory. 

Second, important historical facts should be learned in chronological order, but to serve as orienting points they should also be

II 

learned in relation to vivid episodes; only thus can they aid in the understanding of movements and contemporary civilization. 

Third, many graphic devices-time lines, bar graphs, cartoons, pictographs, etc. - should be employed to help the pupil to understand and remember important facts. 

Fourth, basic generalizations, cue concepts, and themes are to be fixed permanently. The understanding of these, however, depends upon a mastery of a wealth of organized facts. 

The stating of these principles must not be construed as minimizing the necessity for utilizing definite and exact information. 

If an understanding of our complicated world is to be built up in the social-studies class many facts will have to be read carefully and discussed. The teacher should note one important principle, however, namely, that many more facts will be read and discussed than should be retained in anything but broad outline for future use. Understanding grows only through the accumulation of a great wealth of information; but much information, once acquired and used, is retained only in the form of organized generalizations and concepts.

Our principles can be restated thus: The development of informed, thinking young citizens depends upon, first, a rich, many-sided experience, including widespread reading, first-hand concrete study of a vast range of situations, and much discussion; second, the critical organization, comparison, and validation of ideas, discriminating selection of important ideas, and the sloughing off of trivial ones.

THE STUDY Of Problems VIII TO X 

Problems VIII to X comprise a second compact part of the unit on power. Each problem deals in one way or another with the same important concepts: (1) the dependence of an industrial country on fuel, (2) our large supplies of fuel (coal and oil) and of water power, (3) the recent startling increase in the use of coal, oil, and water power, (4) the irreplaceability of coal and oil (but the replaceability of water) and the corresponding danger of exhausting our fuel supply, and (5) the waste and conservation of our fuel resources.

In Problems VII to X the Workbook utilizes several types of tests 

Note the several kinds of tests: (1) true-false, (2) historicalranking, and (3) similarities tests. Two of these appear for the first time in Problem VII; thereafter they recur frequently. Each renders an important service to the work of the class. 

The true-false test provides an excellent stimulus for vigorous class discussions. Note, for example, the first item of the test. 

An excellent opportunity is provided for a discussion of the pupil's response. Here is a chance for pupils to question each other's reasons for doing or saying things. "Why?" is an important question to ask concerning the responses to the items in these tests. Use the true-false tests, therefore, to stimulate critical class discussion. Why is it true, for example, that mechanical slaves are more serviceable than human slaves?

We recommend, likewise, the historical-ranking test, which is used for the first time in Problem VII. Be sure to make clear to pupils what is wanted. Show them that it is merely an economical and approximate way of checking up their knowledge of the sequence of historical events. 

Note also the initial use of the interesting similarities test.

Discuss the sample solution which is given at the beginning of the test, and do one or two other items with the class to make sure that pupils understand what is to be done. This is one of the more difficult kinds of test and will be particularly appropriate for the best thinkers in the class.

A new exercise in graphing: How to read and make a line graph 

Seventh-grade pupils have more difficulty in reading or making line graphs than they do in making bar graphs. Hence the need in Problem VIII for special exercises on constructing and reading a line graph. The special difficulty seems to be this: the pupil

II 

does not recognize that the point on the line graph above a given point on the scale stands for a particular quantity exactly the same as though a bar had been drawn from the point down to the base line. Hence the teacher should show the relation between the line graph and the bar graph by drawing, on a sample line graph, vertical lines or narrow bars joining the points on the line graph with the base line. Remind the pupils that a line graph is merely a line joining the middle of the tops of a series of bars which might have been drawn down to the base line and then erased.

The line graph has been proved by investigation to be clearer than the bar graph for representing a general historical trend or for the comparison of one historical trend with another.¹

Care in planning will insure a much better discussion and a clearer understanding on the part of the individual pupil.

How should the study of industrial processes be conducted? 

In this problem arises a teaching difficulty that will be met in many other problems, — namely, how should the processes of manufacturing, mining, trade, agriculture, etc. be studied? 

How much mastery of detail should be expected? What are the chief outcomes to be sought?

The chief intellectual outcome sought in the social studies is a clear understanding of modern civilization. Such a clear understanding can be developed only by building in turn an understanding of the key industries upon which our civilization depends. 

This course selects as the six key industries (1) the power industries (coal, oil, and water), (2) iron and steel, (3) manufacturing, especially in the machine-making industries, in the textile industries, etc., (4) agriculture, (5) transportation, and (6) communication. In each of these key industries complicated processes of production, distribution, etc. have been developed in modern times. How much understanding shall be expected of pupils of the details of these processes? This question is raised explicitly in connection with the teaching of Problem XI, which deals with iron and steel.

TEACHER'S GUIDE 

Several definite principles can be stated concerning this psychological problem.

First, an adequate intellectual and emotional understanding of modern civilization arises only out of the study of many illustrative details. Specifically, an understanding can be developed of each of the six key industries only by studying examples of the development and the current status of each. Pupils will come to visualize the crucial importance of the iron and steel industry in the United States only by reading and discussing examples of the way in which the iron and steel industry developed and is carried on today. Understanding cannot be developed in a vacuum; it grows only through the handling of specific facts. Necessarily, therefore, the course must abound in facts. 

For that reason in the discussion of the iron and steel and other key industries there is included a brief description of essential industrial processes. In the iron and steel chapter, for example, we describe briefly the steps by which iron ore becomes steel. Pupils should read and discuss these processes briefly; they should do more than that if real understanding of the processes is to be developed. If the school is near a steel mill an excursion should be made through the mill. First-hand observation is the best method of developing understanding. In the absence of such an opportunity, motion pictures of the manufacture of steel and steel parts should be used. (These can now be secured from such agencies as those listed in Appendix C.) In short, employ every possible avenue of learning - excursion, motion pictures, dramatic reading, and discussion.

Second, an understanding of contemporary life can be developed most effectively by means of clear contrasts with earlier modes of living. Hence processes of production, transportation, etc. in earlier times are contrasted with those of today. Note how this is done in the treatment of the key industries. In 

Problem XI the contrast is brought out between the crude and inefficient methods of producing iron and steel before the Industrial Revolution and the standardized, highly efficient

methods of today. Thus the description of the old methods and of the processes used today should be studied merely for the contrast between the crudity, inefficiency, and inadequacy of iron and steel production in earlier times and the efficient large-scale production of today.

The use of science was illustrated in Unit III in the study of the power-producing industries. Now in Unit IV it is illustrated in the description of the Industrial Revolution and the new methods of producing iron and steel.

The use of statistics of resources, production, etc. to develop an appreciation of the American standard of living The principles for the study of industrial processes are paralleled by similar ones for the study of statistics of key industries and resources. In times past geography textbooks have demanded of the pupil the mastery of a great number of isolated facts of natural resources, products, etc. This new course in the social studies makes use of a considerable body of statistical facts, but the authors conceive of their use in a new way. For each of the key industries they give in bar graphs and occasional tables the approximate amounts of production at different stages of the development of the industries and also as compared with those of the other leading industrial countries. Understanding and vivid appreciation of our national wealth cannot be acquired except through the study of specific illustrations. 

Hence the necessity for studying definitely the statistical facts of resources, production, location, etc. 

Such facts are employed and should be studied primarily to develop a growing appreciation of the high standard of living in the United States. This standard of living is illustrated by the facts of our production of large amounts of coal, oil, and water power, iron and other natural resources, our large production of raw materials, and our large production of manufactured goods.

Granted the necessity for the study of specific facts, how can they be organized and used by pupils so as to contribute most effectively to the desired appreciation and understanding? Certainly most pupils will not be called upon for permanent mastery of the large number of statistical facts presented in this course.

V 

These facts are studied, therefore, not for permanent memory of the statistics, but for the contribution that studying and discussing them will make to the general growth of the pupils' appreciation and understanding.

Understanding of historical development grows through the use of two kinds of material: First, historical statistics (in bar graphs) in which the statistics of production in one period are contrasted with similar statistics for another period. Second, episodes descriptive of life in one period are contrasted with similar episodes for another period. Thus by means of sharp contrasts appreciation is conveyed of our rising standard of living. 

The principle can be summed up by saying that specific and detailed statistics should be studied and discussed, but that permanent retention should not be insisted upon of more than a few key facts.

Practice in getting exact meanings for cue concepts aids in clear thinking 

How can the course in the social studies produce clear thinking about modern life? Only by building up a clear grasp of important meanings and concepts. How does this clarity of understanding of concepts grow? In the following ways: first, by the acquisition of a body of definite information, that is, by studying concrete illustrations of ways of living; second, by systematically summarizing and selecting the important characteristics of concepts which have been illustrated; third, by practice in using the concept, illustrating it frequently and employing it in critical discussion. Pupils, therefore, acquire a clear grasp of meanings, first, by studying concrete illustrations within their own experiences; second, by practice in frequent detection of central meanings, big ideas; and third, by frequent practice in illustrating these ideas. 

The Reading Book (An Introduction to American Civilization) 

provides the material for this practice in clear thinking. It presents episodes and narratives illustrating voluminously the important concepts of modern civilization and its historical development. The Workbook (and to a smaller degree the Reading 

Book) provides the pupil with help with the second of these steps. It supplies definite practice in recognizing important concepts, in distinguishing between different or related concepts, in defining concepts and in illustrating them. Thus, through finding the concepts in illustrations, through finding illustrations for concepts, and through defining concepts, pupils will increase their mastery of the basic meanings which are important to an intelligent grasp of the economic phases of American civilization.

Inform the class that in subsequent problems of the course they will be considering other examples of the Industrial Revolution, such as the development of a remarkable system of transportation and communication, the startling growth of cities, changes in the lives of workers with the development of the factory system, specialization of jobs, standardization of machine parts and processes of manufacturing, the development of corporations, and the increase in world trade. All these are phases of the Industrial 

Revolution, and young people should study them in relationship.

CHAPTER X . TRANSPORTATION AND COMMUNICATION 

The large scope of the unit In this unit of work seven problems (XIII to XIX) are studied and should be treated as one compact unit, as they all deal with the development and current status of transportation and communication in the United States. To the study of this unit is devoted nearly one fifth of the semester's work-seven chapters in the Reading Book and seven problems in the Workbook. Why this large amount of time for a separate unit on transportation and communication? There are several reasons.

The first is the importance of the themes which it illustrates. 

Eight of the basic themes of American civilization are concretely illustrated in each of these seven problems. These themes can be succinctly summarized in the eight following concepts: (1) the influence of the natural environment, (2) man's increasing control over nature, (3) the rapid rise and spread of industrialism, (4) the change from self-sufficient to interdependent ways of living, (5) the trend toward specialization of labor and standardization, (6) the rapid rise in the economic standard of living, (7) the tendency toward concentration in industrial life, and (8) the development of mass production. 

Second, the success of the unit in providing a wealth of concrete illustrations of these important themes and concepts would in itself justify a very large amount of time. There are, however, other important reasons for the careful study of transportation and communication. Witness, for example, the important way in which these chapters build up a richer historical understanding 111

TEACHER'S GUIDE 

of the development of our country. Each provides concrete historical material; each uses historical development to convey a clear understanding of contemporary modes of living; each, in short, provides systematic topical history. 

Third, transportation and communication comprise two of the key industries upon which our modern civilization absolutely depends. In no other chapter of the book have we illustrated more vividly the concept of interdependence. It is almost impossible to overemphasize the importance of an uninterrupted, nation-wide, interrelated system by which people and goods can be transported and by which almost instantaneous communication can be maintained between the most remotely separated parts of the country - indeed, of the entire world. 

There is a fourth reason for the large amount of space allotted to this unit, namely, the interest of the students. Necessarily, this course is organized around themes and concepts which, although of crucial importance, are difficult to understand and frequently difficult to illustrate adequately. Treated historically, it provides many dramatic and easily understood illustrations of the big ideas which we have tried to develop throughout the course. Teachers will find that the episodes and narratives of these chapters will be eagerly read and clearly understood. 

Standing as a large and important central unit in the course, they will help to fix a growing mastery of important meanings. 

Additional important concepts specially illustrated in this unit In addition to the eight principal ideas referred to in the foregoing section, this unit on transportation and communication also illustrates vividly six other important concepts: 

1. Throughout the Industrial Revolution the increasing dependence of people upon a world-wide system of transportation and communication.

2. The westward movement and the conquest of the continent.

1. Systematic practice in the interpretation of episodes. Unit V

Have a summarizing exercise at the close of the study of transportation and communication In Unit V the evolution of seven different kinds of transportation and communication is discussed. Cumulative learning and the growing recognition of change The materials of this course have been so selected and organized that meanings accumulate and concepts grow in scope and depth of interpretation. No single concept is more important in this respect than that of change. It is this fact of constant change and development that helps to make the study of the social sciences so difficult. For example, political boundaries shift with the fortunes of war; statistics of production vary sharply from year to year; the distribution of population is in constant flux. 

So it goes; the train of events will not stand still. The most important single characteristic of the western world and especially of the United States is its rapidly increasing rate of change. 

Unit VI provides an especially favorable opportunity for showing the implications of this concept in changing modes of living.

Two important purposes govern the work of this problem: first, an understanding of the rapid increase of city populations, with the changes in manufacturing and agriculture brought about in the Industrial Revolution; second, an understanding of the way in which the Industrial Revolution caused cities to grow at particular places. These two phenomena are new illustrations of the way in which the coming of the machine age changed modes of living. Populations grew with unprecedented rapidity, and after 1850 concentrated in hundreds of urban centers. (2) What makes a good manufacturing center? The illustrations which are provided in the

Problem XXIII. The chief purpose of this problem is to provide pupils with the data from which they answer the question How is it possible that a country of towns and cities can feed itself? The whole emphasis in the preceding twenty-two problems of this course has been upon the production of things

by power-driven machines, and upon the exploitation of natural resources and their distribution to people most of whom live in towns and cities. At this point, therefore, pupils must find the answer to the important question How can these urban workers be fed, since they do not engage in the production of food from the earth?

Two kinds of material are provided for pupils in the Reading Book: first, illustrations of the way in which the Industrial

Break the study of Problem XXIII into these two important topics. 

Treat each one systematically. The material of the Reading Book is organized for the definite and orderly answering of basic questions which would throw light upon these two important matters. 

Every important change in modern civilization has been brought about by a combination of several factors, never by one alone Each of the four problems of Unit VI provides an excellent opportunity to illustrate this important principle thoroughly. 

Consider several examples: emigration to the United States was caused by several factors, such as desire for a better living, political or religious persecution, longing to keep families together, or restless desire for adventure. It is true that the economic reasons were probably the most important, but others also played a part.

Consider a second example: the improved condition of the worker, his shorter hours of labor, higher wages-in general, his higher standard of living. These were brought about in part by the organized effort of workers joined together in labor unions. 

No amount of labor organization, however, would have produced the present high standard of living of the worker if power-driven machines and plentiful supplies of raw material had not made

possible a relatively cheap mass production. Thus the new standard of living is a result of the coöperation of several factors, not of any one.

As a third example, take the matter of the growth of cities and the location of particular ones. Pupils learned in Problem XXII 

that cities grow for a variety of reasons: some because of the discovery near their sites of valuable natural resources, others because of location on rivers or other favorable means of transportation, others on the edge of a rich hinterland, and still others because of the location of natural harbors or nearness to markets. 

Most communities, however, owe their growth not to any one cause but to several operating together. 

Finally, a fourth example is to be found in the collaboration of the many factors which made possible the large-scale production of food. Problem XXIII shows how it was possible to feed our nation of towns and cities because of a fortunate combination of circumstances: (1) a large supply of level, fertile land located in a region of appropriate rainfall and temperature, and (2) many people ambitious and ready for work, most of whom were of the race that at that moment was inventing engines and machines and applying them to agriculture as well as to manufacturing. 

It was the coming of the Europeans to North America at the very time that the scientific movement and the Industrial Revolution were developing in Europe that really brought about the great transformation in the region which we now call the United States. An interesting exercise could be developed in the class by asking the pupils what would have happened in North America if the Europeans had discovered it, say, before the year 1200.

Had there been 4,000,000 European people on the Appalachian coast by the year 1200, would the same astonishing development have come in the next three hundred years in the settlement of the continent, in the development of coal, oil, and the use of the rivers, and in the building up of cities and a nation-wide system of transportation and communication? The discussion will bring the conclusion that it was the fortunate combination of the

Industrial Revolution and the acquiring of a large, continuous body of land with fertile soil and appropriate climate that brought about American civilization. 

This unit, therefore, provides another good opportunity for training in clear thinking. Emphasize repeatedly that the various factors which finally brought about each important change in living were not of coördinate importance. An excellent illustration of this is found in the comparison of the standard of living of the United States with that of other western countries. The

However, in class discussion the teacher can anticipate somewhat the work of the next semester. Remind the pupils that they will learn that the general standard of living of people in western Europe is not so high as that in the United States. Remind them also that the 

Industrial Revolution actually started and was carried to a high development in Germany, England, and several other European countries. One of the chief reasons, however, for the difference between the standard of living of our country and of European ones is the fact that the adoption of power-driven machinery in several of the European countries was not accompanied by the inheritance of great supplies of all the essential resources. CHAPTER XII . THE CHIEF SECTIONS OF THE UNITED STATES: WHERE THE AMERICAN PEOPLE LIVE AND WHY THEY LIVE THERE 

A problem in geography 

Problem XXIV is essentially a summary of the geographic factors which determine where the American people live and why they live where they do. The problem and the corresponding chapter in the Reading Book constitute, therefore, a sort of regional geography of the United States. 

Two definite aims should control the teaching of this unit: (1) an understanding of the chief characteristics of each of the seven chief economic sections; (2) a mastery of the generalization that the distribution of population is determined largely by combinations of climate, topography, and natural resources. 

In the organization of this course two principles have been followed with respect to geographic content. The first is that all necessary geographic facts and generalizations shall be included in the course; the second, that they shall be presented in meaningful relationships and at the most favorable places for the clear understanding of American civilization. These principles have been carried out by including a large amount of geography in every unit of work. Tabulations show that this course contains more fundamental geographic material than does the conventional geography textbook for the seventh grade. This material has not always been presented in conventional geographic form; hence we caution the teacher against missing the point that the course abounds in geography. The chapters on coal, oil, water power, iron, etc. illustrate this. 138

Problem XXIV divides the United States into seven sections. 

These sections, with one important exception, correspond to the 'agricultural sections" chosen by the United States Department of Agriculture. A cursory examination of Chapter XXIV of the 

Reading Book will show that the basis of our division into sections has been essentially that of economic life, as determined by the geographic factors of topography, climate, and natural resources.

The teacher should be cautioned at this point that every attempt to divide the United States into a clear-cut scheme of sections is arbitrary and depends upon the point of view of the person doing it. There are many different bases upon which our country can be sectioned. The employment of any one will produce a different scheme of sections from the use of any other. 

Consider, for example, the obvious different ways in which it might be done: (1) Division from a political standpoint, sectioning the country into forty-eight states, or arbitrarily into New England states, Middle Atlantic states, South-central states, Southwestern states, Central states, etc.; this is the method followed by some geographers. (2) We might divide from the standpoint of productive agriculture, as is done by the United States Department of Agriculture. (3) We might classify according to climatic conditions or distribution of natural resources. 

These methods would give rainfall regions, temperature regions, and topographical regions, or coal regions, iron regions, etc.

Another method would be to divide into sections according to population. Still other methods could be devised. 

Which method of classification shall we employ, knowing that each will produce a somewhat different scheme of sections from the others? That is the question which we, like all other designers of materials of instruction in the social studies, have been compelled to confront.

TEACHER'S GUIDE 

To answer our question let us recur to our central purpose, namely, convenience in thinking about the United States as a whole. We desire young people to think clearly about the distribution of our people, to know where they live and why they live there. This scheme seems to be productive of the most meaningful background. Hence we have sought to divide the country into the smallest number of approximately mutually exclusive sections each of which has distinctive ways of living. 

The attempt to arrive at a classification of sections none of which overlaps on adjacent ones is doomed to failure because of the diversity of activities in many sections of the country. For example, the chief agricultural belt of the United States overlaps definitely the chief industrial section; many diversified industries are springing up in the cotton-growing South; oil is drilled, coal is mined, and many kinds of goods are manufactured within a few hundred miles of a large corn-growing region. These are only a few of many illustrations that might be given. In beginning the study of sections, therefore, the teacher should make clear this important fact of overlapping, and that the scheme of sectioning followed in this course is chosen merely for convenience in thinking about the economic life of our country. 

The sevenfold scheme of sectioning that we have finally adopted agrees fairly closely with the agricultural sections of the United States Department of Agriculture, and our map studies, in the main, agree with theirs. 

Important caution: Emphasize the lack of clear-cut dividing lines between sections In conducting a study of the chief sections of the United States, therefore, the teacher must be on the alert to point out constantly that there are no hard and fast dividing lines between these seven sections. On the borders of each of them there are large areas in which agricultural products, industries, and corresponding ways of living change gradually from being predominantly of one kind

to being distinctively of others. Indeed, it may be said that the chief characteristic is the diversity of economic life in any one section and the nation-wide distribution of many important activities. However, areas of concentration of certain occupations typify each region. For example, the cattle map in Chapter XXIV of the Reading Book shows that cattle are raised all over the United States, with, however, much concentration in the Middle West, centering about Iowa. Similarly, corn and wheat are raised in all of the seven sections, but far more people are engaged in raising it in the so-called corn and wheat belts than in any other part of the country. The same thing is true of the distribution of manufacturing, the raising of vegetables, etc. 

Having this caution in mind, the pupil will be helped to visualize economic life in the United States if he thinks of it in sections and thinks of each one in terms of a few economic concepts. He will think of the South, for example, as a cotton-producing section, but one which is beginning to turn to manufacturing; he will see the chief industrial section as a large northeastern region in which people are concentrated largely in cities and towns, most of them engaged in manufacturing and trade; he will remember the central agricultural belt as a region in which a large proportion of people are engaged in raising corn, wheat, and cattle; to him the great grazing plains will mean chiefly irrigated farming, the raising of cattle, etc.

The understanding of trade is basic to the understanding of civilization One of the most important phenomena of the development of western civilization is that of the dominance of traders. The chapter on world trade brings out one important distinction between trade in earlier times and trade today; namely, that whereas in earlier times world trade dealt chiefly with luxuries and materials of comfort, today it deals largely with necessities.

They may have acquired isolated bits of information, but an interest in learning itself has not been created. Curiosity in fundamental things has not been stimulated.

In this general course, therefore, we have attempted not only to make the materials interesting in themselves, as well as to supply content that is really necessary for true understanding, but also to arouse curiosity and a desire to know more. I. GENERAL SUGGESTIONS FOR USE OF VISUAL MATERIALS 

These materials are not intended to take the place of the textbook but to supplement it. They may be used in three ways: 1st, before considering a topic, to stimulate interest in it; 2nd, during the consideration of a topic, to drive home certain facts; 3rd, after considering a topic, to review it.

The presentation of visual material should not be regarded as a show, as is so often the case. The teacher should survey slides or films before presenting them, so that she may prepare her pupils for what they are going to see. During the presentation an operator is advisable, so that the teacher may be available for answering questions and guiding observation. Finally a follow-up will check on the attainment of desired objectives.

Since the child cannot learn many new things in rapid succession, it is good practice to present a few things and to know that the purpose has been accomplished.

Because films are sometimes difficult to obtain on short notice they should be booked early. If they are to be purchased, a preview is advisable, since they vary in quality as well as subject.
