For more than thirty years Virginia has permitted voting by those expecting to be absent from the localities of their residence on election day. 

This statute, now carried as Chapter 13, of Title 24, of the Code of Virginia, has been amended many times. Originally limited to those who might be "required by . . . regular business and habitual duties, to be absent from the city or county and precinct" in which registered to vote, the privilege has been extended to those on vacation or away as students or physically unable to go in person to the polls. Other amendments to the original statute have dealt with the mechanics of the handling of absentee ballots and precautionary measures which, it was thought, might reduce the possibility of improper use of this method of voting. 

Many allegations have been made of improper use of the privilege of casting an absentee ballot. The subject has frequently been before the 

General Assembly of Virginia for consideration and many remedies have been proposed. Feeling that some of the proposals made during the 1952 session merited more careful study than was possible during the limited time available to it, the 1952 regular session of the General Assembly adopted the following resolution:

SENATE JOINT RESOLUTION NO. 58 

Directing the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council to study the laws relating to absent voters. 

Whereas, it is alleged that there have been many instances of fraud and abuses in connection with the use of absentee ballots in elections in this Commonwealth; and

Whereas, these allegations, if true, bring disrepute upon this State: now, therefore, be it Resolved by the Senate, the House of Delegates concurring, that the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council is directed to make a thorough and complete study of the laws of Virginia relating to absent voters with the view to determining whether some effective method can be found to eliminate any opportunities for fraud or abuse which exist in the present system. The Council shall complete its study and make a report containing its fir ings and recommendations to the Governor and General Assembly not later than October one, nineteen hundred fifty-three.

The Council designated Honorable Harry B. Davis of Princess Anne 

County to serve as Chairman of a Committee to make the preliminary investigation and report to it under this resolution. Associated with Mr.

BACKGROUND OF THE RECOMMENDATIONS 

The absent voters law was adopted in 1916 to meet a situation of increasing frequency. There were many persons whose daily vocation required them to be absent from their polling places on the date fixed by the Constitution or the statutes for an election. The 1916 act also recognized that many citizens of the Commonwealth might be absent from their homes in the service of their country and provided for the certification of ballots by commissioned officers of the armed services as well as by postmasters and their assistants (who were, under this act, the only persons who could witness the execution of the forms required for absentee voting). Other occasions requiring voting by mail were recognized in the later amendments to the statute which have been commented on above. 

Evidence was presented that the absentee ballot has been improperly used in elections in many places in Virginia. Statements were made during the course of the public hearings incident to this study that such improper use is not restricted to any group, faction or party. All the persons appearing at the hearings recommended that steps be taken to correct the alleged abuses. Some went so far as to advocate that, unless this could be done, the statute be repealed.

Fraud in elections is probably as old as the election process itself. 

The absentee ballot, while it has played a major part in fraudulent elections in some instances, is not the only device which has been resorted to here and in other states. Election laws throughout the country have evolved over many years in an effort to curb these and other abuses as they have been resorted to. In the development of these statutes it has always been necessary to close loopholes which could be fraudulently utilized and yet not burden the election process with such restrictions that the exercise of the franchise would be too burdensome and the freedom of elections thereby hampered.

The very thing which makes the absentee ballot so valuable to the honest voter is also the chief attraction to those who use it for illegal purposes. This is the certainty of voting for a designated candidate when the voter is away from the polls. In the case of the interested citizen it is a valuable privilege. In the case of those not interested enough to vote or willing to prostitute this privilege an opportunity for misuse of the absentee ballot exists. Little can be done about the citizen who votes for anyone whom a partisan suggests. The voter who sells his vote can only do so because the partisan can get delivery. The solution to this appears to be to make delivery uncertain.

The remedies proposed to correct the situation were varied. Some were based on the theory that laws make people honest. Others were founded on putting impediments on the use of the absentee ballot. The se last are ineffective because they would impose an intolerable burden on honest election officials. They would merely pile additional prohibitions on disregarded prohibitions.

The proper approach to this problem is best summarized by a quotation from Senate Document 8, 1946 session of the General Assembly: 

"The proposals advanced in this report are based on these conceptions but like all other human institutions, are largely dependent for their effectiveness upon the personal equation. If public sentiment in Virginia favors pure elections, the proposais will 2

On the other hand if substantial segments of public opinion favor trickery and illegality, no set of laws can combat successfully their existence. Responsibility for an enlightened and properly moulded public opinion rests with the people themselves. To achieve this end the people, under the guidance of their public officers and other leaders, must insist upon and practice the same degree of honesty and integrity in elections which should characterize honorable men in their personal and business relations with each other. Unless this is achieved efforts at electoral reform will be of little avail. "The proposals advanced are predicated on the theory that the people, as a whole, want fair elections, and that if partisans, through misguided enthusiasm, try to thwart this desire, their path should be made as difficult as possible. "The realistic approach to the problem is that if both parties to a contest are adequately policed, neither is apt to violate the rules, and that bi-partisan control is the most adequate policing that can be offered. Stated more simply the thesis is that if each side has full opportunity to watch the other, then both will behave. 

With these considerations in mind the following recommendations are made:

RECOMMENDATIONS 

1. Require applications for ballots to be made not less than eight days (presently five days) before a primary or general election; no change is feasible in the case of second primaries. 

2. Require each registrar to transmit by registered mail or in person, to the clerk to whose office returns of the election are to be made, at least five days before elections, a list of all persons applying for absentee ballots; in case of personal delivery require a signed receipt; require the clerk during such five day period to maintain such lists in his office as public records and open to public inspection during office hours. 

3. Repeal the present provision of the statute for the posting by the registrar of such lists at each polling place on the day of election; but require that such lists be furnished to and posted by the judges of election of each precinct. 4.

Permit a voter who has executed and returned an absentee ballot to appear personally at the polls on the day of election, demand the production of his ballot, and have the same voided, in his presence, and then permit such voter to vote in person. 5. 

Restrict the protection of the immunity statute to those persons who are compelled under subpoena to testify concerning an election before a grand jury or in criminal proceedings in a court of record. 

6. Provide that each application for an absentee ballot state that the same is made in conformity with the election laws and not in violation thereof, and be signed under penalty of perjury. 

REASONS FOR RECOMMENDATIONS 

1. The law presently requires that applications for absentee ballots be made to the registrar not less than five days before primary, special or general elections. The ballot, in cases where it is sent by mail, must travel by registered mail from the registrar to the voter and back to the registrar. Experience has indicated that this five-day period is not long enough. Many ballots are received on the day of election or even 3

Reducing Fraud in Absent Voting in Virginia thereafter. In the first case this unduly complicates the duties of the registrar and in the second case the ballot cannot be counted. It is accordingly recommended that this five-day period be increased to eight days in the case of any primary or general election. While the same difficulties may arise in the case of special elections and second primaries, the short period of time now provided during which absentee ballots may be obtained and forwarded does not permit a change. 

2. The chief reason for the improper use of absentee ballots in elections is to insure that the voter casts his vote in the way that the person soliciting it desires. This is true whether the vote is bought and paid for or whether it is influenced in any other way, legally or illegally. Votes may be contracted for by the offering of monetary or other rewards; but unless the purchaser can be sure that the vote will be cast in the way it is desired, payment therefor is a risky investment. A voter venal enough to sell his vote will in most cases sell his vote again to the other side if he can.

According to testimony received the absentee ballot as presently used is tailored precisely to the end of making the purchased vote stay bought. The person soliciting the vote can be present when it is marked and sealed into its envelope and can thus be reasonably sure that the vote will be counted on the side for which it was cast. If this certainty of how the voter votes is removed then much of the motive for the improper use of absentee ballots will have disappeared. 

One of the best curbs on misuse of the absentee ballot is publicity. 

Publicity should be given to the names of the persons using absentee ballots. 

It is therefore recommended that each registrar be required to send by registered mail or deliver in person, obtaining a receipt therefor, to the appropriate clerk of court, five days before a primary or general election, and at least two days before any second primary or special election a list of all persons who have applied for absentee ballots. The clerk should be required, during the period prior to the election, to maintain such lists in his office as public records and open to public inspection during office hours. These lists are now required to be sent three days before elections, but there is no requirement as to making them public. Such publicity will not concern any person who is using such a ballot for a legitimate purpose. 

It will tend to deter any person who is using the ballot for the purpose of violating the election laws. 

3. The registrars are, under the present law, required to post at each polling place a list of those who have applied for absentee ballotṣ. 

This requirement for posting imposes on some registrars a task which is almost physically impossible where many precincts are involved. It is recommended that the requirement that the registrars post the lists be omitted and that in lieu thereof these lists be sent to the electoral board and by them furnished to the judges of election who can readily post them at the respective precincts. 

4. The most important single step which can be taken to destroy the motive for the fraudulent use of absentee ballots in elections is to provide that a voter, after having cast his absentee ballot and returned the same to the Electoral Board, can then personally appear at the poll, demand to have his ballot voided in his presence, and then vote by secret ballot. 

This will prevent use of the absentee ballot as a device to insure in advance that the voter will vote in the manner desired by the person who solicits his vote. Combined with the provision for giving publicity to the names of those who have applied for absentee ballots (which will put on notice any candidate if absentee ballots are likely to be used against him), it will give the candidates as well as those citizens of a community who are interested in maintaining the purity of the election process an opportunity to interview the persons in whose name absentee ballots have been obtained, to determine if there is evidence of fraudulent intent, and to take effective action to counteract any fraud which may be found. Such voided ballots should be

Our present law permits a person to be openly and notoriously involved in election fraud and yet evade punishment. § 24-449 of the 

Code of Virginia is designed to assist those prosecuting in election fraud cases to obtain evidence on which convictions may be had. It provides that a person giving evidence in any prosecution or other proceeding in connection with violation of the criminal provisions of the election laws can never be proceeded against under any of these penal provisions. 

This section, designed to assist in the conviction of violators of the election laws, has been used as a shield by those guilty of such violations. If unscrupulously used it can be made the vehicle of rendering forever secure from prosecution anyone who has been so guilty. 

It is recommended that the protection given by this statute be limited to those testifying concerning an election under subpoena before a grand jury or in criminal proceedings before a court of record. This, while preserving the initial beneficial purpose of the statute, will hamper its indiscriminate use to serve as a cloak for nefarious activities. 

6. The present application for absentee ballots merely contains certain general information concerning the voter, and requires him to indicate the reason why the ballot is required and sign the application. 

There is noting on the application form to caution the prospective voter that it may be used in pursuance of fraudulent activities, or that by participating in such use he may subject himself to a penalty. It is recommended that the application form be required to indicate that application is made in conformity with the election laws of the State and not in violation of any such laws and that it further contain a declaration under the penalty of perjury that the statements contained in the application have been examined by the applicant and are true and correct. The psychological effect of these provisions on the application form should aid in discouraging the illegal use thereof.

In connection with applications for ballots, attention is called to the present provisions of § 24-327 of the Code, which provides that upon receipt of the application, the applicant being duly qualified, the electoral board shall send the ballot by registered mail or deliver it to the applicant in person. Strict adherence to this provision of law would minimize opportunities for fraudulent use of the absentee ballot. 

CONCLUSION 

In conclusion it is again stressed that the recommendations of this report are designed to strike at the basic motive which induces fraudulent use of the absent voting process of our law without imposing unduly burdensome restrictions, thus preserving the law for the purposes for which it was originally intended. The recommendations of the report do not go as far in many directions as the advocates of particular reforms would have them. These recommendations are such, however, that their adoption will, in large measure, accomplish the ends desired. The alleged abuse of the absentee ballot has been due to the certainty with which votes can be delivered. The changes will remove this certainty and, it is hoped, any abuse of the law.
