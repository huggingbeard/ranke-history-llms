FOREWORD 

Just two years ago, in 1954, a personnel crisis of serious proportions faced the Armed Services. Reenlistments, including those of career personnel, had dropped sharply in all Services. Both regular and reserve officers were leaving in alarmingly large numbers. 

Time was running against us. Each month brought a further depletion of our greatest defense resource: skilled, trained personnel. It was evident not only to the Services, but to the President and the Congress, that urgent remedial measures were necessary. 

To combat this situation with its dangerous implications for our national military capabilities, the Career Incentive Task Force was conceived and born. 

The job of this special team of selected members from each of the Armed Services was to plan, coordinate, and accelerate a legislative program to restore the attractiveness of military careers.

The first major accomplishment of the Task Force was the Career Incentive Act of 1955, which provided selective pay increases to career personnel. As a result of the prompt action taken by the Congress, the increased pay checks were being cashed within two months from the time committee hearings had been commenced.

The Task Force was then given the responsibility for five additional legislative measures of far-reaching importance to all service personnel and their families. Four of these -- survivor benefits, dependent medical care, augmentation of regular officers, and doctors' career incentives 

I have now been enacted into law. 4949

Much progress has been made since 1954. In addition to the new legislation, service life has been improved in many other respects. The downward trend in reenlistments has been checked, and fewer officers are resigning. A much stronger case for a service career can now be made to attract the high caliber, dedicated personnel we must have. 

Chronicled in this book is the career incentive legislative program in which the President, the Congress, the Secretary of Defense, and the Armed Services can take just pride. Moreover, the Career Incentive Task Force can point to its short history as an effective example of inter-service unity working for the benefit of all.

The positive benefits of this epochal program will accrue to all present and future members of the Armed Services, active and retired, together with their family dependents.

But the most important single element in any career incentive program remains that of effective leadership. Legislative and other similar measures can do no more than to improve conditions whereby such leadership can better fulfill its responsibilities.

INTRODUCTION 

The high rate of personnel turnover in all of the military services is a matter of serious concern to the Department of Defense. The problem has been under study for over two years and a number of factors, tangible and intangible, contribute to the current lack of personnel stability in our Armed Forces. The low reenlistment rates which exist in all services and the fact that officer turnover is increasing, particularly in the young officer group, have serious implications for the future quality of our career group of officers and enlisted men. 

Modern weapons are becoming increasingly complex and expensive. Our military establishment cannot operate efficiently unless we can retain an experienced nucleus of competent highlytrained and motivated career personnel. 

All indications point to the current disparity between military and civilian pay and related benefits as a major causative factor in the present situation. 

There is no easy solution to the manpower problem. The military services are doing what they can to ease the strain on their personnel and to improve the conditions of service. An ad- justment in military compensation and improvements in certain important benefit areas are essential if the mass exodus of experienced and skilled personnel from the military services is to be checked. 

On the following pages the primary factors which support the necessity for prompt action are summarized and illustrated. In like manner, the major aspects of the proposed Career Incentive Act are briefly presented. 1

BACKGROUND OF CURRENT PAY STRUCTURE Hook Commission and Career Compensation Act of 1949 

The Hook Commission Report of 1948 to Secretary Forrestal stated that "the Services, in the procurement of technically trained officers and men, are in competition with industry" and that military pay should be raised to enable the Services to secure and retain this personnel. The report also recommended frequent review to maintain this correlation. 

The report of the Hook Commission was adopted in substance by the Congress in the Career Compensation Act of 1949, but at a considerably lower scale than proposed (5% lower for officers, 3% for warrant officers, and 2% for enlisted grades).

Armed Forces Pay Raise Act of 1952 

Three years later, in 1952, the Congress authorized across-the-board increases in basic pay, quarters and subsistence allowance by an over-all average of 5.7%. There has been no change in military pay since 1952. 

Changes in Benefits since 1949

In 1950 Congress improved rental allowances for enlisted men. 

In 1954 an increased reenlistment bonus was provided. 

Also in 1954 government insured home mortgages were extended to active service personnel. 3

VOLUNTARY SEPARATIONS OF OFFICERS ARE EXCESSIVELY HIGH 

Not only is there evidence of a declining interest in embarking upon a military career, but there is also a declining desire on the part of those officers now in the service to remain beyond the point of their obligated service. The next chart shows the magnitude of the officer turnover problem in all services. More than 75,000 officers have left or will leave the services voluntarily during fiscal years 1954 and 1955. This is 5,000 more than the present total officer strength of the Navy. It is significant to note also that the number of regular officer resignations in 1954 was one and three-fourths times the combined number of graduates from the Military and Naval Academies. It is clear that the net result of the present reluctance of young men of outstanding qualifications to seek a career as a military officer or to continue upon completion of obligated service is to create uncertainty as to the availability of the best in military leadership for the future. Thus the problem is not only one of the present, but one of the gravest implications for the future. 14

INCENTIVES FOR HAZARDOUS DUTY 

The overall problem of turnover is serious. However, it is doubly serious in its effect upon our air and submarine services. Upon these services devolve unique responsibilities in our present defense plans. It is clear that we cannot allow our state of readiness to be impaired by excessive losses of skilled air and submarine personnel. 

Both air and submarine crews are volunteers. 

Because of the hazards involved in these duties, the services may not order men to crew duty. In recognition of these hazards the Congress has authorized special pay incentives to encourage men to volunteer for these types of duty. 

Nevertheless, in spite of these pay incentives, difficulties are being encountered in securing enough volunteers or in retaining those who have previously volunteered.

Part of the difficulty may well lie in the increased hazards of air crew duty with the coming jet age.

This chart shows that as plane speeds have increased there has been a corresponding increase in the ratio of fatalities per major accident. In 1930 the ratio was 1 fatality for each 13 major accidents. Today the ratio is one to every five accidents. So serious is the problem of pilot turnover in the Air 

Force that it is necessary to train 2-1/2 pilots to achieve a net increase of one pilot. 16

INCENTIVES FOR HAZARDOUS DUTY (CONTINUED) 

Similar problems are being faced in the submarine service. While vacancies in the Submarine School have doubled since 1948, applications have decreased onethird. The rate of submarine officer resignations during the same period has increased fivefold.

The realities of the present hazards are attested by the experience of the 40% 

of the Naval Academy Class of 1939 who chose hazardous duty careers. Of these 

24.4% of the submariners and 22.6% of the aviators are dead against a class average of 12%. A new level of dangers faces the young man now considering flight and submarine service as we approach 100% jet operation and the atomic submarine. Confirmation of the extent of hazard is given by the fact that jet flying is ranked by insurance companies as the most hazardous of all occupations, and premium rates are almost prohibitive for the young jet flyer. 

So long as these services must be staffed by volunteers the incentives offered must be adequate to attract and retain the number required to maintain peak performance capabilities. 18

TRAINING COSTS ARE HUGE! 

It is a truism to state that as turnover increases, the cost of training must increase and therefore the total cost of maintaining a necessary level of readiness.

This chart emphasizes the dollar investment we have in personnel. 

Nearly one million men--one third of the planned size of the active forces-will become eligible for some form of release during the coming year. 

At the present overall reenlistment rate of about 24% it will be necessary to replace about 750,000 of these men during the coming year. It costs approximately $3,200 to give basic training to each replacement, which will mean an outlay of nearly $2 billion.

If an additional 10% of the million men can be induced to reenlist, an investment of $320 million would be preserved, an amount equivalent to the cost of 985 F86D Jet Interceptors. 

These figures are limited to basic training costs only. It costs much more to train a man in typical technical skills (electronics, armament, aircraft maintenance). A reduction of 100,000 trainees would release approximately 30,000 instructors, supervisors and support personnel in basic training activities alone. The prime objective of the proposed Career Incentive Act is to increase the attractiveness of the military career, and thus reduce the exodus of skilled personnel from the services. A study of attitudes of military per somel and a review of civilian pay levels has led to the conclusion that adjustments in military pay are required.

The selective increases proposed in the Career Incentive Act have been developed in accordance with tile basic principles stated on the facing chart. In addition to the proposed adjustments in pay, the bill includes provision for an increase in the maximum per diem allowance and for a dislocation allowance equal to one month's quarters allowance for personnel with dependents making a permanent change of station. 34

WILL THE CAREER INCENTIVE ACT SOLVE THE PROBLEM? 

There is no single solution to the complex manpower problem of the military services.

HOW THE BASIC PAY SCALES OF THE CAREER INCENTIVE ACT WERE DEVELOPED 

Recognizing that the 1948 Hook Commission Report laid a sound basis in terms of spread between Grades and Ranks and recognition of levels of skill, responsibility and leadership, the basic pay scale recommended by that Commission was used as a base. This action had the effect of restoring the reductions made by the Career Compensation Act of 1949. 

The following chart shows that these subsequent steps were taken: 

Step 1. A 4% across-the-board increase was added for officers with over three years' service and warrant and enlisted personnel with over two years' service. This reflects the increase enacted by Public Law 346 of 1952. officers with less than three years' service and warrants and enlisted with less than two years' service would be given no increase in pay above the present level. Since this proposal is an incentive plan, increases are proposed only for those who are not obligated to serve.

Step 2. 

A $25.00 arbitrary increase was added to the W-1 grade to increase the spread between warrant and enlisted grades and to avoid the problem of reducing take home pay and allowances as a consequence of promotion to warrant grade. 

Step 3. Incentive increases, in most cases doubled longevity increments, were added at critical career points to offer financial incentives for remaining in the service and for seeking promotion. 

Examples of the effect of these increases for typical incentive purposes are shown on the facing chart. 38

INCENTIVE-HAZARDOUS DUTY PAY FOR FLYING AND SUBMARINE DUTY 

In arriving at the proposed scale of incentive-hazardous duty pay for flying and submarine duty, as reflected in the Career Incentive Bill, certain specific principles were adhered to as follows: 

1. 

The basic concepts of the Hook Commission as enacted into law by the Career Compensation Act were adopted as a fixed point of departure. 

2. The recommendations of the Strauss Commission (in 1953) which proposed that flying and submarine pay should bear a direct relationship to base pay were adopted. 

3. The necessity of establishing pay rates for flying and submarine duty which would have a realistic incentive value for each grade, with respect to the longevity pay of the grade and in consideration of the need for retaining more young flying and submarine officers, was recognized. 

On the basis that the Career Compensation Act of 1949 established an acceptable relationship between incentive and base pay under conditions existing at that time, the ratios then existing were adopted as the initial point of departure. 

Between the lowest and the highest grades however, it was determined that certain adjustments are necessary to give incentive-hazardous duty pay a realistic incentive value under conditions that exist today.

FOREWORD 

The purpose of this pamphlet is to outline in the simplest possible. form and with a minimum of explanatory detail the essential features of the proposed Servicemen's and Veterans' Survivor Benefits Act (H. R 7089). This legislation will establish a new and equitable Survivor Benefits 

Program for members and former members of the Uniformed Services. 

The bill is the product of the combined efforts of the Select Committee on Survivor Benefits of the House of Representatives, and all interested agencies, both Government and private. It was passed by the House of Representatives on July 13, 1955 and at present is awaiting action by the 

Senate Committee on Finance. 

The President of the United States endorsed the bill in a letter to the Chairman of the Select 

Committee on Survivor Benefits of the House of Representatives dated June 28, 1955, as follows:

It is my hope that your bill will be favorably received both by the Congress and by the civilian groups directly interested, so that we can see it written into law at the earliest possible date. 

Will you please do me the favor of conveying my appreciation and compliments to your colleagues on your Select Committee? With cordial regard,

SCOPE OF DEATH BENEFITS UNDER PRESENT AND PROPOSED LEGISLATION 

When a member of the Armed Forces dies, the type and amount of the Government benefits payable to his survivors largely depend upon whether the death is adjudged to be service-connected or nonservice-connected.

If the member dies while in inactive status (i. e., in reserve or in retirement) and the death is not caused by an incident of military service, his survivors may be eligible for Veterans Administration death PENSION payments. This is the principal element of the benefit program for non-service-connected deaths. H. R. 7089 does not change existing non-service-connected death PENSIONS in any way.

When a member of the Armed Forces dies while on active duty his death is presumed to be serviceconnected. If the member dies while in inactive status, and death results from an injury or illness incurred while performing military service, his death may be adjudged to be service-connected. 

The principal element of Government benefits payable to the survivors of members who sustain service-connected deaths is the Veterans Administration death COMPENSATION payment. Additionally, survivors of members who die in active service may receive benefits under Social Security, Government insurance or indemnity programs, and a death gratuity payment from the military service concerned. Reserve members may elect the more liberal benefits under the Federal Employees 

Compensation Act in lieu of Veterans Administration Compensation. 

The Bill, H. R. 7089, is concerned only with those elements of existing Government survivor benefit programs which relate to service-connected deaths. 2

PRINCIPAL ELEMENTS OF EXISTING PROGRAMS 

This chart shows the sources of the benefits payable to survivors of members of the Armed Forces whose deaths are service-connected. 

Veterans Administration Death Compensation is payable in case of a service-connected death whether the member died on active or inactive duty.

Social Security benefit entitlement is not dependent on the circumstances of death, but rather upon such factors as length of military service, extent of civilian participation, conditions of separation from military service and other criteria in Social Security law. 

Federal Employees Compensation benefits may be payable to the survivors of reserve personnel who die in peacetime under certain limited circumstances. Death must be service-connected and may occur while on active or inactive duty. Eligibility requirements under the law vary among the military services. Eligible survivors may elect to receive the more liberal benefits provided under this coverage if they qualify therefor or, if not, they then receive VA Death Compensation. Survivors of Regular members are not entitled to this benefit. Survivors of National Guard personnel have not been covered up to the present time.

Veterans Administration Indemnity benefits are payable in all cases to survivors of members who die on active duty, or within 120 days after leaving active duty, and who are not otherwise insured by the Government. Where the member had Government insurance (USGLI or NSLI) in an amount less than $10,000, his survivors would receive indemnity payments equal to the difference between the amount of Government insurance and $10,000. 

Death Gratuity lump sum payments accrue only to the survivors of military personnel who die while on active duty.

INEQUITIES OF THE PRESENT SYSTEM 

Under existing law the part-time reservist has better survivor benefit coverage than the full-time regular. As an example, the widow of a regular member, whatever his grade or length of service, will receive not more than $87 monthly (wartime) in death compensation from the Veterans Administration. The widow of a reserve member who elects a Federal Employees Compensation benefit in lieu of a Veterans Administration benefit may receive monthly payments equal to 45% of her husband's gross pay not to exceed $525 per month. Both widows may receive additional benefits from other 

Government sources. 

When the deceased member leaves a widow with minor children, the initial monthly benefit payment may be entirely adequate. However, when the VA Indemnity terminates at the end of ten years, and the children reach maturity, the widow may be left with as little as $69.60 a month (peacetime). 

Several elements of the present survivor benefit program may pay benefits to persons who were not dependent on the serviceman at the time of his death. The diversion of these benefits reduces the level of payments to needy survivors who are in fact dependent on the earnings of the deceased member. 

Existing Social Security coverage for military personnel is deficient in many respects. Begun as a wartime expedient, and temporarily extended from time to time, it is now an integral part of the overall survivor benefit program. Most of the existing deficiencies stem from the temporary and gratuitous nature of the coverage. These deficiencies can be eliminated by full inclusion of military personnel under contributory Social Security on the same basis as civilian wage earners. 

A major shortcoming of the present system is the failure to relate the principal benefit payment (VA Death Compensation) to the income level of the serviceman at time of death. The FECA monthly benefit for reservists is related to income. The Death Gratuity lump sum payment is related to income. 

The Social Security monthly payment may be partially related to income. These benefits may or may not accrue to survivors depending upon the circumstances of the deceased. VA Death Compensation, the one basic benefit payable in all service-connected death cases, is entirely unrelated to the earning level of the serviceman.

H. R. 7089 WILL MODERNIZE AND IMPROVE THE PRESENT SYSTEM 

The purpose of the proposed legislation is to improve and streamline the present survivor benefit system so as to provide a uniform, equitable and efficient program for the future.. 

The Bill will have a broad impact, affecting not only the survivors of present and future servicemen, but also those of former servicemen now in a veteran status (if death is service-connected), the survivors of members currently in retirement (if death is serviceconnected), and the survivors of servicemen already deceased, who are now or who may become eligible for survivor benefit payments under the present system.

Benefits for Survivors of: 

1. ACTIVE DUTY SERVICEMEN- 2. ALREADY DECEASED SERVICEMEN- 3. VETERANS AND RETIRED PERSONNEL SERVICE-CONNECTED DEATH ONLY

MAJOR AREAS OF IMPROVEMENT 

Veterans Administration Death Compensation payments to widows would be raised from the present flat rate of $87 monthly (wartime) and $69.60 (peacetime) to a variable rate ranging from $122 to $266 monthly. The present system of wartime and peacetime rates would be eliminated. 

By merging the indemnity and compensation benefits and utilizing Social Security payments for minor children, a stable and continuing benefit is provided over the unremarried lifetime of the widow.

Social Security payments, which recognize the needs of widows with minor children, are used to supplement the widow's compensation payment so that the combined benefits provide full family protection before the children reach maturity. 

The proposal to relate death compensation to the income level of the serviceman brings this benefit into line with other benefits. The range of the combined benefits under the revised program insures adequate minimum and reasonable maximum payment levels for all family groups. 

Eligibility requirements for each benefit are designed to insure more adequate payments to survivors who were directly dependent on the serviceman at time of death. 10

COMPARISON OF MAJOR ELEMENTS PRESENT LAW VS. H. R. 7089 

The basic differences between present law and H. R. 7089 are illustrated on this chart. Currently, VA Death 

Compensation pays a flat amount to beneficiaries, regardless of the grade or rank of the member, the only variation being whether death is attributable to war service or peacetime service. Current peacetime rates are $96.80 per month for a widow with one child, and $23.20 for each additional child. Where there are no children, the widow may receive $69.60 per month. 

Under the proposed bill the present fixed death compensation schedule would be replaced by a new system of Dependency and Indemnity Compensation. The new schedule of compensation would provide a rising scale of benefits to all widows, with or without children, based upon a fixed amount of $112 per month plus 12% of the deceased member's basic pay. The flat sum principle is retained in payments to orphaned children and dependent parents but benefits are increased over present levels. 

FECA benefits are payable during peacetime only, but are considerably higher than VA payments and are pay. able to none except survivors of reservists. The proposed bill would remove the present discrimination by repealing FECA death benefit coverage for military personnel. 

The present system of partial, gratuitous Social Security coverage based on a $160 monthly wage credit is deficient in several respects. New entrants into service, having no previous wage credits, receive no coverage for the first 18 months of service. Members who eventually retire lose their military credits upon leaving active service except under certain limited conditions of retirement. Furthermore, since the present gratuitous coverage is a wartime expedient and very costly to the OASI Fund, its continued existence is uncertain. Under 

H. R. 7089, military personnel would be placed permanently under Social Security on a contributory basis. New entrants would have insured status immediately. No member would lose coverage upon leaving service; Government costs would be reduced, and the range of benefits for most servicemen would equal or exceed those provided under the present free coverage.

The existing "Free Indemnity" of $10,000 is paid to wife, children, parents, or brothers and sisters at the rate of $92.90 per month for 10 years. Dependency is not a requirement, and the ultimate amount paid is $11, 148. 

Under the proposed plan, the Free Indemnity is terminated and the resulting savings are used to significantly increase the level of death compensation payments. This has the salutary effect of providing more adequate payments to dependent survivors while reducing Government gratuities to nondependent beneficiaries. Present holders of NSLI and USGLI policies retain all contractual rights under such policies. 

Death Gratuity at present is calculated at six times the current monthly items of pay, exclusive of allowances. 

This results in an insufficient minimum of $468 per month, an excessive maximum of over $7,000. The proposal would retain the present method of computation but would establish a minimum payment of $800, and a maximum of $3,000. 12

BENEFITS ARE PRESERVED OR IMPROVED FOR SURVIVORS PRESENTLY ON VA ROLLS 

H. R. 7089 carefully preserves the rights and privileges of survivors now on VA rolls.

DEATH COMPENSATION IS PARTIALLY RELATED TO INCOME 

As indicated on the facing chart, military income (basic pay) ranges from a minimum of $78 per month for the E-1 (Recruit) to $1076 per month for the 0-8 (Major General). This income differential recognizes the variation in responsibility and seniority between the lowest and the highest ranks and is consistent with American traditions.

H. R. 7089 brings death compensation payments into partial relationship with the lifetin e income of the serviceman. But there is still a much greater spread between the range of incomes than there is between the range of death compensation payments. This pay-related death compensation is not a new concept in the annals of American Service life. As early as 1780 and continuing until 1933 (except for deaths from 

World War I service) death compensation payments to survivors of servicemen were geared to the pay of the serviceman. FECA benefits are now and have been pay-related since 1925 for Navy reservists and since 1939 for Army reservists (in peacetime). 

Since all other survivorship programs in and out of the Government embrace the principle of compensating survivors according to the lifetime income status of the deceased, the Department of Defense strongly supports the adoption of this feature in H. R. 7089. 20

DEPENDENT MEDICAL CARE MUST BE PROVIDED BY THE ARMED SERVICES 

The providing of medical care to the dependents of Armed Services personnel has been a traditional responsibility of many years' standing. The conditions of military life are such that it becomes a matter of simple equity and justice. It is a vital element to morale and well-being and certainly constitutes one of the most important and significant of all military career inducements.

Since the end of World War II, due to a variety of causes, it has been impossible to furnish such care to large numbers of military dependents. 

During this same period that such medical care has declined in the Armed Services, it has been furnished to an ever-increasing degree by private industry to its employees and their families. 

The assurance that medical care will be available to the family of any serviceman contributes immeasurably to his morale, well-being, and effectiveness. However, the fact that existing service conditions preclude such assurance is considered to be a major factor for the increasing difficulty of the Armed Services to attract and retain an adequate mumber of competent career personnel. -2-

MEDICAL CARE FOR DEPENDENTS MUST ALWAYS REMAIN A CONTINUING FUNCTION OF MILITARY MEDICAL FACILITIES 

The provision of medical care for dependents at armed services medical facilities is absolutly essential to the maintenance and development of a career medical service. 

The fact that medical care is provided dependents contributes directly to the primary medical mission by attracting and retaining competent career personnel who find it possible to maintain their professional proficiency through the opportunity to extend their professional service to military dependents. 

The retention of professionally motivated, well-qualified, career personnel in turn reduces the military requirement for new physicians with the resultant decrease in the need for obligated service for doctors. 

Since military hospitals must necessarily maintain a capacity in excess of normal military needs, it would be most uneconomical not to utilize these facilities for the care of military dependents. -6-

MILITARY MEDICAL CARE NOT AVAILABLE TO 40% OF DEPENDENTS 

Because military medical facilities are not always accessible, medical care cannot be provided for approximately 40% of our military dependents. 

This percentage seems surprisingly large in view of the extensive amount of medical care presently being provided to dependents; however, indications are that, if anything, this estimate is probably conservative. The Moulton Commission, in 1953, and the Hoover Commission, in 1955, accepted this estimate as valid.

The development of the 40% estimate is predicated upon many considerations; however, particular significance was attached to a comparison of the rate of both outpatient and inpatient care provided dependents overseas as contrasted to the same care provided dependents in Continental United States. 

Since dependents overseas look to military medical facilities for practically all medical care, the ratio of the number of treatments provided to the number of eligible dependents should approximate complete care for all dependents. This ratio, when compared to t'e number of treatments provided eligible dependents in Continental U. S., indicated that the rate of care was less than 60%, and hence the estimate of approximately 40%.

This large group of dependents who do not receive military medical care fail to do so for one or more of the following reasons a. Distance

Many dependents reside at considerable distance from service medical facilities and therefore find it impractical or impossible to avail themselves of this care. 

b. Overtaxed facilities 

Unfortunately, dependents sometimes find themselves located near military medical facilities which, due to an unusual concentration of military personnel and dependents, find it impossible to provide medical care to all dependents. 

Necessary treatment unavailable - A third and often most important reason for the unavailability of medical care is due to the inability of a service medical facility to provide the care or treatment required. This may be because the treatment is beyond the capabilities of the medical facility or, in the case of the Navy, the treatment is specifically excluded by law. -12-

SCOPE OF MEDICAL TREATMENT PROVIDED 

The scope of medical treatment provided the dependents of military personnel varies among the Services. Generally speaking, the Army and Air Force both provide the same degree of treatment with the only restriction being the "availability" criteria. However, the fact that care is provided on an "availability" basis and without compromising the service rendered to military personnel on active duty, in most cases, effectively reduces the scope of treatment to practical limits.

Army and Air Force - There are no specific statutory limitations in the Army and Air Force. However, the extent of medical care provided in Army and Air Force installations is based upon the number and qualifications of assigned medical officers, the adequacy of supporting staffs, the accommodations available and other pertinent factors. Parents requiring domiciliary care because of age and chronic invalidism are not admitted except under emergency conditions for a short period.

Outpatient care, physical examinations and whole blood are provided without charge. 

Prosthetic devices are not included in dependent medical care with the exception of spectacles, which are sometimes provided outside the continental United States where spectacles are not ordinarily available from existing civilian sources. Emergency and limited dental care is provided when it does not interfere with treatment of military personnel. This constitutes a minimum service due to the magnitude of the military requirement. Navy In the case of Naval dependents, P.L. 51, 78th Congress, specifically limits the care and treatment which is available through military facilities. Regulations established by the Bureau of Medicine and Surgery of the Navy Department provide that treatment shall be limited to dispensary and hospital care for acute medical and surgical conditions, exclusive of nervous, mental or contagious diseases. Unlike the Army and Air Force and because of the restrictions of P.L. 51, dental treatment is not provided dependents except as an adjunct to medical treatment.

PURPOSE OF THE ACT (Now P.L. 569) 

One of the most disturbing influences on the family man in our Armed Forces is the frequent inability to secure medical treatment for his wife and children when they need it. This is particularly true when the serviceman is overseas or otherwise separated from his family. Surveys reveal that the decisions of many men to leave the service and return to civil life are influenced by the non-availability of medical care for their families. 

Again it should be noted that private industry is constantly extending and improving such medical care programs for its employees and their families. 

This is one of the major obstacles in Department of Defense efforts to enhance the attractiveness of military service. 

It is thus significant that the high purpose of the Act is to provide an IMPROVED and UNIFORM program of medical care for dependents and thus create and maintain high morale throughout the uniformed services. -20-

ESSENTIAL PROVISIONS OF H. R. 9429 (NOW P. L. 569) 

Under the provisions of H. R. 9429, medical care will be provided all Service dependents by continuing the present practice of providing medical care at Service facilities, when available. Also, in order to provide medical care for those dependents for whom it is not presently available, this program will be supplemented by 

a. Requiring the establishment by the Secretary of Defense of a group health insurance or medical service plan, providing certain minimum services, for the spouse and children of active duty personnel. Coverage is automatic and at no cost to the member; however, dependents will continue to be required to pay a per diem charge of $1.75 for each day of hospitalization at military facilities. DEFINING THE PROBLEM 

The President has determined that the strength of the active military force will remain in the neighborhood of 2,850,000. The President, the Secretary of Defense and the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff have made public statements that we must expect to maintain such a level of forces for periods ranging from "decades" to "the forseeable future."

Under this indefinite duration concept it is essential that the active forces be provided with a corps of officers who will give it guidance, continuity, immediate combat readiness, professional competence and of particular importance, stability.

Today the officer corps of the active force is not characterized by stability. 

Three-fourths of the commissioned officers now in service are reservists and thus do not have the assurance of career status in the permanent establishment. 

The lack of career security and opportunity, and the uncertain prospects for a military future is influencing too many able officers to return to civil life. 

Indications are that without corrective action, this situation will worsen in the immediate future.

The President has stated that "lack of reasonable stability is the most wasteful and expensive practice in military activity."

REGULAR OFFICER "HUMPS" ARE A PART OF THE PROBLEM 

Any discussion of regular officer structures and problems must take cognizance of the World War II "humps." 

These "humps" were created in each Service when large numbers of combat experienced officers of like age and service were integrated after World War II. 

Unforeseen developments over the past 10 years have kept these "humps" virtually intact. They have now moved forward in the officer structure to the point where they give rise to many personnel problems which will increase in severity in the immediate future.

The continued existence of these "humps" will have a deteriorating effect on officer morale which can only intensify the instability problem. 

The establishment of higher regular officer authorizations for the Army and Air Force as proposed in H.R. 11683 would materially assist those Services in eliminating many "hump" problems. 18

DOCTORS' CAREER INCENTIVES 

HR 9428 (As presented to the Congress)

FOREWORD 

The critical and alarming shortage of career medical and dental officers in the Armed Forces has become a problem of serious concern to the Department of Defense. 

Because of the fact that proper medical care for members of the armed services is vital to the combat readiness of our fighting forces, and since the resource of experienced physicians eligible for call under the Doctor Draft Act is dwindling rapidly, it is apparent that immediate and positive steps must be taken to improve the military career for medical and dental officers. 

The problem was assigned to the Department of Defense Inter-Service Committee on Military Career Incentives in June 1955 with the objective of expediting corrective actions to the maximum extent possible. Study of the problem by the Committee revealed that there were a number of improvements that could and should be made by the Services administratively to enhance the military medical career. These internal actions have been initiated. It is evident, however, that to solve the problem completely and eliminate the ultimate need for the Special Doctor Draft law, certain legislative improvements are essential. 

This pamphlet gives background information on the development of the existing situation, the factors involved and recommendations for legislation needed to assist in establishing incentives that will attract more physicians and dentists into the military service as a career. 1

COMBAT READINESS REQUIRES HEALTHY FIGHTING MEN 

The combat readiness of our military force, which is the largest in our peacetime history, depends upon the health and well-being of the fighting man. The Congress traditionally has assured that medical care would be provided for military personnel, even though it required the conscripting of doctors. 

One-third of our military personnel are required to be overseas or on the seas, many of them in remote locations throughout the world. 2,000 medical facilities or units throughout the world must be staffed. Military medicine is in itself a specialty and a very important one. 

Many specialties, such as aviation, submarine, and atomic medicine, medical logistics and readiness planning are, for the most part, peculiar to military medicine and require a great amount of technical training. Medical care for nearly 400,000 dependents overseas can only be provided by military doctors. 

It is a simple fact that just as our shipyards must keep our fighting ships at sea and just as our air depots must keep our bombers flying--so must our military doctors keep our men fit to fight. This fact is sometimes overlooked by those who are not familiar with constant readiness requirements and other factors inherent in the military system. 

Since World War II there have been tremendous opportunities in civilian life at increased incomes for this country's physicians and dentists. The military medical career today offers little in competition with a civilian career. This fact will be shown more clearly in later charts. The Department of Defense is faced with the problem of counterbalancing the superior attractions of a civilian medical career in order to provide a stable corps of career military doctors.

These needs can best be met by voluntary service rather than involuntary service. 2

During the last three fiscal years, 13,593 physicians have entered the military services under the impetus of the Doctor Draft Act. During the same period 16,031 medical officers left the service and returned to civil life. These gains and losses have produced a to tal turnover of almost 30,000 doctors against a requirement of about one-third that number- It is especially significant that only 515 were appointed as regulars while 1,169 regulars resigned or otherwise left the services. 96% of the gains came through the compulsion of the draft, either directly or indirectly.

HOW MANY CAREER DOCTORS DO WE NEED? 

Since it is almost impossible to obtain experienced volunteer physicians from the reserve, a corps of 100% career doctors would be ideal. This is not feasible from the standpoint of procurement capability, however, and would be impractical due to possible fluctuations in the future size of the Armed Forces.

It is the objective of the Department of Defense to increase the career medical and dental officer strength to 2/3 of the total as a minimum requirement for adequate stability and experience levels. 

The additional one-third would be obtained through a modified regular draft. 

Establishment of this two-thirds career structure would have the two-fold effect of producing a stable, experienced medical and dental corps and eliminating the need for drafting experienced doctors from civil life. ONE CAREER MILITARY DOCTOR REPLACES FOUR DRAFTEES EVERY SIX YEARS 

An excessive amount of lost motion is involved in replacing short service, non-volunteer doctors every two years. It has been estimated that each additional career military doctor would eliminate the need for drafting four civilian doctors over a 6-year period. 

At the present time approximately 7,000 civilian physicians are brought into the military services every two years under the impetus of the draft. Over a six-year period this adds up to over 20,000 doctors.

Each incumbent physician must be processed administratively, including such things as security checks, physical examinations, initiation of records and other time-consuming actions. 

Each one must be given a certain amount of military indoctrination and special training. 

These necessary preparatory actions, plus the more frequent moves generated by short service tours, reduce the time these doctors spend in productive practice of medicine by an estimated 25%.

The cost of travel for the officer, his dependents and his effects to and from active duty within a two-year period, plus other essential travel to relatively short-term military assignments, is expensive in dollars and results in inefficient use of scarce professional personnel. 10

ECONOMIC OPPORTUNITIES ARE MUCH GREATER FOR THE CIVILIAN PHYSICIAN 

This chart shows a comparison of the average annual net incomes (after office expenses) of civilian physicians in comparison with incomes (total pay and allowances) of military doctors over the period 1928 to 1955. 

In 1928 the average civilian physician was receiving only slightly more annually than a Lieutenant Commander or Major in the military service with about 13 years service. 

During the period of the depression in the early thirties, the military doctor was generally in a better position financially than his civilian counterpart. It is significant to note that during this period the services had up to 10 applicants for every vacancy in the Medical Corps and thus could be highly selective, and comprehensive written and oral professional examinations were required for military internships and regular commissions. 

On the other hand, the average annual income of the civilian physician since 1943 has far outstripped the income of the military doctor. The average net income of civilian physicians (after office expenses) doubled in the period from 1929 to 1949 and has nearly tripled in a period of about 27 years. By contrast, the income of a military physician (Major or Lieutenant Commander) has increased by less than 75% during this period. This great differential in income is a vital factor in the decision of many outstanding career military doctors to leave the service and return to civilian practice. 

The value of retirement and other traditional military benefits, which once gave the military career certain distinct advantages over civilian pursuits, has largely been neutralized in recent years by the overall upsurge in civilian incomes. As shown on this chart; it is clear that the the economic opportunities open to physicians in civil life are such that the military services cannot compete with them. 

This disparity in income potential was dealt with by the Congress when the $100 monthly special pay was authorized in 1947, as can be seen from the cross-hatched area on the chart. However, the gap between the income of military and civilian physicians has widened sharply and steadily during the intervening years. 12

NON-MILITARY FEDERAL GOVERNMENT PHYSICIANS HAVE MANY ADVANTAGES OVER THE MILITARY DOCTOR 

Civil Service or Veterans Administration physicians have advantages of generally higher pay and shorter working hours with less night and weekend duty, as compared with military doctors. 

The former can choose the place where he wants to live and work. He is not subject to frequent moves and he is generally located in or near large population centers with associated professional and cultural advantages. 

The military doctor must accept living conditions as assigned, often overseas, or in isolated continental areas with associated limitations, including at times inadequate or expensive housing. Separations from family and exposure to operational and combat risks are unavoidable for the military doctor. Although the military doctor has certain advantages, the balance is not favorable to a military career. 

Military retirement is the chief advantage; however, Civil Service and VA retirement benefits are fairly comparable. Also, military service counts in increasing benefits of Civil Service or VA retirement. The overall effect, in view of the pay and other differences cited above, does not favor a military medical career. Military incomes should adequately compensate for these differences and disadvantages, if the military medical career is to compete. 

Extensive use of Civil Service physicians is not possible at present because of Civil Service Regulations and pay scales. Even if these restrictions were removed, however, extensive use of civilian physicians would not be a desirable solution to the problem because of the increased cost to the Government and a further decrease in the attractiveness of a military medical career.

In general, civilian physicians in significant numbers could only be obtained in or near large population centers. This would decrease the number of military doctors who would receive assignments to these more desirable posts and bases and, conversely, would increase the number of undesirable assignments such officers would receive during a service career. COMPARATIVE RETURNS ON EDUCATIONAL INVESTMENTS 

According to a report by the Institute of Life Insurance, lifetime earning potentials increase in proportion to the level of attained education investment. 

A person with an elementary education earns only about one-third as much as the college graduate. Likewise a medical education, even though it delays earnings for several years and is expensive, proves a good investment in civilian life as lifetime earnings are increased by 50% or more over the college graduate. 

Under military pay scales, however, the extra education delays earnings, increases costs and results in no increase in earnings. The extra education, in fact, tends to cause a net loss for a medical officer under military pay scales. 

The existing situation, whereby the years required to qualify for the practice of medicine place the medical officer at a disadvantage in comparison with his line contemporaries, is difficult to justify and is an inequity that should be corrected. 18

A MILITARY MEDICAL CAREER VERSUS A CIVILIAN CAREER 

As this chart indicates, aside from the opportunity for professional service, a military medical career today offers little in competition with a civilian career. 

The military physician is generally faced with a fixed ceiling of $12,776 annually, whereas a civilian career offers unlimited possibilities. While the career military doctor with ten years service earns approximately $8640 per year (total pay and allowances) the civilian physician in private practice averages over $16,500 (net income after office expenses).

The doctor in the military services must conform to military discipline, have a knowledge of the service of which he is a part. He is subject to the frequent moves and separation from his family and the austerity which are inherent in military life. In civilian practice he has independence, more prestige, stability and affluence. 

The present necessity for compulsory service is made apparent by the contrast in opportunities open to the young doctor today. The unlimited economic potentials and the individual freedoms inherent in civilian practice far outweigh the limited advantages of a military medical career.

The problem faced by the Department of Defense in meeting requirements for career-motivated military physicians is one of supply and demand. 

The military demand has been met since 1950 only through the operation of the special Doctor Draft. As succeeding charts will show, the supply of experienced physicians eligible for draft is not sufficient to meet continuing requirements without recall of individuals who have served during World War II or subsequently. 

Establishment of a military medical career that will attract a sufficient number of volunteer doctors is essential to the well-being of our servicemen and thus to the combat readiness of the Armed Forces.

CLINICAL SPECIALISTS ARE REQUIRED FOR THE ARMED FORCES 

In addition to adequate numbers of experienced physicians for military medical specialties and military medical readiness positions, clinical specialists are also required. 

There are some 43 general hospitals and 222 station hospitals located throughout the world which require trained and qualified specialists in the general fields of Surgery, Medicine, Neuropsychiatry, and related or subspecialist groups. Large ships and certain other units also require physicians with two or more years of clinical residency training to insure adequate care for personnel. 

The minimum requirement for specialists in the Department of Defense is 3861, which amounts to a slightly lower percentage of specialists than are available in civilian life.

During FY 1956 the number of specialists (board qualified or board certified) available in the military services is 3440. This comes within about 400 of the requirement; however, in 1957 this number reduces to 2217, or less than 60% of minimum requirements. 

Adequate medical care will be seriously jeopardized under such circumstances. 

The trend must be reversed so as to retain more specialists and other experienced physicians in the Armed Forces. Otherwise, the difference will have to be made up from among civilian physician specialists. This would necessitate a continuation of the Special 

Doctor Draft law and, ultimately, the calling of Priority IV World War II and Korean Veterans to meet minimum needs for specialists. 24

OUR GOAL 

Previous charts have indicated the need for a solution to the problem of maintaining an adequately stable corps of military physicians. Our goal in accomplishing this end is to so improve the incentives of the military medical career that voluntary service will replace the compulsory service now provided by the Doctor Draft Law. 

Specifically, our objective is: (1) to emphasize educational opportunities through provision of residency training and adequate professional experience opportunities; (2) to emphasize professional prestige by providing the opportunity for the doctor to practice in his specialty and in the profession of medicine to a maximum degree; and (3) to increase the attractions of the military medical career and, at the same time, afford maximum opportunity for the doctor to continue the practice of clinical medicine. 26

MEDICAL EDUCATION YEARS PLACE MEDICAL AND DENTAL OFFICERS AT A DISADVANTAGE IN COMPARISON WITH THEIR LINE CONTEMPORARIES 

A significant fact not generally recognized is that under existing circumstances the medical or dental officer starts his career four to five years behind his line contemporary. Whereas the line officer accrues service for promotion and pay purposes from the date of entry into military service after graduation from college or one of the service academies, his age contemporary must spend five additional years securing a medical education or four years qualifying for a commission as a dental officer. This differential has been critically significant in the non-acceptance of a military medical career, particularly because of the supply and demand problem of doctors. 

In the case of the medical officer, he begins his military service five years behind his college contemporary and is thus penalized for that time spent in qualifying for his medical commission. While the same delay applies insofar as qualification for civilian practice is concerned, the doctor who enters civilian practice has unlimited economic potential whereas the military medical officer faces fixed income ceilings.

These promotion and pay differentials between contemporary medical and line officers have been the source of general dissatisfaction among the medical officers. This is particularly true when they observe the unlimited opportunities open to them in civilian practice. 

It is the specific purpose of H.R. 9428 to increase the attractiveness of a military medical career. This includes equalizing promotion eligibility and longevity pay between medical officers and their line contemporaries. The bill would authorize up to five years' constructive service for promotion and longevity pay purposes for the years spent in acquiring his medical education. This would permit the adjustment of service credit for officers now in the active service and would allow officers newly appointed to enter the service under circumstances generally equal to that of their line contemporaries. 

As reported and passed by the House of Representatives, the bill also authorizes increases over and above the present $100 monthly Doctor Special Pay of $50 monthly after 3, 6, and 10 years of service. By this feature, it makes the military medical career more financially competitive with civilian medical practice. 28

THE PROPOSED PLAN WILL REDUCE THE INCOME SPREAD BETWEEN THE MILITARY AND THE CIVILIAN OR CIVIL SERVICE DOCTOR 

The proposed one-year increase in constructive service, coupled with administrative adjustment of temporary promotion criteria, will enable the medical officer to qualify for promotion to 0-3 (Captain/Lieutenant) upon completion of internship. This will increase his pay from $501 to $577 monthly (total pay and allowances). The comparisons shown on this chart reflect this adjustment as the point of departure, based on years of professional service.

The proposed authorization of longevity credit for the 4 years of medical education, plus one year for medical internship, will equalize the medical officer's pay with the starting rate in Civil Service. This increased longevity credit will also reduce the disparity as shown by the cross-hatched area in later years and higher grades, as will the special pay increases authorized after 3, 6 and 10 years active service as a medical or dental officer.

There will still be differences of one sort or another in such factors as working conditions, night and week-end duty, frequent moves, separation from family, exposure to operational and combat hazards, retirement, and the like, which may be more or less favorable to one group or the other. However, despite these factors, the military doctors' income should at least approximate that of other Federal Government physicians. 

This is particularly true since other Federal agencies are having difficulty in attracting and retaining physicians even under these higher pay scales and often with opportunity to supplement Civil Service income by part-time private practice. Both military doctors and Civil Service or VA doctors have difficulty in competing with the financial and other attractions of civilian medical practice. 30

THE LONGEVITY CREDIT PRINCIPLE IS NOT NEW 

Longevity pay for service personnel originated about 1835, but did not become applicable to officers until 1870. However, the Army and Navy did not include academy time in computation of length of service until 1884 when the Supreme Court in the case of U.S. vs Morton rendered a decision that under existing statutes, a West Point graduate was entitled to include his academy service for longevity pay purposes. The Supreme Court's concluding sentence stated -----a cadet at West Point is serving in the Army as fully as an officer retired from active service is serving in the Army, under the statutes which apply to him so far as the question of longevity pay is concerned. " 

Academy service therefore accrued to officers for pay purposes from 1884 until the Army Appropriation Act of 1912 and the Navy Appropriation Act of 1913 specifically provided that Academy time ". - shall not be counted in computing for any purpose the length of service of any officer

The act of July 1, 1922, authorized only active-commissioned service to be counted in the computation of longevity credit for pay purposes. The concept of that act was considerable altered by the Pay Readjustment Act of 1942. The 1942 Act authorized longevity credit for active duty and virtually any and all inactive service in some 16 different Reserve components, plus active duty in the Philippine Scouts and Philippine Career Compensation Act of 1949 continued the 1942 concept.

PURPOSE 

Basically, the bill under consideration, H. R. 10212, has been designed to attain two objectives. The first of these, and of major importance to the services, is to eliminate present pay inequities by removing the discriminatory provisions of the Career Compensation Act of 1949 which deny longevity credit for ROTC and Academy service but authorize credit for practically every other type of service, both active and inactive. The elimination of inequities has been one of the major goals of the Department of Defense during the past few years. 

In recent months the press and other news media have expressed concern over the loss of trained personnel in the Armed Forces, thus focusing attention on the need for additional career incentives. An increase in longevity credit after four years of active duty represents a monthly pay increase of $47 to the young Navy lieutenant (Army, Air Force Captain) - an attractive inducement to stay in the service. 10

From the standpoint of career incentives, enactment of this bill should reduce officer turnover, and at the same time enhance regular officer procurement. "THE POSITIVE SIDE OF THE DEPARTMENT OF DEFENSE LEGISLATIVE PROGRAM TO IMPROVE MILITARY CAREER INCENTIVES" 

Personnel is the number one problem Mr. Wilson and all of us who share the privilege of working with him at close hand recognize this problem and it is at the Secretary's direction that I give you this action report today. 31 You are the leaders and it's to you that we look for the best administration of our personnel legislation and the Defense policies which stem from these enabling laws. 

The best law can be neutralized by inept personnel policies and sluggish administrative practices and in many instances inadequate legislation can be vastly enhanced with the results of positive leadership and enlightened personnel practices. 

Because of the intense interest and concern that have been expressed in many quarters, and because of its impact upon our efforts to improve the military career, the Secretary of Defense has asked that we take stock of the situation to see what might be done to dispel some of the negative attitudes and highlight the positive sides of our over-all personnel picture and program. 

We try to keep fully aware of the many problems being faced by all the services today in retaining their trained personnel. We realize also the seriousness of the situation as it affects our combat readiness. We have been wrestling with these problems strenuously and, we believe, with good effect. 

We have been working closely with the President, the Executive Branch and with the Congress with the common objective of expediting the enactment of much-needed remedial legislation and altering administrative practices to fit the changing times, We have had good success with what is called "the career incentive legislative program, as I will show you presently in more detail. First, however, I should like to be re-emphasize the fact that we have been receiving the strongest kind of help and support from both the Congress and the President, 11 2

I should also point out to you that we have observed in some members of Congress a growing attitude of confusion and some resentment, due largely to the fact that there seems to be too little recognition or understanding service-wide of the scope and intent of legislative actions recently completed or underway to improve the personnel situation. This lack of understanding, combined with the clamor for more is beginning to create a feeling on their part that they can neither solve the problem nor satisfy the armed services. 

Just recently a prominent member of Congress stated as follows: "We thought we did rather well last year in the Career Incentive Act, and found it had hardly been signed when certain officials, both military and civilian, were immediately advocating an additional pay increase so as to leave the personnel feeling that nothing had been done for them lately.' 11

Considering the size of the over-all military establishment and its huge cost to the taxpayers, these are understandable problems. All things considered, the attitude toward the armed services has been better during the last two Congressional sessions than at any other time in recent years perhaps better than at any other peacetime period. We certainly must try to maintain this favorable climate and to avoid, insofar as we can, causing this pendulum to swing in the opposite direction. 

Just recently, Mr. Paul Kilday, a key member of the House Armed Service Committee and one of our strongest and best informed supporters in the Congress, commented on this very situation during public hearings on one of the career incentive bills. He stated in effect that he did not feel that the armed services themselves were doing all that they could to emphasize the advantages to be found in career service, especially as they have been recently improved. He pointed out that, in seeking corrective legislation, as it is their duty to do, the armed services have an equal obligation to assist in working out their own problems and also in "counting their blessings" where they exist. 

So, taking a cue from Mr. Kilday, we would like to review with you some of the positive features of the career legislative program.

A very significant fact that must be faced by all citizens today is that military service has become an important and sizeable part of the American way of life. 

When two men meet, one no longer asks the other, "Were you in the Service?" but rather, "What Service were you in?" Or, if they are younger, not, "Are you going into the Service?" but more likely, "What Service are you going into?" The fact that most men have been or will be in one of the military services is now taken for granted everywhere.

It is well for us to remember that our future political, business, educational and religious leaders will have all spent their hitch in the service, as will the great mass of farmers and workers of every category. 11 It is this great flow of manpower in and out of the services that we know as "personnel turnover. Certainly for those in the armed services occupying positions of leadership and authority it presents a serious military problem. And to individual Americans in other walks of life in and out of service it presents serious personal problems. To deal with it effectively, we as citizens must be aware of its impact on the Country as a whole. To solve it the whole Country must learn to live with it. 

We are keenly aware that the services are being hard put to maintain a high state of combat readiness and to keep pace with the advancing technology of complex weapons and equipment. Each year huge numbers of men who complete their obligated service return to civilian life, to be replaced by like numbers of new men who must be trained and indoctrinated. But, while this complicates our military problem, it enhances the over-all skill level of the working force in the country and at the same time spreads the military training throughout the population.

The inconvenience and nature of military life as opposed to -- The lure of greater comforts and more stable opportunities in civilian life.

The Survivors Benefits Bill which is regarded as a number one morale builder is only awaiting final action by the Senate. 

Until April 1955, Survivor Benefits legislation had been before the Congress for several years. At that time, the Task Force took over responsibility for the legislation working in the closest coordination with Congressman Porter Hardy's Select Committee. This bill won final House approval well before the close of the 1955 session.

This bill is a career incentive measure of far-reaching importance. 

It will remove many of the inequities and inconsistencies existing under present law, and greatly improve the over-all survivorship structure. 

The widow of any regular is presently entitled to only $70 from the Veterans Administration per month if the husband's death was service-connected and occurred in peace time, and $87 where such death occurs during wartime. Additional payments are authorized for minor children. 

On the other hand, the widow of Reservist may be entitled to as much as $525 under the Federal Employees Compensation Act. This unintentional but glaring affront has long shouted the question: "Why be a regular?" 

The proposed bill will substitute for the present flat payments, a more equitable benefit bearing at least some relation to attained pay. Thus all widows of those who die on active duty or from service-connected causes would receive monthly compensation according to a new formula: $112 plus 12% basic pay. This produces compensation payments ranging from a minimum of $122 to a maximum of $266.

The bill will place all active duty military personnel under Social Security on a fully participating and contributory basis. 

In addition to the Social Security Benefits payable to a widow and minor children up to $200 monthly, the bill in its present form will also provide additional Social Security old age benefits to both the serviceman and his wife after they attain age 65.

It should also be pointed out that this bill will in no way affect any existing contractual rights under either Government or National Service Life Insurnace. Nor does it change in any way the present Contingency 

Option Act, whereby retired personnel may elect annuities to be paid after their death to their widows and minor children. 32

The Dependent Medical Care Bill, just recently enacted into law, provides very important extension of medical care to the dependents of all service personnel. 

It was estimated that, prior to its enactment, due to the shortage of doctors and over-crowding of service hospital facilities, at least 40% of service dependents were unable to receive adequate medical care. 

The new law specifically authorizes nedical care for the dependents of both active and retired personnel in military facilities whenever they are available. 

A significant new feature of the law authorizes the use of civilian hospitals and facilities for the wives and children of active duty servicemen whenever they do not have convenient access to military hospitals either in this country or abroad. 

The cost to a service family for this civilian hospitalization will be limited to an initial payment of $25, or an amount equivalent to the subsistence charge being charged in military hospitals, whichever is the greater. 

By thus assuring adequate hospital care at all times to the wives and children of active duty personnel, it removes one of the major deterrents to career service. 

Those who may feel or have termed this new law, just recently signed by the President, to be deficient and merely of some help fail to take into account the long lead time usually required to enact complex legislation of this nature. 

Such uncalled for efforts tend to accentuate what we don't have and we may well never get. We could all better devote our time and effort in highlighting the positive advantages of this new and extremely beneficial legislation that will in fact do so much for so many and for the first time. 34

The Doctors' Career Incentive Act was must legislation to prevent the imminent dissolution of the hard core of experienced doctors within our medical and dental corps. In order to maintain the important health and medical research aspects of combat readiness and to provide care for both the serviceman and his dependents, it is essential that the number of experienced doctors on active duty not fall below a certain minimum strength. 

To maintain the required number of doctors on active duty since the Korean emergency, a special Doctors' Draft has been necessary. This unpopular legislation terminates in 1957. Less than one-third of our doctors are regular career personnel, and during the past three years these have been resigning in prohibitive numbers in order to enter private practice. Only a very small percentage of the drafted reservists have been persuaded to remain on active duty beyond their obligated service. While these conditions have produced a tremendous turnover of doctors, we have been able nevertheless to maintain the proper levels of medical experience. Commencing next year, however, we are faced with a situation whereby the pool of experienced doctors eligible to be drafted will be depleted.

It therefore became clearly evident that, unless drastic measures were taken to reverse the present trend, the armed services were faced with the grim prospect of its active duty doctor corps being comprised almost entirely of conscripted, "green" doctors fresh out of medical school. 

As the result of a careful study made during the past year by a special task group comprised of both doctors and line officers, certain inequities were uncovered that apply only to military doctors. For instance, all such doctors were four to five years behind their college contemporaries in the line by reason of the years they must spend in medical or dental school in order to even qualify for a medical or dental commission. They were behind in both seniority and longevity pay. 

This was further accentuated by the rapid promotion of junior line officers during recent years. Even more important was the fact that the potential doctor had to finance entirely his own medical education following his graduation from regular college; and this during a time while his line contemporary had a military status and received a salary.

The bill as it went to the Congress provided for promotion and longevity credit for all doctors for the years spent in medical school, thus placing them on a par with their line contemporaries. The Congress, in its consideration of the bill, concluded that the proposed corrections were not sufficient inducement to meet the critical medical situation. Accordingly, the bill was amended to provide increases in their special pay for those doctors who remain voluntarily on active duty after completion of their obligated service. 

I believe we should recognize the fact that doctors perform a very personal and essential service in caring for the health of our service personnel, both active and retired, and also dependent members of their families. 

While a doctor's training is a long and expensive one, his services are in great demand everywhere, and his financial opportunities in private practice are usually far greater than we can ever provide under a military pay structure.

On both this bill and that to improve medical care for dependents the Task Force has worked in close conjunction and sponsorship with Dr. Berry, the Assistant Secretary of Defense (Health and Medical). Dr. Berry has been conducting a vigorous campaign to attract more doctors into voluntary military service. In this connection it is significant to note that if we were able to add an additional three thousand career doctors, then we could avoid having to draft 12 thousand doctors over the next six-year period. 40

This career incentive program, together with internal actions by all services, has produced results. The over-all regular reenlistment rate has nearly doubled in the two-year period since 1954 from the low of 24% to 43%, the latest reading being in March of this year. 

The rate for those completing their first enlistment, the so-called "first termers", has increased from an extremely low 10% in 1954 to 23% through March 1956. While this rate is still too low, it is a great improvement over the low point of 1954. 

Six-year reenlistment rates have jumped from 47% of the total reenlistments in 1954 to 77% in 1956, which is a very healthy trend. This is undoubtedly traceable in part to the

Now for a look at the "first termer" reenlistment experience by skill groups. Here, too, we have made good progress; but it is also clearly evident that here lies our most serious enlisted personnel problem. 

Reenlistment rates in the most critical skills are still well below the average for all other "first termers", and far below those for the least critical skills. Electronic maintenance technicians are reenlisting at the rate of only 14% compared to 40% for food service personnel. The rate for communications specialists is 

20%, whereas security personnel are reenlisting at the rate of 38%. 

It is encouraging to note that the rate of 27% for Aircraft and Engine Maintenance Technicians is above the average "first termers" rate. It is also good to see that reenlistment experience in the critical specialists skills has improved substantially in 1956 compared to 1955. Rates for Electronics and Aircraft Engine 

Technicians have doubled in one year's time. The rate for communications personnel has increased by 7%. 

The fact remains, however, that the lesser skilled individuals are reenlisting at a far greater rate than are those who require lengthy and expensive training. This is the real crux of the problem--to bring these rates into better balance and thus keep pace with the increasing complexity of modern arms. 

There is no discrimination between men of the same grade and length of service for different levels of special military qualification. All receive the same. This situation leads men in the hazardous and non-hazardous fields to seek out the less arduous and less demanding assignments because the compensations are the same. 

That is why we are 25% short in the combat arms MOS of the top three pay grades in the Continental Army and 25% over in administrative specialists. Similar examples of imbalances could be found throughout the Services. 54

The Committee will also give special attention to the administrative improvements that might be made within existing law. For example, increased service obligations for academy and certain ROTC graduates, also for other officers and enlisted men who receive lengthy and expensive training. The possibility of greater utilization of industrial contracts for those missions that can be handled that way will also be studied. 

In addition these additional matters will be looked into thoroughly: 

1. The control of accrued leave pay, currently costing about $170 million annually. 

2. Mental qualitative distribution. 

3. Unrealistic physical standards which hamper our ability to acquire and retain certain critically needed personnel. 

4. Assignment, rotation and conversion policies and programs. 

5. Career development and counseling.

These are the principal areas in which the Cordiner Committee will concentrate. And, of course, a precise determination will be made as to what type of legislation is needed to augment the administrative actions. Our chances for further legislation in the next Congress will be greatly enhanced by our ability to show results from the administrative opportunities that are available to us. We hope each of you will give the Cordiner Committee the benefit of your long experience and close personal contact with these and other problems by submitting any ideas you have for the Committee's consideration through your service representatives. 

It is important to remember, however, that increased pay alone will not be the complete answer. I am not trying to second guess the Committee. Mr. Cordiner has said - and we have all seen the evidence of it--that pay by itself has not solved the personnel problems of industry. The closer military pay gets to civilian pay, the more certain it is that industry will adjust its scales to keep their distance. The important fact is that non-pay benefits are being used more and more by industry to satisfy the wants of employees, coupled with enlightened personnel practices. 

There is no doubt that some form of variable compensation for different skills is needed. It will be the job of this Committee to come up with the right formula. In the meantime, as a transition measure, the Department of Defense has already sent to the Bureau of the Budget a proposed plan for special reenlistment compensation, with the hope that it might be acted upon before the close of this session of the Congress. This proposal would allow the Service Secretaries to pay special rates of reenlistment compensation to personnel in various skills, ranging from one half to three times the present reenlistment bonus. 

We have the feeling that we can do more with the same amount of money we now use for reenlistment compensations. This visualizes a system with flexibility in it. The system would pay a higher reenlistment compensation to the individuals who are in short supply and a lower amount to individuals in over supply. This would be an incentive system at work within the Services which, by the law of supply and demand, would compensate for overages and shortages in personnel with special military qualifications by motivating individuals with the needed talents to enter the shortage fields. 58

Further, we have made some detailed comparisons of military and civilian compensation and benefits in several selected skills. Here we see some reflected situations where the value of all free benefits have been added to basic pay and allowances of the military man and to the pay of the civilian. 

Taking the case of a 4-engine commercial pilot and a flying Air Force captain with equal experience and family, adding up all income and benefits, we see that their net income is very nearly the same. After taxes are paid, the civilian pilot takes home approximately $1,100 per month compared to $1,010 for the military pilot. And the civilian pilot still must make a contributory payment for his retirement, his group health insurance and for part of his life insurance out of the $1, 100 net pay. 

Three other civilian occupations are also shown in comparison with an E-5 Staff Sergeant or Petty Officer 1st Class. In each case, the military income adds up to more than that of the civilian median when all free benefits and allowances are added in. The civilian aircraft mechanic and electronics technician are airline employees; the fire control technician is employed by an aircraft manufacturing firm.

Just as in the case of the airline pilot, all of these employees have to pay part of the cost of certain benefits such as retirement and insurance out of their net pay. 

The value of military retirement alone is a big factor in the serviceman's pay. In the case of the military pilot an investment of 45% of base pay each month over a 30-year period would be required to produce an annuity equivalent to his 30-year retirement pay. The civilian airline pilot actually pays $116 per month toward his retirement fund, with the company making up the difference as required. Further, Defense still has an advantage in the man's opportunity to retire after 20 years' service. 

Doctors can retire after 20 years and still start another practice without having had to pay for their retirement annuity with tax paid dollars. 

The Staff Sergeant would have to invest 60% of base pay each month to match his retirement pay at 20 years.

The survivor benefits provided by present law would cost him about $86 per month. Improvements now under consideration would add another $35 to the value of these benefits. 68
