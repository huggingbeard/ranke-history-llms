PHOTOGRAPHY OF FINGERPRINTS ON MULTI-COLORED OBJECTS (Introductory Note: The following article describes a novel method for photographing fingerprints, which has been produced by Detective Inspector Alan Evans of the Derbyshire Constabulary (England). This splendid police organization is one of the leaders in the development of all practical phases of police science. Under the leadership and encouragement of 

Major F. R. Anley, its Chief Constable, the C.I.D. maintains single fingerprint and modus operandi files, numerous crossindices to general criminal records, and pursues its investi gations with the aid of microscopy, photo-micrography, chemical analysis, ultra-violet light, and the examination of clothing, stains, blood, dust, and other traces. 

Inspector Evans' paper holds special interest. There are many methods for adding coloration to latent fingerprints on paper. Edmond Locard, in his Traite de Criminalistique (Vol. I, pp. 130-141), lists 69 of them, and there are cer tainly a number of others which possess varying degrees of utility. Yet neither in Locard's monumental work, nor so far as we are aware, in any other place, is there described a satisfactory method for photographing fingerprints on a multi-colored surface. All efforts have heretofore been most unsatisfactory in their results when the surface was glossy or glazed, and have proved fruitless in the case of flat-finish multi-colored papers bearing latent fingerprints. 

It appears, however, that the problem has at last been solved, and Detective Inspector Alan Evans, of the C. I.D., Derbyshire Constabulary, describes in the following article. how he accomplished it.

This article has been rather thoroughly annotated for the convenience of American police officers who will have occasion to employ Inspector Evans' simple and practical method. .... Bruce Smith.) 721146

For the past four years research work has been done in this Department to devise a satisfactory means of photographing fingerprints on a multi-colored object. Objects with only two or three colors in the background presented but little difficulty as the interference of the colors could be negativ. ed by inserting suitable filters between the source of light and the object or between the camera lens and the object. 

In most cases panchromatic plates were helpful. 

When the object had a glossy surface the difficulty was overcome by arranging the lights so that a diffused light was reflected on the surface of the object immediately behind the fingerprints which then showed up black upon a white back. ground no matter what kind of powder had been used for their development. The resulting plate was, of course, a negative, and this method has frequently been used in this Department for marks on glass and other glossy surfaces to eliminate the unnecessary labor and expense of first making a positive. 

The difficulty had, however, only been partly overcome and various other means were tried to obtain the same result with marks on a multi-colored matt surface. For the past year we have continued the experiments, using ultra-violet light as the source of illumination and developing the latent prints with fluorescent powders.

The object used for these experiments was the matt paper label of a fruit tin bearing the colors of the visible spectrum (reds, yellows, gold, greens, blues, purple and white). Fig. 2A is a photograph of the label taken with a reversed panchromatic plate and ordinary light. Finger marks on the label were developed with various fluorescent powders and photographed on various kinds of plates. Some of the powders did not adhere well to the latent impressions, in 1

some the fluorescence was of low actinic value so that long exposures were necessary with 2000 H. & D. plates, while other powders lost their fluorescent properties after a few days exposure to light and air.

In referring to a fluorescent powder I mean one that has the power to absorb the short ultra-violet rays and emit rays of longer wave lengths. the incident radiation is usually invisible and the emitted radiation visible. 

Anthracene (1) powder was suggested by Superintendent Else, F.F.Sc., F. R. M. S., of this Force, and experiments proved it to be vastly superior to any of the powders previously used. Photographs of the fingerprints on the matt label of the fruit tin (developed with anthracene) however, still showed the design of the label in the background and it was found that the fluorescence would have to be isolated by screening off the ultra-violet rays reflected from the surface of the paper.

The camera used had ordinary lenses which we believed would not transmit ultra-violet radiation but we found that sufficient short wave rays were transmitted to spoil the ef fect. The difficulty was overcome by using a filter of "Andrews Super Protex Lead Glass" (2) over the lens of the camera to filter off the ultra-violet rays reflected from the object and the result was that the fluorescent anthracene pow. der was photographed without any background at all, with a very short exposure.

Photograph at Fig. 2B shows that the ridge characteristics of the fingerprints are perfectly clear. The anthracene powder on the prints has retained its fluorescent properties for two months though it has been left unprotected in the lab. oratory the whole time. (1) Anthracene is a hydro-carbon derivative of coal tar.--(Ed.) (2) A heavy glass containing lead; especially useful in X-ray work. It is described as having "a sharp cut-off on the violet end of visible light and is almost abruptly impervious to even the near ultra-violet."
