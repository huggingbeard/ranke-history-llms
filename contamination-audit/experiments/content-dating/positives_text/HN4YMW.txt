INTRODUCTION 

The shortage of suitable Jewish literature in English for young people is well known in Jewish education circles, and numerous attempts have been made, with varying degrees of success, to meet this need. The present volume, adopting the popular technique of the question and and answer form, covers a vast amount of material drawn from the living storehouse of Jewish culture and serves a most useful purpose in this field. Rabbi Williamowsky's selection of material is excellent: the first sets of questions furnish a handy reference to the highlights of Biblical personalities and events; the later sections deal with well chosen precepts and maxims, affording a review of Jewish ethical values; and the questions on the various holidays will be found of informational value to both young and old.

In short, the book is an anthology of pearls of Jewish culture, combined with selected bits of information which may be employed to test the reader's background or to add to his knowledge. 

Do not look for anything new in these pages. Only that which is deathless has been included. Biblical stories which will live for all time are adequately summarized; law and legend regarding Jewish observ ances are cogently presented. We have need of such books, for they lead the reader temptingly on from question to question, from page to page, and ultimately to the original sources, where we may drink more deeply of the riches which we sample here.

AUTHOR'S NOTE 

This volume has been made available for public use as the result of a sincere desire on the part of the author to promote a deeper interest in the hidden treasures of the Bible, both in the religious school and in the home. Its purpose is to serve as a guide for teachers, group leaders, and parents in the furthering of religious knowledge. Conse quently, no suggestion has been included as to the method of presentation to the pupil.

Reactions from those who use this volume will be appreciated.

SCRIPTURE RIDDLE I 

Five hundred begins it; five hundred ends it; 

And five in the middle is seen.

The first of all letters, the first of all numbers, Have taken their station between. 

And if you correctly this medley can spell,

The name of an ancient king it will tell. 

I end as I began 

RIDDLE II 

The weal and woe of man; 

Yet do not harshly blame, I bear my mother's name.

RIDDLE III 

Four heads have I but body none, And without any legs I run. 'Midst bliss supreme my lot was cast 

And joys that could not be surpassed 

Yet these delights did I forsake 

And far away my course I take.

Yet, while I wander far or nigh Still ever in my bed I lie. 

RIDDLE IV 

Afar they watch my whole arise, Its summit seems to touch the skies. "When all is done," the crowds exclaim, "Then shall we make ourselves a name!" 

Remove a letter, and behold! 

A shepherd issues from his fold;

With blood, devoutly, draws he nigh, Himself, alas! how soon to die. 

Remove the letter still and now- 

Before an idol-god they bow,

To wood and stone is worship paid, 69

And men adore what men have made. 

Remove a letter yet once more,

We see an altar stained with gore; 

And he who built it named it thus, To teach a precious truth to us.

RIDDLE V 

In every clime, in every age,

In history's eventful page, My first will always rise to view, And wake our love and hatred too. 

My second and my third will each 

Express the selfsame part of speech, And, though two interjections brief, May paint a world of joy or grief, My whole most surely was my first, But far more firm and brave in faith.

This wife a mighty patriot nursed, Who nobly died a hero's death.

RIDDLE VI 

Oh, skillful the workers, and mournful the day When, within its recesses, they hid him away. 

So gracious, so noble, the pride of the state, Their friend and their patron, the good and the great. 

Oh, wondrous the moment when forth from the land

They bore it, fulfilling the solemn command, Still truly remembering the vows of the past, And keeping the long-cherished promise at last. 

Oh, great the rejoicing when, after long years,

Its treasure unfolded still changeless appears, Unfolded awhile, then for ever concealed 

Till the day when the secrets of all are revealed. 70

What is the purpose of the "Yomin Noroim?"

To urge the Jew to self-examination and reconciliation with God.

Do the Jews in Palestine observe one or two days of Rosh Hashanah?

Why is Rosh Hashanah (doubled) two days instead of one?

Rosh Hashanah, according to the Talmud, is a day on which the Lord considers, investigates and judges criminal cases, which involve human life. Such cases, according to Jewish law, must stay over night, so that the defendant may have time to produce evidence in his favor. (Talmud Yerushalmi, Rosh Hashanah)

What is the shofar made of?

The shofar is made of a ram's horn. It is always bent, has a finished mouth-piece, and is unadorned. 103

10. Q. When, during the day's devotions, is the shofar sounded? 

A. Before the scrolls are returned to the ark. 

11. Q. What are the sounds produced by the shofar? 

A. There are four different sounds: 12. Q. A. (a) Tekiah-the unbroken sound. (b) Shevarim-the broken sound. (c) Teruah-the wave-lip sound. (d) The long Tekiah-the prolonged unbroken sound. 

How are these sounds arranged?

In three sets of blasts. The first is Tekiah, Shevarim, Teruah, sounded three times in succession. The second is Tekiah, Shevarim, Tekiah also sounded three times in succession. The third is Tekiah, Teruah, Tekiah; sounded three times, with the exception that the last Tekiah is prolonged.

16. Q. On what occasions in the life of the Hebrews was the shofar blown in ancient times?

A. The blowing of the shofar in ancient times had many functions. on Mount Sinai. The shofar was also sounded as a sign of freedom and liberty in the Jubilee Year. It was sounded at the conquest of Jericho by Joshua and Israel. It was blown in the war of Gidon against the Midianites. The shofar was sounded as a sign of rejoicing. At the coronation of kings. The shofar was sounded as an alarm in distress or danger. 104

A. It is customary to partake of sweet fruit or bread dipped in honey. 19. Q. 

What is the ceremony of "Tashlich?"

A. Worshippers go to the edge of a running stream to shake their coats or empty their pockets of crumbs of food into the water, thus to symbolize their casting away their sins. While doing so, prayers are recited, concluding with the words, "May God cast our sins into the depths of the sea".

A. 

What is the Lulab?

A festive palm branch which is waved by observant Jews during the Sukkot festival.

Of what fruits and plants does the Lulab combination consist? 

A palm branch, three myrtle twigs (Haddasim), two willow branches (Arovos), and a citron (Esrog). The term Lulab is applied to the combination of all these four species. 

8. Q. What moral lesson may one derive from the use of the Lulab on Sukkot?

A. The four species, when kept together, teach us the value of unity among the various sects and factions of Israel. The Esrog being 109

tasteful and odorous, is representative of the class that is both intelligent and active; the Lulov, that is not fragrant but produces fruit, symbolizes the class that is only active. The 

Haddas, that is odorous but has no products, is likened to the class that is only intelligent or educated. The Arovos, devoid of either of these qualities, is like the inactive and unintelligent among people. Each has its specific worth and its designation. 

When united, they are invincible, and are capable of withstanding all outside destructive forces, regardless of their strength.
