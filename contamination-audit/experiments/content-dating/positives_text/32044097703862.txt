Annual Report of the Governor of American Samoa H. Rex Lee, Governor 

​Review of the Year 

During the early part of fiscal year 1962 every phase of Government was devoted to the planning of a complete rehabilitation program for American Samoa. The new Governor with the strong support of the Department and Administration spent the early months of the year outlining a 3-year rehabilitation program that would strengthen and reorganize every phase of public service in American Samoa. While the program goals were to provide Samoa with good public services in all fields and to encourage industry and commerce so as to provide an economic base, the first year's program was concentrated on completing those public projects which were needed by July 1, 1962, for the Fifth South Pacific Conference, which was to be held in Pago Pago in July 1962. 

The first phase of the program was accepted by the Congress and a total of $9½ million of Federal funds and $1 million in local funds was appropriated and made available by October. At the request of the Governor, a skilled survey team of top-flight Naval engineers reviewed the existing and proposed construction projects and made general recommendations. An agreement was completed with the 

Fourteenth Naval District in Pearl Harbor whereby skilled engineers and technicians were loaned to the Government of American Samoa on a reimbursable basis to assist on the projects authorized in the rehabilitation program. These technicians not only supervised the various construction projects but also trained many Samoans to do their work. The technicians returned to Honolulu upon the completion of the training. Later in the year two detachments of the Fourteenth Naval District Reserve Construction Battalion volunteered a month's reserve training duty in the territory to provide further assistance in the construction program. 1

By March 1962 virtually every able-bodied man in Samoa was working 10 hours a day, 6 days a week to insure completion of the most needed projects by July 1. By this date an imposing list of projects was completed and was ready for the Conference. They included a 9,000-foot jet runway, which will provide facilities for through jet service from Hawaii and West Coast points to Australia and eventually New Zealand; a striking Polynesian style civic auditorium that seats 800 people; a new power plant with two 1,000-kilowatt diesel electric generating units and with additional space to be expanded to 6,000 kilowatts in the following fiscal year; 27 new housing units for Government employees; 3 new high school buildings; 

812 miles of new paved highway from the airport to downtown area, with a contract let for an additional 12 miles to be completed by October; a complete renovation of many public buildings and a Government clean-up campaign which included the tearing down of dozens of old, dilapidated Government buildings throughout the island. A general clean-up and paint-up campaign was sponsored by the Government and through the sale of paints at cost over 90 percent of the buildings on the island of Tutuila were painted by July 1.

Education 

An agreement was completed with the University of California at Berkeley to assist in the recruitment of outstanding teachers and 40 new teachers were hired to bolster the school system. With the three new high school buildings and the new teaching staff, for the first time American Samoa was prepared to enroll 100 percent of her graduates from the intermediate schools. Heretofore only one-third could be enrolled, because of space limitations. Among the many steps taken to bolster the grossly inadequate elementary school system was the Governor's proposal for an educational television system which, through the use of a dozen outstanding teachers in the studio, could bring the best in teaching to some 300 classrooms that are now being staffed by poorly trained local teachers. It would also be the media for adult education, ranging from such subjects as public health to classes in self-government. The project had the enthusiastic support of the Secretary, and the sympathetic Congress appropriated funds for a survey of the feasibility of such a program. The National 

Association of Educational Broadcasters made a survey of the possibilities and made a strong report recommending the system. By the year's end the Congress had adopted the program and advance planning was underway.

Other steps taken to bolster the basic system included a dropping of the entrance age for schools from 7 to 6 years. Salaries for stateside-qualified teachers were increased by one-fourth and a comprehensive new construction program to replace all inadequate school buildings was presented and approved by Congress. An agreement was reached with the University of Hawaii to train 20 Samoans in agriculture and home economics under the Area Redevelopment 

Administration Act. 

Health 

A team of doctors from Yale University and the University of California at San Francisco was brought to Samoa to survey overall health needs and an additional team from the U.S. Public Health 

Service to initiate the planning for a new hospital. An agreement was entered into with the Hawaii Medical Association whereby they agreed to send experts to American Samoa for a month of free service in specialized fields. Four surgeons under this program had given outstanding service by the end of the year and many specialty cases were located and corrected. Arrangements were made with the State of Hawaii to care for mentally ill persons from American Samoa and also to provide facilities for the hospitalization and medical treatment of unusual diseases which could not be handled in Samoa. An agreement was reached with the University of California at Los Angeles for a filariasis and internal parasite control program and three doctors from that university were in service in American Samoa before the year's end. Agriculture

Several thousands of various varieties of citrus and nut trees were planted in American Samoa during the year. A widespread poultry program was instituted and steps were taken to improve existing agriculture crops. A marketing specialist from Washington, D.C., made a survey of marketing facilities with a view to strengthening a very inadequate system. Legislature

The Legislature was called into special session in November and during this and the regular session in March, 29 bills were passed that virtually rewrote the Code of American Samoa, which was out of date. The ground work was laid for replacing law by regulations of the executive with legislative law.

Economic Development 

A contract was signed for the establishment of a coconut processing plant in the Pago Pago area which will process all of American Samoa's fresh coconuts and will spur coconut production throughout the Territory. The bulk of the coconuts for the plant, however, will come from Tonga and Western Samoa and will increase trade between those areas and American Samoa, thereby creating many new jobs. 

While the basic process of obtaining fresh coconut oil will create jobs for approximately 100 American Samoans, several specialty products, such as coconut flour, cocomats, charcoal briquets from coconut husks, and various derivaties from coconut milk, are planned, which will add an increased number of jobs. 

Negotiations were virtually completed for a second fish cannery and plans were about complete for the formation of an all-Samoan development corporation to build a hotel and other facilities to provide the basis for tourist industry. At the end of the year $150,000 had been pledged from hundreds of workers and other Samoans for such a development. While three other corporations were negotiating for hotel sites, these were being held in abeyance pending the completion of the Samoan corporation's plans. An additional spur to 

Samoa's tourist potential was the inauguration in March of 1962 of the South Pacific Airline's service between Honolulu, Tahiti, and

Samoa. 

Higher minimum wage rates under the Fair Labor Standards Act were set for industries in American Samoa as a result of Special Committee No. 4 which met in the territory. These wages became effective on September 14, 1961, in the following categories. In the fish canning and processing and petroleum marketing industries, a minimum wage of 90 cents an hour replaced the hourly rate of 75 cents. The minimum rate for the shipping and transportation industry was also increased to 90 cents, replacing the current 75-cent rate. Seamen included in this industry were covered by a minimum wage of 50 cents an hour. All other covered industries were included in a minimum wage of 70 cents an hour, as compared with the 55-cent previous rate. 

Geography General Information 

American Samoa is an insular possession of the United States administered by the Department of the Interior. It comprises the eastern islands of the Samoan group which are located along the 14° of latitude south of the Equator at about the 170 meridian west longitude. These islands are approximately 2,300 miles southwest of

+  

Hawaii and 1,600 miles northeast of the northern tip of New Zealand, in a direct line. Western Samoa is an independent state located 77 miles westward.

There are seven islands in the American Samoa group: Tutuila, where the capital, Pago Pago, and central administrative offices are located; Aunu'u; the Manu'a group, which includes Ta'u, Olosega, and 

Ofu; Swains, and Rose, both of which are coral atolls, the latter uninhabited (total area of the seven islands 76 square miles). 

The people of American Samoa are American nationals and represent one of the few remaining societies of Polynesians with a relatively small group of persons of mixed blood. The population of the territory has increased from 5,697 in 1900, when the jurisdiction of this Government was taken over by the United States Navy, to 20,051 according to the 1960 census. 

The main islands are of volcanic origin and are now mostly a series of ridges rising abruptly from the sea. Tutuila is of irregular shape, approximately 18 miles long and 6 miles across, at the widest point, and contains 42 square miles of land. A mountain range extends almost the whole length of the island which is nearly bisected by Pago Pago Bay, one of the finest and most beautiful harbors in the South Pacific.

The climate of the islands is tropical but pleasant. From May to 

November moderate southeast trade winds blow; during the other months the winds are variable. The heaviest rainfall occurs from 

December to March and the average precipitation per year for the past 40 years has been approximately 200 inches. The yearly temperatures range from 70° to 90° F., and the humidity is about 80 percent. 

The soil of the islands is of two kinds-clay and sandy loam. In the valleys the soil is alluvial and quite fertile for growing tropical fruits and taro. Due to the heavy rainfall the soil on hillsides is thin with little subsoil. Certain elements necessary to temperate zone plant life were apparently burned out at the time of the formation of these islands. In certain localities one kind of tree or vegetable will grow well and only a short distance away the same tree or vegetable will not thrive. Lack of growth in certain localities is due to mineral deficiencies in the soil. The natural resources of the territory are meager. 

History 

American interest in the islands of Samoa commenced with the report made by the United States exploring expedition, which visited the islands under the leadership of Lt. John Wilkes in 1839. It was not until some 30 years later, however, that a formal relationship was entered into with the people of Samoa by a representative of the

United States. Primarily as a result of commercial interest in obtaining harbor facilities and rights for a coaling station on the shores of 

Pago Pago Harbor, the U.S.S. Narragansett visited Tutuila in 1872 

and Comdr. Richard Meade entered into an agreement entitled "Commercial Regulations, etc." with High Chief Mauga, the high chief of

Pago Pago. While this treaty was never ratified by the U.S. Senate, it served effectively to prevent foreign influence from asserting any strong claim to the harbor.

In January of 1878, a further treaty of friendship and commerce was negotiated with the leaders of the villages adjacent to Pago Pago and this treaty was ratified later in the same year. It was proclaimed jointly by the United States and "the Government of American. 

Samoan Islands." The treaty remained in force for more than 20 years until it was superseded.

As a result of international rivalry between Great Britain and Germany and because of warfare between various factions of the Samoan population, the United States, Germany, and Great Britain entered into a general act on June 14, 1889, for the purpose of providing for "the security of life, property, and trade of the citizens and subjects of the respective governments who were residing in or having commercial relations with the islands of Samoa." This act also had as its aim the desire "to avoid all occasions of dissensions between their respective governments and the people of Samoa" while at the same time "promoting as far as possible the peaceful and orderly civilization of the people."

Under this tripartite agreement a form of government for the islands was brought into being; however, after a trial of some 10 years, it proved to be ineffective and destructive of the ends for which it was created and was superseded by the convention of 1899. This treaty between the United States, Germany, and Great Britain had the aim of "advancing amicably the questions which have arisen between the three powers in respect to the Samoan group of islands" and provided that it would supersede and annul all previous treaties between the powers relating to Samoa.

A major provision of the convention was the renunciation by Great Britain and Germany of any claims to the islands of the Samoa group east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich. Reciprocally, the United

States renounced in favor of Germany all rights and claims in respect to the islands of Upolu and Savaii and all other islands west of 171° longitude west of Greenwich. This treaty was proclaimed, after ratification on February 16, 1900, and on February 19 of the same year, President McKinley directed the Secretary of the Navy to "take such steps as might be necessary to establish the authority of the United

States" in the island of Tutuila and other islands of the Samoan group east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich.

The Secretary of the Navy established a naval station at Pago Pago. 

In April 1900, deeds of cession were negotiated with the leading chiefs and orators of Tutuila and Aunu'u, and on April 17 the U.S. flag was raised for the first time over these islands. On June 14, 1904, High

Chief Tuimanu'a, the King of Manu'a, and the chiefs of Manu'a agreed to cede their lands to the United States. 

The islands remained under naval administration with Pago Pago as an active naval base from 1900 to June 30, 1951. During this time considerable progress was made in the establishment of public works and medical and educational facilities. Little, however, was done to disturb the traditional village life of the people. 

Under the terms of the deeds of cession, the United States agreed that the chiefs of the villages would be permitted to retain their individual control over their separate villages, provided that their control was in accordance with the laws of the United States pertaining to Samoa and provided that such control was not obstructive to the peace of the people and the advancement of civilization. The United States also agreed to respect and protect the individual rights of the people, especially in respect to their lands and other property. As a result of this commitment, no large tracts of Samoan communally owned land have been alienated during the 61 years during which the United States has had administrative responsibility for these islands. 

Although the deeds of cession were not formally accepted by the Congress until February 20, 1929, the fundamental personal rights of the inhabitants of Samoa were protected by the Constitution and the people of American Samoa were given the status of American nationals. On June 29, 1951, the President of the United States by means of Executive Order 10264 transferred the administration of American Samoa from the Secretary of the Navy to the Secretary of the Interior, effective July 1, 1951.

People Ethnic composition. The inhabitants of American Samoa are 

Polynesians and are closely related to other people of the central and eastern Pacific. They form a part of the family which includes the 

Hawaiians, the Tahitians, the Tongans, and the Maoris of New Zealand. They are a friendly and generous people and their culture is rich in ceremony and mythology. The Samoan language is closely related to other eastern Polynesian tongues. The best evidence available to date indicates the ancestors of the present Samoans arrived in the islands from a homeland in southeastern Asia between A.D. 400 and 600. Although the Samoans have had increasingly close contact

+ 

with European institutions, they remain today an outstanding center of Polynesian culture. After more than 125 years of contact with 

Western influences, the fabric of the Samoan social organization remains intact and continues to be the dominant influence in the lives of the Samoan people. Samoa's basic economic and political unit is the extended family composed of related kin tracing their origins bilaterally back through the generations to mythological ancestors. Within their extended group, which is administered by a matai or chief, a collective family economy prevails. The matai is responsible for control of family lands and property and represents the family in the political affairs of village, county, and district councils. He is responsible for their welfare and is trustee of family lands and property.

Since the middle of the 19th century, the people of Samoa have been converts to Christianity and have been active in sending missionaries to other islands of the southwest Pacific. They have, however, at the same time retained those customs and traditional ceremonies which have remained meaningful within the context of the contemporary island environment. To a large extent they have also retained the warmth and the grace of their ancestors and have preserved unspoiled much of the charm of the Polynesians. Government American Samoa is an insular possession of the United States administered by the Department of the Interior by Executive order signed by the President of the United States, effective July 1, 1951. 

Executive Branch 

The executive branch consists of the Governor, Secretary, and departmental and office heads. As the chief executive, the Governor is appointed by, and exercises his authority under the direction of, the 

Secretary of the Interior. The Secretary of American Samoa assists the Governor in the administration of the territory and serves as Lieutenant Governor. He is also appointed by the Secretary of the Interior and represents the Governor during the latter's absence from the territory.

The executive branch department and office heads are appointed by the Governor and are responsible to him. The following departments and offices comprise the Governor's staff: Agriculture, Audit, Administrative Services, Communication, Education, Office of Samoan In formation, Legal Affairs and Public Safety (Attorney General), Lo cal Government, Medical Services, Personnel, Port Administration, and Public Works.

Legislative Branch 

Article II of the Constitution of American Samoa provides for the establishment of the Legislative Branch of the territory. The Legislature of American Samoa consists of the Senate and House of Representatives. Each of the 14 political counties of American Samoa elects a matai (chief) by Samoan custom to the Senate to serve for 4 years. One additional senator, who serves for 2 years, is elected on rotation from four counties in the Western District.

+ 1 The 17 members of the House of Representatives are elected by popular vote at the polls from the 17 representative districts. There is one delegate from Swains Island which the adult permanent residents elect at an open meeting to the House of Representatives, who has all the privileges of a member of the House except the right to vote.

The 17 Representatives and the delegate from Swains Island each hold office for 2 years. In the present Seventh Legislature, all the members of both the Senate and the House of Representatives, with the exception of the delegate from Swains Island, are matais (chiefstalking chiefs). Business of the two houses is carried out in the 

Samoan language and later translated into English.

It was signed by 68 duly appointed delegates to the Constitutional Convention and the Governor, the Chairman of the Convention. The 

Constitution became effective on October 17, 1960, as the basic law of the territory. Under the Constitution, the Legislature assumed increased authority in enacting laws for American Samoa. Judicial Branch 

The judicial power exercisable in American Samoa is vested in the High Court and five district courts. In accordance with the American principle of separation of powers, the American Samoan Constitution provides that the judicial branch shall be independent of the executive and legislative branches. The judicial branch is under the administrative supervision of the Chief Justice of American Samoa. 

All courts are courts of record.

The High Court consists of the Chief Justice, the Associate Justice, and four Samoan Associate Judges. The High Court has an Appel late Division, a Trial Division, and a Probate Division. The Appel late Division consists of the Chief Justice, the Associate Justice, and the four Samoan Associate Judges; the Chief Justice or the Associate

Justice and two Samoan Associate Judges being necessary to constitute a quorum for the hearing and determination of an appeal. 

The Appellate Division has jurisdiction to review on appeal final decisions of the District courts and of the Trial and Probate Divisions of the High Court.

The Trial Division ordinarily consists of the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice and two Samoan Associate Judges; the presence of the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice and at least one Samoan Associate Judge being necessary to constitute a quorum for the trial and determination of a case or controversy. For the trial and determination of a charge of treason, murder, manslaughter, or assault with intent to kill, the Trial Division consists of the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice and four Samoan Associate Judges, two of whom are temporary associate judges assigned by the Chief Justice from the panel of persons so designated by the Governor. 

The Probate Division consists of the Chief Justice and the Associate Justice, either of whom may hear and determine a probate matter.

American Samoa is divided into five judicial districts, each of which has a district court consisting of the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice, if present, and the Samoan associate judge assigned to it from time to time by the Chief Justice.

The district courts have jurisdiction, concurrently with the High Court, of all civil cases of debt or injury to persons or property in which the amount in dispute is less than $1,000 and which does not involve the title to or rights claimed in land. The district courts have original, exclusive jurisdiction of all criminal cases in which the

? maximum penalty which may be imposed is a fine of not more than $10 or imprisonment for not more than 1 month. The district courts also have original jurisdiction, concurrently with the Trial Division of the High Court, of all other criminal cases of misdemeanor. District courts in Manu'a, which comprise the fourth and fifth districts, in addition have original jurisdiction, concurrently with the High Court, of cases of divorce, annulment of marriage, adoption, all probate matters, and all criminal cases of felony except treason, murder, manslaughter, and assault with intent to kill.

A Samoan judge, or judges, sits in all cases, except cases in probate in which the court is exercising original jurisdiction. The Chief 

Justice and the Associate Justice do not sit together in any of the divisions of the High Court nor do they sit together in a district court. 

During the fiscal year ending June 30, 1962, there were 719 persons tried on criminal charges. Of these, 609 were convicted and 110 acquitted. Of those convicted, 31 were sentenced to jail and 562 paid fines. Ten performed labor for the public benefit in their respective villages under the direction of the pulenu'u (village mayor) of the village or the county chief. The fines of six were suspended. During the fiscal year the court disposed of 245 civil cases. 

Criminal cases are disposed of with as much promptness after they are filed as circumstances permit, due consideration being first given to the defendant's rights under the Constitution of the United States and the Bill of Rights in the Constitution of American Samoa. 

The fundamental human rights of the inhabitants of American Samoa are protected not only by the Constitution of the United States but also by the Bill of Rights in the Constitution of American Samoa, which is patterned very substantially after the Bill of Rights in the 

U.S. Constitution. 

The Attorney General prosecutes charges of felony. If the defendant pleads guilty to such a charge, the Court, before accepting the guilty plea, requires the Government to prove a prima facie case in order to avoid the possibility, remote though it may be, that the guilty plea may have been made by mistake or through a misunderstanding. 

In the case of a plea of guilty to a charge of misdemeanor, the court must be satisfied that the defendant clearly understands the charge and makes his plea voluntarily. If in any case it should appear to the court that there may be a reasonable doubt as to the defendant's guilt, he is permitted to withdraw his guilty plea and enter a plea of not guilty in order to prevent a possible miscarriage of justice. 

The Government of American Samoa provides at its own expense a public defender and an assistant public defender to assist persons charged with crime.

All court personnel are Samoan with the exception of the Chief Justice, the Associate Justice, and the Clerk of the High Court, who are from the United States.

The Samoan Affairs Office continues to have as its primary function the administration of the local government organization and acts as a link between the local Samoan officials and the various departments of the territorial administration. The major emphasis of the work of this office has been the development, consistent with traditional policy, of a self-sustaining and self-reliant system of local government, competent to deal with village, county, and district interests and concerns of both traditional and modern nature. The office also acts as an instrument for the coordination and dissemination of information from departments of the Government to the local political organization, and to assist in effecting departmental programs and providing useful assistance to the villages in solving their problems such as agriculture, water supply, sanitation, public health facilities, schools, and roads. The office is responsible for census-taking in the territory and for conducting general and special elections. 

One of the leading chiefs was appointed Secretary of Samoan Affairs during the fiscal year. The Secretary is the head of the Department of Local Government and is responsible for the proper administration of the district, county, and village affairs as provided by law and the supervision of all ceremonial functions. 

The administrative head of each of the three political districts within the territory is presently a Samoan district governor. He is selected by the district council of chiefs and appointed by the Gover-

!וד al nor for a 4-year term. The district governor has a district administrative assistant and a policeman on his staff. Within each district there are a number of counties and each of these 14 counties has a county chief, who is also selected by his county council and appointed by the Governor for a 4-year term. Within each village of the territory, a village Government representative is selected by the council and appointed by the district governor. His term of office may vary in length, being subject to the continuing approval of his village council, his county chief, and the district governor. All local officials are responsible to their respective district, county, and village councils. Under the provisions of the Code of American Samoa these councils are empowered to recommend laws which they consider to be desirable or necessary and to enact regulations of a strictly local nature in respect to the cleanliness of their villages, the making and clearing of roads, the planting of their lands and other local matters. 

A local government council composed of 3 district governors and 14 county chiefs was created during the fiscal year 1957. This council is gradually assuming the role of a policymaking body with the local government organization. It appoints its own officers who form an executive committee. This body maintains constant liaison with the 

Governor and the Secretary and acts as the coordinating body for all matters pertaining to local government. The council meets in the administration building and its officers are provided office space in order that they may be readily available for consultation with the Governor and other territorial officials.

Structure of Island Administration 

For purposes of administration the territory is divided into 3 districts, 14 counties, and 52 villages. The islands of Tutuila and Aunu'u are divided into two districts, known as the Eastern District and the Western District. The three islands forming the Manu'a group constitute the District of Manu'a. Swains Island, privately owned, is not included in any of these districts. For purposes of administration of governmental services, it is directly under the Governor of American Samoa. By an executive order in 1954 a village council, with mayor and policeman, was set up and a Government agent appointed. 

Status of Samoans.-Persons residing in American Samoa on April 

17, 1900, and persons born in American Samoa after that date are nationals of the United States of America and owe allegiance to, and are entitled to the protection of, the United States. Nationals have free access to the United States and become citizens after having met the necessary requirements of the Immigration and Nationality Act.

Qualifications of voters.—All residents of 5 years' standing, over the age of 20 years, not having been convicted of a felonious crime for which no pardon has been issued and not owing allegiance to any foreign power, who have resided for 1 year within the county in which they intend to vote, are eligible to vote in elections in American Samoa. 

All persons who have these qualifications, and who are at least 25 years of age and are either American nationals or American citizens, are eligible to run for and to hold office in the House of Representatives. 

At the village level the heads of families each year select one village chief to be the village Government representative and the county and district councils make recommendations to the Governor with regard to the appointment of county chiefs and district governors whenever vacancies occur in these offices or whenever elections are held. 

Section 7 of Article II of the Constitution provides that "Every person of the age of 20 years or upwards, belonging to either of the following classes, who shall have lived in American Samoa for a total of at least 2 years and have been a bona fide resident of the county or division where he offers to vote for at least 1 year next preceding the election and who meets such registration requirements as may be prescribed by law shall be deemed a qualified elector at such election: (1) U.S. nationals; (2) other persons of at least one-half Samoan blood who owe no allegiance to any foreign sovereign state. No person under guardianship, non compos mentis, or insane shall be qualified to vote at any election; nor shall any person who has been convicted of a felony be qualified to vote at any election unless he has had his civil rights previously restored to him or unless he has maintained good behavior for 7 years following the date of his conviction or his release from prison, whichever is the later."

Social Conditions 

The population of American Samoa is predominantly Samoan. 

There is a minimum of conflict of cultures, and no problems are created from diversity of races. It is a well-established policy of this 

Government to foster understanding and respect for Samoan customs, culture, and traditions. The Government endeavors to conduct its affairs in upholding the laws and policies of the territory without interfering with the Samoan way of life. 

Human Rights 

The Constitution of American Samoa contains a bill of rights. The fundamental personal rights of the inhabitants of American Samoa are also protected by the Constitution of the United States. There has been no need for specific antidiscrimination in American Samoa.

Labor and Employment Conditions 

The Personnel Office is responsible for recruiting Government employees and for processing documents pertaining to their employment. 

The policy of the Government is to employ persons from outside of American Samoa only for those positions requiring skills and administrative abilities not available in the territory and to train local personnel to fill these positions as rapidly as possible. 

At the end of fiscal year 1962 there were 2,046 local career employees on the rolls of the Government. In addition there were 151 persons f employed in the local government of villages and counties. There were 6 Federal employees and 47 contract employees from the United

States and other sources outside of American Samoa. An 8-hour day and 40-hour week is standard and straight time is paid for overtime. 

Occupational Organization 

There are no employers' organizations although employees are encouraged to exercise their rights under Executive Order 10988, which recognizes employee organizations. Labor or employment disputes are settled by discussion and arbitration. 

Equal Employment Opportunity 

The Government of American Samoa has appointed an Employment Policy Officer and an assistant and has issued Executive Regulation 1.02 dated January 26, 1962, in conformity with the Non 

Discriminatory Employment Policy Program established by Executive 

Order 10590 and the Equal Employment Opportunity Program directed by Executive Order 10925. A new Merit System Law was passed by the Legislature of American Samoa and approved on May 9, 1962. This law is based on the Federal plan and is revised as to meet local conditions. Candidates for employment are interviewed, examined when practicable, classified, and placed on employment registers from which positions are filled. Positions are subject to field or desk audits and reclassification as changes occur. Under the Merit 

System Law employees have the right to appeal to the Personnel Advisory Board for any personnel action they feel is unjust. This board conducts hearings and makes the terminal decision on appeals.

Unemployment

Unemployment is not a substantial problem at the present time because persons who are not gainfully employed are needed on plantations. The labor shortage on plantations is partially caused by the continued emigration of a sizable number of young adults to the United States for the purpose of employment, education, or travel and partly by the fact that an increased number of persons previously employed on plantations now prefer to work for wages. 

Migratory Labor 

There is no appreciable number of migratory laborers entering the territory. A moderate number of applicants for work appear from 

Western Samoa. 

The migratory problem is mainly that of emigration. Many of the better educated and trained young Samoans leave the territory for reasons shown above. This reduces the effectiveness of the labor force as well as the efficiency of Government service. Training In accordance with the policy of the Department of the Interior and the Government of American Samoa to replace stateside employees with Samoans as soon as they are qualified, an extensive off-island training program is maintained. At the present time many top administrative and operational positions are held by Samoans. At the beginning of this fiscal year there were 34 students on scholarships in colleges and universities in the United States, mainly in the teaching field, with the understanding that they will return to Samoa to teach for at least 2 years upon completion of their study. This year the 

Government is sending 31 additional students to colleges. In addition to teaching scholarships, two students will study to become physicians, one as a dentist, one as an sanitary engineer, one as a medical technologist and three for advanced training in special fields of nursing. The Government also sends Samoan medical personnel to seminars under the auspices of the World Health Organization, the South Pacific Commission, and other activities. At the present time four American Samoan students are attending the 3-year courses in the Avele Agricultural College in Western Samoa. Two graduates of this college are presently employed. Six students are attending the

A quota of students has been maintained in these schools for many years. Social Security and Welfare Services 

The Social Security Law was extended to American Samoa by Public Law 87-878, effective January 1, 1961. Under this act American 

Samoans will be eligible for survivors benefits as of July 1, 1962.

Government employees, private business employees, and self-employed persons are covered by this act. 

Free medical and dental care is provided the Samoans as a Government service. The charge for in-patient hospital care is 25 cents per day.

An Employees' Disability Compensation Act was placed in effect for employees of the Government of American Samoa on November 21, 1961. This act provides for payment of two-thirds or threefourths of the salary to an employee incapacitated by on-the-job accidents or occupational disease. It also provides for death and survivor benefits. The Government is by far the largest employer in 

American Samoa. Stateside affiliated businesses such as the local fish cannery and processing, shipping and transportation and petroleum marketing industries are covered by insurance to cover claims in the event of injury or death of employees. Other local businesses have not assumed this responsibility as yet. 

A limited retirement plan is still under study by the Social Security Administration for employees of the Government of American Samoa who have long service and who will be unable to continue work until they reach retirement age under the Social Security Act. Group Life 

Insurance is also a distinct possibility within the next year for Government employees.

There are no orphanages or children's homes in Samoa. Children are welcomed into the homes of closely knit large family groups. 

There is one women's welfare society in American Samoa comprised of local women who work closely with the local unit of the Red Cross in Samoa. This group of women collects contributions through donations and community benefit programs. They perform social services in the hospital, leprosarium and at public gatherings and they grant loans and contribute cash grants for emergency need.

Juvenile delinquency is not considered a serious problem in Samoa. 

A qualified social worker is employed in the schools in the child guidance program and in counseling in homes when this is considered advisable. An athletic program in the schools and for the general public has been successful in attracting the interest of young people.

Town and Rural Planning and Housing 

Until recently Samoa has had none of the problems of urbanization such as housing shortage, overcrowded living conditions, or conflicting uses of land. For a large part of Samoa this is still true. However,

due to increasing population, immigration of people from other islands, an increase of individual dollar income, and the effort to attract new business and industry to the island some of these problems have started to appear. Certain villages in the Pago Pago Bay area suffer from overcrowding, and hazardous and unsanitary living conditions. A real shortage of industrial and commercial land has developed. Due to a growing population and industrial development in the area the bay waters are in danger of being polluted. Although the misuse of land outside the bay is limited now, there is real evidence that certain natural resources such as watersheds, forest reserves, beach reserves, and recreational areas need protection. 

In October a land use study was conducted to establish overall land use areas for the bay area and to make recommendations on a broad range of island planning problems and objectives. This planning has been partially implemented in the 1961-62 construction program. As recommended in this study the Office of Architect-Planner was established in December 1961. This office was made responsible for island planning, Government planning, building design development and construction supervisor, private building and planning standards and inspection of private construction.

In cooperation with the Department of Public Health a set of building regulations covering structural safety, public safety, density, sanitary and sewage disposal has been developed. All building permit applications are reviewed for compliance with these regulations and Samoans are being advised on good building planning and construction. Local merchants are being encouraged to import better quality building materials.

The island planning work was also continued. The objective is to prepare legislation and maps for next session of the legislature to establish a zoning law and a planning commission that provides greater Samoan participation and is related to the growing economy. 

The first engineering studies for a Pago Pago Bay area sewage disposal system were made in April 1962. The Pago Pago Bay area land use studies were continued and refined with an objective of encouraging healthy industrial and commercial growth and yet preserving the bay as a natural resource of great value to the growing population and to future tourist industry. 

The first work was done to program and coordinate the construction programs of all Government departments and to relate the building construction and capital improvement program. 

The planning work done this past year is the groundwork for continuing programs that can assist in the development of the economic. potential of the island. Industrial and commercial land is to be made available through consolidation of scattered parcels, the elimination

Village development is a major concern. Public Health and Sanitation The main health problems in American Samoa are as follows: First, the physical facilities of the Medical Services are totally inadequate and need to be replaced completely. This applies to the hospital, dispensaries, student nurses quarters and the Public Health buildings.

The first step in planning for a new facility was taken by having a survey done by members of the U.S. Public Health Service. This survey was completed and will be used as a basis for future planning. 

The second problem in magnitude is one of increasing the level of proficiency of the medical personnel. This is being implemented by

more on-the-job training, higher levels of education by scholarship in stateside institutions and increased supervisory contract personnel. 

As the third problem, the lack of water supplies and adequate sewage disposal makes any Public Health program extremely difficult. 

Water and sewage disposal have been given high priority in the overall planning for the territory and should be well advanced in the coming fiscal year.

Fourth is the previous inadequate attack of disease states; these are chiefly filariasis and intestinal parasitism. Toward the end of the year, however, a program was underway in conjunction with the University of California Medical School at Los Angeles to bring these problems under control. The pilot programs were completed and will form the basis for a mass treatment program in the coming year. 

Although the nutritional state of the people remained generally good, certain problems are arising as a result of economic changes. 

With more people working on a monetary basis more canned food is being eaten and the diet of the Samoan is changing. The results are mainly seen in increased dental problems. Obesity continues to be the main nutritional problem, however.

Dental Services.-Dental Services is a section of the Department and functions within the sphere of supervision of the Medical Director. 

The dental facilities occupy a wing of the hospital where complete operatory, X-ray and laboratory services are available. Additional consultation services, both professional and technical, are available in adjacent hospital divisions.

The Dental Services personnel include one contracted stateside dentist as Chief of Dental Service, four Samoan Dental Practitioners, one of whom is Assistant Chief of Dental Services, and one Dental Assistant.

A total of 6,928 patients was seen during the period July 1, 1961, to June 30, 1962. This figure includes the hospital clinic, the outlying villages, and the islands of Manu'a and Swains. A gross of 15,367 treatments were completed. The treatments include examinations, prophylaxis, X-ray exposures, extractions, fillings, and prosthesis. 

The figures generally show that the bulk of the dental service is a complaint-diagnosis variety. The method whereby Dental Services is attempting to improve the caliber of dental health is in the public school dental health programs.

The present fiscal year has given every indication of an improved dental health program in the schools. A vehicle to transport personnel and equipment to the schools for examinations and prophylaxis and to bring school age children to the main hospital clinic for restorative work and minor surgery has been made available and additional new portable equipment has been purchased. 

Dental Services historically speaking faces an ever mounting burden of dental disease. Ironically, as the Samoan adopts refined foods, processed foods, and preserved foods the destruction of his dental tissues increases tremendously. Civilization with its greater knowledge and technology nonetheless destroys the hardest tissues in the human body faster than remedies can overcome. The "endemic" periodontal destruction so classical in the Samoan people from local mechanical factors (particularly the huge subgingival calculus deposits with the resultant lifetime of chronic gingival inflammation) will then become more complex and severe due to increasing tendency toward stress syndromes.

Dental Services must plan and direct greater effort into preventive channels, in particular with school age children, through a program of education and care (prophylactic and maintenance) and through fluoridation of water supplies and training of auxiliary dental personnel (dental hygienists) to work with the children constantly during the early school year. A program of fluoridation of the Pago 

Pago Bay area water supply was investigated and found unfeasible at the present time because of varied and unmetered supply from the water shed area.

The level of immunization in the population of American Samoa is high. Exact statistics are difficult to quote, however, because of the constantly shifting population of Western Samoans, which is estimated at approximately 15 percent of the total population. Routine immunizations against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, typhoid-paratyphoid, smallpox and polio (Salk) are given by the division. A filariasis control program was begun in June 1962. This is planned to be a cooperative program between the Pacific Tropic Diseases Project, School of Medicine, Universtiy of California at Los Angeles, under the supervision of Dr. John Kessel and the Department of Medical Services. The pilot project involving 1,000 persons in four widely scattered villages includes a clinical survey, complete blood survey, and mosquito survey, after which treatment with diethylcarbamazine will be instituted. The survey will be repeated after treatment and again in 6 months. A mass treatment program will be inaugurated as soon thereafter as possible. This will be coordinated with a mosquito and rat control program. 

Intestinal parasitism remains a problem due to the large number of persons infected and the generally poor environmental sanitation. A program of mass treatment is at present under study. 

Transportation of personnel and supplies to the more remote villages of Tutuila and Manu'a and Swains Island remains a considerable problem. The difficulty of communication adds to the above in programming effective medical care in these areas.

Objectives. The basic approach to education in American Samoa is to provide training which will enable the people to serve more effectively within their social, economic, professional, and political structure. At the same time, education must provide suitable background for those who will find it possible to take advantage of opportunities for higher education in the United States or other countries. Not only must the mastery of the English language be taught but also mastery of basic skills related to science, medicine, law, business, the arts, and other various fields. Therefore, it becomes the responsibility of the Government as the Samoans are influenced more and more by contact with Western civilization, to conserve the best of Samoan culture, and at the same time to guide them in developing social changes which are to their advantage, and to bring them to a better understanding of what is best for their well-being, health, and general improvement.

It also becomes necessary to help them preserve and develop their economic resources, to enable them to cope with the problems of an increasing population and increasing demands which accompany modern society, and to become a government as self-supporting as possible.

As English is the international language and the official language of the Government, it is essential that the children become literate in that language while maintaining the bilingual nature of their society.

Natural Resources Economic Conditions 

Of the approximate 40,000 acres of agricultural land in American Samoa only 10,000 acres are developed or partially developed. Of almost 30,000 acres of land presently in forest only the mere fringes have been touched, and that mainly for firewood. Millions of board feet of useful and exotic lumbers are yet to be harvested. The nearby seaboard also abounds in good grades of fish more than suitable for local consumption. Consequently, efforts are being made to develop agriculture, forests, and fisheries and to educate the people to the proper use of these resources. No mineral resources are known to exist nor are there any rivers of enough volume and strength to provide hydroelectric power.

Types of Soil.-The soil ranges from tuff plains to rich alluvial valleys. Much of the soil on slopes is residual, some on the plains is transported. There is little near-surface clay and the bulk of the soils can be classed as loam, containing quantities of humus, and quite dark in color. Because of the heavy rainfall, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and calcium are quite rapidly leached out, and earlier generations of Samoans developed a system of shifting agriculture because of this. Most of the group is underlaid with tuff, cinders, and stratified lava flows expansively broken and cracked because of a vast subsidence toward the end of the hardening process. The only clay substrata of any size known is that underlying the Aoloau plateau at approximately 40-800 feet above sea level.

Fisheries. Fish of all types are found in the waters surrounding the islands. The local cannery concentrates on marketable tuna for canning and export. The Fisheries Division of the Department is endeavoring to encourage fishing (fish, crab, lobster) for the local market, as well as stocking fresh-water ponds and swamps with Tilapia mosambica and salt-water ponds with Chanos chanos, Mugil cephalus, and Mugil vaigiensis. Interest is developing in building small craft for offshore market fishing.

Forestry. Indigenous forest covers more than seven-tenths of the total land area. A new milling project is being planned which will provide a source of income and an increased source of lumber. 

Conservation. Much of the land area is deficient in various nutrients and elements. The heavy, consistent rainfall and the porous nature of the substrata induces speedy leaching. In soil conservation, farmers are encouraged to fertilize little and often and are guided toward organic rather than inorganic fertilizers. The establishment of the forest reserves will provide education in forest conservation methods and techniques. Proposed legislation on fish conservation includes control of size of fishes; size, sex, and seasonal control on crab and lobster; and seasonal control on shellfish. Methods of fishing are also being established.

Because of the consistent rainfall no irrigation is needed. All but a few small areas need no drainage. Programs presently underway are designed to promote the use of agricultural machinery and training in use and servicing is being provided.

Agricultural Education and Research.-General agriculture is taught at the Vocational High School. Experimental and demonstration farms provide practical field experience for these students in carrying out demonstration and experimental work with established crops and experimental work with new and improved crops. Programs presently underway are designed to promote the use of agricultural machinery, and training in use and servicing is being provided at the Vocational High School. The Department provides a limited amount of equipment with skilled operators to farmers for a small rental fee. Both animal and plant breeding projects are carried out. on the Government farms and new and improved strains of livestock and plants are offered to farmers at low cost. 

Policy.—Agriculture policy and programs include expansion of existing and introduced cash crops for income and encouragement of production of truck crops for better general nutrition. Throughout the year many thousands of coconut palms and cacao trees have been propagated and planted under supervision of the Department. 

Other programs include the expansion of taro, banana, and pineapple propagation for distribution and establishment of vanilla and pepper nurseries for additional cash crops. With the exception of cauliflower and celery, all varieties of vegetables are now growr. on all Government farms, as well as in several individual projects. 

To further encourage projects, several thousand cockerels for broiler production and pullets for egg production have been sold at cost to interested individuals. Various cattle/coconut projects have also been commenced and pig breeding has been accelerated. The Department proposes to continue to develop and distribute coconut, cacao, and vanilla under supervised planting and to include pepper in fiscal year 1965. Although these programs require considerable Government expenditure, it is felt that each project undertaken in this territory is a step toward educating the people to better land use and economic progress through good farm management and better conservation practices. Industry Power Production.-During fiscal year 1962 a significant improvement to the island power system was completed in the form of two new 1,025 kilowatt diesel driven generators, housed in a new aluminum steel framed building having space for an additional two generator units of 2,000 kilowatts each. In addition, approximately 19 miles of 2.4 kv. transmission line was converted to 12.5 kv. Plans are underway and funds are available to add the generators and to install an addi-

tional 32.4 miles of transmission line, thereby providing firm power from the central plant to virtually the entire island of Tutuila. 

At the present time, a total of 1,500 kilowatt installed generating capacity in the old S.R.U. powerplant is being kept in service, primarily because of a residual demand at 2,400 volts and to safeguard the capacity in the old S.R.U. powerplant is being kept in service, priunits are installed.

During the year, a total of 7,860,900 kilowatt-hours of electrical energy was provided, an increase of 640,500 kilowatt-hours over the fiscal year 1961 production. Metered consumers totaled 1,047 at an increase of 36 over the previous year.

The nearby island of Aunu'u and the Manu'a group had no change in the electric power supply. These islands continued with a few small generators for isolated and special purposes, such as for dispensary and communication requirements. 

Water System. A single integrated water supply and distribution system serves a major portion of the Pago Pago Bay region from Faga'alu to Leloaloa. This system is the responsibility of the Department of Public Works. In other villages on Tutuila and other islands of American Samoa the villages themselves maintain and opérate the existing water system, supported by the Department of Public Works for material and guidance during installation or improvement. 

Total rainfall for fiscal year 1962 measured at Faga'alu reservoir was 252.7 inches, as compared with a total rainfall of 294.60 inches for fiscal year 1961. The total storage capacity of the system serving the Pago Pago Bay area complex is 2,165,000 gallons. The average daily consumption is 800,000 gallons. The storage backlog is obviously very small, with the result that water rationing is required if a few days go by without a fairly heavy rainfall.

During fiscal year 1962, water rationing totaled 764 hours as compared with 215 hours for 1961. Total consumption for fiscal year was 

375 million gallons, an increase of 1 million gallons over that of fiscal year 1961.

A stream gauge and rainfall record study was continued in cooperation with the Surface and Ground Water Branch of the U.S. Geological Survey.

A few test borings were made to determine the existence of ground water and the suitability of this source as a major supply of water but due to the great emphasis placed on an expanded construction program, sufficient studies have not been made for firm conclusion on ground water. Results to date indicate the character of the rock formation under the weathered zone may yield water readily to drilled wells. However, no positive pump test could be accomplished with respect to pumping rate, drawdown, and chloride content due to the

size of the test holes. Plans are underway to 'continue this study for ground water.

Fish Canning Company.-A U.S. company leased an established canning plant in American Samoa in 1954 and today the plant employs approximately 350 Samoans. Fish is supplied to the cannery by Japanese catcher boats and approximately 50 of these boats with a total crew of about 1,300 operate out of Pago Pago. It is expected with the establishment of a second cannery, this figure will double. 

Fuel Oil Company. With the leasing of the fuel tank farm facilities to a U.S. oil company in 1956, the territory became a fueling station for vessels and planes. Stocks of Navy standard bunker fuel are dispensed to both privately owned and Naval vessels, and different types of aviation gasoline are available to aircraft.

Copra Fund.-The Copra Fund is maintained under the direction of a board of directors whose members are appointed by the Governor and who serve without pay. The board is the policymaking body of the Copra Fund and has authority to make administrative decisions. 

The fund is the medium through which all the producers market their copra for shipment and sale in the United States. It's function is to carry out the policies and procedures set up by the board, purchase copra from the producers, negotiate copra sales, and maintain equipment and accounting records. Copra exports were 861 tons for a value of $133,086 compared to 356 tons for a value of $61,190 for fiscal year 1961.

Handicrafts. Items produced in the territory include laufala floor mats, woven goods, tapa cloth, woodcraft, and shell articles. Most of the women and young girls of Samoa are skilled in mat weaving. 

Products, of this type are marketed to tourists on ship-visit days and to local merchants who in turn export these handicrafts to Hawaii and the mainland.

Telephone. At the present, only the Pago Pago Bay and airport regions are served by a telephone system, which is Government-owned and operated by the Public Works Department. The main exchange located in Fagatogo is an antiquated relay type. The entire main exchange is to be replaced during the forthcoming year. A new stepby-step automatic satellite exchange was installed near the airport.

There is a total of 349 lines from the Fagatogo exchange in service, covering a total of 540 instruments. Of these, 50 are commercial and 

81 are private. The balance are all for Government purposes. 

The longest telephone trunkline is 7.14 miles. Cables vary from

Radio Communications.-The Communications Office operates on a continuous 24-hour basis, with a staff of 29 employees under the Communications Officer. The main office and message center is located in the Administration Building at Fagatogo. Here Government and commercial radiogram service is available connecting with all parts of the world, including South Pacific points and other islands of the Samoan group and ships at sea. A subscription press service is operated for posted and broadcast news coverage. 

Facilities providing these services include a multichannel system of radioteletype, linking American Samoa with terminal and relay facilities in Hawaii, where traffic is routed through connecting commercial carriers into worldwide systems. An emergency circuit via

Canton Island is also available. Radiotelegraph circuits are maintained additionally to Western Samoa and to Fiji for rapid service to South Pacific points. A coastal radiotelegraph service is maintained for communication with ships at sea. Radiotelephone service is also available. A radiotelegraph and radiotelephone service is operated to the islands of Ta'u, Ofu, Swains, and Aunu'u. An overseas public telephone service is projected for the near future.

Radio transmitting facilities are located in a separate building in Pago Pago, which is also the radio materiel office and electronics repair facility. This office is responsible for transmitter operations and maintenance, the maintenance of all communications electronic and teletype equipment, the radio broadcast station, and all other Government-owned electronic equipment, including sound and movie picture systems. As a courtesy service, emergency repairs are made to radio equipment aboard merchant and other privately owned shipping, as well as aircraft, at a nominal fee.

The Communications Office operates an aeronautical en route, aeronautical point-to-point, and control service at Pago Pago International 

Airport for servicing overseas scheduled airlines and other air traffic including nonscheduled, military, and interisland carriers. A meteorological office is included at the aeronautical radio station, providing observations for local and international dissemination. Radio-navigation beacon facilities are available. Radio transmitting and receiving equipment for the aeronautical station is located entirely at the airport, some of this equipment being remotely controlled. Preparations are underway for servicing of jet flight operations which are expected to commence shortly, and likewise for night flight operations which will commence upon completion of airport lighting facilities. 

The bay area, where the main Communications Office receiving facilities and transmitting station are located, has reached a stage of growth where it has become necessary to plan new remote receiving and remote transmitting sites for the overseas, ship-to-shore, and other radio services. Preliminary planning has been accomplished to bring this about. A further goal which will be realized as a result of these improved locations is a considerably extended range of coverage on important circuits. This will be attained by choosing sites having sufficient space available for adequate antenna systems and located outside of the deep pocket formed by the mountains around Pago 

Pago Harbor. Freedom from manmade noise will likewise result in improved reception conditions as a result of the new sites. 

At the close of the fiscal year, the former single channel teletype circuit to Hawaii was converted to a multichannel service. The press service was also improved. In the closing month of the year, a new type of receiving equipment was procured with stability and selectivity suitable for the multichannel equipment. Preparations were made at the same time for special circuits and handling of press and other message traffic in connection with the South Pacific Conference. 

A total of 32,992 messages, comprising 1,212,139 words was handled. 

Revenue traffic increased 26 percent and nonrevenue traffic increased 24 percent. Total revenue was $33,049 which reflects a decrease of

a revision of the rate schedule for a more equitable basis of charge to airlines for aeronautical traffic.

Port Administration The Department of Port Administration completed its sixth year of operation at the end of the fiscal year.

The Department comprises four divisions: Customs, Harbor Crafts, Airport Management, and Port Patrol. 

During the year, monthly inspections were carried out on aids to navigation in American Samoa in accordance with an agreement between the U.S. Coast Guard and the Government. Tidal observations, temperature and density readings of the water and air temperature were made daily and reports submitted to the District Officer, U.S. 

Coast and Geodetic Survey in Honolulu. Monthly sea wave warning systems tests were made by the Honolulu Magnetic Office of the above mentioned service and acknowledged by the tide observer here. These tests are conducted in order to evaluate the adequacy of the system and to keep personnel on the alert in the event of an emergency. 

The local tuna company continued under the terms of its lease to train Samoans in the art of long-line fishing methods using their vessel Atu E. During the year the vessel made six fishing voyages. 

The largest catch was 55.8 tons, with an average of 50.41 tons per trip. 

This average compares favorably with catches made by the Japanesemanned fishing vessels under contract to the tuna company. highest share paid to the local trainees was $192.81. The There are three vessels over 100 gross tons registered in American Samoa: Manu'a tele, Isabel Rose, and Atu E. 

Airport Management.-Fiscal year 1962 saw the most aircraft landings at Tafuna (now Pago Pago International Airport) under civilian operation. The total number of landings was 888 or 427 above the previous fiscal year. An additional commercial airline, operated by 

Tasman Empire Airways Limited began operations into American Samoa in September 1961 on a New Zealand-Tahiti-New Zealand route via Fiji and American Samoa. 

Passenger traffic increased from 9,470 persons in 1961 to 13,714 in 1962. Surface traffic accounted for 5,844 persons and airport operations for 7,870 of the 1962 total. Surface and air arrivals from 

Western Samoa were 9,511 and departures from the territory was 13,368, of which 8,263 were by air and 5,105 by surface transportation.

Export and Import.-The Customs Division entered 304 vessels aggregating 347,674 net tons and cleared 278 vessels with a total of 346,140 net tons. A total of 95 overseas vessels entered port during the fiscal year, including 13 Naval vessels. Eleven yachts visited the territory during the same period. Public Finance 

The financial management of the territory is divided into two parts: (1) Federal activities-composed of the Office of the Governor, the 

Judiciary, and the Legislature. (2) Territorial activities-composed of the operation and maintenance of the territory, which consists of eight executive departments, several staff functions, and the rehabilitation and construction programs. 

Federal activities enumerated above are financed by direct Federal appropriations.

All territorial activities, including construction, are financed by the collection of local revenues which are supplemented by a congressional grant-in-aid. The grants are payable to the Treasurer of the Government of American Samoa and are subsequently comingled with local revenue and made available for obligation and disbursement by the 

Treasury of the Government of American Samoa. 

The Department of Administration Services, the former Department of Budget and Finance, has been delegated the responsibility for the collection of all revenues, the disbursements of all federal and territorial funds, the fiscal and financial accounting, the complete budget process, the managing of all business-type activities, including the supply operations, land transportation, printshop, the hotel and related services. Congressional grants and direct appropriations amounted to $9,605,400 for the fiscal period as compared to $2,642,125 for the fiscal year 1961. Local revenues were $1,321,000 for the period as compared to $1,068,000 for the fiscal year 1961. 

The General Supply Officer was assigned the duty of procuring and distributing liquor for those individuals who held approved liquor permits.

The staff of the department consisted of approximately 87 Samoan employees, 2 stateside employees, and 1 European. While the volume

of work increased by five times, personnel increases were kept to a minimum.

There are five operating divisions within the department in addition to the Treasurer's Office, i.e., the Revenue Division, the Accounting Division, the General Supply Division, the Medical Supply Division, and the Management Services Division. 

Inventory valuation reflected an increase of $126,000 over the previous period.

Audit Office.-The Internal Audit Office serves a staff or advisory function rather than a line or operating one and receives supervision and direction from the Governor and the Secretary. The primary mission of the internal audit office is to conduct audits of all phases of financial management, review and appraise policies, plans, and procedures throughout the Government of American Samoa, and regulate sponsored activities. It also conducts special investigations. 

The Audit Office conducted six audits of various departments, offices, and divisions and two special investigations. 

Banking and Credit.-The Bank of American Samoa was estab lished by the Government of American Samoa in 1914 to provide a depository for the people of American Samoa and to carry on general banking business.

The affairs of the bank are managed and its corporate powers are exercised by a Board of Directors which consists of the Governor, as chairman, the Secretary, Attorney General, Treasurer, Cashier of the bank, and three residents of American Samoa appointed by the Governor.

The fiscal year ending June 30, 1962, was the most successful year in the history of the bank. New records were reached in earnings, total deposits, total resources, and number of customers. 

A net profit of $135,000 was earned during the past 12 months. This represents an increase of 96 percent over profits reported for the previous year. This sharp upward trend in earnings can be traced directly to increased governmental, commercial, and industrial spending in American Samoa.

The regular $5,000 annual dividend was paid to the Government of American Samoa. All remaining profit was directed to the Capital and Reserve Accounts, which now stand at $358,578. In July 1961 the bank charged off a $102,000 loan which had been granted in 1955. 

It has been the bank's policy to retain earnings and thus provide, through reserves, additional protection for the depositors. 

Deposits of $6,430,000 are up 150 percent in comparison to last year.

The increase in Government spending along with increased business activity has made this gain possible.

During the past year, 1,840 loans, totaling $516,000 were granted to customers. This is an increase over the $415,000 loaned during 

1961. These represent individual personal loans. 

More and more, American Samoa appears to be emerging as a tourist. attraction in the South Pacific. With the completion of Samoa's new jet airport additional business activity is expected to result, not only resulting from tourism but from general stateside interest in this area. for industrial development. 

Construction and Maintenance 

The Department of Public Works carried out its activities during fiscal year 1962, primarily in operation, maintenance, and repairs to all Government facilities. It also rendered assistance in the field of special projects and contractual services to departments of the Government as well as to private concerns in the territory. 

The total operating budget of the department at the close of the fiscal year was $798,928.34, an increase of $274,358.34 over the previous fiscal year, due to the extensive cost of maintenance and repair to wornout and aged automotive equipment and antiquated buildings and also the painting program in preparation for the Fifth South 

Pacific Conference.

Marine Railway and Yard.-This Division is operated by one supervisor with the assistance of 17 regular employees and 20 contractual service employees. It is solely responsible for the hauling out of 

Government, private, and commercial vessels, including the Japanese fishing vessels under contract to the local fishing canning company. 

The total number of vessels hauled during the fiscal year was 85, an increase of 14 over the previous year, with a gross revenue of $102,- 

074.64 as compared with the previous $75,412.17.

Engineering and Surveying.-For the duration of the construction. program while the Government prepared for the Conference, the personnel of this section was divided into four parts. Three survey crews from the local survey office were assigned to an engineer and draftsmen from the Fourteenth Naval District. One crew accomplished. the necessary surveys for the airport construction while the other two crews handled the various surveys necessary for new road construction, topographic surveys, leveling, and miscellaneous surveys for new housing and schools. The fourth crew continued with the section's normal duties of cadastral surveys, miscellaneous topographic mapping and new building layout.

The Topographic Division of the Geological Survey began a new topographic map of American Samoa during the year; and in September 1961, the Coast and Geodetic Survey, in cooperation with the Geological Survey, obtained almost complete aerial photography of

This Section of the new paved road from downtown Pago Pago to the Airport. the territory. In May of 1962, the Division assigned a project engineer to accomplish control surveys, complete the aerial photography of Ta'u, and to complete the final field check of the new maps. work is currently being completed and a modern map of Tutuila is now being compiled. To accomplish this mapping, the Topographic 

Division provided over $30,000 in surveying equipment, including electronic measuring instruments, precise leveling equipment, and modern theodolites. The project engineer trained personnel from the local survey department for assistance in the project and gave instructions in operation of the instruments. 

In addition to providing control needed for the new map, the proj ect objective is to establish the basic control from which future construction and cadastral surveys can be started. Forty-four miles of second order levels (more than 60 control marks) have been established from the east to the west end of Tutuila. A temporary tide gauge was installed and observed on Ta'u, from which 2 miles of levels were completed, and approximately 60 control stations were established on various islands in the group. These control marks were established with a minimum of property and crop damage by the use of the electronic equipment.

Roads.-Current maintenance and repairs to roads and bridges during the fiscal year were limited to a minimum inasmuch as the road equipment and crews were assigned to complete the roadwork from the airport to downtown Pago Pago, a distance of 10.3 miles, before the Conference. This project continued after the Conference on a total of 9.5 miles from Pago Pago Bay to Leone in the Western District and from Pago Pago Bay to Breaker's Point in the Eastern District.

Airport Project.-After the inauguration of the new 9,000-foot runway at Pago Pago International Airport, work continued on the necessary lighting facilities and associated jet services in order to establish full-time jet operations before the end of the year. Legislative Branch

In accordance with the Constitution of American Samoa the Legislature shall consist of a Senate and a House of Representatives. At the present time 15 

Senators are elected from the several counties in accordance with Samoan custom through county councils and 17 Congressmen are elected by popular franchise from the several counties on the basis of population ratio. There is a regular session each year beginning on March for not more than 30 consecutive days. 

The Legislature may meet in special session during any calendar year not to exceed 15 days. Members of the Legislature are paid an annual salary of $300 plus transportation.
