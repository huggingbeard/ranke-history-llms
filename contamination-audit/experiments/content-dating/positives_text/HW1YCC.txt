INTRODUCTION 

THE purpose of the laboratory work in physics is not only to teach the subject but to develop in the student the ability to do experimental work. The latter implies a great many qualities and includes skill in grasping the method of the experiment, in manipulating the apparatus, in making quick and accurate observations, in deriving conclusions and in preparing a concise record of the experiment. The characteristics which make a good experimenter are exceedingly valuable not only to those who may wish to continue in the study of an experimental science but to those who follow other lines of work.

The experiments of the course vary greatly in character. Some may be the same in principle as experiments already performed in elementary physics, but, in these cases, more accurate results are now desired through the use of more precise methods and better apparatus. Other experiments are of value more because of the principles which they teach. There are some experiments in the course which deal with engineering problems, but they are interesting because of the commercial application of some of the physical principles.

The manual is not intended to take the place of a text-book in preparation for the laboratory work, but is meant merely as a guide in indicating the object and method of the experiment. Satisfactory results cannot be obtained in the laboratory unless outside work is done in preparation for the experiments. The text-book should be studied on the principles involved, and, if necessary, other books should be consulted.

GENERAL DIRECTIONS 

Students are expected to become familiar with these directions at the beginning of the course. Instructors may refer to paragraphs as "G.D. No. ...," for brevity, in criticizing reports. 

1. Assignments.

Each experiment is to be performed on the day assigned. An experiment performed without the permission of the instructor, may be given no credit. 

2. Notebook. 

The instructor in charge will give directions as to the form in which the data taken in each experiment should be recorded and written up. 

3. Report. 

If a formal report on an experiment is required, it should include the following items: (a) The date of the experimental work. (b) The names of the observers. (c) A statement of the purpose of the experiment. (d) The original observations. (e) A brief statement of the method of procedure and the central idea of the experiment (usually not more than 50 words). (f) Any formulas used in the computations with definitions of the meanings of all symbols. (g) A simple diagram of the arrangement of the apparatus where feasible. (h) Graphs of results where called for in the manual. See sections 15-21 of these General Directions for instructions in regard to graphs. (i) The computations (in the back of your book). (j) The results.

4. Original data.

The original data are all to be entered directly into the notebook. 

The use of loose sheets of paper for a provisional record is forbidden. 

This rule saves copying and in the long run is always a time-saver. 

5. Units. 

Every numerical reading should be accompanied by the units employed, excepting that, in tabular forms, one statement of the unit is sufficient. In the metric system, only one unit is to be used: for instance, a length of 4.26 cm. must not be recorded as 4 cm.

6. Estimation of tenths. 

Every reading, unless there are directions to the contrary, must be made to the smallest possible fraction of a scale division, e.g. tenths of mm. on a metric scale; and these must be recorded, even if they are zeros. Estimation to halves, thirds, or quarters is not usually sufficiently accurate. 

7. Tabular forms. 

Wherever possible the observations and computations should be set down in tabular form. A carefully prepared table is essential if the record is to be compact and intelligible, but in order to prepare one at the outset it is necessary that the student think through the whole experiment before he begins any observations. This means that the description of the experiment in the manual must be studied. before the student comes to the laboratory. 

8. Suspected errors.

Suspected inaccuracies in individual observations should be noted in the record and any which are thought to be worthless may be deleted by drawing a line through them. Not even a bad observation should be erased. 

9. Working in pairs.

The experimental work and the report, if required, are to be the work of the student whose name is presented with them; but when two students are assigned to the same piece of apparatus as partners, they may collaborate in the computations and are urged to complete them while together. They may also divide the observational work, but each should take some share in all the observations, and should serve as a check on the other's accuracy.

10. Reliability of Results. 

Every direct measurement of a physical quantity, and every result derived from any number of observations or measurements have a certain reliability dependent upon the following factors: the method of observation, the care exercised in manipulation, the nicety of the apparatus, etc. In other words, to every result there is assignable a certain definite reliability which is sometimes difficult to determine, but which, in most cases, can be found approximately, as will be explained below. By the proper use of significant figures the reliability of a result can be clearly indicated, and, conversely, a misuse of significant figures is very misleading and may inspire greater reliance upon a result than the work justifies. 

11. Significant Figures. 

Significant figures are those figures in a number or decimal which remain after all ciphers, necessary only to locate the decimal point, are removed. For instance, in 25,000 and .0025, the 

2 and the 5 are significant. In some cases a cipher may be significant as, for instance, in the expression of the length of an object as .001250 cm. The final cipher may here be necessary in order to show that the measurements were made to that figure, but the result happened to come out as given, and not as .001251 or .001249.

As an example to illustrate the way in which the proper use of significant figures can indicate the reliability of a measurement, consider the following. If, in weighing with balances sufficiently sensitive to detect milligrams, the weight of a body is exactly twelve grams, it should be entered as 12.000 grams. The three zeros, in this case, are significant and are necessary to express the full accuracy of the observation. As another example to illustrate the use of significant figures, suppose the volume of a certain body, as found by calculation from its dimensions, comes out as 2463.072 c.c., whereas from considerations given below it is known that this volume is doubtful in the place occupied by the 6. All of the succeeding figures are of no use and must be discarded, keeping only three significant figures. The result will be written 2460 c.c., and in order to show that, in this case, the final zero is not significant, the last significant figure, which is the doubtful one, is underlined. This practice of underlining the doubtful figure is exceedingly helpful, and will be used in this course.

12. Likely Errors as Percentages. 

A better method of discussing the results, and one which applies to logarithms also, is given below. If the calculation involves only multiplication and division, the percentage error introduced into the result by any factor is equal to the percentage error in that factor. Thus, in the example above, the error which could pass undetected in the measurement of the length is estimated at .002 cm.; this is .04 per cent of 5.215. Therefore, the error in the result arising from this source alone is .04 per cent of 37.3446 . or .014 c.c. Obviously, therefore, the first figure 4 in the result is slightly in doubt, and the subsequent figures are unknown and must be discarded. If one of the factors is squared, the percentage error in the result is twice as great. If a factor is cubed, its error is tripled in the result. If the square root of a factor is taken, the possible error is halved in the result. In general we have the addition of errors of the individual factors entering in a product or a quotient. Thus, in the above calculation of the volume of the cylinder, the radius is squared. The error in the measurement of the diameter which could pass undetected is assumed to be .002 cm. This is nearly .07 per cent of 3.020-the diameter. Since the radius is squared, the possible percentage error arising in the result from this source is .14 per cent; .14 per cent of 37.3446 c.c. is .05 c.c. Thus, the first figure 

4 in the result is in doubt, subsequent figures are unknown, and the result should be written 37.34.

The above discussion has shown how the accuracy of a result can be approximately determined when the errors in the several factors are known. How now may the accuracy of the factors themselves be determined?

As a result any statement as to the actual error in a given measurement or set of measurements must be in the nature of an estimate. 

Despite this fact the desirability of giving a measure of the reliability of a physical result is of sufficient importance to have led to rather elaborate treatises on the subject. Here we can indicate only a few of the significant points. 

A study of the errors in physical work has shown that the uncertainty in the final result can be traced to three types of errors. 

Instrumental Errors. These errors (also often known as systematic errors) are fixed throughout an experiment. They occur in clocks, electric meters, rules, balances, weights, in fact all devices having a scale or calibrated with numbers. In addition heat losses, friction, "zero errors," etc., resulting from poor design or imperfect construction are sources of this type of error. An estimate of their magnitude requires often a good deal of judgment and usually experimentation with different apparatus. Despite the difficulty of estimating these errors they may often be quite large. 

Personal Errors. When a given observer tends for example to estimate the time a pendulum passes a given mark either consistently too soon or too late we say this introduces personal error. In the case of a given experiment this type of error may be remarkably constant, but varies a great deal from one observer to another, depending on care, training, and temperament. In the case of estimates of the transit of a star image across a set of cross-hairs, accurate chronographs may be used to determine one's "reaction time" and hence greatly reduce the personal element. In addition, of course, attention, eyesight, speed and understanding of the nature of the measurement all enter and account for the large errors blunders and mistakes which occur in addition to the more systematic personal errors. Such sources of large uncertainty can only be removed by careful work, systematic arrangement of apparatus, immediate recording of data and, where possible, checking of results by another observer. 

Random Errors. When all determinable instrumental and personal errors have been removed or reduced there remain certain small random or chance errors being errors due to unobserved or uncontrollable factors. They are usually spread about a certain average error, some higher, some lower, quite at random. Examples are repeated independent observations of length, weight,

point of impact, speed, time, etc. An average or mean value is then taken as best representing what we are trying to measure and the average departure from the mean as indicating the reliability of our average measurement. 

14. Determination of Likely Error.

We can then obtain a single number by an averaging process which we may regard as a more trustworthy measure of the actual length than any of the individual readings. 15. Relative Importance of Errors.

It is well to note that, although the final result is desired to the greatest possible accuracy, it does not follow that every factor is to be determined with the greatest care. From the discussion previously given, each factor introduces into the final result an error proportional to the error in the factor, so that a result cannot be more accurate than the least accurate factor. It is, therefore, time wasted to determine one factor with extreme care when the inevitably large error in another factor will make the result so inaccurate as to completely mask the care taken in the measurement of the first factor. The time should be spent on the determination of the least accurate factor. The formula connecting the data should be examined beforehand and the accuracy required for each factor be determined in view of the desired overall accuracy.

17. Smooth Curves.

It is not always desirable, or possible, to draw a curve through all of the points marked, but if it is known that the curve should be a smooth curve, and if the points are numerous, it should be drawn between them, thus averaging, in a measure, the errors of observations. 

18. To Show Variations in Large Quantities. 

If the quantity plotted is large in comparison with its variations, the scale on which the curve is plotted must be correspondingly large in order that it may show these variations. This may be done without using an awkwardly large sheet of paper by plotting only the curve and dispensing with the paper below. This is illustrated in Fig. 2, which is the curve representing the load required to sink a Nicholson hydrometer in water at various temperatures. The load scale does not go down to zero. 

19. Position of Origin. 

If a quantity is being plotted as ordinates, some of whose values are positive and some negative, the positive values are plotted upward from the axis of abscissae, the negative values downward. 

Thus, in the first example, if the body had cooled below zero, the curve would have crossed the axis of abscissae. When the abscissae are negative they are to be plotted off to the left of the origin.

The vernier caliper (Fig. 3) is a device making use of a cleverly designed auxiliary scale (usually known simply as the vernier see Figs. 4, 5 and 6) to enable fractional parts of the smallest main-scale division to be read directly. In use the object to be measured is placed between two jaws, one fixed permanently with respect to a metric scale upon which the other jaw slides. On the movable jaw is the index mark which should coincide with the zero of the main scale when the jaws are together; when the object to be measured is placed between them, this line marks on the main scale their distance apart, and thus indicates the length of the object. After recording the number of whole centimeters and millimeters, if a fraction of a millimeter remains, it may be read off directly and accurately by means of the vernier. It is thus solely designed to enable the main scale to be read accurately without having it rendered illegible by fine subdivisions. A more careful examination of the vernier will also give some information in regard to the next decimal place. 

The zero error, to be applied as a correction to all measurements, should be carefully determined by taking the reading when the jaws are pressed gently together. State clearly whether the correction is one to be added or subtracted. 

Holding the instrument to the light, note at what point the jaws touch, and use this part of the gauge in subsequent measurements. 

A vernier scale can be attached to any apparatus for enabling relative motions of two parts to be determined accurately. One part bears the main scale and the other an index mark which moves along the scale and indicates the distance the one part has moved with respect to the other. The number of whole divisions the index mark has moved from the zero of the main scale can be read off directly, and tenths of the smallest division estimated, but the vernier enables the parts of the smallest division to be accurately and easily determined. 

Thus in Fig. 4 the true reading on the main scale is 33.4, the .4 being determined by the fact that the 4th mark on the vernier scale coincides with a line on the main scale. 

The vernier consists of a small subsidiary scale marked off beginning with the index mark as zero, and moves in contact with, and flush with the main scale. The n divisions of the vernier are made equal to a certain number m of the smallest division on the main scale. In most cases m is equal to n -1; but occasionally m may be equal to n + 1, in which case the vernier is called retrograde. Each division of the vernier is, therefore, equal in length to m/n times the value of the smallest division of the main scale. The difference between the value of a main scale division and a vernier division is the least count of the vernier, and is the smallest scale value directly readable by the vernier. 

The following examples will make the above considerations clearer. The vernier, shown enlarged in Figs. 4 and 5, would be used to indicate the tenths of millimeters on a centimeter scale. Suppose the smallest divisions of the main scale are millimeters. The n, i. e. 10, divisions of the vernier are equal to m, i. e. 9, divisions of the main scale or 9 millimeters, so that each vernier division is equal in length to m/n, i. e. 9/10, of a millimeter. The difference between this and one main scale division is 1/10 m.m., which is the least count of the vernier, and is the smallest value that can be directly read by the vernier.

2. MASS AND DENSITY WEIGHTS AND WEIGHING. Strictly speaking, the weight of an ob ject is a measure of the attractive force of the earth for that object and the process of weighing involves the measurement in some arbitrary unit of this pull or force. It is customary to take the pull of the earth on a certain standard piece of material at some fixed point on the earth's surface as the unit of weight and to measure the weights of all other objects in terms of this standard weight. 

Devices for making this comparison are numerous though two characteristic forms may be mentioned: the spring balance (Fig. 8a) and the arm balance (Fig. 8b). In the first, weight is measured | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | 1 2

The pull of the earth varies with the quantity of matter, or the mass, of a body. A spring balance measures the mass of a body (a constant quantity) by means of its weight (a quantity varying with the distance from the center of the earth).

The other and more accurate sort of balance makes use of the principle of the lever. In scales for rough purposes a small weight attached to a long arm may be used to balance a large weight on a short arm. For precise purposes, however, resort is always had to the equal-arm analytical balance such as is sketched in Fig. 8.

Weight in Vacuo. The operation of weighing consists, strictly, in finding the number of standard masses called grams which are attracted by the earth with as much force as is the body which is being weighed. If a weighing has been made in air by means of the equal-arm balance, it does not follow that the earth is attracting with equal force the weights and the body which is being weighed, even after a correction has been applied by double weighing for any accidental inequality in the lengths of the arms. The experiment is complicated by the buoyant action of the air which tends to lift not only the body which is being weighed but also the brass weights. Properly, therefore, the experiment should be performed in vacuo. It is, however, usually more convenient to work in the air and then apply the necessary corrections. The buoyant force of the air, that is, the lifting force which it exerts, is equal to the weight of the air displaced. 

It is desired to find from the result of any weighing the number of grams which would have balanced the body being weighed had the experiment been performed in vacuo. For a moment regard only the buoyant force exerted by the air on the brass weights. Had the experiment been performed in vacuo

not so many weights would have been required, since they would not be lifted in part by the air. The first correction, therefore, is to subtract from the above result the weight of the air displaced by the brass. To find the value of this correction, calculate first the volume of the brass by dividing the number of grams by the number of grams required to occupy one cubic centimeter of space. Multiply this, the volume of the air displaced, by the weight of each cubic centimeter of air as determined from Table I given in the Appendix. 

The result is the correction to be subtracted. On the other hand, the body being weighed has also been buoyed up by the air, and consequently appears lighter than when weighed in vacuo. A second correction must therefore be applied by adding the weight of the air displaced by the body. The result thus corrected is the number of grams which are attracted by the earth with the same force as is the body, and hence is the mass of the body.

4. SPEED OF A BULLET 

Introduction. The method employed in measuring the speed of a given object depends to a great extent upon the magnitude of the speed to be determined. The most direct method is that of timing the motion on a measured path. Two observers are placed a convenient distance apart and each observes on a clock the time at which the object passes a given point. The distance between the points divided by the difference in times gives the average speed of the moving object or particle. If the particle, however, be a bullet its speed is too great for it to be seen. Its passage by the given points may still be determined, for example, by allowing it to cut successive fine wires or puncture two networks and thus record the times of passage on some sort of electrical clock. Such an experiment would, however, give only an average speed and would in addition require a good deal of space and an accurate clock. For that reason an indirect method is often resorted to. The following is a particularly elegant one of these since it requires small space, no clocks at all and gives practically instantaneous speeds. It can be used for all sizes of bullets from the small ones used in the experiment to shells weighing a ton or so.

An equivalent statement of Law 1 is as follows: All forces acting at a point, or on a body, or on any group of bodies which is in equilibrium, form a closed polygon if drawn end to end and all pointing around the polygon in the same direction.

Law 2. The sum of all of the positive moments or torques about any point of all the forces acting upon a body or group of bodies in equilibrium, is equal to the sum of all of the negative moments or torques of the forces about the same point. Positive and negative torques tend to turn in opposite directions about the point. 

This law implies that there is no tendency of the body or group of bodies as a whole to turn about any point. 

It must be borne in mind that the first law of statics whether used in the component form or the polygon form can determine only two things about unknown forces. These may be both the direction and magnitude of a given force, the magnitude of two forces or the directions of two forces. 

The second law on the other hand can determine only one quantity, that is, a direction or a magnitude but not both. Hence the two laws can at most give us three unknown facts. If the problem can not be stated so as to involve only three such unknowns a unique solution by the principles of statics cannot be given.

EFFICIENCY 

The efficiency of a machine is the ratio of the power output to the simultaneous power input, or the efficiency may be expressed as the output of the machine in useful work for a certain interval of time, divided by the work input for the same interval of time, the two being expressed in the same units. 

Efficiency is usually expressed in per cent.

If the machine is one which does not run continuously as, for instance, a lever, or a chain hoist, it is necessary, in order to find the efficiency, to do a measured amount of work upon the machine, and measure the amount of useful work which results.

If the machine runs continuously, it is customary to measure the amount of work put in for some interval of time, say a second or a minute, and at the same time measure the amount of useful work performed by the machine for the same interval of time. In some special cases it may be easier to measure the input or the output, and to measure or to calculate the losses, from which data the efficiency can be determined. This is often the case with electrical machines.

For practically every machine the efficiency depends upon the conditions of load. In general, the efficiency increases up to a certain maximum as the machine is caused to do more and more work, and then finally drops off as the machine is loaded down still more. The load for which the machine gives its maximum efficiency is the load for which the machine is designed. 

It is, therefore, usually of great interest to have the complete history of the way in which the efficiency changes with increasing load. This history is usually plotted in an efficiency curve, for which the ordinates represent the efficiencies corresponding to the outputs as abscissae. When the machine is operating with no load or output, the efficiency is obviously zero. Both scales should, therefore, begin with zero at the origin. The efficiency curve is always a smooth curve.

The shape of the efficiency curve is of practical interest in showing the maximum efficiency attainable by the machine, for what load the machine works at this maximum efficiency, how rapidly the efficiency falls off as the load is either increased or decreased from the load for maximum efficiency, etc.

TORQUE AND THE MEASUREMENT OF WORK AND POWER 

The tendency of a force to produce rotation about an axis is dependent not only upon the magnitude of the force, but upon the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force. The product of the force and

this perpendicular distance, measures the tendency of the force to cause rotation, and is called the torque or moment of the force about the axis. If the force is in pounds and the distance in feet, the torque is expressed as so many pound-feet, but not foot-pounds, which is the unit of work. MECHANICAL ADVANTAGE OF A MACHINE 

When a mechanical force ƒ acting upon a machine results in a force F exerted by the machine, as, for instance, in the case of the lever, the jack screw, etc., the ratio F/f is called the actual mechanical advantage of the machine. 

This mechanical advantage depends upon the amount of friction, and is, therefore, a variable according to the conditions under which the machine is operated. 

The theoretical mechanical advantage of a machine is the ratio of the corresponding forces assuming no friction or 100% efficiency. The theoretical mechanical advantage is, therefore, dependent only upon the kind and dimensions of the machine, and is a constant. It is obtained from the assumption that the output equals the input. WATER POWER AND WATER MOTORS 

Water which is under pressure, or which is in an elevated position, possesses potential energy, and if allowed to escape or fall from its elevated reservoir can be made to do work. The amount of power available depends not only upon the pressure or height, but also upon the quantity of water discharged per unit of time. The same quantity of work could be done in a given time by a small amount of water discharged under high pressure, or falling from a great height, as might be done by a very large quantity of water discharging under low pressure, or falling through a small distance. The latter is sometimes known as a low grade water power, but may be just as useful as the high grade system. 

There are many examples of the low grade power in this part of the country, a very good example of which is the new power derived from the Mississippi river at Keokuk. The high grade powers are very common in Norway and Switzerland, where water is often drawn from heights of a thousand feet or more. 

The two kinds of power require different types of water motors. The low grade power is converted by the so-called turbine wheels. The other type of motor which is applicable to the high heads and small amounts of water is known as the Pelton or jet motor, and is the form which will be tested in this experiment. 

In the Pelton type of water motor, water issues in a powerful jet from a properly designed nozzle, and impinges on a series of buckets fastened on the periphery of the rotating wheel. Fig. 31 shows the general appearance of the rotor. In some motors there is more than one nozzle. The buckets are usually double and so arranged that the jet of water strikes the sharp-edged partition between the two halves of the bucket and is thereby deflected, one half the stream going into each pocket. Each half of the stream, in following the curved bottom of the bucket, is reversed in direction and leaves the bucket without interfering with the main jet. amount of work is derived from the jet of water when the bucket has one half the velocity of the issuing jet, for then the water leaves the bucket with zero velocity and, therefore, zero energy.

19. YOUNG'S MODULUS 

Introduction. We have thus far treated the solid bodies with which we have worked as though they were perfectly rigid, whereas all solids are deformed more or less when subjected to stresses. If now the body returns to its original condition as soon as the stress is removed we say that it behaves as an elastic body. Such bodies are found to obey Hooke's law quite accurately, the deformation or strain being always proportional to the stress. When the stress exceeds a certain rather well defined limit, called the elastic limit, the material on being freed from stress fails to return to its original shape by an amount depending on the extent to which the elastic limit was exceeded. If the stress is still further increased a point is reached where the strain becomes greater as time goes on the body exhibiting a sort of plastic flow. If a rod or wire be subjected to a tension of this sort it is only a matter of time before the sample is ruptured. The minimum tension for which this will occur is the rupture point of breaking stress. In general in order to stay safely within the elastic limit it is sufficient never to subject the specimen to more than half the breaking stress. 

Since the force required to produce a given elongation varies with the cross-section it is convenient to define the stress as the force per unit area. Similarly we reduce the total elongation to that per unit length (which gives the strain) by dividing it by the length. When therefore we divide the stress by the strain we have a quantity which is characteristic of the material of which our sample is made and independent of its particular physical dimensions. This gives 

Young's Modulus or the coefficient of linear stretching. Since the elongations per unit length are quite small, it is of great advantage

to have as long a wire as convenient. Furthermore we should like to be entirely free from changes produced by temperature alterations and bending of the support to which the upper end of the wire is fixed. An ingenious method of accomplishing both of these latter results is to have two lengths of the same kind of wire hanging from the same support, one carrying a scale and a weight holder and the other (stretched taut by a fixed weight) a vernier scale. (See Fig. 40.) The scale and vernier are arranged to slide freely relative to one another and enable relative motions alone to be measured.

SECTION II. HEAT 20. DETERMINATION OF THE MECHANICAL EQUIVALENT OF HEAT 

Introductory Remarks. Heat is a form of energy, and a fundamental physical law states that a quantity of heat is equivalent to a perfectly definite amount of mechanical energy. The amount of mechanical energy which is equivalent to one unit of heat is called the mechanical equivalent of heat. Mechanical energy can be completely converted into the equivalent heat, but no device will convert all of the heat available into its equivalent mechanical energy. This non-reversibility of the transformation is because of the fact that heat is the lowest form of energy and is the form toward which all others tend to pass. 

Object. The object of the following experiment is to convert a measured amount of mechanical energy completely into heat, and to measure the resulting quantity of heat. From these determinations, the mechanical equivalent of heat can be obtained. The mechanical energy is easily measured, but the determination of the heat is difficult because of the inevitable loss of some of the heat during the experiment, which necessitates certain corrections. 

The corrections for the loss of heat, however, add interest and value to the experiment because the method by which the corrections are made is illustrative of much of the more advanced experimental methods.

Introduction. The freezing (or melting) of a pure substance takes place at a sharply defined temperature, depending only on the pressure, and the nature of the substance. This is the temperature at which the liquid and solid states are in equilibrium; addition of heat to a mixture of the liquid and solid at this temperature increases the amount of the liquid, and decreases that of the solid, but the temperature remains constant until the melting is complete. Similarly, cooling the mixture increases the proportion of the solid state, the temperature remaining constant until freezing is complete. Consequently, if a pure liquid is uniformly cooled to its freezing point and beyond, and the temperature plotted against the time, a curve will be obtained having a horizontal portion at the melting temperature. This statement is no longer exact for liquids which exhibit the phenomenon of supercooling. The freezing curve in these cases shows a small dip before the horizontal section.

Introductory Remarks. Both from a physical and a musical standpoint the pitch of a note is its most important characteristic. 

It depends upon the number of vibrations per second. If the sounding body is a stretched wire (or string), the number of vibrations per second, when the wire is plucked transversely, depends upon its mass, its length, and the tension with which it is stretched, and can be expressed exactly by a formula involving these quantities. The vibration of a string, therefore, furnishes a very accurate and convenient method of determining the pitch of a note. 

A series of eight notes whose pitch numbers or frequencies bear to each other certain simple ratios forms the musical scale used in scientific work, and closely resembles the scale of "equal temperament," which is used in music. These ratios determine what are called the musical intervals of the notes. When the musical interval of two notes is simple, that is, when the ratio of the pitch numbers is a simple ratio, as 2: 1 or 4: 3, etc., the notes, when sounded together, give a pleasant sensation, and are said to be in harmony or in accord. The eight notes constituting the scale are denoted, beginning with the lowest, by the letters C, D, E, F, G, A, B, c. The musical interval of the eighth note to the first, c to C, is called an octave; the interval of the fifth to the first, G to C, is called the interval of a fifth; of E to C, the interval of a third; and so on.

43. EQUIPOTENTIAL LINES AND LINES OF FLOW FLOW OF ELECTRICITY THROUGH EXTENDED CONDUCTORS 

Introductory Remarks. A current of electricity which passes through a thin conducting sheet of metal or liquid spreads throughout the conductor, forming what is termed a current sheet. The density of the electric current at different points in the conducting sheet is obviously different. In general, the density is greatest in that region of the conductor lying in the shortest path between the electrodes which lead the current into and out of the conducting sheet.

Two points in the conducting sheet are said to be at the same potential when no current will pass through a conductor, as for instance a wire, which connects the two points outside the conducting sheet. The locus of all points at the same potential is called an equipotential line. 

A line of flow is a line representing the complete path of a filament of current as it passes from one electrode to the other. The direction of the lines of flow, therefore, shows the direction of the current at every point in the conducting sheet.

A few general propositions may now be stated. (1) The electromotive force acting on a quantity of positive electricity must be at right angles to the equipotential line passing through the point at which it is situated; for, if not, it would have a component along the equipotential line, which is impossible from the definition of this line. Hence the lines of flow are everywhere perpendicular to the equipotential lines.

(2) In those regions where the equipotential lines are nearest together, the rate of change of potential with distance is greatest and the current density is greatest. (3) At the boundary between the conductor and an insulating medium the lines of flow are parallel to the boundary line, and hence the equipotential lines are perpendicular to the boundary. (4) If any part of the conducting sheet has a different conductivity, produced, for instance, by a greater or less thickness of the sheet, or by the presence of another material of different resistance, the lines of flow and the equipotential lines will be distorted. (5) If any part of the conducting system has a negligibly small resistance compared with the resistance of the rest, there is no change in potential throughout the better conductor, and the boundary lines are equipotential.

GALVANOMETERS AND AMMETERS 

Galvanometers are instruments for detecting small currents of electricity, and may or may not be used to measure the strength of the current. 

Ammeters are less sensitive galvanometers made in portable form and used to measure the strength of the current passing through the instrument. 

Since galvanometers and ammeters will be used in many of the following experiments, a brief description of their construction is given below. 

The effect which causes the deflection in practically all galvanometers is the force exerted between one or more wires carrying a current and a magnetic field. There are two main types of galvanometers according to whether the magnet which supplies the field, or the coil carrying the current, is the moving system.

In the first type or moving magnet form of galvanometer, a light magnetic needle is acted upon by the current circulating in one or more stationary coils of wire. The tangent galvanometer is of this type, and consists of a single short magnet suspended or pivoted at the center of a large ring-shaped coil. Although insensitive, this instrument is useful for measuring a current in terms of the angle of deflection, the dimensions of the instrument, and the strength of the earth's magnetic field. Such an instrument is used as a primary instrument for determining absolutely the value of an unknown current, and is never used solely as a detecting instrument. If the coils of wire are brought very near to the needle and are wound with many turns of fine wire, the moving needle instrument can be made extremely sensitive. 

This form of instrument is often called a Thomson galvanometer.

The moving coil or d'Arsonval galvanometer is the most common type and consists essentially of a coil 

FIG. 52 

of fine wire pivoted or suspended in a powerful magnetic field. In the most sensitive instruments, the coil is suspended by a very fine wire or ribbon of metal, which also serves to lead the current into the coil, the current leaving the coil by a spiral wire or ribbon below. Fig. 52 shows one form of the d'Arsonval galvanometer used in the laboratory. 

The rotation of the moving system of the sensitive galvanometers is measured by means of a telescope which views the image of a stationary scale reflected in a small mirror attached to the moving system of the instrument. 

Most ammeters are forms of d'Arsonval galvanometers made for rough portable use. The moving coil is usually pivoted and controlled by means of two spiral hair-springs, which also serve as electrical connections to the coil.

47. ELECTROLYSIS Introductory Remarks. The practical unit of quantity of elec tricity is the coulomb. The rate at which electricity is transferred or flows along a conductor determines the strength of current which is measured in amperes. An ampere is a current flowing at the rate of one coulomb per second. If the current is perfectly steady, then the current I is equal to the quantity Q which flows in the interval of time t, divided by t. The total quantity of electricity transferred through a conductor is equal to the current times the time, provided the current remains constant. 

An ammeter is an instrument for measuring the strength of a current of electricity, and if the current strength remains constant for a certain interval of time, then the ammeter reading multiplied by the time gives the number of coulombs which have flowed. In this way the ammeter can be used to measure quantity of electricity.

When a current of electricity passes through some aqueous solutions the latter are decomposed. This process is called electrolysis, and the solution thus decomposed is termed an electrolyte. 

The products of decomposition usually appear at the electrodes which conduct the current into and out of the solution. If the electrolyte is an acid, hydrogen appears at the cathode or the terminal by which the current (assumed to be of positive electricity) leaves the solution. If the electrolyte is a solution of a metallic salt, the metal is often deposited on the cathode. 

The amount of material liberated depends directly upon the quantity of charge transferred. The volume of hydrogen, under standard conditions of pressure and temperature, which is liberated by one coulomb of electricity, and the weight of copper and silver deposited under certain specific conditions by the unit quantity of electricity, have been very accurately determined.

With these constants, the electrolytic cell or voltameter may be used to determine the strength of an unknown current. In the early days of electric lighting, electrolytic cells, known as coulombmeters, were installed in the houses to measure the consumption of electricity. 49. MECHANICAL EQUIVALENT OF HEAT BY ELECTRICAL METHOD 

Introductory Remarks. The electrical units are so defined that the relation between them and the mechanical units is definitely known. For instance, the work done by a current is, by definition, equal to the product of the three factors, the current in amperes, the potential difference in volts, and the time in seconds. The answer comes out in joules, where one joule is simply a multiple of the erg, i. e., ten million ergs. Definition does not establish the relation between the electrical units and the heat units any more than does definition establish the relation between the mechanical units and the heat units.

Tel T₁ The very important constant, the mechanical equivalent of heat, can be determined by measuring the quantity of heat produced directly by a known amount of mechanical work as was done in an earlier experiment, or by the much more accurate method of determining the electric equivalent of heat by finding the quantity of heat produced by the expenditure of a measured amount of electrical energy, and then converting this result into the mechanical equivalent of heat by means of the known relation between the electrical and mechanical units.

50. TEMPERATURE MEASUREMENT BY THERMO- COUPLE. LATENT HEAT OF FUSION AND MELTING POINT OF TIN Introductory Remarks. The two junctions formed by connect ing a wire of one metal in a circuit consisting of another metal are sources of oppositely directed electromotive forces, the magnitudes of which depend upon the temperatures of the junctions. If the junctions are at the same temperature, the electromotive forces are equal and opposite, and, therefore, neutralize each other so far as producing a current in the circuit is concerned. If, however, one junction is at a higher temperature than the other, there will be a resultant electromotive force in the circuit which can be used to measure the difference in temperature of the junctions. Such junctions are called thermo-couples, and are very commonly used as thermometers.

The usual practice in thermo-electric thermometry is to maintain one junction at 0°C, and to subject the other to the temperature being measured. The resulting electromotive force is then measured by means of a potentiometer, or as in this experiment. If the junction has already been calibrated in terms of temperature, or if the law connecting temperature and electromotive force for the junction is known, the unknown temperature can be obtained. 

Various combinations of metals are used. For very high temperature measurements, the refractory metals, platinum and an alloy of platinum and iridium, or of platinum and rhodium, are the best. For lower temperatures, copper with constantan, an alloy of copper and nickel, gives very good results because of the high electromotive force produced, and because of the practically linear relation between the electromotive force and the temperature of the hot junction, if the latter temperature be not too high. Chromel and alumel are two alloys of recent discovery which form a combination giving a still more nearly linear curve. 

Thermo-couples are usually calibrated by subjecting the hot junction to several standard temperatures such as the boiling point of water, the melting points of tin, lead, etc.

The great majority of electrical machines and devices depend in their action upon the magnetic effects of an electric current. In these devices, strong magnetic effects are usually obtained by the use of many turns of wire, and by the use of iron as a part, or as the whole of the magnetic circuit. 

A knowledge of the behavior of iron in a magnetic field is, therefore, of great importance.

Object. The object of the following experiment is to give some elementary knowledge of the properties of iron when subjected to magnetic fields of various strengths. The magnetic strength of a piece of soft steel and a piece of hardened steel will be traced as the specimen is subjected to a first increasing and then decreasing magnetizing force.

Apparatus. The instrument used for the comparative measurement of the magnetic strength of the specimens is known as a magnetometer. 54. ALTERNATING CURRENTS 

Introductory Remarks. An alternating current is one which periodically reverses in direction a certain number of times per second. The time T during which the current completes one cycle of changes is called the period, and its reciprocal n is known as the frequency of the alternating current. The common commercial frequencies are 25 and 60 per second.

The almost continuously varying character of alternating currents causes effects which are not obtained in direct current circuits. Most of these effects are very useful and make possible, with alternating currents, many results not so easily accomplished with direct currents, such as easy transformation of voltage, etc. 

For this reason alternating current service for use in lighting and for power has practically replaced direct-current systems. 

Although the phenomena obtainable with alternating currents are immensely more complicated than the steady current phe-

Discussion. If an alternating electromotive force of effective value E (reading of voltmeter) is impressed on a circuit containing a resistance R and a coil of wire, then the effective current (ammeter reading) is given by the expression √ R² + 4π²n² L² where n is the frequency per second, and L is a new property of the coil called inductance. It is in virtue of this inductance that there is produced in the coil an opposing electromotive force whenever the current changes, which depends upon the rate of change of the

current. This opposing electromotive force and the ohmic resistance R of the circuit both serve to reduce the current. If L is zero, the above formula reduces to Ohm's law. The full formula may, therefore, be spoken of as the extended Ohm's law. 

If the circuit contains no iron, the inductance L is constant and depends simply upon the geometry of the circuit. If iron is inserted in the coil, the inductance depends both upon the frequency and the current strength and is, therefore, not constant. The value of L in the formula represents, in this case, therefore, something analogous to the average inductance.

THE LUMMER-Brodhun PhOTOMETER AND PHOTOMETRY 

There are many types of photometers of which the most common commercial forms depend upon the law of the inverse square of the distance. The two sources of light which are to be compared, the standard and the light under investigation, are placed at a considerable distance apart, and a screen moved until it is equally illuminated by each. The relative intensities of the two

sources are then proportional to the squares of their distances from the screen. 

Of this type the Lummer-Brodhun form is a very satisfactory example. In this photometer the two sources of light are placed at opposite ends of a long scale on which slides the so-called head of the photometer. The head of the 

Lummer-Brodhun photometer is shown in Fig. 68. It consists of a white M' plaster of Paris disc S, both sides of which are viewed at the same time through the telescope T. 

This is accomplished by means of a system of mirrors, two of which are shown at M and M'. The light from the mirror M is reflected by the right-angle prism P into the telescope by total internal reflection from the back of the prism. 

There is a corresponding prism P'. 

The two prisms P and P' do not touch except over a certain area where they are pressed together in optical contact. Where the prisms do not touch, the light from the side S of the screen is entirely reflected by the prism P, and all the light received is from the mirror M and from that side. Where the prisms are in contact no light is reflected, but the light is entirely transmitted from the prism P', and the light entering T at the middle comes from the mirror M' and the side S' of the screen. The telescope is focused on the surface of separation of the two prisms, and the observer, thus seeing side by side the two surfaces S and S', can see when the two sides are equally illuminated. The head of the photometer is moved backward and forward until this is accomplished. The position of the head on the long scale is then read. The photometer head is now inverted, thus reversing the sides of the screen SS' and compensating for optical inequalities, and a new position of the head found and recorded. 65. THE DIRECT-VISION SPECTROSCOPE 

Introductory Remarks. The study of spectra is of great interest because of their wide theoretical and practical application. In many lines of theoretical research, spectra are either the direct phenomena studied, or they are necessary as experimental means in the study of other phenomena. The practical uses of spectra include qualitative spectrum analysis, and such special problems as the determination of the constitution of light coming from various artificial sources and the light transmitted through colored screens, in the attempt to combine artificial lights with color screens to imitate daylight, etc., etc.

SPECTRA Continuous spectra are given by incandescent solids and liquids and, in general, give no means of telling the identity or nature of the incandescent substance other than its temperature. All gases and the vapors of the elements, and even some gases which are chemical compounds, give characteristic spectra when caused to emit light. These spectra are usually composed of bright lines or bands, and the fact that each substance has its own characteristic spectrum makes possible qualitative spectrum analysis. 

When white light passes through a gas or vapor, those wave lengths of light are absorbed which the gas or vapor is capable of emitting. This absorption of definite wave-lengths from the continuous spectrum of white light gives dark line spectra known as absorption spectra. All transparent liquids and solids absorb certain portions of the spectrum, and it is this absorption which accounts for their color.

If the salt of a metal be vaporized in a Bunsen flame, a characteristic spectrum of the metal will in general be emitted. If the metal be made the terminals of an electric arc, a characteristic spectrum of the metal will be emitted, which spectrum may differ in many respects from the flame spectrum. 

Similarly, the spectrum emitted when the metal is made the terminals of an electric spark-gap, across which the discharge of a Leyden jar or condenser be made to pass, is characteristic of the metal, but is usually somewhat different from either the flame or arc spectrum. The mode of excitation must, therefore, always be stated.

The spectrum of a gas is usually obtained by causing a current of electricity under high voltage to pass through a glass tube containing the gas at low pressure.

Apparatus. A spectrometer, a dense glass prism, and a mercury vapor lamp are provided for this experiment. 

THE MERCURY VAPOR LAMP 

The mercury vapor lamp is used as the source of light and consists of a long glass tube into the ends of which platinum terminals are sealed. Two pools of mercury, contained in pockets at the ends of the tube, make connection with these terminals and serve as electrodes. The tube is highly exhausted. When the tube is tipped until a continuous stream of mercury connects the two terminals, the circuit is completed and a current flows from the direct current lighting mains through a resistance and the lamp. When this thread of mercury breaks, an arc is formed which then spreads throughout the tube, giving the familiar greenish-blue light. The mercury in the cups should never be allowed to boil away enough to lower appreciably the level in the cups.

69. ROTATION OF THE PLANE OF POLARIZATION OF LIGHT Introductory Remarks. 

One of the most satisfactory methods of producing plane polarized light is by means of the Nicol prism. 

The Nicol prism is made of a rhomb of Iceland spar, cut in two at a suitable angle, and recemented with Canada balsam. This

LIGHT 

181 method of producing polarized light will be used in the following experiment.

Many substances, notably quartz, solutions of the sugars and the essential oils, have the peculiar property of rotating the plane of polarization of light. In some cases this rotation is to the left (counter-clockwise) as the light comes toward the observer, and in other cases toward the right (clockwise). In every case, however, the specific rotation, which is the angular rotation under standard conditions of distance traversed in the substance by the light, of concentration of solution, and of temperature and wave-length, is a perfectly definite quantity for each substance. 

The most important commercial application of this fact is in the analysis of various commercial products in order to find the amount of optically active constituent present. The analysis of the sugars and the products made of sugar are the most extensive analyses of this kind. The government regularly tests in this way the importations of raw sugars in order to ascertain the duty which shall be imposed.

Object. The following experiment is chiefly concerned with the study of polarized light, and the effect of some substances in rotating the plane of polarization, but, in order to enforce the commercial application of the purely physical principle, some raw and refined cane sugars will be analysed in just the same way as is done at the custom house. The process is called saccharimetry. 

Apparatus. The more elaborate apparatus for testing the amount of rotation of polarization is usually called a polariscope, or, if applied solely to the analysis of sugar, a saccharimeter. The simple arrangement used in this experiment consists of two Nicol prisms mounted at the ends of a brass cradle as shown in Fig. 87. 

One Nicol is fixed and is called the polarizer. The other Nicol is capable of rotation through angles indicated on an attached scale, and is called the analyser. The cradle between the Nicols is arranged to receive glass tubes about 8 inches long containing the solutions to be tested, the direction of sight being through the tube lengthwise. Such an arrangement comprises a very simple polariscope.

In addition to the simple polariscope and solution tubes, glass flasks, funnels and some filter papers are provided to be used in mixing the solution.
