FOREWORD 

A noted educator once said, "Every effort that, in a friendly way, reminds us of our lapses in the use of good English, is deserving of hearty support." 

Five hundred words that are often mispronounced, even by well-educated men and women, are interspersed through this story. The correct pronunciation and the common mis-pronunciation are indicated by phonetic re-spelling, without the use of puzzling diacritical marks or symbols.

No single dictionary, of course, can be considered a final authority on correct pronunciation, or best usage. 

Attention is called, however, to the fact that two leading authorities-Webster's International Dictionary, and 

Funk & Wagnall's Standard Dictionary-are in substantial agreement on the pronunciation of the words selected for this booklet. 

It is hoped that this little story will provide mental entertainment for the reader, and help to correct careless and slip-shod speech. F. F. T. 3

EXPLANATORY NOTES 

In the "Key To Pronunciation," the exact sound of every letter in the alphabet can not be indicated by phonetic re-spelling alone, but the system should easily be understood if the reader devotes a minute or two to these few notes.

The so-called "long" sound of "a" is indicated by the spelling “ay”—(bay sin for basin); the broad sound, by "ah"-(fah ther for father); the short sound of “a”as in ban ner- is obvious, as is the sound of "a" in "a bout." 

The sounds of final “e,” “i," and "o" in a syllable are long, unless otherwise indicated. The short sound of final "e" is indicated by the spelling "eh"; short "i" by "ih"; and short "o," by "oh.” 

The sound of "g" is hard, as in "go"; when soft, it is indicated by "j"-(jen er al for general). When "s" has a "z" sound, it is spelled "z”—(confuze for con fuse).

The syllable that should be accented or stressed is printed in italics.

PAUL CHAPTER I 

AUL PEARY, a young graduate of old Amherst, regretfully bade adieu to his beloved alma mater, and, with a sigh, drove slowly away from the little New England college town, bound for home. "Four glorious years! What other period of life will ever be comparable to them," he reflected. True, there had been tiresome, even onerous, duties under college regime, but there was solace in the realization that long, weary hours of study and research were now to be followed by a year of travel.

His doleful manner quickly changed, however, and his sober, dolorous thoughts gave way to a jocund mood in anticipation of the care-free days just ahead. 

He pictured sun-splashed ports of the blue Caribbean gay throngs in gala attire at charming resorts along the Mediterranean-sea of legend and romance ... 

gondolas gliding along the entrancing canals of Venice Egypt, the Nile, and the great pyramid of Cheops tropical isles and distant, unfrequented archipelagoes! As the familiar Palisades of the Hudson came into view a few hours later, Peary, with joyful thoughts of the itinerary he had planned, exclaimed gleefully, "It won't be long now!" A strange potpourri of sounds came from the pier. 

The ribald jests of a clique of water-front habitues could be heard now and then, their voices contrasting markedly with the soft, modulated tones of a demure young lady alighting from a handsome sedan. CHAPTER IV 

BLATANT voice attracted Peary's attention. A 

man of herculean proportions was approaching, well over six feet in height and having the indisputable features of a Westerner. "Any porters around?" he bellowed, pushing back his sombrero.

A steward, hearing the inquiry, hurried forward. "Shall I take your luggage to your suite, sir?" he asked. "You mean my sweety, don't you?" replied the big man, with a loud guffaw. Extracting a bank-note from a bulky wallet, he said, "Here's a little coupon for you, bub. I don't expect nothin' gratis, see?"

The steward's eyes dilated and his usually immobile and flaccid countenance was transformed into a wide grimace at sight of the proffered largesse. "Thank you very much, sir," he said, humbly. "And now you just grab that duffel of mine and show me where my boudoir is located," said the man from the West, with a chuckle.

Peary, a robust athletic specimen of young manhood, was impressed with the giant stature and confident mien of the Westerner. Cowardice was alien to his make-up, but he could not help thinking that, in an imbroglio with this man from the wide open spaces, he would feel rather impotent without the aid of an able ally. CHAPTER VI 

HE bugle blew, customary amenities and last good TH byes were exchanged, handkerchiefs fluttered, and the giant ship moved slowly from the pier. 

As the lofty office buildings of Manhattan faded into indistinguishable dots, the pilot was dropped and the de luxe liner, now amenable to the accurate guidance of her officers, steamed majestically on her way. 

After a brief inspection of the cabin where he was to be domiciled for the duration of the cruise, Peary returned to the promenade deck to enter a new and entrancing world.

A few hours later, his appetite sharpened by the tang of the salt sea air, he entered the dining saloon, pausing for a moment on the balustrade of the ornate staircase to view the brilliant scene. 

At the captain's table, in a soft clinging gown of cerise chiffon, was seated a famous prima-donna, her glamour heightened by gorgeous white orchids at her shoulder.

In striking contrast to the vivacity and chic of the exquisite diva were the somber clothes of her spouse, a rotund little man whose visage bore the blase expression of the much-traveled man of the world-a cosmopolite equally at home in New York, Rio Janeiro, or Timbuktu. CONCLUSION 

As the reader has discovered long since, this story has no plot. If it lacks cohesion and has a stilted style, the writer can only say, in extenuation, that the necessity for using several hundred mispronounced words-many of them ordinarily unrelated-has been responsible for the sacrifice of smoothness, and even plausibility, at times.

The "story," of course, is only secondary, and merely the vehicle for presenting the five hundred mispronounced words. Many hundreds of other words were excluded because of alternative pronunciations, and also because of differences of opinion among leading authorities regarding their correct pronunciation.
