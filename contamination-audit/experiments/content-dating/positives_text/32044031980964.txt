Fiduciaries and Their Security 

HAVE listened with a great deal of interest as Mr. 

Winans, for his Committee, traced the history and development of guardianship administration. 

At the risk of making a self-serving declaration, I follow him on your program because the interests of corporate sureties and practicing attorneys are closely related on the points of recommendation made in his report. My remarks will be addressed to the second recommendation having to do with personal sureties. Anything that makes for improvement in handling guardianships and other fiduciary estates results in safer underwriting on the part of the corporate surety, and in turn reduces premium rates to a point where even the small estate may afford the security provided by the large assets of good surety companies. 

A fiduciary may serve in any one of many capacities, but in every case his trust is a sacred one. In a broad sense the fiduciary is entrusted to receive, handle, and ultimately account for funds and properties of others. He may have in charge public moneys or the assets of an insolvent corporation, but perhaps no higher trust exists than that just emphasized of a guardian who acts for one incapable by age or mental condition of acting for himself. The fiduciary always should be required to give security to assure the faithful performance of his duties and to save the beneficiaries of his trust from loss in event he is unable to properly account.

It is academic to lawyers that the guardian of a minor, upon appointment, should assemble the ward's assets and file with the court an accurate inventory of the property in his possession. An orderly guardianship requires the keep- 

Page one

ing of accurate accounts concerned exclusively with affairs of the ward. All receipts and expenditures should be carefully recorded and vouchers preserved. If the ward's estate consists of uninvested funds, it is the duty of the guardian to invest them according to law in legal securities. First mortgages with incidental complications, such as the relation of the amount of the loan to the value of the land, may be involved. The guardian is bound to sell investments at the proper time and in the proper market. He should never lend his ward's money on unsecured notes; he should not commingle trust funds with his own; he should have a safe deposit box properly designated to hold securities of the estate; and he should at all times exercise the utmost good faith in all his dealings with the trust estate. 

When the guardian has satisfied all these requirements and more, he should file with the court periodic accounts in such form and at such times as may be required by statute or court order or rule. These should be more than superficial accounts that some clerk files as a matter of habit without a careful inspection by the court or someone appointed for the purpose. Finally, when the ward attains his majority, the guardian must render a final statement of his account to the court that appointed him and have his account judicially settled. If he has been ignorant, negligent or dishonest, he and his surety will be liable for losses. 

It is special training that has opened lawyers' eyes to these requirements which even to intelligent laymen usually are very confusing. Advice of counsel should more often be sought, particularly in substantial or complicated estates and corporate sureties repeatedly insist upon this. Personal sureties are generally uninformed as to such requirements and should not be imposed upon to the detriment of their own families and estates. 

Hazards of

Suretyship, which is so closely related to guardianship administration, is likewise of ancient origin. For centuries the individual has become surety for his friend. The Bible points out that personal suretyship was practiced, and 

Personal 

Suretyship

Page two

warns against it. In Proverbs 11-15 is the admonition that: "He that is surety for a stranger shall smart for it; and he that hateth suretyship is sure." (See also Genesis 43-9; 

Proverbs 6-1, 2.) The grave dangers in the use of the personal surety are the reasons why individual sureties are never encountered in some modern businesses and are not acceptable as security for public officers in some jurisdictions. Likewise, these dangers are the reasons for the growth of surety companies within the last fifty years. 

Corporate suretyship is comparatively new. It was not until 1878 that a corporation in the United States became surety for a consideration. The first business of surety corporations was giving fidelity bonds to guarantee the honesty of individuals taking positions in banks and business houses. Bonds required of guardians and other fiduciaries were viewed at first by surety companies as too hazardous. It must be remembered that while fiduciary bonds contain all the risks of loss caused by dishonesty, they involve vastly more than the fidelity hazard. A fiduciary may default and shift a heavy loss to the shoulders of his bondsman when there has been no moral turpitude or even bad faith.

Today, the surety companies, licensed to do business in every state of the Union, write thousands of guardianship and other fiduciary bonds every year. Fortunately the majority of these reaches a normal termination in the final accounting without loss, but many have claims made upon them and, in the aggregate, corporate sureties pay out millions of dollars to cover defaults. Corporate sureties that have a wide spread of risks assume their responsibility for reasonable premiums. They absorb shock losses with no painful results to the lives and fortunes of individuals. 

Surety corporations are glad to work hand in hand with courts and with fiduciaries and lawyers to maintain orderly procedure and conserve trust estates. 

The fiduciary generally is allowed some compensation for his work and responsibility. The individual who becomes Page three

surety almost invariably does so as a matter of accommodation for a relative, friend, business associate or political ally. Personal suretyship has been judicially described as an unrequited act of friendship. Rarely does the personal surety realize that becoming surety makes him a principal obligor whom the obligee may pursue immediately, should his friend, although financially able, fail to meet his responsibility. He seldom understands the instrument he is signing and rarely appreciates the obligation he is assuming. 

Were he told that his responsibility goes beyond that of an endorser on a promissory note, he would be shocked. If the personal surety reflects at all upon the principal's financial standing, he does not think of how this may change or of complications that may be caused by the unexpected death of friend principal. Recognizing this, courts have pointed out that the law gives to the beneficiary the right of security, and it is for the surety-whether personal or corporate to consider the nature of its responsibilities, and the uncertainties thereof, when it enters into the bond (McBrown v. Governor 6 Porter (Ala.) 32).

It should be the duty of public officials and fiduciaries, including guardians, to give bonds that will fulfill the real purpose of the statute, court rule or other instrument that requires the bond. As your Committee has pointed out in the case of guardianships: "Every possible safeguard should be utilized to insure adequate protection to the ward." 

Whatever the requirement is that makes a bond necessary, you may be sure that it anticipates real protection for someone. Careful

Handling By Courts 

The discretion of the Probate Judge is allimportant in the case of all fiduciaries, and should not be influenced by circumstances that are not for the best interests of those for whom the fiduciary must serve. We can safely say that in the jurisdiction where the security exacted of the fiduciary is left to the court's discretion, it varies according to the wisdom and the practical experience of the particular judge making the appointment. Frequently the security

Page four

exacted is pitifully inadequate. It has been said of judges of our criminal courts that as they become more experienced their sentences are apt to increase in severity. If this be so, it is because experience has taught them that it does not pay to be soft. But to send a crooked guardian to jail does not restore to infants the estate of which they have been robbed. Our older and more experienced Surrogates and Probate Judges have looked down from their benches upon painful scenes, and therefore you will find among most of them a tendency to exact not only security, but adequate security. 

Public and judicial officers whose duty it is to accept or approve such bonds should do more than receive them in a routine manner. The interest of those for whose benefit and protection the bond or security is taken should be given paramount consideration. These beneficiaries rarely have anything to do with the procedure of approving or accepting bonds and seldom understand their technical nature. They must be protected by our judges and public officers whether the bond is filed pursuant to statute to qualify a tax collector; is accepted in court to assure the return of one charged with crime against the state, or is given to guarantee the faithfulness of one under the sacred trust of handling money of infants. A thought might be given also to the personal surety who in innocence is about to be exposed to grave and unknown dangers. It does not excuse the surety that the law requires of the fiduciary more than should be expected of one with his business ability and moral fiber.

In too many cases personal sureties awaken all too late to the fact that the friends for whom they became sureties are not friends at all. More than one Probate Judge has been shocked to find, in time of default, that personal sureties accepted by his court have become worthless. Wards have been made paupers because guardians have furnished bonds of irresponsible sureties. On the other hand, the savings of honest men have been wiped out and the welfare of their Page five

families jeopardized as the result of bonds hastily signed for unworthy relatives, friends or political acquaintances. 

In a great many cases, the fiduciary and his personal sureties at the time of appointment are totally ignorant of the business sense required to take over the assets of a trust estate; the diligence necessary to preserve them; and the rigid requirements of laws and court procedure to be followed. Many attempt to manage even substantial and complicated estates without advice of counsel. All too frequently this results in confusion out of which develops loss to the estate, the fiduciary, his personal surety, or all of them. Not infrequently embarrassment is caused the Judge, who, in the eleventh hour, desperately tries to bring order out of chaos.

When one has spent years in a fascinating business he feels an intense interest in any change that affects that business. Today there is a noticeable change in attitude toward constant and adequate protection for beneficiaries of estates of all kinds and legislatures are recording it in statutes. Students of Probate Practice are viewing this trend with enthusiasm. We in this business have seen legislation introduced in several states during the past winter that would require personal sureties to qualify their bonds by showing real assets out of which their obligations could be met, and would put some restraint upon these assets so that the security offered at the time the bonds are filed will remain intact during the entire time this protection is needed.

To me the personal surety is as old-fashioned and unsafe as is the horse and buggy on the modern streamlined highway. This statement may have to be forgiven on the grounds of prejudice as coming from one who, for most of his professional life, has been engaged in the corporate surety business. Personal suretyship, always a dangerous business, was perhaps necessary in times when there was nothing better. Today, corporate sureties are scrutinized and supervised by trained insurance examiners in every state, and can do the job much better than personal sureties Page six

can ever hope to do it. And when the guardian or other fiduciary defaults the corporate surety meets the loss out of reserves accumulated for the purpose. No suffering is caused individuals and the trust estate is more often safe for the ward or other beneficiary.

Examples of Defaults The files of the Surrogates Courts throughout the country contain actually thousands of heartbreaking records in which wards and heirs, crooked fiduciaries and personal sureties take the leading parts. This is no melodramatic statement. 

From a southern state comes the story of a bank cashier, qualified as guardian for two minors who inherited an estate valued at approximately $12,000. The assistant cashier and a teller in the same bank signed the guardian's bond in the amount of $24,000 and made affidavits that they were each worth $12,000. Later the bank closed and the guardian and both sureties lost their positions. Subsequently an investigation disclosed that the guardian had made some bad loans for his bank and had used money belonging to his wards for the purpose of paying off these loans to keep directors of the bank from becoming acquainted with the true condition. The guardian and the personal sureties proved to be insolvent. 

From another state comes the story of a trustee of an estate who gave as his personal sureties two cousins of an elderly lady who was beneficiary of the trust. The trustee died owing the estate. Nothing could be collected from the trustee's estate and the cestui que trust refused to pursue her cousins, the personal sureties, on the ground that she was as able to stand the loss as they.

From a mid-western state comes the story of a banker who was appointed conservator of an estate. He filed a bond with two personal sureties. Two years later he was obliged to file an additional bond and gave the same personal sureties. Several years later under pressure of the 

Veterans Administration the conservator filed his final account and resignation. The account was approved by the Page seven

court, the conservator discharged and his sureties exonerated. Then a new conservator was appointed and filed a corporate surety bond in the amount of $22,000. At the time the superseding conservator receipted for the assets of the estate there were included in the investments many that were clearly illegal and which subsequently proved to be entirely worthless. Some $7,000 of the incompetent's funds had been placed in irregular investments. The superseding conservator sought counsel upon the advisability of suing his predecessor and his personal sureties but finally decided that this would be a useless and expensive gesture because the first conservator was entirely without funds and his personal sureties were bankrupt. 

From the same state comes the story of another, appointed guardian of a minor nephew and niece. He qualified by filing a guardian's bond in the amount of $11,000 with three personal sureties. Subsequently he filed a new bond executed by a corporate surety, which soon started an investigation of the estate that led to a petition by the corporate surety for its release under the bond. Principal then filed a third bond signed by two personal sureties. 

Exceptions were taken to the account principal filed pursuant to the corporate surety's petition for release and a substantial claim was made upon the various sureties. It was then disclosed that of the three original sureties, one had died leaving no estate and a second was insolvent. The two sureties on the last bond disclaimed any responsibility whatsoever and claimed that they had no financial worth. 

The estate was later settled when a comparatively small amount was paid with difficulty by the third of the first personal sureties and a substantial amount was paid by the corporate surety.

In another state lately a cashier in a small bank became administrator of an estate and furnished two farmers as his sureties. The cashier was later found short $5,500 in the estate and committed suicide. It appears to be problematical whether or not collection will be made from the personal sureties. They will pay, if, as and when they can, Page eight

but the default will work a real hardship upon these farmers who looked up to the cashier of their bank. 

Just let me give you one more example out of my own experience. A few years ago, a man with a family made a will in the office of his attorney. He had a wife and several children. He named a brother-in-law as executor to serve without bond. The husband and father died, and not long thereafter the widow and mother died leaving the children. 

In a short time, the executor committed suicide because during the depression he had squandered the proceeds of his sacred trust in a selfish endeavor to save his own business. Orphans were robbed and the executor's own family was wrecked. A responsibility had been placed upon a weakling who failed. How much better it would have been had a corporate surety bond been specified in the will. The vigilance of a surety company, would, in all probability, have prevented this disaster; in any event, the heirs would have been protected.

Although there are many instances where personal sureties have paid and where many have been bankrupt by having to make good the defaults of others, there is a list of heirs in almost every county in this country who have been deprived of estates to which they were entitled, because personal sureties were unable to respond. Personal bonds are secured by fiduciaries in the interest of economy. 

A fiduciary who gets a personal bond usually pays nothing whatever for it, and the protection which the estate receives is frequently worth exactly that. 

Statistics 

Show Variations

In spite of the ancient admonitions against personal sureties and the frequent examples of disaster resulting from this form of security, it continues to be taken in a surprising number of cases. The business of guardianship and other fiduciary administrations should, it would seem, not vary a great deal between states or different counties in the same state, yet, we find no consistency when it comes to furnishing sureties for such fiduciaries.

Page nine

Examination of the court records in one county in Pennsylvania disclosed 388 bonds filed within a period of 12 months. Of these, 351 or about 90% were filed by personal sureties. In another county in the same state, 343 bonds were filed, of which only 27 or but 7% were by personal sureties. In one county in New Mexico, there were 287 bonds filed, of which 170 or 59% were by personal sureties. 

I am informed that in a second county in the same state, 82 bonds of corporate sureties were filed for fiduciaries and in the same period not one bond of a personal surety was taken. In one county in Michigan there were 10,488 bonds filed in about a year's time, of which 10,015 or more than 95% were personal surety bonds. In another county of the same state, 2,689 were filed of which 1,659 or about 60% were personal sureties.

Other statistics can be furnished of a similar nature. To me they are startling. One would hardly expect, in this modern day of financial strain, to find so many individuals willing to expose their assets to the hazards of suretyship obligations. Probate Judges are apparently not united in their views of the personal surety. It would seem that modern business methods and court procedures should more uniformly demand security that will actually protect the estates involved. Judge Gary, for many years head of the

U. S. Steel Corporation, regarded personal bonds as so dangerous that in making his will he especially warned his heirs against them, using the following language: "I earnestly request my wife and children that they steadfastly decline to sign any bonds or obligations of any kind as surety for any other person or persons."

The Honorable John Hammill, while Governor of Iowa, said: "In so far as sureties on bonds and casualties are concerned, no person can afford to become a personal surety on any bond of any type or character. 

Insurance in this type has become a matter of business, and it would be in the interest of public policy Page ten

if every state in the Union would pass a law providing for corporate surety. I am not speaking in the interest of those engaged in the insurance business, but I am speaking in the interest of those who should be concerned in preserving their private fortunes for their families. We must go forward and our people must come to realize the hazards of acting as a surety."

In some jurisdictions there is no such thing as actually qualifying personal sureties and little pretense is made to establish worth. Many personal sureties are not worth their obligations, despite qualifying certificates and affidavits to the contrary. It is needless to say that in the absence of statute or express contract the beneficiary of a trust has no lien on the real or personal property of one who is personal surety for the fiduciary. Frequently personal sureties who may have a little property use it as an excuse to become surety over and over again-setting up obligations they can never meet. In most jurisdictions there is nothing to prevent personal sureties, after qualifying, from immediately disposing of or covering up their assets. States

Improve 

Laws

It is because of these conditions that many, including lawyers, Surrogates and their clerks, feel that something should be done in the case of personal sureties to give more protection to obligees under the bonds. If increased protection is to be given, then it is perfectly apparent that some restrictions must be placed upon the personal sureties. It is only right that one who signs a bond giving some guardian access to an infant's estate, should be aware that he is taking on an obligation that may ultimately restrain him in the transfer of his own property. The giving of a bond should be something more than an empty legal formality and personal sureties should be made to feel this. 

In Alabama, there has been a statute on the books for years (Section 2603, Ala. Code, 1923) which provides that the bonds of the Judge of the Probate Court, of the County Page eleven

Clerk, of the Tax Collector, and others, are liens upon the property of the principals from the date of execution of the bonds. This statute makes no mention of bonds furnished by guardians or other fiduciaries and it is to be noted that the bonds become liens only on the property of the principals-not the sureties. Missouri has a similar statute (Section 12279, Rev. Stat. 1929) in connection with the bonds of Township Trustees and Treasurers. In 

Georgia, the statutes dealing with County Treasurers' and 

Tax Collectors' Bonds (Sections 23-1012 and 92-5510 of the 

Georgia Code, 1933) go further in that a lien is created not only against the principal's property but against the property of the surety as well. Iowa and Illinois have done the same thing in connection with undertakings of bail (Section 

13-625 Iowa Code 1935 and Chapter 38, Section 640-2 

Illinois Rev. Stat. 1935). Bills have been introduced before the legislatures of at least seven states during the past winter for the purpose-not of eliminating personal suretyship, but to do away with some of its dangers. 

One group of critics has suggested that the surety companies are interested in this proposed legislation in order to eliminate personal suretyship, so that the companies can monopolize the business and charge high premiums. In taking this attitude such critics entirely overlook the fact that the companies are not suggesting legislation for elimination of personal sureties. Surely there can be no condemnation if, out of their wide experiences, they join reasonable men in recommending safeguards for present needs. Remember also that as in many another competitive business under government supervision, surety companies could not charge excessive rates over any extended period even though so disposed.

More than half the fiduciary bonds throughout the United States are signed by personal sureties. Any experienced actuary can demonstrate that the conversion of any substantial portion of these into corporate bonds would reduce rather than increase premium rates. All that surety companies suggest now is that heirs to estates (be they infants, Page twelve

incompetents or normal adults) are entitled to have the best there is of real collectible security, either from a sound surety company or from personal sureties who are compelled to be financially fit throughout the period of their obligations. Anything short of this seems to me to be nonsense.

Behind the obligation of a sound surety company are its capital, its surplus, its undivided profits, and large reserves for losses. Each surety company whose bonds are acceptable to the Federal Government is subject to investigation by the U. S. Treasury Department, which three times a year fixes the underwriting limits of each company. Surety companies are regularly examined by the Insurance Departments of the various states. Surety companies are compelled to make a most complete detailed statement of their business and investments regularly to every state in which they operate, and must procure annually certificates of solvency. 

They quickly justify before courts upon notice to do so.

In view of all the safeguards surrounding the operations of surety companies, made for the protection of holders of its bonds, including wards and other beneficiaries, it is incredible that personal sureties continue to be accepted and approved, without requiring from them evidence of their continued worth. This personal surety situation has continued to the present day only because there is a constantly new group which needs the protection of fiduciary bonds. 

If the same persons were frequently heirs to estates they would soon demand that the practice of accepting inadequate personal sureties be stopped. New

Methods Proposed 

Several methods of making personal surety bonds safe for obligees have been suggested. 

Under the most drastic method the bond would be made immediately a lien upon the property of the surety. Again it has been suggested that without making the bond a technical lien, the personal surety might be required to give some kind of effective assurance that he would not transfer property used for qualifying the bond Page thirteen

while it is in force. A modification of this suggestion is to let the personal surety transfer or encumber property listed in the qualifying affidavit only after he has given advance notice of intention, thereby giving obligee the right to demand other security. Another variation of the plan would permit the personal surety to dispose of property listed at the time of executing the bond upon condition that other property of an approved class and value be given to take place of the original security. Another method would require the personal surety to carefully qualify at the time the bond is written and justify his position periodically thereafter. The last suggestion and one already in use, but not permanently effective, is that an affidavit be taken at the time the bond is given, causing the surety to qualify by listing property in some detail. 

Last year a committee of the State Bar of California made a study of personal sureties and reported. The committee recommended that any amendment of statute should be applicable only to certain types of bond, to wit: attachment bonds, bonds given in release of attachments, bonds given in claim and delivery actions, and release thereof, and stay bonds on appeal. It was not considered advisable to include bonds given in probate matters, nor in criminal matters. The report also was against creating a lien against the property of the individual surety. Its recommendation was that separate affidavits be required from each surety setting forth in detail the assets which were being offered as security, and that there should be some requirement compelling a surety to give notice of intention to convey or voluntarily encumber the property described in the affidavit, with a penalty attached for failure to give such notice. 

The theory of the plan is that no legal complications should be created that would place a cloud on the title to property or unduly restrict personal rights of the surety in relation thereto. However, there would be certain inconveniences to the personal surety and there could be a reasonable expectation that he would be serious in his act and attempt at least to understand the risk taken. As a

Page fourteen

result he more likely would be able to respond in time of default. In my opinion, the committee unnecessarily narrowed the scope of the remedy offered by eliminating probate bonds. That part of the recommendation that prefers some form of control other than by a fixed lien on the property of the surety, is worthy of serious consideration, yet, I believe that there should be adopted by all the States such changes in the law as will add actual security for those who have a right to expect it.

The Legislature of the State of California had submitted to it in 1939 a bill intended to amend Section 1057 of the Code of Civil Procedure. This proposed amendment in part followed the recommendations heretofore mentioned, but did not include the provision for notice by the surety of a desire to transfer or otherwise voluntarily encumber the property set forth in the affidavit which was offered as a basis for the suretyship.

A more complete bill was offered this year in the Legislature of Massachusetts. This bill sought to amend Chapter 

205 of the General Laws by requiring the filing of a certificate which constitutes in much detail a complete financial statement of the personal surety. All statements in this certificate would be deemed to be material statements in prosecutions for perjury. In place of such certificate the personal surety would be permitted to deposit money or a savings bankbook, or non-registered bonds of the United States or of the Commonwealth, or of a County, City or Town within the Commonwealth, equal in amount or face value to the penal sum of the Probate Bond. Where real estate has been offered as his qualification by the personal surety, and the surety later desires to dispose of or encumber such real estate, he would have to apply to the court in writing for permission to do so or to otherwise bring about the termination of his liability by causing the principal to file a new bond. Violation of this provision would be subject to penalty. There was also a saving clause that nothing in the amendment would in any wise affect the title of such real estate. In Pennsylvania a bill was introduced (Pennsyl- Page fifteen

vania Senate Bill 465) which would require a similar certificate under oath.

In the States of New York and New Jersey proposed legislation has been offered on the lien theory (New Jersey 

Assembly No. 293; New York Senate No. 623). In New 

York this has been attempted by a proposed amendment of the Lien Law of the State. Upon the execution of any bond or undertaking by a personal surety he would attach thereto. an affidavit accurately describing his real estate and thereupon there shall attach to such real property a lien in favor of the bond or undertaking. Under the New York Town 

Law, the County Clerk in whose office a tax collector's undertaking shall have been filed must make an entry thereof in a book provided for the purpose in the same manner as judgments are entered of record and every such undertaking shall be a lien on all the real estate held by the collector or his sureties within the county at the time of filing (New York Town Law, Section 35). Bail Bonds filed in New York State become a lien upon the property of the sureties until a certificate of discharge has been filed through the office of the Attorney General of the State or District Attorney of the County (Code of Criminal Pro cedure, Section 556-B). In Alabama the statute referred to above was amended some years after it was passed to provide for termination of the lien one year after the expiration of the office bonded. It has been said that the difficulty of discharging the personal surety is one of the biggest obstacles to the lien theory. This point does not come up in New Jersey as there is a statutory provision for the terminating of responsibility of sureties on the bond of a fiduciary after three months from the approval of the final account. (Revised Statutes of New Jersey, 1937, Sections 3:8-17 and 3:8-18).

In the State of Oklahoma legislation has been offered (Oklahoma House Bills 639, 634) reading in part as follows: 

"SECTION 2. The Court Clerk of the county in which such bond is filed shall keep a docket in the form of an index in which the name of each person Page sixteen

obligated on said bond shall appear in alphabetical order together with the number and style of the action in which the bond is given and the description of the property so scheduled, and it shall be the duty of the clerk immediately upon the filing and approval of such bond to enter the same on such docket; the docket herein provided shall be notice to the world of the lien created herein."

We should perhaps not rush into these changes Caution and expect results immediately. Effective law Is changes slowly. It may be that the lien theory is Urged too drastic. If so, then it seems to me that an advisable change would be the so-called form of affidavit or certificate which compels the complete exposure of the position of the individual qualifying as a surety, with a penalty attached to punish for wrongful misstatements of fact, and to add the requirement which would permit a surety to transfer his property on notice so long as it was not to the detriment of the obligee. It may be that legislation along the line that has been offered in Massachusetts will be the most practical step toward the correction of the unsatisfactory conditions that have existed in connection with personal suretyship.

In my opinion, however, there are types of bonds, especially in the Probate Courts, that require even more than the suggested control under the proposed legislation in Massachusetts. As far as that legislation goes, it protects against voluntary acts on the part of the surety. It does not protect as to involuntary events, such as deaths, unpaid taxes and judgments. For this reason, I believe that if that plan is to be followed then personal sureties should be checked each year by the court taking the bonds. This may take a little more work in some courts, but certainly if the surety has to re-qualify each year, this would make for an additional safeguard. Perhaps some day Probate Courts will have officers of the type referred to in the first recommendation of your Committee who can make such an annual review.
