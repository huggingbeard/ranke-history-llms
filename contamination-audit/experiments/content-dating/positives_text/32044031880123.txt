FOREWORD 

Split-second judgment in driving can spell the difference between crash avoidance and accidents. In the crashes that do occur, impact force patterns complete their lethal damage to the torsos and internal organs of occupants in a matter of several thousandths of a second. 

Motor vehicle and highway safety program standards deal with these complex bio-engineering phenomena, and cannot be established with confidence except through accurate laboratory and field measurements and careful analysis of the results. In turn, these test results must be correlated against information derived from actual accidents. 

The needed information, however, on the immediate and underlying causes of accidents and the results of injuries, largely does not exist. 

Little is known about the manner in which people are injured or vehicle design factors that can lessen injuries in crashes. Little is known about the effectiveness of many countermeasures that have been tried, making it entirely possible that some ineffective measures are now in practice, while others that could save many lives are being overlooked. In the absence of adequate, authoritative information, the pinpointing of effective countermeasures is painfully slow, if not impossible, while thousands of people needlessly die or become seriously maimed in vehicle crashes. 

The capability to pinpoint effective countermeasures depends on the quality of experimental and test results, which in turn are linked directly to the quality of the facilities and tools used to obtain the results. Factual, objective and technically sound information in any field of inquiry and application to action programs clearly cannot be obtained without the appropriate laboratories and associated measurement capability. 

This principle, amply demonstrated over and over again in the brilliant breakthroughs in medicine, space exploration, nuclear energy, and other major national programs, is equally valid for motor vehicle and highway safety. The facts, however, are that modern research and testing technologies that have contributed so heavily to the broad advances in these and other fields, have not been applied until now in any significant amounts to the amelioration of the unfortunate tragedies that occur daily on our Nation's highways.

The unmistakable dependence of safety program effectiveness upon appropriate research and testing technology has been recognized by the Congress in providing for an investigation of needs for accident and iii

injury research and test facilities.

This report, as required under Title 

III of the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act, describes the results of this investigation. It identifies the kinds of facilities and equipment with which engineers, research workers and supporting technical staffs can perform the types of coordinated engineering, physiological, behavioral and other measurements that are required for:

Establishing Federal motor vehicles safety standards and evaluating manufacturers' compliance with them.

Establishing Federal standards in such correlated areas to vehicle safety performance as driver education and licensing, periodic motor vehicle inspection, and other State and community highway safety programs.

An important aspect of this report deals with the existing capabili ties, equipment, and facilities, either publicy or privately owned or operated, that can be made available for use in the safety programs. Some existing capabilities are already being utilized by the Department of Transportation, and are described in the report. Continued and expanded use of these and other facilities, coupled with drawing upon the skills and talents of the scientists and technicians trained to operate them, is central to all program planning of the Department of Transportation. Apart from the obvious economic and technical benefits in pursuing this policy, there is no alternative course of action for the next several years. 

In the meantime, the work that has been started with the investigations described in this report must proceed to detailed planning for the systematic shift from the present makeshift arrangements to research and test facilities that will meet the unique requirements of highway safety. 

It is only with appropriate laboratories and equipment, coupled with workable provisions for their full and economic utilization by the Nation's scientific manpower both in and out of Government, that realistic and effective solutions can be anticipated to the ongoing tragedy of deaths, injuries and destruction on our Nation's highways.

For convenience, the report is presented in two volumes. This part, Volume I presents a summary of the investigations and the determinations which have been made to date; Volume II provides expanded descriptions of the investigation.

1. INTRODUCTION THE FEDERAL ROLE 

Congress has authorized and initiated, through passage of the "National 

Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966" and the "National Highway 

Safety Act of 1966", an extensive and energetic program to reduce deaths, injuries and property damage due to traffic accidents. This legislation establishes the Federal Government's role in improving the safety of traveling on the nation's highways, and is the result of the collective opinion of Congress and the vast majority of traffic safety experts that the appalling losses due to traffic accidents can be reduced through Federal action. 

The essence of the Federal role in the overall national safety effort is the establishment and enforcement of standards for high levels of safety performance for:

Motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment under the motor vehicle safety law 

State and local highway safety programs under the highway safety law.

INVESTIGATION OF FACILITY NEEDS 

In enacting the legislation, the Congress recognized that both types of standards would have to be based on substantial, objective data, and that the acquisition of reliable information, as with any field involving physical forces and interrelated measurement on humans, required appropriate laboratories and test facilities. The Congress accordingly directed the 

Secretary of Transportation: 

to make a complete investigation and study of the need for a facility or facilities to conduct research, development, and testing in traffic safety (including but not limited to motor vehicle and highway safety). 

Public Law 89-563 80 Stat. 729 1

PURPOSE AND SCOPE 

The purpose of this report is to present the results of this investigation called for in the law. Specifically, it seeks to identify the types of facilities with which the complex array of tests and measurements can be performed that are essential to implementing an effective motor vehicle and highway safety program.

It then seeks to incorporate these requirements into a coherent overall facilities program that will utilize existing facilities where it is economically and technically feasible to do so, but will provide for new construction where it is indicated. 2

DEVELOPMENT OF FEDERAL MOTOR VEHICLE SAFETY STANDARDS 

The safety performance standards which are to be developed by the 

Department of Transportation under Public Law 89-563 apply to both motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment. They must be performance standards that refer to how the vehicle or equipment will act or react in certain circumstances and not the manner or design whereby this action or reaction is achieved. They must derive from physical reality while reflecting economic and marketplace realities as well. They must cover the significant properties of a vehicle that relate to the desired ends of saving lives and preventing injuries, and which may be grouped into three classes: 

Crash prevention properties (braking and steering are examples)

Crash survivability properties (energy-absorbing steering is an example)

Post crash properties (relative ease of entry into wrecks to remove occupants is an example)

The process of setting performance standards for vehicles thus must deal with an array of complex interactions between vehicle, human, and roadway conditions. Wide variations must be considered in each class, foremost being the differences among drivers; for clearly, standards must be developed that are attuned to the usual, manipulative, and judgment skills of all types of drivers--not only finely trained racing or test track professionals. 

Safety performance that can be translated into practical vehicle designs is the central objective of the Federal standards. Millions of vehicles produced each year at very high rates of mass production must meet these standards which must accordingly reflect cost and production factors as well as safety. While the standards are intended to, and must enhance safety; they should not detract from the vehicle's other performance features nor stifle innovation. Instead, they should permit many alternative design solutions for meeting safety performance requirements and at prices that people can afford to pay. 

Stated simply, each standard must be physically reasonable and attainable. It must be written such that the performance is both measurable and reproducible with minimum, if any, ambiguity in later evaluations of whether or not it has been met by a particular design solution. 

Measurement and testing capability is the foundation requirement for these and other key aspects of the standards development process. Government scientists and engineers charged with this vital national responsibility must be provided with the facilities and test equipment needed to assess properly the highly complex tradeoffs involving safety performance goals, cost and other production factors. 

DEVELOPMENT OF STATE AND COMMUNITY HIGHWAY SAFETY PROGRAM STANDARDS 

The Highway Safety Act of 1966 establishes a broad nationwide effort in which the Federal, State, and local governments can work cooperatively to reduce death, injuries, and property damage resulting from motor vehicle accidents. A primary feature is its provisions for Federal grantsin-aid to State and local governments for improvement and expansion of their highway safety programs, and particularly for implementing higher standards of safety program performance. 

In this regard, the Department of Transportation, after consultation with the States, local governments, other Federal departments and agencies, public and private organizations, has issued standards for State and local safety programs in: Periodic Motor Vehicle Inspection; Motor Vehicle 

Registration; Motorcycle Safety; Driver Education; Driver Licensing;

Codes and Laws; Traffic Courts; Alcohol in Relation to Highway Safety; 

Identification and Surveillance of Accident Locations; Traffic Records; 

Emergency Medical Services; Highway Design, Construction, and Main tenance; Traffic Control Devices. Now in the draft stage are standards for Pedestrian Safety; Police Traffic Services; and Accident Cleanup.

In addition, a manual is now being developed that will provide guidance and assistance to the States in the implementation of the standards and guidelines that have been issued or are under development which represent the best collective judgment of experts as to the present state of the art. But the sobering situation is that, as with the highway safety field in general, the development of these materials has been severely handicapped by a dearth of scientifically sound technical information and statistically sound data bearing upon their substance. In the absence of meaningful cost/benefit information, many high payoff activities are probably underfunded while programs with less payoff may be implemented and emphasized.

A wide variety of problems must be solved in order to upgrade the substance of the activities whereby the States implement each standard. 

The overwhelming need is to test and establish the effectiveness of both those activities which have been generally accepted without proof for years as well as promising innovations. Toward this end, carefully designed research programs must be implemented under closely controlled conditions of measurement and with precise instrumentation. 

Few if any individual States, however, have sufficient numbers of trained researchers, facilities, instrumentation, and other research resources needed to obtain conclusive answers to many of the as yet unanswered and perplexing questions on safety program substance. Furthermore, much of the needed research is so complex that notwithstanding its fundamental importance to the effective implementation of State and local highway safety programs, it is not the kind of work that could reasonably be attempted at State or local levels, at least not without massive Federal support. Hence, the States must look to the Federal Government for leadership in research aimed at testing present safety practices as well as the development of more effective procedures.

It is for the State and local governments to make the highway safety programs work; it is for the Federal Government to furnish leadership and guidance for an integrated program of applied and basic research that will enable the State and local governments to accomplish this purpose. 

COMPLIANCE EVALUATIONS OF MOTOR VEHICLES Under the provisions of the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety 

Act, two classes of evaluations of production vehicles are required dealing LO

respectively with motor vehicle standards and defect notifications. The compliance responsibilities of the manufacturer and derivative enforcement responsibilities of the Department in these two respects may be generally described:

With regard to motor vehicle standards 

Manufacturers must design and manufacture cars which meet the Federal safety standards and certify that they do so The Department must enforce the law through tests and other procedures that indicate whether or not the finished products meet the standards. 

With regard to defect notification campaigns 

Manufacturers must identify safety related defects in their products and notify owners and dealers of corrective measures through appropriate notification campaigns

The Department must provide assurance that the public has been informed of the possible presence of a safety related defect and the necessary corrective measures. The Department has the further responsibility to call to manufacturers attention any safety related defects they have failed to detect, report, and correct. 

To implement its enforcement responsibilities, the Department must have the capability of corroborating through its own independent laboratory and test track procedures the correctness of any supporting data and other demonstrations provided by the manufacturer in support of his certification that his products comply with the applicable Federal standards. The Department must also be able to determine with its own test procedures if consumer experience in actual driving of the vehicle matches performance certified by the manufacturer. Finally, this all important responsibility of providing consumer protection extends as well to independent testing of consumer complaints of unsafe vehicle features not covered by standards.

With regard to manufacturer's defects notifications, the Department has similar responsibilities for independent tests of the technical sufficiency of corrective actions that manufacturers announce in their safety related defects notification campaigns. Again, appropriate laboratory and testing capabilities are central to meeting this responsibility. 

It should be noted that these various responsibilities for safety enforcement by the Department through independent testing extends beyond the

complete motor vehicle to motor vehicle equipment such as tires, brake hoses, brake linings, brake master cylinders and other safety related items in the so-called "aftermarket" of the automotive industry and the vast subsidiary industry of rebuilt parts. 

ACCIDENT AND INJURY COUNTERMEASURES RESEARCH 

The need for better and more fundamental understanding of motor vehicle and highway safety problems has long been unmistakably clear. 

The unavoidable reliance on subjective judgment, if not outright guesswork, today instead of objective information in many critical program decision areas is directly traceable to the fact that highway safety has not received, until recently, anything approaching proper levels of research attention and support. 

This dearth of scientific information cuts across the board in all motor vehicle and highway safety areas, and is reflected in the fact that both safety laws mandate that research, testing, development, and training be conducted in support of their separate provisions. The needed research must draw upon an extremely broad base of knowledge in almost all disciplines: engineering and the physical sciences, physiology and related biomedical fields, psychology and other human-behavior areas of inquiry, education and training methodologies, legal and related social science fields. The needs can be focused on man, the vehicle and the highway environment: 

Man, in terms of drivers, and occupants as well as pedestrians Vehicle, of which there are many types ranging from motorcycles to large trucks

Environment, in terms of the roadway, obstacles, traffic-control system elements, and natural environmental conditions With the field having received little sustained research attention almost endless lists of subject areas can now be identified for each of these three elements, understanding of which is vital to progress in motor vehicle and highway safety. To mention several representative subject areas for each: Man 

Physiological Factors: motor responses, vision, fatigue, and auditory abilities 

Psychological Factors: effects of stress on driver performance

Vehicle

Structures: crashworthiness, energy absorption capacity, capacity of doors to stay shut in crashes and be opened after crashes, dangerous projections on the instrument panel, seat, and anchorage strength

Traffic Controls: signs, signals, pavement markings, speed change and auxiliary lanes; one-way street systems, channelization, construction detours The countermeasures required to reduce traffic deaths and injuries involve understanding of how these three elements in combination can:

(1) prevent accidents, (2) reduce injuries in the crashes that do occur, and (3) improve the capability of post-crash recovery of injured occupants.

In pursuing these countermeasure orientations, much work is possible in the overall subject area of "man," or of "the vehicle," or of "the environment." However, the needed effort, by far, must involve programs in which these problems are treated in combination. Thus, for example, the driving skills of people must be studied in relation to the design characteristics of the vehicle; and both must be considered within the context of the road surface on which the driver is operating the vehicle. 

Many general as well as unique measurements and tests are required to assess the extensive performance variations in humans, vehicles, and environment. FOREMOST IS THE NECESSITY TO TEST TO WITHIN THE THRESHOLDS OF DANGER. This inescapable fact cannot be overstated, for the entire program deals with the subject of danger. All research and testing must embody fail-safe devices and conditions to pursue the work safely under inherently dangerous conditions. 

IMPROVEMENT OF THE SAFETY OF MOTOR CARRIER OPERATIONS 

A fifth major category of Department of Transportation programs that bears directly upon highway safety is related to the improvement of the safety of motor carrier operations. Whereas the previously described four program areas are the responsibility of the National Highway Safety Bureau under the provisions of the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle 

Safety Act of 1966 and the Highway Safety Act of 1966, the safety of motor carrier operations in interstate commerce is the responsibility of the 

Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety, a sister Bureau of the National Highway Safety Bureau in the Federal Highway Administration. Both Bureaus have regulatory responsibilities, but while the rulemaking actions of the National Highway Safety Bureau are directed toward the manufacturers of motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment, the rulemaking actions of the Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety (formerly part of the Interstate Commerce Commission) are directed toward the operators of trucks and buses in interstate commerce.

With the enactment of the two new safety laws, each of which calls for broad programs of research, tests, and development in support of their respective provisions, the means become available for the first time to provide at least some of the long-needed research in support of the safety regulation of the nation's motor carriers both in for-hire and private carriage. With comparatively minor additions, the research and testing required for the development of safety performance standards for the manufacturer of trucks and buses used in interstate commerce, will provide the research support for Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety regulation directed toward the operators of the equipment.

TYPES OF TESTS RELATED TO MOTOR VEHICLE AND HIGHWAY SAFETY PROGRAMS 

The five classes of program responsibilities discussed, generate a broad array of test and measurement requirements. Some center on highly intensive probing, one time analyses under precise research conditions and controls. Others involve repetitive tests of similar items in which results of tests of each item are compared with a testing standard. 

Some of the more important tests are as follows:

INJURY TOLERANCE TESTS 

The purpose of these tests is to determine the exact magnitude and direction of the forces which cause specific types of injuries. Among the important measurements are the deformations (both plastic and elastic) of human skin and internal tissues, breakages of bones, and the motions of and damage to the various parts of the human body under crash type stresses.

VEHICLE PERFORMANCE TESTS 

Systematic measurements with careful instrumentation must be made of the capability of steering linkage, brakes, springs, shocks, doors, door locks, and many other vehicle components and subsystems to perform safely under a broad range of static and dynamic loads. Other safety properties such as flammability of cabin materials and the susceptibility of fuel tanks to breakage and propagation of fires must also be studied. 

Each critical safety performance characteristic must be studied and evaluated for all types of vehicles ranging in size from small motor bikes and passenger cars up to very large multi-bottomed truck trailer combinations. All ranges of speeds must be considered, and the work must be conducted on lightly loaded vehicles with comparatively poor traction as well as heavy tandem axle loads with major problems of braking capacity. 

Some of the testing can be done in laboratories, but most of the work must be done under actual test track driving, because many of the phenomena of lateral, longitudinal, and vertical accelerations, vibrations, driver handling efforts are so complex that meaningful simulations in laboratories are out of question. Many of these tests can only be conducted with highly skilled test drivers at the wheel, particularly where highspeed driving and vehicle handling problems in inherently dangerous 11

situations are under study. Other tests of human driving skills in relation to vehicle design must utilize representative drivers of all age groups from the overall driving population, including people with normal and below normal sensory capabilities, driving skills or judgment capacities. In addition, the roadway surface over which the vehicles are tested must be measured and known to a high degree of accuracy. 

DRIVER PERFORMANCE TESTS 

Evaluations here involve a large number of psychological and physiological effects, and hence require appropriate measurements of sensory and motor skills, traits, and aptitudes of all classes of drivers in all states of health. Also included are tests on drivers under the influence of alcohol, patented medicines, addictive and nonaddictive drugs.

Possibly the most difficult aspect of driver performance evaluations relates to the broad area of judgment and risk taking behavior under conditions of impending danger. Virtually no systematic study has as yet been attempted, and hence very little is known in this all-important aspect of driving. Work in this area must first be conducted in laboratory simulations of hazardous driving conditions, during which stress reactions such as eye movements, brain activity, respiration, heartbeat, braking, and steering forces can be measured and related to the simulated conditions of driving stress. These laboratory results then have to be compared with results obtained under controlled conditions of test track driving.

Actual driving tests under controlled conditions on test tracks are necessary as a bridge between the laboratory results and driving performance in traffic. Included are tests of many different types of visual, auditory, and tactual skills that are basic to good driving. The special demands upon drivers of trucks and buses must be evaluated. 

HIGHWAY AND ENVIRONMENT TESTS 

This comprises another important facet of a total systems approach to the problem, for it is clear that driver performance and vehicle characteristics must, at some point, be related to the highway itself. 

Laboratory as well as field tests are required in such important features of the highway system as strength and energy absorptive capacity of median barriers and guardrails, roadway illumination and sign visibility under conditions of darkness, inclement weather, and other environmental conditions, and such design factors as curvature, sight distance, lane and shoulder widths, on and off ramp configurations for limited access highways. 12

No less important is work in the entire field of anti-skid and other properties of the road surface. This highlights the need for testing capability on a system basis, for the effects of slippery road surfaces that are wet or icy must be studied in relation both to the handling properties built into vehicles, and the associated driving skills of all types of drivers.

CRASH INJURY AND VEHICLE DAMAGE STUDIES 

This vital field of inquiry presents somewhat different types of testing and measurement requirements since it must center almost entirely around victims of actual crashes and the vehicles involved. Autopsies must be performed on the bodies of the fatally injured. Injured survivors must be the subjects of detailed clinical follow-up on the recuperative processes including cosmetic as well as internal damage repairs. 

The broad array of modern medical research must be brought to bear including, for example, X-ray and other techniques for assessing bone and internal organ damage and recuperative rates in the course of diagnosing and treating injuries produced in crashes.

Concomitantly with the clinical studies on the injuries, "after-thefact" engineering analysis of the vehicles in which the injured parties were riding at the time of impact are required in order to relate specific elements or features of the vehicle to the injuries that were produced in the crash.

This integrated medical and engineering effort has two major goals. 

First, it seeks to develop methods for rapid diagnosis of injuries occupants suffer in crashes and for treatment of the injuries. Second, it seeks to trace the injury and tissue damage back to the impact forces that caused them, or more specifically, back to the vehicle elements that were involved.

TESTS RELATED TO MOTOR CARRIER SAFETY 

The operation of for-hire and private carriers on the highways of the nation is expanding constantly. The presence of these heavy vehicles on the roadway, coupled with their limited acceleration has a decided impact on the safety aspects of the man-vehicle-environment combination. Tests and measurements must be conducted to establish these interactions as well as the safety characteristics which pertain to the commercial carriers themselves. 14

These tests and measurements include such areas as the stability of doubles and multi-vehicle trains and the effects of such trains on other highway users. The effects of inspection and maintenance practices must be studied, for example, on accident causation, on emissions and noise levels, and on the probable safe working life of components under varying conditions of use. Work must also be conducted to determine the components and accessories which are necessary for safe vehicle operations. This will include mud and water spray control, anti-jackknife devices, prevention of dangerous spillages, visibility requirements, improved coupling methods and emergency exits. 15

4. FACILITY ELEMENTS FOR MAKING NEEDED TESTS CLASSES OF FACILITY ELEMENTS 

A total of 68 different general and special purpose laboratories, special rooms, equipment and field test tracks have been identified as the complement of facilities required for proper conduct of the broad scope of tests and measurements just described. These may be broadly classified as:

Test Track Elements Physiological Test Elements Vehicle Test Elements Human Behavior and Sensory Skills Test Elements Driving Simulation Test Elements Highway Research Test Elements Injury Research and Treatment Test Elements Motor Vehicle Test Elements Total Environment Simulation Test Elements 

Each of the 68 facility elements is specific to one or more types of unique tests or research measurements. All, however must be supplemented by and can share with each other various supporting items including: computers for real-time control of the special laboratory equipment and for scientific data reduction; instrumentation, including fail-safe supervisory controls; photographic and other instrumentation; technical support shops, including the capabilities to calibrate and repair the instrumentation, machine shops, glass blowing units, animal handling units, capability for reception, processing and other handling of volunteer subjects used in "average driver tests.' 

CRITERIA FOR ESTABLISHING OPERATIONAL UNITS 

A number of different criteria have been identified for combining the various requirements into operational units such as laboratory buildings or experimental field stations. The primary criteria are: Manpower

The entire research, test, and development programs for all purposes pose substantial problems in the manpower required for 17

their implementation. The problems relate primarily to the diversity of skills needed to conduct the work, and not the number of workers. The skill requirements cut across engineering, medicine, psychology and a number of other disciplines, as well as many of the subdisciplines within each. Thus, the availability of a manpower supply from which the many specialists can be drawn to conduct the work is a major planning criterion. 

Equipment 

The economics and efficiency in the utilization of both general purpose and special purpose equipment are, along with the manpower issues, also of critical importance in the planning of facility groupings. 

Shared Manpower and Equipment 

The program requirements are complex and overlapping in several dimensions. The reciprocal relationships between manpower and facilities are illustrated by the fact that one group of research and test workers will require several different types of facilities; and one class of facilities will be used by several different research groups. 

Test Subjects 

Much of the research and testing program will depend heavily on a large number of test subjects covering the spectrum of American drivers in respect to age, experience, training, motor and perceptual skills, and size, sex, intelligence, and state of health. It is especially important to plan facility groupings to achieve greatest economy and efficiency in the use of the test subjects, which invariably are in short supply. 

Accident and Injury Patterns

The integrated medical and engineering crash injury research must be conducted in locations where the largest concentrations of representative accident types and injury patterns are occurring. This strongly suggests a number of dispersed locations, each at a hospital. The alternative is to transport crash investigation and trauma research teams over great distances to the scenes of accidents, and then to transport wrecked vehicles back to test laboratories and the injured parties or the bodies of the fatalities back to the trauma research hospitals for clinical follow-up or autopsies. 18

Varying Traffic Densities 

Numerous field tests and observations must be conducted, some using elaborate equipment, under real traffic conditions. Specialists must be able to proceed rapidly to the scene of accidents for study in various locales: on primary and secondary highways, in urban and rural areas, and of varying traffic densities and patterns. 

Topographical and Other Land Criteria 

The various types of test tracks are the primary generators of topographical and other land criteria. Terrain that provides long level distances that are essentially flat is required. Other requirements are for hilly areas with steep grades up to a half-mile long. Climate The test tracks and any related outdoor testing places the most stringent requirements on climate criteria to be incorporated in facilities planning. The overriding requirement is maximum availability, both as to frequency and duration, of clear days suitable for unrestricted outdoor testing for which precise instrumentation including photography are vital. 

Vehicle Standards Enforcement 

In the enforcement of Federal vehicle standards, test centers should be located comparatively close to the major vehicle assembly plants.
