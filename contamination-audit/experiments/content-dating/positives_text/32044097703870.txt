Introduction The Sixth Year 

Fiscal year 1967, extending from 

July 1, 1966, to June 30, 1967, marked the end of the sixth year of an accelerated development program that started in about mid-1961. The basic intent of the program, established and administered by Governor H. Rex Lee, is to bring better education, better living conditions, a better economy, and better government to the people of 

American Samoa. 

During fiscal year 1967, many of the programs implemented in previous fiscal years were refined and polished, and some new accomplishments were made. 

A Constitutional Convention convened early in the fiscal year and revised the 

Territorial Constitution to head American Samoa more directly on the road to self-government. The new constitution, ratified by the voters in November, brought a number of changes in government and laws. Among other things, it lowered the minimum voting age from 20 to 18, reapportioned the legislature, and transferred more authority from the Governor to the legislature. Details are explained in chapter 

II of this report.

The legislature of 1967 produced a number of significant new laws, especially in a special session called in late May. Ten bills, in particular, closed loopholes in certain existing laws, boosted the territory's economy, initiated action designed to lower the cost of living, and in general enabled more government efficiency. Brief summaries of these significant new laws are as 

House bill 41.-This bill fulfills a long-standing desire of Governor Lee to abolish customs duties in American Samoa, making the territory a dutyfree port. Luxury items such as cameras, tape recorders, watches, and other goods may now be imported without entry cost. Only a few items (automobiles, light alcoholic liquors, guns and ammunition, among a few other things) are subject to excise taxes when imported into the territory. 

House bill 42.-The "Junk bill" imposes an excise tax of 30 percent of either the price or the fair market value of second-hand motor vehicles, secondhand machinery, and second-hand appliances that are brought into the territory for resale. The purpose of the bill is to discourage the accumulation of junk in American Samoa, especially machines and equipment that cannot be repaired because of lack of parts. 

House bill 32.-This bill was passed to enable the Government of American Samoa to break a monopoly in the distribution and storage of petroleum products in American Samoa. It authorizes the use of loans, bonds and surplus revenues from electricity to build and operate a second governmentowned petroleum storage and distribution facility in the territory. Signing this bill into law, Governor Lee noted that in 1956 the government-owned petroleum storage facility was leased vii

"at absurdly cheap rates" for up to 50 years. This law is the result of a joint concern among legislators and Governor Lee that petroleum prices are too high. (Electricity rates are also comparatively high because fuel oil runs the generators.) Governor Lee said: "This is our attempt to lower those prices through fair competition." 

House bill 48.—Another "Junk bill", this legislation provides punishment for the possession of discarded or junked vehicle bodies. It reads, in part: "In order to keep American Samoa clean and thereby promote tourism, it is hereby made unlawful for any person to have in his or her possession a junked vehicle. Punishment shall consist of a fine of not less than $10 or (accruing) not more than $500." The law defines a junked vehicle as "any vehicle incapable of being licensed for operation in American Samoa." The law contains a fair warning procedure.

House bill 49.-This law is designed. to discourage aliens from marrying 

American Samoans only to avoid immigration laws. It states that the government shall not issue a marriage license to any couple if one of the two persons is an alien who has not been in American Samoa 60 days after an application for the marriage license has been filed. 

House bill 44.-This law sets clear guidelines for government personnel procedures and provides rights of government employees. Among other things, it states that no government employee may receive a rate of pay less than the minimum wage rate or more than the maximum wage rate. 

Senate bill 42.-This law provides new regulations dealing with the condemnation of land. In general, it ensures more protection for the landowner, speedier payment for condemned land, and more clearly-defined procedures of condemning land for government use.

House bill 43.—The Criminal Code of America Samoa is amended by this and the following two new laws. House bill 43 fills loopholes in the prosecution of false impersonation, perjury, threat, obstructing justice, concealing knowledge of a felony, escape from custody, assisting escape, flight to avoid prosecution or giving testimony, causing death or bodily injury while driving while intoxicated, giving false statements, intimidating voters, promising employment in return for political activity, the promise of employment by a political candidate, paying money to influence voting, harboring or concealing an alien, and alien being present illegally.

Senate bill 44.—This law permits the 

High Court of American Samoa to impose fine or imprisonment for contempt relating to certain misbehaviors and disobediences of court procedures.

Senate bill 49.-This law provides a more thorough prosecution of certain traffic violations, especially in the areas of careless and reckless driving.

The territory's Department of Education continued to serve one of the youngest populations in the world.

The public works department continued to be the pillar of many improvement efforts. Efforts to build a professional police force in the territory were marred on

Construction of a new $3.5 million hospital to be named the Lyndon B.

All kinds of records were established in the territory's economy. Record numbers of tourists came to the territory, a record amount and value of fish and fish products were exported, the net tonnage of vessels arriving in 

Pago Pago Harbor was nearly double that of fiscal year 1966, a record number of planes arrived at Pago Pago 

International Airport, and the Bank of American Samoa reported records in loans granted and numbers of savings and commercial accounts. The number of Asian fishermen working for the territory's two tuna canneries increased from 2,500 in fiscal year 1966 to about 4,000 in fiscal year 1967.

General Information Geography 

American Samoa is a territory of the 

United States administered by the De partment of the Interior. It comprises the eastern islands of the Samoan group which are located along 14° of latitude south of the Equator at about the 170th meridian west longitude. 

These islands are approximately 2,300 miles southwest of Hawaii and 1,600 miles northeast of the northern tip of 

New Zealand, in a direct line. Western

Samoa is an independent state located 

77 miles westward. 

There are seven islands in the American Samoa group: Tutuila, where the town of Pago Pago and central administrative offices are located; Aunu'u; the Manu'a group, which includes 

Ta'u, Olosega, and Ofu; and Swains and Rose, both of which are coral atolls, the latter uninhabited. The total area of the seven islands is 76.2 square miles. Of that total, about 2.3 square miles, or 1,400 acres, is owned by the

Government of American Samoa. A 

much smaller portion is owned in fee simple. This leaves more than 96 per cent of all the land in American Samoa owned communally, according to 

Samoan custom.

The people of American Samoa are American nationals and represent one of the few remaining societies of Polynesians with a relatively small group of persons of mixed blood. The population of the territory has increased from 

5,697 in 1900, when jurisdiction was given to the U.S. Navy, to 20,051 according to the 1960 census. The estimated population of American Samoa as of the end of fiscal year 1967 was 26,000.

The main islands are of volcanic origin and are mostly a series of ridges rising abruptly from the sea. Tutuila is of irregular shape, approximately 18 miles long and 6 miles across at the widest point. A mountain range extends almost the whole length of the island, which is nearly bisected by Pago 

Pago Bay, one of the finest and most beautiful harbors in the South Pacific.

The climate of the islands is tropical and pleasant. From May to November, moderate southeast trade winds blow; during the other months, the winds are variable. The heaviest rainfall occurs from December to March, and the average precipitation in Pago Pago per year for the past 40 years has been approximately 200 inches. The yearly temperatures range from 70° to 90°

F., and the humidity is about 80 percent.

The soil of the islands is of two kinds: clay and sandy loam. In the valleys, the soil is alluvial and quite fertile for growing tropical fruits and taro. Due to the heavy rainfall, the soil on hillsides is thin, with little subsoil.

Swains Island 

Swains Island, a little more than 200 miles north of Pago Pago, is the northernmost island of American

Samoa, although historically it has been associated more with the Tokelau Islands than Samoa. It is a coral atoll about eight miles in circumference with a lagoon in the center that contains brackish water. 

Swains was discovered by the Spanish explorer Pedro Fernandez de 

Quiroz on March 2, 1606, and named

La Peregrin by him. In 1856 it was settled by Eli Jennings, an American, and his wife, the daughter of a Samoan chief. Today it is still owned by the 

Jennings family-the descendants of 

Eli Jennings.

Jennings, the then matriarch of the 

Jennings family, suffered a stroke. She was brought to Pago Pago in a Coast 

Guard boat, but she died in the Hospital of American Samoa on August 2. 

Before her death, the island was jointly owned by her and two sons, Wallace and David. Now David and Wallace are the major owners of Swains Island; 

Mrs. Jennings' will had not been probated by the end of fiscal year 1967, but when it is, the estate will probably be divided equally among her four children, consisting of Wallace, David and two daughters.

The Government of American 

Samoa maintains an agent on Swains 

Island; he is also a school teacher. The government also maintains a public health nurse and a radio operator on the island.

History 

American interest in the islands of Samoa began with the report made by the U.S. Exploring Expedition, which visited the islands under the leadership of Lt. John Wilkes in 1839. It was not until some 30 years later, however, that a formal relationship was entered into with the people of Samoa by a representative of the United States. Primarily as a result of commercial interest in obtaining harbor facilities and rights for a coaling station on the shores of Pago Pago Harbor, the U.S.S. 

Narragansett visited Tutuila in 1872 

and Comdr. Richard Meade entered into an agreement entitled "Commercial Regulations, etc." with High Chief 

Mauga, the high chief of Pago Pago.

While this treaty was never ratified by the U.S. Senate, it served effectively to prevent other nations from asserting any strong claim to the harbor.

In January of 1878, a further treaty of friendship and commerce was negotiated with the leaders of the villages. adjacent to Pago Pago, and this treaty was ratified later in the same year. It was proclaimed jointly by the United States and "the Government of American Samoan Islands." The treaty remained in force for more than 20 years when it was superseded.

As a result of international rivalry and because of warfare between various factions of the Samoan population, the United States, Germany, and Great

Britain entered into a general act on 

June 14, 1889, to provide for "the security of life, property, and trade of the citizens and subjects of the respective governments who were residing in or having commercial relations with the islands of Samoa." This act also sought "to avoid all occasions of dissensions between their respective governments st.

Under this tripartite agreement, a form of government for the islands was brought into being. However, after a trial of some 10 years, it proved to be ineffective and destructive of the ends for which it was created and was superseded by the Convention of 1899. This treaty among the United States, Germany, and Great Britain had the aim of "advancing amicably the questions which have arisen between the three powers in respect to the Samoan group of islands" and provided that it would supersede and annul all previous treaties between the powers relating to 

Samoa. 

A major provision of the Convention was the renunciation of any claims to the islands of the Samoa group east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich by

Great Britain and Germany. Recipro cally, the United States renounced, in favor of Germany, all rights and claims in respect to the islands of Upolu and 

Savaii and all other islands west of 171° longitude west of Greenwich. This treaty was proclaimed, after ratification on February 16, 1900, and, on 

February 19, President McKinley directed the Secretary of the Navy to "take such steps as might be necessary to establish the authority of the United 

States" in the island of Tutuila and other islands of the Samoan group east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich.

The Secretary of the Navy estab lished a naval station at Pago Pago. 

In April, 1900, deeds of cession were negotiated with the leading chiefs and orators of Tutuila and Aunu’u, and, on 

April 17, the U.S. flag was raised for the first time over these islands. On 

June 14, 1904, High Chief Tuimanu'a, the King of Manu'a, and the chiefs of Manu's agreed to cede their authority to the United States.

The islands remained under naval administration with Pago Pago as an active naval base from 1900 to June 

30, 1951. During this time, considerable progress was made in the establishment of public works and medical and educational facilities. Little, however, was done to disturb the traditional village life of the people.

Under the terms of the deeds of cession, the United States agreed that the chiefs of the villages would be permitted to retain their individual control over their separate villages, provided such control was in accordance with the laws of the United States pertaining to Samoa and provided that such control was not obstructive to the peace of the people and the advancement of civilization. 

The United States also agreed to respect and protect the individual rights of the people, especially in respect to their lands and other property. As a result of this commitment, very few large tracts of Samoan communally-owned land have been alienated during the 65 years during which the United States has had administrative responsibility for these islands. 

Although the deeds of cession were not formally accepted by Congress until 

February 20, 1929, the fundamental personal rights of the inhabitants of

Samoa were protected by the Constitution, and the people of American Samoa were given the status of American nationals. On June 29, 1951, the 

President of the United States, with 

Executive Order 10264, transferred the administration of American Samoa from the Secretary of the Navy to the 

Secretary of the Interior, effective July 1, 1951. 

People: Ethic Composition 

The inhabitants of American Samoa are Polynesians and are closely related to other people of the central and

eastern Pacific. They form a part of the family which includes the Hawaiians, the Tahitians, the Tongans, and the 

Maoris of New Zealand. They are a friendly and generous people, and their culture is rich in ceremony and mythology. The Samoan language is closely related to other eastern Polynesian tongues.

The best evidence available indicates the ancestors of the present Samoans arrived in the islands from a homeland in southeastern Asia between A.D. 

400 and 600. Although the Samoans have had increasingly close contact with European institutions, they remain today an outstanding center of Polynesian culture. After more than 

125 years of contact with Western influences, the fabric of the Samoan social organization remains intact and continues to be the dominant influence in the lives of the Samoan people. 

Samoa's basic economic and political unit is the extended family, composed of related kin tracing their origins bilaterally back through the generations to mythological ancestors. Within their extended group, which is administered by a matai or chief, a collective family economy prevails. The matai is responsible for control of family lands and property and represents the family in the political affairs of village, county, and district councils. 

He is responsible for their welfare and is trustee of family lands and property. 

Religion in American Samoa 

Since the middle of the 19th Century, the people of Samoa have been converts to Christianity. By far the largest single church in the Samoan 

Islands since that time has been the 

London Missionary Society, recently renamed the Christian Congregational Church. In recent years, however, several other denominations have established churches in American Samoa.

The judicial power in American

Samoa is vested in the High Court and in five district courts. The American Samoan Constitution provides that the judicial branch shall be independent of the executive and legislative branches.

The High Court consists of an Ap pellate Division, a Probate Division, and a Trial Division. It has six judges: 

the Chief Justice, the Associate Justice, and four Samoan Associate Judges.

The business and government center of American Samoa is Fagatogo. Executive Office

The Chief Justice or the Associate 

Justice and two Samoan Associate 

Judges are necessary for the hearing and the determination of an appeal.

The Appellate Division has jurisdiction to review on appeal final decisions of the district courts and of the Trial and 

Probate Divisions of the High Court. 

The Trial Division ordinarily con sists of either the Chief Justice or the 

Associate Justice and two Samoan Associate Judges, the Chief Justice or 

Associate Justice and at least one Samoan Associate Judge being necessary to constitute a quorum of the court. 

However, for the trial and determination of a charge of treason, murder, manslaughter, or assault with intent to kill, the Trial Division consists of either the Chief Justice or the Associate Jus tice and four Samoan Associate Judges, two of whom are temporary associate judges assigned by the Chief Justice from a panel of persons so designated by the Governor.

The Probate Division consists of the 

Chief Justice and the Associate Justice, either of whom may hear and determine a probate matter. 

Neither the Chief Justice nor the Associate Justice nor any Associate Judge of the High Court may sit in the Appellate Division of that court in the hearing and determination of any appeal from the decision of a case or question decided or in the decision of which he joined in the trial court.

American Samoa is divided into five judicial districts, each of which has a district court consisting of the Chief 

Justice or the Associate Justice, if present, and the Samoan Associate Judge assigned to it from time to time by the 

Chief Justice. 

The district courts have jurisdiction, concurrently with the High Court, of all civil cases of debt or injury to persons or property in which the amount in dispute is less than $1,000 and which do not involve the title to or rights claimed in land. And the district courts have original, exclusive jurisdiction of all criminal cases in which the maximum penalty which may be imposed. is a fine of not more than $10 or imprisonment for not more than 1 month.

The district courts also have original jurisdiction, concurrently with the

Trial Division of the High Court, of all other criminal cases of misdemeanor.

There is special jurisdiction in Manu'a where the district courts for the fourth and fifth districts have, in addition, jurisdiction in cases of divorce, annulment of marriage, adoption, probate matters, and all criminal cases of felony except treason, murder, manslaughter, and assault with intent to kill.

A Samoan judge, or judges, sits in all cases except cases heard in the Probate

Division of the High Court in which the court is exercising original jurisdiction.

During the fiscal year under review, the courts disposed of 2,622 cases. Of this total, 1,894 were traffic cases, 148 were misdemeanors and 580 were civil cases.

The criminal code was reenacted by the Legislature and approved by the 

Governor in February 1963. 

In accordance with American practice, the American Samoan Code specifically provides every defendant in a criminal case in a court in American

Samoa shall be entitled: 

1. To have, in advance of trial, a copy of the charge upon which he is to be tried. 

2. To consult counsel before trial and have a representative of his own choosing assist him in his defense at the trial. 

3. To apply to the court for further time to prepare his defense, which the court shall grant if it is satisfied that the defendant will otherwise be substantially prejudiced in his defense. 

4. To bring with him to the trial such material witnesses as he may desire or to have them summoned by the court at his request. 

5. To give evidence on his own behalf at his request at the trial, although he may not be compelled to do so. 

The courts dispose of criminal cases as promptly as circumstances permit, due consideration being first given to the defendant's rights under the Constitution of the United States and under the Bill of Rights in the Constitution of American Samoa. 

The Government of American Sa moa provides, at its own expense, a public defender and assistant public. defender to assist persons charged with crime.

Charges of felony are prosecuted by the Attorney General. Should a defendant plead guilty to such a charge, the court does not accept it until the 

Government proves a prima facie case.

This eliminates the remote possibility that the guilty plea may have been made by mistake or through misunderstanding.

If a person pleads guilty to a misdemeanor, the court must be satisfied that the defendant clearly understands the charge and makes his plea voluntarily. Should it appear in any case that there may be a reasonable doubt as to the defendant's guilt, he may withdraw his guilty plea and enter a plea of not guilty in order to avoid a possible miscarriage of justice.

The fundamental human rights of the inhabitants of American Samoa are protected both by the Constitution of the United States and by the Bill of 

Rights in the Constitution of American 

Samoa, the latter being patterned substantially after the Bill of Rights in the U.S. Constitution.

All personnel of the Judicial Branch are American Samoans with the exception of the Chief Justice, the Associate Justice, and the Clerk of the 

High Court, the latter three being stateside American citizens in the 

Federal Civil Service.

Legislative Branch Article II of the Constitution of 

American Samoa provides for the establishment of the legislative branch of the Territory of American Samoa. 

The legislature of American Samoa consists of the Senate and House of 

Representatives. Each of the 14 political counties of American Samoa elects, by Samoan custom, a matai (chief or talking chief) to serve in the

The 17 members of the House of

There is one delegate from Swains Island which the adult permanent residents elect at an open meeting. He has all the privileges of a member of the House except the right to vote.

Business of the two legislative houses is carried out in Samoan and later translated into English.

The legislature meets each year in regular session on the second Monday in March and continues for 30 consecutive calendar days (or less). Special sessions are called by the Governor, but they may not exceed 15 days in any calendar year.

The new Constitution advances the opening date of the annual regular session from March to February. Local Government The Department of Local Govern ment is the link between the Samoan people and the officials of the various departments of the territorial government. The major emphasis in the department has been the development, consistent with traditional policy, of a self-sustaining and self-reliant system of local government, competent to deal with village, county, and district interests and concerns of both traditional and modern nature.

The department acts as an instru ment for the coordination and dissemi- 11

nation of information from departments of the Government to the local political organizations and to assist in effecting departmental programs. It helps villages solve their problems concerning agriculture, water supply, sanitation, public health facilities, schools and roads. The department is also responsible for census-taking in the Territory and for conducting general and special elections.

The Secretary of Samoan Affairs is one of the Territory's leading chiefs and is responsible for the proper administration of the district, county, and village affairs as provided by law and for the supervision of all ceremonial functions. 

The administrative head of each of the three political districts within the

Territory is presently a Samoan District Governor. He is nominated by the district council of chiefs and appointed by the Governor for a 4-year term. The 

District Governor is assisted by a district administrative assistant, who handles reports and acts as district clerk during general meetings of the district. 

Within each district, there are a number of counties. Each of these 14 counties has a county chief, nominated by the chiefs in his county council and appointed by the Governor for a 4year term. Within each county, there are villages and, each year, the village councils, consisting of the village chiefs, nominate one of their members as mayor. His appointment is by the Governor for a 1-year term. All local officials are responsible to their respective district, county, and village councils, which are empowered to recommend laws they consider to be desirable or necessary and to enact regulations in respect to the cleanliness of their villages, the making and clearing of roads, the planting of their lands, and other similar matters.

A Local Government Council com posed of the District Governors and county chiefs was created during fiscal year 1957. This council is gradually assuming the role of policymaking within the Local Government organization. It appoints its own officers who form an executive committee. The council maintains constant liaison with the 

Governor and the Secretary and acts as the coordinating body for all matters pertaining to local government.

Educational Policies and Goals 

The following points summarize the objectives of the American Samoa Department of Education: 

1. A simultaneous upgrading of all school instruction and adult education programs for the purpose of raising educational standards and skills not only for all children in the territory but for all age groups. 

2. Extension of the Samoa-for-Sa moans program to education. This involves the training of new Samoan teachers and the in-service training of Samoans already employed as teachers. 

In fiscal year 1967, Samoans continued to move into areas of increased responsibility. For the first time, a Samoan was appointed principal of one of the new consolidated elementary schools. In the production section, two 

Samoans were appointed to crew chief positions that were formerly held by stateside contract employees. Under the supervision and guidance of stateside producer-directors, all Samoan cameramen began to direct a limited number of instructional and public information productions. And late in the fiscal year Samoan newscasters replaced stateside newscasters on the Englishlanguage television news program, Tala 'Ese 'Ese. 

3. Providing the Samoan student with the intellectual tools he needs to compete and achieve on a level with stateside standards.

To achieve these basic objectives it is necessary that Samoans become truly bilingual, for competence in a world language is a prerequisite for understanding the world. Without a command of the English language, it is 16

impossible for a Samoan to receive a higher education or advance much beyond menial employment. Instruction in English, therefore, is given highest priority at all levels of instruction-but not at the expense of Samoan culture. 

Although all societies undergo change, the Department of Education seeks to preserve the more meaningful characteristics of fa'a-Samoa ("The Samoan way of life") and to prevent the alienation of the young 

Samoan from his own culture. Televised instruction in the 

Samoan language is provided to students in lower levels (on the principle that a youngster must become proficient in his own language before learning another), and a special course describing (in Samoan) the history and customs of Samoa is presented to all students in level 5 (grades 8 and 9). 

The 12-year goal of the Department of Education is to raise the level of stateside-standard educational achievement from less than 1 percent of all

Samoan students in fiscal year 1966 to 50 percent by fiscal year 1978. It is felt that today's elementary students, particularly those currently in levels 1 and 2, will provide the most meaningful and convincing evaluation after they have graduated from high school. 

In the meantime, short-term evaluations are being made. In April 1966, George Pittman (see latter part of "Structure" section, below) tested grades 5 and 6 in two consolidated elementary schools and compared the results to those of the same test given to youngsters in one of the larger village schools, where instructional television had not yet been introduced. The test consisted of 82 items representing basic features of English. Children in the village school scored an average of 33 corrrect responses out of a possible 82; children in the two television schools averaged 63.5 out of a possible 82. ecas The difference in the two tests suggests that the new program is accomplishing its priority goal of teaching 

English as a second language. This supports observations within American

Samoa that Samoan students are speaking much better English in and out of the classroom and that Samoan teachers are speaking more and better

English. There is also some evidence that the evening entertainment and information programs are beginning to similarly affect the amount of English used and understood by adult Samoans. (For other information about evalua tion, see Tests and Measurements sec tion of this chapter.)

Hypothesis: In excess of 60 percent of the ninth grade children read at or above the fourth grade level on a U.S. test with U.S. norms. About 7 percent read above the sixth grade norms, and less than 1 percent are at grade level. 

4. Question: Does this reflect difficulty with reading or difficulty with. English? 

Hypothesis: Both. It appears that

Samoan is read no better than English, if as well.

The Tests and Measurements Division has devised a test of oral English, a sentence-matching test (in Samoan and English), and a reading readiness test that measures English usage independently of maturation. Also, the mathematic fundamentals test has been administered, norms have been aptitude examination and the Gates developed for the high school academic reading survey, and the Ohio State psychological test has been put into ex- 25

perimental use to assist in the selection of scholarship loan recipients (See the 

Scholarship Loan section below). All of these tests are undergoing further evaluation to establish much-needed criteria of academic success. There is a continuing effort to assess those nonacademic variables that contribute to academic success. An interest inventory is also being developed.

There are methodological problems encountered in Samoa which are uncommon in the United States. Perhaps the most frustrating is the difficulty in following a child from one time to the next on the basis of the usual identifying data of name, birthdate, village, school and level or grade. This is a problem if one is interested in test reliability, prediction of success, increments in achievement, learning rates, and the like. Various solutions to this problem have been proposed. The two most reasonable solutions are: (a) go to the schools and have the teachers assist in identification, and (b) assign each student a permanent number when he enters the first grade (e.g. 

Social Security). The first of these is feasible only with limited numbers of children. Thus, the second would probably be most desirable, although it would be difficult to implement.

Scholarship Loan Program 

One of the outstanding features of the Department of Education in recent years has been the Scholarship loan program. This program makes further educational opportunities possible for capable Samoan youths and employees who could not otherwise immediately afford the costs. By providing Samoans with both full-time college work and special short courses, the needs of the government and its allied services for highly qualified personnel will be met.

Scholarship loan students are se- lected on the basis of financial need, test results, high grades, and character. The yearly amount of a loan is determined by the differences between the institutional costs and the amount the family can afford to pay. Loans for college attendance are renewable after the first year if there is evidence of successful completion of a year's work. 

The total amount appropriated for scholarship loans during fiscal year 1967 was $200,000. During this fiscal year 159 Samoans furthered their education under the scholarship loan program: 23 college freshmen, 17 college sophomores, 24 college juniors, 21 college seniors, two graduate students, three students at the South Pacific 

College of Tropical Agriculture, six at the Fiji School of Medicine, 21 at the 

East-West Center in Honolulu, 11 in short courses, and 31 at American 

Samoa's Feleti Teacher Training School. 

During the fiscal year under review, more than $17,500 in financial assistance was given to the Government of American Samoa by various colleges with Samoans in attendance. This money was put toward scholarships for Samoan students entering colleges in September 1967 (fiscal year 1968). 

During fiscal year 1967 policies were formulated concerning the scholarship loan program. A complete audit was also made of all accounts. 

Adult Education 

The primary long-range objectives of adult education in American Samoa include: 

1. Improvement of adult English language skills. 2. Development of informed, responsible citizenship for effective encounter with continuing social change. 

3. Facilitation of individual and group adjustment to increasing impact of 20th century patterns and products. 26

4. Development of vocational skills to fit the territory's resources and economic potential. 

5. Upgrading of individual, family and community standards of nutrition, sanitation and general health. 

6. Decisive retention of the high values in Samoa culture and tradition. 

An adult education supervisor for

Samoa's new educational system was first employed in Fiscal Year 1967. His responsibilities include development of formal adult instructional programs, as well as over-all responsibility for selection, purchase, scheduling, and production of evening television programs the latter, in coordination with the television production division.

Evening and adult television programing saw the following major development in fiscal year 1967: 

1. The inauguration of two-channel evening programing. Such two-channel operation makes possible a greater variety of programing, simultaneous broadcasts of programs designed for different levels of viewer comprehension, and broadcasting of special programs of limited audience appeal without preempting that television viewing time for the entire population. 

2. Increased local production of evening programs. New offerings include a weekly bilingual television news film "roundup" with special background commentary on the week's news (in Samoan), a completely revised weekly agricultural program, an interview series featuring interesting and important visitors to American Samoa, expanded "Reports to the People" of significance, preelection television appearances of all candidates for the legislature, legislative action reports, television introductory appearances for elected members of the legislature, "specials" featuring Polynesian performing arts groups, and speciallyfilmed programs on local celebration and historical events. 

Exclusive of continuing production of spot announcements on safety, public health, community improvement and government information, and exclusive of the nightly, locally-produced newscasts, some 150 programs-each averaging 30 minutes in length-were produced in fiscal year 1967 specifically for general adult evening television. viewing.

Teacher Training 

One of the Department of Education's major goals is to upgrade the teaching effectiveness of the Samoan classroom teacher. Experienced teachers improve themselves on the job, work with stateside principals, and participate in inservice training workshops. 

The primary responsibility for teacher training, however, falls upon the Feleti

Memorial Teacher Training School. 

All students at this two-year school are high school graduates. They must agree to remain in American Samoa and teach school for at least two years after graduation. 

A supervisor and instructors in teacher education are responsible for planning and teaching the training courses in the school. The small Feleti teaching staff is regularly assisted by school principals and studio personnel. 

The training program utilizes the consolidated elementary schools for observation and student teaching. In the course of one year, student teachers are assigned to a minimum of four schools; they work under four different stateside principals and Samoan teachers, observing the problems and procedures that result from differences in school size, staff and location. 

Worthy American Samoan teachers were able to gain invaluable educational experience again in fiscal year 1967 through continued grants from 

Title V of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. Late in the fiscal year (and continuing into fiscal year

Center in Honolulu. The former group also visited parts of California and 

Nevada, and the latter group toured the Hawaiian Islands. Teachers chosen to participate in each trip had demonstrated ability, interest and potential for improvement but had little formal academic background or had never experienced life beyond the shores of 

Samoa. The culture of the United 

States, as seen in California and

Hawaii, made many images in the minds of the Samoan teachers become clearer, more accurate, and more real.

American

The Constitution of Samoa contains a Bill of Rights. The fundamental personal rights of the inhabitants of American Samoa are also protected by the Constitution of the United States. There has been no need for anti-discrimination legislation in American Samoa.

The women of American Samoa today enjoy privileges equal to those of the men. In some cases, they have held matai titles and have represented their districts as members of the House of 

Representatives. 

Medical and dental care are provided free to American Samoans; there is a small charge for hospital care. 

There are no orphanages or old people's homes in American Samoa.

Closely-knit, large family groups careby tradition-for these people. There is one welfare society composed of local women; they are also members of the 

Red Cross unit of American Samoa. 

Juvenile delinquency has not been a serious problem in American Samoa, but government and legislative leaders are concerned about a comparatively significant increase in recent years. This has been caused in part by parental neglect, and by some Samoan youngsters returning to the family-oriented culture of Samoa after living in the more individualistic culture of Hawaii or the mainland. Village chiefs and the government are working closely to provide a child guidance program at home and in the school. Recent efforts in this direction have included the establishment of an athletic program and the growth of 4-H and Boy Scout movements in American Samoa. 

Prostitution is of little consequence in American Samoa; it is unorganized and sporadic.

A number of Samoans continued to receive benefits under the Social Security Act during fiscal year 1967. 

Town and Rural Planning 

Town and rural planning in American Samoa in recent years has largely assumed the position of providing first things first. All of the planning and development, that has existed since 1961 has been designed to make up for the many years of neglect that existed in the territory from 1900 to 1961. Development, in general, has been largely in utilities and necessary buildings (electrification, improved telephone service, new schools, new airport and hotel for the tourist industry, paved roads, improved sanitation standards). 40

The extension of roads and power lines and the construction of schools have been done so as to create the least problems in villages. Consolidated elementary schools have been located and planned as community centers for adult education, village recreation, and community use, although the use of the schools for these purposes has so far existed on an open rather than an organized basis. The school playgrounds provide recreation space and minimum equipment for many villages that previously had none.

Almost all of the planning-urban and rural-in American Samoa emanates from the Governor. One plan, the Fagatogo Redevelopment Plan, calls for a complete renovation of the main part of what outsiders know as

Pago Pago. (The village of Pago Pago actually lies at the end of Pago Pago 

Bay, away from the main business and government area of Fagatogo.) The plan proposes selective demolition to clear certain areas near and on the main dock in order to overcome problems that have hampered port operations, restricted business growth and created automobile bottlenecks.

Work is under way to establish a 

Samoan park system. Although none of the important scenic, recreational, wilderness or historic sites have been threatened as yet, pressure from a growing population, a growing economy and the possibility of diversion of these natural resources for limited gain must be anticipated. The intent is to identify and protect forest reserves, wilderness areas, recreational areas, underwater reef areas and historically important locations and assure their preservation through equitable compensation to the land owners. 41

In guiding American Samoa toward brighter economic horizons, the government is charged with preserving as much of the culture as possible. What does this all mean as far as agriculture is concerned? It means a loss of workers from farms to wagepaying industries; it means fewer farms; it means more mouths to feed with less food; it means a rise in food imports and an undesireable outflow of money to pay for these imports. 

Subsistence farming can no longer meet the demands of a fast-growing urban population. Market situations must be stabilized. 

These, then, are agriculture's most urgent needs: 

1. A massive effort to establish commercial farms. 

2. Land utilization studies to promote the most efficient use of land. 

3. A change in farming techniques to get the most production from what little available farming areas there are. 

4. Farming must be made profitable.

If this can be done, those already in farming will be encouraged to expand their operations; those not engaged in tilling the soil will be encouraged to put their lands into production of foods.

The following are the land utilization percentages of what relatively little land there is in American Samoa: forest, 49 percent; farm land, 28 percent; scrub and grass, 20 percent; village sites and other uses, 3 percent.

The underbrush and vine problem in coconut groves could be combatted by wider use of herbicides. Farmers have shown a reluctance to use chemicals to fight brush. Perhaps a method more likely to win acceptance would be the introduction of beef cattle to graze among the groves. 

The introduction of beef cattle would represent a heretofore untried multiple land-use theory in American 

Samoa. The thick grass and brush would be good forage for the cattle; free of strangling weeds, the trees would be more productive; the cattle would return richness to the soil through their droppings; and, finally, the cattle would represent a local source of beef. All beef consumed in

American Samoa must now be imported. 

Thus far, the Department of Agriculture has been unsuccessful in getting funds for beef cattle to implement the multiple land-use theory. 

Subsistence farmers still persist in the practice of "shifting agriculture," however, there is some movement toward cropping land continuously through 54

the use of fertilizers and other soil additives. 

Government policy on land ownership remains unchanged-that of keeping lands in the hands of the Samoan people. Thus, all laws and regulations are directed toward that end. 

Most types of land tenure are freehold, communal, family and individual. (See chapter I) In the village, land tenure is vested under the matai (chief) who serves as the caretaker of the land for the family.

Soil Fertility 

The islands' lush vegetation is too frequently misconstrued by the layman to mean a fertile soil. It is true that organic matter content is high in virgin soils. But these are quickly depleted by soil microbiological activities, hastened by warm temperatures and moisture. 

Actually, soil mineral content in the tropics is lacking or fixated to be unavailable to plant life. Proof of this is the ready response by plants to fertilizer.

American Samoa's very heavy rainfall-an average of more than 200 inches a year-affects availability of plant nutrients. Leaching of plant nutrients in continually cropped areas has been observed.

Division of Production and Experimental Farms 

Major attention in this division is pointed toward production of vegetables, local food crops, poultry and swine. Within this area, the division has two outstanding thrusts: (1) Development of a strong market situation to the end that imports of food be reduced or curtailed altogether; and 

(2) Introduction of the growing of new fruits as well as varieties of vegetables suited to this area.

Always mindful that the department must not be competitive with private commercial farming, food production is reduced commensurate with in- 55

creased production by American Somoan farmers. Thus, the department seeks only to stabilize the food market to the end that imports be reduced, with the goal to curtain production altogether once private commercial farmers show a capability of producing enough food to meet growing demands.

Total vegetable production for the fiscal year was 191,040 pounds, an increase of only one per cent over the previous year. Although other vegetable production climbed from 125,323 pounds to 144,383 pounds, the production of taro dropped from 52,968 pounds to 46,657 pounds.

Department Policy

The Department of Agriculture strives to greatly improve the quality and quantity of existing crops and introduce new fruits and vegetables compatable with conditions here to feed a fast growing population.

Agriculture must be strengthened through improved techniques of crop production, plant protection, land clearing, processing and marketing.

The department encourages multipleuse of what little arable land exists.

The primary purpose of government farms is experimentation and demonstration. Beyond stabilizing the market to reduce imports, government should yield to commercial farmers consistent with their abilities to produce foods of sufficient quantity and quality. 64

With certain exceptions, the Personnel Office of the Government of American Samoa is responsible for the recruitment of both stateside and local personnel, and for the custody and processing of all personnel records. The policy of the Government of 

American Samoa is to employ persons from outside of American Samoa only for those positions requiring skills and administrative abilities not available in the territory. The goal is to train 

American Samoans to fill these positions as rapidly as possible. 

It is an article of faith among stateside workers that the most conclusive proof of good performance is that they work themselves out of jobs. Most stateside workers come to American Samoa under two-year contracts. They are employed by the Government of 

American Samoa. Only a very few enjoy the benefits of U.S. Federal

Civil Service status. 

Some contract workers renew work agreements for a second 2-year period. A few remain longer. However, turnover among contract workers is relatively high.

During fiscal year 1967 the average number of stateside contract workers employed by the Government of 

American Samoa was about 240. Some of these employees were husband-wife combinations. Although the exact number of palagis (outsiders) has never been counted (only a small number of palagis in American Samoa are not employed by the government), it is estimated that the total palagi population as of the middle of the fiscal year under review was about 700 persons, including children. 

The standard workweek is five 

8-hour days, or 40 hours weekly. 

Work in excess of 48 hours in a week is compensated for at the rate of one and one-half times the basic hourly rate. Contract workers do not receive any overtime pay.

The minimum wage for most localhire government employees is 49 cents an hour. However, starting July 1, 1967, under guidelines established by the U.S. Department of Labor, the minimum wage for local hire employees of the Departments of Education and 

Medical Services was increased to 62 cents an hour. That minimum will increase to 70 cents an hour starting Julv 1, 1968. Unemployment 

Unemployment, as it would be known on the mainland, does not exist in American Samoa. In fact, a labor shortage exists, particularly in agriculture, for the following reasons: Many 65

agricultural workers have abandoned subsistence farms to take better paying jobs in construction or in other government agencies. There is a constant emigration of young persons to Hawaii or to the mainland United States for purposes of obtaining higher education or better jobs.

A massive effort is under way by the 

Department of Agriculture to encourage a "return to the soil" through vocational education, 4-H Club work, and the profitable potential of commercial farming.

Migratory Labor 

The shortage of labor and the dearun of land places American Samoa "on the horns of a dilemma." Many workers from Western Samoa, Tonga and other relatively close South Pacific islands seek to come to American

Samoa for the higher wages and a generally higher standard of living. But only some of these workers are needed. 

Thus, stringent immigration restrictions and medical requirements have been imposed to protect both job opportunities for American Samoans and the limited housing and living space. 

Before a government agency or private industry can hire an outsider, that agency or industry must satisfy the Personnel Office that it has made every effort to first find a qualified American Samoan for the position. 

Equal Employment Opportunity 

An employment policy officer and deputy carry out the government's executive regulation 1.02 of January 26, 1962, which conforms with executive orders 10590 and 10925, relative to the government's employment policy and equal employment opportunities.

The Merit System Law is a part of the Revised Code of American Samoa and is based on the federal plan, although revised to meet local conditions. 

Candidates for employment are interviewed, examined when practicable, classified and their applications placed in an employment register from which positions are filled. 

Positions are subject to field audit and reclassification. Under the Merit 

System Law, employees have the right to appeal all personnel actions to the

Personnel Advisory Board. This board conducts hearings and makes terminal decisions on appeals.

A completely new classification and pay plan for the Government of American Samoa has been signed into law and should be in operation by 

January 1968. 

Travel 

The Personnel Office of the Govern ment of American Samoa, which functioned during the fiscal year under review on a budget of $46,830, also makes all travel arrangements involving government employees.

The Personnel Office assumes the responsibilities of preparing travel authorizations, procurement of reservations and tickets, and scheduling the packing and shipping of personal and household effects of contract employees. 

Social Welfare 

American Samoa continued to feel a favorable effect of the Social Security Act. As of December 1966, a total of 

476 persons received benefits in excess of $12,000 monthly. A considerable number of workers have achieved eligibility for disability benefits.

The Government of American Sa moa is by far the largest employer in the territory, and disability compensation is available to government work- 66

ers. Among certain private industries, such as local fish canneries, shipping and transportation and petroleum marketing, employees are covered by insurance against claims resulting from injury.

During the 1967 session of the American Samoa Legislature, the territory's first workmen's compensation law was passed. When this law (Public Law 

10-15) becomes effective on July 1, 1968, it will require compensation for both medical treatment and loss of salary resulting from accidents on the job. 

As of the close of fiscal year 1967 the Government of American Samoa was considering insurors who might be interested in providing insurance service under this law.

Asphalt Plant.-The operation of the asphalt plant is closely allied with the crusher plant. The same crews op- : erate both. The hurricane housing program necessitated the addition of an additional full-time secretary because of the detailed records that had to be kept.

Funds for the financing of loans for phase II of the hurricane housing program emanated from a $2.5 million grant made by the U.S. Office of Emergency Planning. Loans of up to $2,600 were made available through the Bank of American Samoa from this grant. The loans were extended on two conditions: that part of the money be used to install a septic tank, and minimal toilet facilities, and that the remainder be spent on improvements to the hurricane house itself (e.g. and not an adjacent lean-to or any other structure). These latter improvements had to be approved by the contract administrator before construction began. 

All of the hurricane houses (phase

Laws and Public Safety The Attorney General The Attorney General is the chief legal officer of the government of American Samoa. His duties, however, range considerably beyond those assigned to the Attorney General in most

State governments. He has the traditional responsibilities of advising the 

Governor and other executive departments on legal matters, preparing the Governor's legislative program, and enforcing statutes and regulations. He also represents the government in court on all matters, whether civil or criminal.

He is also responsible for a broad range of other social and legal concerns. He supervises approximately 160 employees, including the Police, Fire, and Immigration Departments, the prison, and major portions of the Civil Defense program. He is chairman of the Immigration Board, chairman of the Commerce Commission, chairman of the Parole Board, chairman of the 

Traffic and Roads Committee, chair man of the Tax Exemption Board, and a board member of the Wage and Hour 

Board, the Maritime Board, the Bank of American Samoa, and a number of other administrative boards and commissions.

Not the least among his major legal advisory responsibilities, the Attorney 

General is legal counsel for the Ameri can Samoan Development Corpora tion and the Bank of American Samoa. 

In fiscal year 1967, he was also legislative counsel.

In addition, because of the lack of trained private attorneys in Samoa, the 

Attorney General's staff is compelled to do a considerable amount of purely private legal work. This work involves the kind usually lumped together elsewhere under the heading, "Legal Aid to Indigents," as well as legal advice for those residents who can afford to pay. A few private stateside attorneys do occasional legal work when they visit American Samoa, but otherwise the only alternative legal advice available. in the islands is from Samoan legal practitioners. These men are not attorneys but simply intelligent men with an aptitude for legal work who try to assist with individual legal problems where they can.

Some legal advice is also given by a special nonlawyer Veterans' Administration representative in the Attorney

General's Office who acts as contact and liaison officer in American Samoa for the Veterans' Administration in discussing legal and personal problems with American Samoan veterans.

States passports were issued; of that number, 57 were renewals, and eight were amended passports. Also during the fiscal year, 64 immigrant visas were issued (a 32 per cent increase over fiscal year 1966), and 334 nonimmigrant visas were issued (a 39 per cent increase over the previous fiscal year).

All of these visas were issued to aliens for travel to the United States.

Although the United States Immigration and Nationality Act generally does apply in American Samoa, the Territory has its own immigration laws. These laws are administered by the Immigration Branch, which is under the supervision of an Immigration 

Officer, who is a Samoan.

The control over the entry of aliens and nonresidents into American Samoa is the responsibility of the Immi gration Branch. Immigration inspec tors meet each vessel and airplane arriving in American Samoa to inspect passports or travel documents of arriv ing passengers. The Immigration Law authorizes the Governor and the Attorney General to grant entry permits. 

Visitors are authorized to enter American Samoa without an entry permit and remain for less than 30 days provided they are in possession of a prepaid ticket for transportation out of American Samoa. Records of the arrival and departure of all passengers are kept by the Immigration branch. 

Arrival and departure forms are completed my passengers aboard ships and airplanes prior to their arrival in American Samoa and are collected by immigration inspectors. Information such as each passenger's nationality, last portof-call, ultimate destination, purpose of visit, etc., is obtained from the forms. 

The use of the arrival and departure form is essential for the control and documentation of aliens. The form also provides information that is useful in the development of American Samoa's tourist industry.

Because they are born in an outlying possession of the United States, American Samoans are U.S. nationals but not 

U.S. citizens; this is defined in Section 

1408, Title 8, of the United States 

Code. Accordingly, upon entering the 

United States, American Samoans must establish their identity and nationality. This is accomplished by means of a Certificate of Identity obtained from the Immigration branch and signed by the Attorney General.

The issuance of the Certificates of Identity has the approval of the United 

States Department of State and the Immigration and Naturalization Serv ice. During fiscal year 1967, 1,447 Certificates of Identity were issued—a 30 percent increase over the previous fiscal year.

Staff During fiscal year 1967 the Attorney General's staff consisted of the attorney general, an assistant attorney general (a stateside attorney), and an American Samoan prosecutor with four years of formal legal training. The workload and responsibilities of the Attorney General's Office have increased so much over the years, however, that two additional stateside attorneys were hired as Assistant Attorney Generals shortly after the end of the fiscal year. 

Police and Fire Protection The Public Safety Division is under the immediate supervision of a Com missioner of Public Safety and the gen eral supervision of the Attorney General. The primary function of the 92

Commissioner of Public Safety is to train and supervise the staff of Police 

Department and Fire Department. He supervises and reviews all cases under investigation and forwards his reports to the Attorney General prior to filing cases in court for prosecution. He also acts as the Safety Officer for the Gov ernment of American Samoa, establishes safety programs and procedures, and ensures their enforcement.

The Commissioner of Public Safety also supervises the prison and the entire program of rehabilitation of prisoners. 

The prison is a subdivision of the = Police Department. 

American Samoa's Police Department consists of six divisions: Patrol, =Traffic, Service, Detective, Prison and 

Administration. Each division is supervised by a superior officer. The Chief of 

Police has overall responsibility of all divisions. He reports directly to the 

Commissioner of Public Safety. 

Two major administrative changes. were made in the Police Department during fiscal year 1967: (1) the Service 

Division was established to include the Records Division and technical services of the department; and (2) the

Detective Division was reorganized to include the old Investigation Bureau and the Juvenile Bureau. These changes were considered necessary to effectively utilize manpower since the department was operating at one-third below its authorized strength due to the inability to hire adequate personnel at the established, inadequate salary level.

For the first time in the history of the territory, there was a walk-out by a dissident group of about 60 policemen (July 1966). The walk-out was the result of several complaints, mainly low salaries. Thanks to an abundance of volunteers, the Police 

Department was able to continue its services, although many of the striking policemen were later dismissed or otherwise did not return, and the deficit in personnel caused some handicap to the department for the remainder of the fiscal year. During the fiscal year 

33 policemen resigned, 29 were discharged, five were transferred, and 

Chief of Police Tuia'ana T. Letuli died. The total police turnover for the year was 68 persons-an extremely high rate for an authorized complement of 105 police personnel. Disciplinary action involving 557 days of suspension was taken against 62 men during the fiscal year. 

Training. In-service training for police personnel continued throughout the fiscal year under the supervision of the Plans and Training section. Class room instruction and on-the-job training encompassed nearly 200 hours. In 

February 1967, three members of the department were sent to Honolulu to attend the Honolulu Police Academy. 

These officers received 14 weeks of instruction in basic police work. Upon their return they were able to pass on their experiences to other policemen through special classes set up for discussions. 

In April 1967, two other members of the department were sent to Maui, Hawaii, to attend a two-week advanced fingerprint classification course conducted by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. 

A great need for advanced supervisory and command level training still exists, and planning is proceeding towards attaining funds. for such training. 

Problem Areas. Rather than sum marize in detail the operations of each of the six divisions during the past fiscal year, a short statement of some of the special problems encountered may be more helpful. A detailed statistical analysis has been included in Appendix VIII to show the more important statistical figures pertinent to police operations. 93

One of the continuing problems is the steady increase in the number of automobiles being brought into American Samoa. The number increased in fiscal year 1967 to 1,363, a 17.5 percent increase over the previous fiscal year, which itself showed an increase of 18 percent over the year before that. The number of drivers increased by 17 percent in fiscal year 1967, doubling the rate of increase of fiscal year 1966.

A considerable traffic problem has resulted. Injury accidents have increased approximately 88 percent (although fatalities have decreased). Concentrated efforts have been made to utilize the available police personnel for effective traffic control. The problem has been complicated because of a number of accidents involving police vehicles. At the end of the fiscal year, only two police vehicles were available for patrol work-one hardly operable-which is totally insufficient for adequate police protection. 

With the increase in traffic, there has been an increase in traffic cases prosecuted. The 1,732 traffic cases prosecuted in fiscal year 1966—a record year was surpassed by the 1,936 prosecuted in fiscal year 1967. It should be noted that the rate of convictions was remarkably increased because of more careful preparation of cases and better training of officers in proper complaint and trial procedures.

Apart from traffic matters, criminal prosecutions generally increased, thrusting a greater workload on the detectives who were investigating the cases and testifying in court, not to mention the additional workload placed on the Attorney General's immediate staff. Out of 1,074 investigations of misdemeanor and felony cases, 210 criminal prosecutions resulted-an increase of 86 percent over fiscal year 

1966. Again, the proportion of convictions increased dramatically because of better training. It is also clear that crime is being better reported to police, better reported in the territory's mass media, better recorded in police records, and better prosecuted than ever before. 

Due to the excellent work of the

Commissioner of Public Safety Dewey 

Allen, who completed his second year in American Samoa during fiscal year 

1967, the Police Department is headed in the right direction. It is visibly improved, and plans have been made which should assist in even greater improvement. There are obviously areas that still need attention, however, and an increase in police salaries at every level would improve conditions immensely. 

An increase in force would be possible if the existing beginning wage rate of $1,127 per annum was raised somewhere near the minimum wage set by the U.S. Department of Labor for pri vate industries in American Samoa. A minimum wage of $1 an hour results in an annual salary of $2,118 per annum. Men who can earn this in industry are not likely to work for the Police 

Department at just more than half that amount. Steps have been taken to obtain additional money for police salaries, and it is probable that after a revision of the merit system set as a first-priority item, police salaries will be increased substantially. The force will then be increased in size, and some of the existing difficulties will be alleviated.

Juvenile Division. The Juvenile 

Division consists of a supervisor and two officers. Less than 40 percent of the reported felonies and less than 10 percent of the reported misdemeanors in fiscal year 1967 were by juveniles. 

Compared to the U.S. juvenile crime rates, these percentages are very low.

The Juvenile Division works with school counselors and the parents of juvenile offenders. Since there is no full-time probation system in American 94

Samoa, this program of active counseling is utilized as a means of helping to adjust juvenile offenders. A great portion of juvenile control is still vested within the pulenu'u (mayor) of each village and the juveniles' parents.

The Police Department has con tinued its sponsorship of a Boy Scout troop. It has become the function of the Juvenile Division to plan and manage devolopment programs for juveniles. 

Prison.-American Samoa's prison is administered by the Police Department under the direction of an assigned police lieutenant. The security of the prison is maintained by police officers in various watches. The total number of prisoners during the fiscal year was 

289, an increase from 206 in fiscal year 

1966. This number fluctuates monthly. 

Prisoners have been used to supplement Government services in general clean-up and assistance during emergencies when labor cannot be obtained immediately. 

Discussions of American Samoa's budget for fiscal year 1969 included consideration for planning a new prison for the territory.

Fire Prevention and Fire Fighting. 

The Fire Department of American Sa moa has the responsibility of fire prevention and fire fighting throughout 

Tutuila, and to some extent in Manu’a. 

Prevention activity includes inspection of fire prevention facilities at schools and government sites.

The Fire Department is composed of 

28 men, including a fire chief and an assistant fire chief. One fire unit is located in the Pago Pago area, and the other is in Tafuna, near to the airport. 

Personnel turnover in the Fire Department is very low. 

Training in the Fire Department has been a regular program, with the fire chief and the assistant fire chief directing this activity. The training consists of simulated fire-fighting experience and classroom instruction. 

During the fiscal year 1967, 48 fires were reported. One major fire was reported in Faga'alu, but there was a delay in reporting and the house was destroyed. The majority of the fires reported were electrical pole fires. The total estimated property loss for the fiscal year was about $10,276.

The Tafuna Fire Station is responsi ble for providing fire protection during landings, take-offs, and refueling of aircraft at Pago Pago International 

Airport. The total flights in the entire fiscal year were 2,829 (including landings and take-offs), requiring an average stand-by time of two hours for each flight. This is a 57 percent increase over last fiscal year. There is every indication that air traffic at the airport will increase during the next fiscal year. Fire equipment at the airport is poor, and replacement is needed as soon as possible. (See end of chapter

V.) 

There were some rescue runs during fiscal year 1967, but the drowning cases were reported too late and the rescue truck was unable to help. 95

chapter 11 Communications 

The Communications Office of the 

Government of American Samoa serves two major functions. It provides local and internal radio-communications service. It also provides telecommunications service to points overseas. In the latter function, communications is American Samoa's major link to the outside world.

These services are achieved through government and commercial telephone and telegraph facilities, and supplementing services. Communications functioned on a budget of $167,982 during fiscal year 1967, which again saw a steady rise in overseas telephone and radiogram traffic.

Overseas Telephone Services 

Through three principal systems, overseas telephone service is available to about 50 countries. The potential exists wherein service to virtually any country can be added as the need arises.

During the fiscal year under review, traffic showed a 30 percent gain over the previous year, with a total of 8,660 calls. This source provided a gross revenue of $57,713.

Two of the telephone circuits provide service within the South Pacific area. The primary service, however, connects American Samoa with the 

United States and most of the 50 countries with whom communications are maintained.

System "A".-This is the primary service, connecting American Samoa via the Oakland, California, gateway to all states of the United States, Puerto 

Rico, Virgin Islands, Guam, Wake and 

Midway Islands, Tahiti and about 50 countries. This service is open from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. Monday through Friday, and 8 a.m. until noon on Saturdays, Sundays and holidays.

Very shortly, this service will be supplemented by a direct service to Hawaii, with each of the primary circuits serving as alternate to the others to provide maximum continuity.

Ultimately, these will be converted to a 

24-hour basis of operation. 

System "B". This service connects

American Samoa with Western Samoa, an independent state about 80 miles to the west. Traffic continues to rise on this vital lifeline between the two Samoas. It serves business and governmental interests and maintains family ties between those in Pago Pago and Apia. This service operates with similar hours to system "A", except that it opens one hour later. 

System "C". This service links

American Samoa with other islands of the South Pacific. Met daily, Monday through Thursday, are Nuku'alofa, capital of Tonga, and Suva, capital of 

Fiji. These two points are served for one hour daily, although Suva may be reached outside this period via system "A". The reason for the Monday- 96

​These overseas operators maintain telephone service to about 50 countries. through-Thursday schedule is that

Tonga and Fiji are on the other side of the International Dateline. Thus,

Samoa's Friday is their Saturday, when most offices would be closed. The little island of Niue is served on demand. 

However, traffic to that island is so light that advance notice is required. 

System "D".-A new coastal telephone service provides communications with ships. It utilizes the same equipment as system "C", but on different frequencies. Separate equipment will avoid conflict with existing schedules.

Overseas Telegraph Services 

Revenue-producing radiogram traffic for the fiscal year in review increased 10 percent over the previous fiscal year. A revenue increase of 17 percent resulted in total income of $69,843. The relatively sharp increase in revenue could be attributed largely to the increase in Telex traffic. 

Telex calls, alone, increased 27 percent, and 'the number of minutes for these calls increased by 350 percent. 

Responsible for most of this increase were the fish canneries and representatives of the fishing fleets. 

This is the second full year of Telex service, and its usage is now expected to level off. Telex service is potentially available to more than 190,000 subscribers in about 150 countries. 

Telex service serves such business firms as banks, airlines, tours, the canneries and wholesalers. It provides direct teletype contact to distant offices, with written records of the rapidlytransmitted data for both sender and receiver.

A public booth at the Communications Office is provided for press representatives and businessmen who wish to make contact with their home offices. 97

The following systems apply to overseas telegraph (radiogram) service available in American Samoa: 

System "A".-This principal radiogram service connects American

Samoa's full duplex radioteletype with most countries of the world, via Honolulu, on a 24-hour basis. International

Telex, which serves the general public, also operates over this system. 

System "B".-This 24-hour duplex radioteletype service, via Honolulu, is used for transmission and reception of official government dispatches of the Government of American Samoa and agencies of the U.S. Government, including the military and Coast Guard. 

System "C". This secondary service connects American Samoa with other 

Pacific areas via Suva, Fiji. This full duplex radioteletype service is used to handle government and public traffic for Fiji, Australia, New Zealand, Rarotonga, Tonga, New Caledonia and the 

Gilbert Islands, among other places.

This service operates on six schedules per day, but will probably become a 

24-hour service within the next year. 

System "D".-This full duplex radioteletype service operates during daylight hours only. It connects American

Samoa and Western Samoa through an assistance program. This service has a 

Morse Code backup, and is our only remaining Morse circuit, with the exception of communications with ships at sea. The latter will remain Morse for the foreseeable future.

Press Services.-American Samoa is immediately informed of current world events through a 24-hour commercial press subscription service from United 

Press International. A continuous flow of news is received through teletype machines in the Office of Samoan Information and at radio station WVUV.

The Office of Samoan Information edits this news for use in the News Bulletin, published Monday through Friday, and for radio and television newscasts.

Teletype service links American Samoa with many distant sources where radio contact cannot be realiably maintained. 

A teletype machine at the Pago Pago 

Intercontinental Hotel provides the latest news to its guests. Other subscribers will be added as the demand develops. 

The press copy originates principally in New York City, with Hawaii news. highlights being added from time to time during the day. Press bulletins of national events frequently arrive in advance of official dispatches that are filed with operational priority. are Teletype news copy is supplemented by UPI radiophotos, which forwarded to the Office of Samoan Information for use with television newscasts or filed for later use with Educational Television System programs. 

Reception of radiophotos is direct. from San Francisco. Night copy has been plagued with multipath effects, resulting in blurred images. Remote control receivers are scheduled to be installed at the Federal Aviation

Agency site in Tafuna during fiscal 98

year 1968. These will utilize a rhombic antenna, beamed on San Francisco, which should improve the quality of radiophoto reception. 

Communications maintains a coastal telegraph service with some 20 ships a month visiting the port of Pago Pago.

These ships can be reached over a much wider area through a mutualclearance program established with the cooperation of Suva radio. 

Stations in Hawaii and California. also assist with clearance of messages to and from ships in the northern Pacific, though additional charges are applicable. Remote transmitters, expected to be operational by Christmas 1967, will greatly extend the direct range of communications radio from 

Pago Pago. 

During fiscal year 1967 American 

Samoa participated in the U.S. Coast 

Guard's automated merchant vessel reporting program. Through this program, position reports from ships are gathered by coastal telegraph stations and forwarded to the New York City computer center of the Coast Guard.

This information is forwarded to flight service stations of the Federal Aviation Agency and to pilot-briefing stations in other countries. The captain of a departing overseas flight thus has in condensed form the location of merchant ships nearest the trackline of his proposed flight. In the event of an emergency, the pilot would know well in advance which direction to take that he might "ditch" close to a ship. 

Coastal telephone service between

American Samoa and ships at sea was undertaken during fiscal year 1967. 

However, shipboard limitations apparently preclude perfect communications for some time. The best ships are not equipped for single-sideband, though coastal telephone stations in the United

States have had this for a long time. 

The capability and desirability of providing good telephone communications to ships cannot be minimized.

Miscellaneous Local Services 

A single-sideband radio network serves the principal villages of such outlying American Samoa islands as 

Ofu, Ta'u, and Olosega of the Manu'a group, and Swains Island. 

Because of a considerable increase in traffic during the past fiscal year, a second network was set up to service educational television schools in these areas, which are not yet reachable by means of VHF. These single-sideband installations have special transistorized equipment. Daytime power only is developed to service the ETV schools.

Thus, at night and during power failure, the battery-operated SSB radios can function for extended periods. 

Lightweight, portable power plants re-charge the 12-volt storage batteries from which the SSB radios operate.

Should even these portable power suppliers fail, SSB can operate daily for almost a month without need for battery re-charging. These outer-island stations have what amounts to an extended-area telephone service through a "phone patch" mode of operations.

This is an important morale factor to people isolated on these islands. A few such stations have been installed on the main island of Tutuila in remote areas where regular telephone service is not yet available. These are being phased out as the telephone cable is extended. 

The past fiscal year marked the first full year of operation of the very high frequency (VHF) network installed and maintained for ETV use. About 15 of the ETV schools have direct network communications, via a repeater, to the ETV control center in Utulei. 

Although this service is primarily a technical function, it is also a morale. factor in that teachers in isolated villages have contact with "civilization" at all times. 99

A contact point was added at the end of the year to the Hospital of American Samoa. This makes possible emergency assistance in health matters, day or night, without the need to relay information. This is a source of reassurance to people in the outlying areas. 

Not all villages can be reached by VHF because of terrain and other factors. Thus a single-sideband has also been installed at the hospital, making it possible for those villages to call for immediate medical assistance. 

Three VHF systems are operating satisfactorily despite the fact that the rugged terrain is not conductive to that type system. All three were engineered and are maintained by the Communi cations Office. 

One VHF system is the police/fire system, serving a fleet of a dozen vehicles. Because a relatively few a relatively few vehicles are involved (and a fire will involve both the fire and police departments), both operate on the same frequency. One repeater station is used in the system. This enables all units to be aware of all calls, regardless of the location of any vehicle. A second repeater, in the planning stage, will extend coverage to the west end of Tutuila.

A mobile system has been installed in all patrol, crash and fire vehicles at

Pago Pago International Airport. A 

second channel is available on this system to enable contact with the FAA

Flight Service Station. Scheduled for fiscal 1969 is the extension of the year police, fire and VHF systems to Manu'a through an additional inter-island repeater site at Mt. Olotele.

Starting with ETV, a gradual transition to an all-VHF system is planned. 

This will eliminate the night-time fadeouts now experienced by the high frequencies on the single-sideband. 

Additional equipment at the repeater site will permit transition from the present phone-patch type telephone service to a VHF system operating through a normal switchboard operation. 

At the outset, manual operation is planned for the telephone service.

Conversion to a dial operation can be made later by utilizing equipment designed for mobile radiotelephone dial systems. Unless a greatly accelerated development program is undertaken, the latter need is some years away. 

A local search and rescue teletype loop connects the Communications Of fice with the Coast Guard, port director, and the Federal Aviation Agency

Flight Service Station. It is a valuable device to coordinate search and rescue missions involving either vessels or aircraft. In a couple of critical situations involving vessels plying between American Samoa and Western Samoa, communications played an important role because it provides the only means of communication with Apira in Western 

Samoa. Whenever the Coast Guard cutter is at sea, Pago radio maintains contact with it. (Pago radio and communications mean one and the same thing.)

The special visit by President John son in February resulted in a flurry of activity at communications. Special press circuits and teletype machines were set up at the airport terminal, along with a “hot line” telephone for long distance calls and coordination with communications downtown.

The quality of communications services to the government and to the public has been considerably upgraded over recent years. This improvement has been achieved expeditiously and economically. However, a greater source of pride is the realization that these gains have been made with virtually all-Samoan personnel. Of the 29 100

employees in Communications, all except the communications officer and one other person are Samoans. 

Despite lack of direct contact with the outside world and opportunity to observe first-hand the operations of

Communications on the mainland, the Samoans have shown remarkable aptitude in learning and performing these difficult skills. Their achievements are a source of great satisfaction to all concerned. 101

Legislative Branch 

In accordance with the Constitution of American Samoa the Legislature shall consist of a Senate and a House of Representatives. At the present time 15 Senators are elected from the several counties in accordance with Samoan custom through county councils and 17 Congressmen are elected by popular franchise from the several counties on the basis of population ratio. There is a regular session each year beginning on March for not more than 30 consecutive days. The Legislature may meet in special session during any calendar year not to exceed 15 days. Members of the Legislature are paid an annual salary of $300 plus transportation.
