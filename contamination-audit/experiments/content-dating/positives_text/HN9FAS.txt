So far as possible all rules have been put in the form of tables in large, easily read type, so that a flick of a tab and a rapid glance will glean the necessary information. Easily understood examples have also been added.

The idea of "Contract Bridge at 

Sight" was evolved from experience gained from teaching and lecturing on the game since the introduction of modern contract in 1924. Aside from

text books, the author has always used printed sheets on which he formulated what he believed to be the most necessary rules and aids to contract bidding. These sheets were placed by the pupils on the corners of the bridge table during classes and lectures for ready reference. The author was somewhat surprised to find that in spite of the fact that a large sheet of paper, flapping about the table, was a great nuisance, the sheets were highly valued by their owners and the requests for copies became legion. Moreover the sheets kept popping up all over-in the hands of people who had never attended the aforesaid classes or lectures. For some time this remained a mystery. Then the dark facts were uncovered. Several enthusiasts had hundreds of the sheets multigraphed, and mailed them around to friends. Some of the sheets were Africa. actually reported in China, Sweden, Italy, and South After the international wandering of the innocent fragile sheets the need and desire of a quick sight aid, compact and complete (which the sheets were), durable, small and readily understood seemed most apparent.

After round 1, partnership has determined spades as final declaration.

Round 2-Declarer shows he can take the first trick in clubs (i.e. that he holds ace of clubs or no clubs and can trump) and partner shows ability to take the first trick in hearts. 

He also denies the ability to take the first trick in dia-. monds or he would have bid the diamonds first, it being the lower of the two. If in round two partner had been unable to take the first trick in either diamonds or hearts he would have gone back to the determined declaration by bidding five spades.

Round 3-Declarer having found the heart ace (or void hearts) with partner, and also knowing that partner is unable to take the first diamond trick, has all possible information and can decide whether to bid five, six or seven spades.

4 hearts

(2) "I also have sufficient strength to raise your two-spade bid to four" (since declarer must bid four spades to take out the four heart bid) and (3)-"I believe that there is a probability of a slam in our combined hands.'

Rank of Bids 

A bid of a greater number of tricks in any declaration overcalls a bid of a lesser number of tricks in any other declaration. When two bids are of the same number they rank in the following order: No Trump highest, Spades next, then Hearts, Diamonds, and Clubs. Vulnerable 

When one side wins a-game in a rubber it becomes "vulnerable." Until a side has won a 

game it is "not vulnerable.' 

Doubling and Redoubling 

Does not affect the premiums for rubbers, slams and honors nor the revoke honor-score penalty. Revoke

The revoke penalty for either side is the loss of two tricks for any player's first revoke. For each additional revoke, 100 points is scored in the opponents' honor-score. Tricks taken or lost for a revoke count just as if taken or lost in play.

Game A trick-score of 100 (or more) points is required for game. Only the number of tricks bid as well as made are scored in the trickscore. All tricks made above the number bid are considered "extra" tricks and are counted only in the honor score.
