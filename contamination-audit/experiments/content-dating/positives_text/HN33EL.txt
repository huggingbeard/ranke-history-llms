LIBRARY The Associated Charities of Worcester “An Organization for Family Service"

ALMOST from its beginning, now forty-six years ago, the Associated Charities of Worcester was privileged to have the services of Miss Miriam F. Witherspoon. By her training, tact, vision and growing experience, she came to be recognized as a pioneer in the field of family welfare work and here in Worcester she gained the respect and love of many to whom she was the Associated Charities. 

Upon her retirement from active duties in 1929, Miss Witherspoon undertook a study and review of the changes and development in family welfare work and problems during the period that she had been identified with them. It is with much pride not only in the manner of its presentation, but also in the character of the work which it presents that the Directors of the Associated Charities have caused its publication. 

As life in Worcester has grown more complex during the past half century, family problems and the facilities for meeting them have kept pace. Many Worcester people have left bequests which have built up the endowment of the Associated Charities to $119,425.00. The income from this endowment has supplemented the support given by the Community Chest and we hope to merit a further increase in endowment in the future. 

Many as the changes have been, there still remains as the fundamental of our work, the understanding, courage and faith which 

Miss Witherspoon brought to it in 1894.

December 1, 1936 

GEORGE AVERY WHITE, President

Introduction 

PLANNING to write this inadequate sketch of the history of the Associated Charities of Worcester, I intended at first to confine it to the thirty-five years during which I was general secretary, 1894-1929; to make it, in fact, largely a record of personal reminiscences. But when I began the actual work, I found that to understand my first years in Worcester, it would be necessary to begin at the beginning of the organization and include the five years preceding my coming. This story, then, covers the first forty years of the work of the Associated Charities of Worcester. 

Although the office was not opened until the fall of 1890, the preceding year was one of such careful study, consultation, and planning for organization on the part of a group of socially minded men and women that the record of it could not be omitted. 

The first general secretary, Mrs. Eliza J. Lee, died in Providence in 

1920, so that, unfortunately, I have not had her memory of those early years to help me with her personal reminiscences, including her experiences in handling the destitution caused by the panic of 1893-4, which I am sure would have been interesting. For those first years, I have had to depend almost entirely on the records of the Board of Directors, the annual reports of the society, and the daily papers of that period. 

One of the sad things in writing this history has been realizing more forcibly than I had done before, how many of our pioneers in social work, both in our own society and in the other societies with whom we had such fine co-operation, men and women who were my good friends and advisers, have passed on. But forty years is a long time. Although I began

this story with an account of the years preceding my coming to Worcester, I have closed it on the day of my retirement, September 30, 1929. Perhaps someone, some time, will think it worth while to take up the story at this point and go on with it. Such a narrative would, I am sure, prove most interesting and informing, the record of family work covering so long a period of business depression, the worst this country, or, indeed, the whole world, has ever seen. Moreover, the records now being kept are so much more complete than our early records were that adequate data will be at hand from which to analyze both successes and failures. 

For several years, I have longed for time to study our old records. I wanted to know what had become of the families in whom I had been interested during my first years in Worcester. And I hoped to obtain data from which valuable deductions could be drawn. When I retired from active work, I looked forward with much eagerness to the fulfillment of this desire. My scheme was to go over our old records, to study them, to follow up the families through our own records, through those of other societies, including the Department of Public Welfare, and to call on such families as I could find. I wanted to learn with what families we had succeeded in our constructive work, with what families we had failed, and why. I wanted to learn whether the present family work is a development out of what the pioneers did, or whether it is based on a new philosophy. I hoped to prove that the work which we did in the old days before the technic of family work had developed as it has now, was in itself worth while, as distinguished from the mere relief giving which had preceded it. Of course, I believed that we early workers had laid some good foundations; but I wanted to prove it, and to do so honestly, facing facts for and against, as I found them.

So I started in with much enthusiasm. I read many records, analyzed them, looked up the families through the Social Service Exchange and the

Department of Public Welfare, and I made calls. Little by little, for I was unwilling to relinquish the task which I had set for myself, I became convinced that I was not going to obtain sufficient material from which to make any worth-while generalizations. And, as I said, I wanted the study to be entirely honest. I did not want to write a pretty essay on our successes, although I could have done it. I could have told stories of the families on whom I called which would have been interesting. I know personally many families, who twenty-five or thirty years ago were in dire distress and are now doing well. Their children, now men and women, with families of their own, are prosperous and happy, with all that tragic past forgotten. In one case I found that the grandson of the man and woman whom I had known was in college; perhaps there are others. As I talked with this grandmother, I thought of what a splendid struggle she had made against utter destitution, caused by accident and sickness. But we did not mention that. We talked about how fortunate she had been in her second marriage, what a pretty home she had, and how well the children were doing. I wonder if the Associated Charities can not take some credit. 

At least the little addition which we made to the small income which she and her sick husband could earn kept the family together without the stigma of pauperism.

I asked another woman how the children were doing, and she answered with great pride, "Why, you know one of them is in a bank, and holding a good position in it." I thought of the wretched little attic tenement in which she had tried to make a home for her children. I meet her often on the street, and we exchange information as to where the special bargains are to be had, and we tell each other what we have been buying. 

There was another family in which the older children were begging from door to door. Now all those days are forgotten, and the children are in good situations. And still another family which I found cold and hungry

in one of the worst tenements in Worcester-today, the mother, a woman of unusually fine qualities, is one of my best friends. And these are not by any means the only cases I could cite; there are many of them. But when I took a group of records as they came, not selecting them, studying methods and results, and drawing inferences from them, I was convinced that my plans could not be carried out. I spent much time on the study before I was willing to come to this conclusion. But I had to do it. 

There were two definite reasons for this: First, many of the families, a large proportion of them, had disappeared in the course of the thirty or more years. Perhaps they succeeded in rehabilitating themselves; perhaps they went on to more abject failure. Perhaps our failure to understand contributed to the failures of others. Perhaps, too, it was our relief giving which was the cause of success or failure. Some unwise aid may have sapped their independence and left them permanently dependent. On the other hand, aid given at the right time and in a friendly spirit may have so encouraged them, the thought that they had friends to whom they could turn in an emergency, may have so lifted the burden of care and anxiety, that their own courage was renewed to keep on with the struggle. These suppositions could not be proved without personal contacts with the families, and that, as I have said, was not possible in a sufficient number of cases.

Second, the records for that period were inadequate for any real study. 

We were even then beginning to appreciate the value of records and statistics. But we had no stenographer, no typewriter, and no telephone. 

The first year I was

I was here we had 575 cases, the next year 512, the third 674, the fourth 651, and the fifth, the last of the period I had hoped to study, we had 558. And we had two workers, Miss Stiles, who was an excellent clerk, and myself as General Secretary. With these facts before us, I make no apologies for poor records. They were simply an artificial memory, 10

carrying the facts which we needed to know, but could not possibly remember for so large a clientele. In saying this I do not mean to imply that conditions in Worcester were any worse than those with which these new and untried societies for organizing charity, with but meager financial resources, had to contend all over the country. But there is a lurking feeling of pride in my inner consciousness that with such handicaps, the social workers of that day did succeed in so commanding the confidence and respect of the public that the present development in family social work has been possible.

But I wanted to deal with facts which could be proved, not what we hoped or believed were the facts. When I found that this could not be done, I reluctantly gave up the project on which I had set my heart. So I closed the book. Sometime, perhaps, I may go back to it from a different point of view and succeed.

The last time I saw Doctor Nichols, at a small committee meeting, he expressed the wish that I would write a history of the Associated Charities of Worcester; and Mr. White suggested that it should bring out the changed and changing standards in social work. I had had it in mind to do this later on. But with old memories awakened by reading the records and reports of my early years in Worcester, and knowing that the material which I had collected would be valuable in such a history, I have written this short review of the forty years from the beginning of the association in the fall of 1889, to my retirement in September 1929. It has been a slower process than I anticipated. I have read all our annual reports, some of them several times, and have gone through the files of the daily papers at the Public Library, to see what the attitude of the public was towards the new organization. I tried to follow the changes towards organized social work; and in our own reports, the changes which have come about in our attitude towards our clients. It is interesting to watch 11

the growth of feeling of responsibility to our clients, as well as our responsibility to the public, both those who were our friends, supporting us with encouraging words, with volunteer help and with financial aid, and also to those who, not understanding, criticised us. 

But I really think that I would not have had the courage to undertake the task, after reading Doctor Nichols' report of our first twenty years of service, and also the address which he gave at our annual meeting, five years later, if he himself had not expressed the desire that I should do so. 

And So, such as it is, I present to our officers, our Board of Directors, and to our many friends, this story of the first forty years of the work of the Associated Charities of Worcester. I hope it will prove as interesting to you who read it as it has been to me in preparing it.

Forty Years of Family Service WORCESTER IN 1889 

IN 1889, Worcester was a city of approximately 70,000 inhabitants, estimating from the official census of 1885, big enough to have many of the social problems of the larger cities without the facilities to meet them. 

It was already a cosmopolitan city, having among its groups of foreignborn people, English, Swedes, Irish, Italians, and French Canadians, with smaller colonies of less known nationalities. It was estimated that over one-fourth of the population was foreign born. Of the ninety-two churches and missions, nineteen ministered to foreign-speaking parishes. 

Doubtless all these churches were doing good work in meeting the needs of their own congregations. But there was besides the church people, a large body of the unchurched, negligent of all religious duties. Naturally, in this group were many of our destitute, our delinquents, and often our defectives.

To meet the needs of these people, there were in Worcester in 1889 ten social service organizations, including the Home for Aged Men and the Home for Aged Women. But the general relief source was the Overseers of the Poor, now known as the Department of Public Welfare. The aid given was small, and there was no attempt at social work. Indeed, family social work was at that time almost unknown to the majority of our citizens.

It was under these circumstances that in 1889 a group of socially minded people, consisting largely of clergymen, began to think and to discuss the advisability of forming such an organization as the Associated Charities. Doctor Nichols tells us in his address, given at our twentieth annual meeting, that Rev. W. H. Thomas, pastor at that time of Trinity 13

Methodist Church, was the first to suggest publicly the need of some co-ordinating agency in social work such as he had known in the Associated Charities of Cambridge. Probably the suggestion was made at some meeting of clergymen.

The Worcester papers of November 5, 1889, gave a report of a meeting of a committee consisting of Rev. W. H. Thomas, D.D., Rev. 

Thomas J. Conaty, D.D., Rev. W. V. W. Davis, Rev. Alexander Vinton, Rev. Calvin Stebbins, Rev. C. H. Pendleton, and Rev. M. H. Harris, held at the home of Mr. Harris on the preceding afternoon, to discuss matters relative to the organization of Associated Charities in this city. From the paragraph, it is evident that this committee had been appointed at a previous meeting at All Saints' Church, but I cannot find any report of this first meeting. The group organized with Rev. Dr. Thomas as chairman and Rev. Mr. Harris as secretary. After some discussion, it was voted advisable to have an organized system of charity in the city of Worcester. 

The point which was emphasized in the newspapers was "that it was to hold a conference with Mayor Winslow, City Marshall Washburn, Mr. 

Gale, almoner of the Overseers of the Poor, and Dr. L. F. Woodward, City

Physician." It is evident that the need of co-operation and of the help which these officials could give was recognized and appreciated. I have not been able to find any report of this conference, either in the records or in the papers. These preliminary meetings were, of course, informal and probably such minutes as were made were not kept after the society was duly organized.

Two of the papers carried the following explanation of the objects: "Briefly stated, the plan of the associated charities" [the small initials are noticeable] "is to have a central office with a registrar in charge where all bodies doing charitable work will report the names of assisted parties. 

These names will be kept secret except from officers of other associations who may have a right to know them. Thus, if a person applied to the 

G.A.R. for assistance, or to the Odd Fellows asking aid, the officers applied to can go at once to the central office and learn whether the parties are being assisted in any other quarter. They will then know how to deal with the 14

case. This will not, of course, interrupt at all any society's charitable work. 

There is need of some such system to protect the churches and charitable bodies from being imposed upon by people who are tapping the stream of benevolence in several quarters at the same time. The plan has excellent prospects of success."

The papers of January 22, 1890, gave a report of a meeting held the afternoon before, which stated that twenty-four clergymen and Mayor Harrington attended; that Doctor Conaty presided; and that a committee consisting of the same clergymen who attended the meeting on November 4, was appointed to draw up a constitution. At this meeting, January 22, a committee of three clergymen and two laymen were selected to nominate a Board of Directors. It seems to me worthy of note that with so large a proportion of clergymen in the group which inaugurated the new society and gave it definite shape, no clergyman was suggested for the first Board of Directors. Possibly, perhaps I should say probably, because of the precedent established by this first nominating committee, there has never been a clergyman on the Board. And again it is possible that because of the example which this committee set, which has always been followed, there has never been the slightest sectarian friction in our work. 

In talking with Rev. Bernard Conaty, who as you will recall, succeeded his brother, Bishop Conaty, as rector of Sacred Heart Church, I asked him what he thought about having clergymen on our Board, saying that I thought personally that he, Doctor Hitchcock and Doctor Garver would be of great assistance to us. His reply was emphatic. "Don't you do it. It would be all right so long as we three were here; but you do not know what is going to happen." I ought to say that this was purely a personal conversation. So far as I know the subject has never been discussed in the 

Board, or by any nominating committee.

In emphasizing the part which the clergy played in organizing the Associated Charities, there are others whose good work should not be overlooked. Especially is this true of Dr. Charles L. Nichols who was one of the moving factors from the inception of the plan, and gave generously of his time and thought from the beginning of the society to the end of his life. 15

Even when taking a much needed vacation abroad, he studied social work both in England and on the continent, and brought back most interesting accounts of it. As early as 1883, six years before any definite move was made towards an organization in Worcester, he gave an address before the Woman's Club on the Elberfeld system of relief. It is more and more a source of regret to me that I cannot have Doctor Nichols' memory of those early days and his discriminating judgment to appeal to in writing this story.

When I first planned to write this sketch there were still living in Worcester three members of the first Board of Directors, Mrs. John H. 

Coes, Mr. Francis H. Dewey, and Mr. Matthew B. Lamb; and Mr. Dewey was still a member of the Board. Since then Mr. Lamb and Mr. Dewey have died, leaving Mrs. Coes the only member of the first Board. 

Of the thirty members of the first Board of Directors, nine were women. Of the twenty-one men, thirteen were business men, four were lawyers, three were financial men, and one was a physician. The list of the first Board, both men and women, shows conclusively that the nominating committee had asked to serve representative men and women who were willing to give freely of their time and interest to what was then an experiment in social service; and always the Associated Charities has had a fine group of men and women directing its affairs. 

To continue our retrospect of the beginnings the papers of January 5, 1890, gave an account of a meeting held the afternoon before at which the by-laws as prepared and presented by the committee were read and accepted, and the Board as nominated was elected. It is evident by comparing the by-laws of the Worcester Associated Charities with those of the Boston society that Worcester had followed closely the by-laws of that society, repeating many paragraphs almost verbatim. The objects of the society as stated in the by-laws were as follows: 

1. Co-operation with "the various churches, charitable agencies, public relief and individuals charitably disposed, in order 2. To raise the needy above the need of relief, prevent begging and imposition, and diminish pauperism; 16

3. To encourage thrift, self-dependence, and industry through friendly intercourse, advice and sympathy, and to aid the poor to help themselves; 

4. To prevent children growing up as paupers; 

5. To aid in the diffusion of knowledge on subjects connected with the relief of the poor."

These objects as stated vary very little excepting in wording from the objects as given in the by-laws revised in 1927. 

It is evident also that the new society was arousing popular interest, for all the papers gave the by-laws in full, with the names of the Board of Directors and the various committees. One paper, especially, gave the report nearly a column on the front page. In fact, all the preliminary meetings were reported in the daily press with emphasis and dignity. The sponsors for the new organization must have convinced the papers that the plan was feasible and would be permanent. In all the newspaper accounts emphasis was laid on the "severance of the work from any question of race or religion."

FIRST MEETING 

The Associated Charities of Worcester held its first meeting as a duly organized body in the Council Chambers of the old City Hall, on March 19, 1890. Fourteen men and women were present. Mr. Stephen Salisbury presided, and Mr. Francis H. Dewey was appointed secretary pro tem. At this meeting Mr. Salisbury was elected president, Mr. A. M. Stone, first vice-president, and Mrs. George Crompton, Sr., second vice-president.

At a later meeting, Mr. Lewis Hammond was chosen treasurer, and 

Dr. Charles L. Nichols, secretary. The society was incorporated under the laws of Massachusetts in 1903. 

On the evening of April 28, 1890, the first public meeting was held in Association Hall with an attendance of about five hundred, surely a most encouraging beginning. The speakers were, besides Mr. Salisbury, who gave the opening address, Mr. Robert Treat Paine, President of the 

Associated Charities of Boston, Mr. Thomas F. Ring, President of the 

Council of St. Vincent de Paul of Boston, and Rev. W. H. Thomas. 17

Mr. Paine stated that he thought "every family should be connected with some church which would be responsible for the relief needed. That all help for a single family should come from one source and be administered by the same visitor." And in 1931, forty-one years after Mr. Paine said this, Miss Gordon Hamilton said, at the Minneapolis Conference of Social Work, "It is expensive and wasteful for one agency to direct the case work and another agency pay the bill." Mr. Paine also said that the "work rested on the cornerstone of constructive personal service." The advice embodied in the last two statements has always been followed by the Associated Charities of Worcester. But Mr. Paine failed to tell us how a family which had not been faithful to any church connection could be forced into such a relation, especially when the family was in distress. Or how a church can be held responsible for the relief of a family which had had no previous connection with it, or only a formal one. 

Mr. Ring gave an interesting account of the history, principles, and methods of the work of the St. Vincent de Paul Society. 

In addition to a full report of the meeting, the papers published a letter from Mr. Thomas in which he said that he thought what should be emphasized was that in most cases the poor needed constructive, friendly advice more than money; that money should not be spent for tramps and beggars, but to prevent people from becoming tramps and beggars through distress and discouragement; and that there should be close co-operation between all the agencies in order to do the best work. He stated that as a preventive of poverty, misery, and pauperism, scientific charity had been successful. "All this," he said, "is embodied in the Associated Charities just organized in Worcester and is not an experiment but an assured success in many large cities.”

From a study of the by-laws and from interviews with the three directors referred to, it seems probable that, although there came to be very early in the deliberations of the Board a keen appreciation of the value of constructive work and friendly service, the first thought of many in considering the need of organizing the society was the suppression of begging and of the duplication of relief. Evidently the clergymen had been especially 18

impressed with the large number of families who might be designated as church tramps, families who went from one church to another, from one clergyman to another, and what was still worse, sent their children from one Sunday school to another, in the hope of obtaining aid, often asking for it directly. They played back and forth, not only between the different 

Protestation denominations, but also between the Catholic and Protestant churches.

There was much begging from door to door, often little children being sent for that purpose. This was a source of distress to housekeepers who felt that aiding these beggars was not wise; but in most cases they had neither the time nor the skill to investigate for themselves, yet feared to refuse lest there should be real suffering. Mr. Salisbury said to me once that he felt that he was not giving anything when he sent a check to the Associated Charities; he was simply paying the society for doing what he could not do himself, and yet felt that he ought to do. 

EARLY PLANNING 

During the spring of 1890, following the formal organization, several meetings were held to plan the general work of the society, and that of the various committees, and to consider the selection of a General Secretary. 

Delegates were appointed to visit other cities to learn the methods employed. It is evident from the records of the early meetings of the Board of Directors, that they were exceedingly anxious to have the new undertaking start right, and that there should be sufficient financial support. 

From the beginning there was a sincere desire expressed that the Associated Charities should have popular support as a means of arousing interest in the service which it was planning to give, rather than large contributions from the few who were already in sympathy. 

FIRST ANNUAL MEETING 

According to the records, the first annual meeting of the Society was called to order at 4.15 o'clock, October 21, 1890, in the Council Chambers, by Mr. Stephen Salisbury. The meeting was practically devoted to com- 19

pleting the legal form of the organization. A meeting of the Board of 

Directors followed. At this meeting it was voted to open offices the first of November, and to appoint Mrs. Eliza J. Lee, General Secretary. It was reported that there had been twelve applicants for the position, two of whom were men. As the first society had been formed only thirteen years earlier, in Buffalo, in 1877, it is evident that there was not a large number trained in the new methods of social service from whom to select a secretary. Mrs. Lee had been General Secretary of the Woman's Educational and Industrial Union in Providence, so that she had had experience in social work which must have been helpful to her and to the society. 

OPENING OF THE OFFICE 

As voted, the office was opened November 1, 1890, at 35 Pearl

Street, with Mrs. Lee in charge. In an interview reported in the Spy of 

November 3, Mrs. Lee said "My duties for the present will include receiving applicants for charity, and investigating the worthiness of those who apply; to examine the merits of cases of destitution reported to me, and to see that the money contributed for the purpose is placed where it will do the most good, and only with those cases who deserve help. We hope to restrain begging on the streets, imposition on kind-hearted people, and intend to place those whom we assist in an independent position. 

I looked over the papers carefully for editorial comment but could not find any, although, as I have said, good space was given to reports of all the meetings.

Doctor Nichols, in his address delivered on the twentieth anniversary of the society, told us that one of the first acts was to distribute blanks throughout the city for reporting tramps and beggars. This must have been a practical demonstration to our citizens of one use which could be made of the new organization. Doctor Nichols also told us that the Associated 

Charities began its work with the firm conviction that it should give no relief other than emergency aid; and that such aid as was needed should be obtained from the church with which the family was connected, or from individuals who might be interested in the family or might become so. 20

This was the practice in all the larger cities where most of the societies had been formed, and for this decision there were two strong arguments. 

DEVELOPMENT OF SOCIAL WORK 

If we go back for a moment to the history of the development of social work, we are impressed with the fact that unwise, often ignorant, relief giving had caused grave apprehension in the minds of many thinking people as to the results to the recipients of such aid, although the donors sincerely desired to be of assistance to them. Read Chalmers if you are interested in this problem, what he taught and what he did in Glasgow in the early part of the nineteenth century. Look up Frederick Ozanam's work in the middle of the nineteenth century in establishing the St. Vincent de Paul Society; or better still, the work of St. Vincent de Paul himself, in the seventeenth century. In our own country, read the lectures of Rev. 

S. Humphreys Gurteen which led to the establishment of the Charity 

Organization Society in Buffalo. In the teachings of all these men, separated by such long periods of time, and by such absolutely different conditions, you will find the same fear of giving relief to any able-bodied person. 

Apparently, they all felt that to give unearned support was as dangerous to the soul as to give morphine to the body. They were tremendously impressed with the conviction that all relief, excepting to the hopelessly incapacitated, or in the direst emergency should be in some way earned. 

And this tenet became a part of the creed of the founders of the new societies.

The second objection to relief giving by the Associated Charities and the Charity Organization Societies, as they were variously called, was the opposition of the older relief societies which already existed in many, perhaps in most, of the larger cities. They felt that this new work was a reflection on their work which they had been doing faithfully for many years. And doubtless in many cases the new societies were a bit cocky, a bit sure that they were the saviours of the poor. But in the smaller cities where such relief funds did not exist, it was soon recognized that in many cases aid must be given promptly, and often continued beyond the mere emer- 21

gency, if we were to have the influence with the family and their confidence in our real sympathy which would make helpful constructive work possible. 

But even where relief was necessary, the fact that our societies had their inception in the fear of alms, and the conviction that what the poor really needed was friendship, understanding and advice from those whose opportunities had given the experience necessary for such helpfulness, has always been a safeguard against careless or unwise aid. 

FIRST ANNUAL REPORT 

Mrs. Lee's first annual report called attention emphatically to the bad housing conditions which she had found, and the evil effects such tenements-they could scarcely be called homes-must have on the health and courage of those who were forced by poverty to live in them. It seems to me an interesting fact that so early in the work, the society was conscious of the need on the part of the poor for decent home life. 

BUSINESS DEPRESSION OF 1893-4 

Almost at the beginning of the life of the Associated Charities, and while the plans were still in an experimental stage, the business depression of 1893-4 brought destitution to the city and a flood of applicants to the office. Realizing quickly that a hard winter was before them, the Board of 

Directors called a special meeting on October 6, 1893, to which were invited the Mayor, clergymen, and others interested. Three points were discussed: 1. The need of more funds; 2. The danger of losing sight of the constructive work of the society if it should become absorbed in the neceessary relief work; 3. The fear of too great publicity in the effort to obtain money, with a resulting avalanche of applicants. 

Even in so short a time, the thinking people of the city were ready to give their support to the principles and practical work of the Associated Charities.

The first plan was that the Associated Charities should handle the whole situation. The Board of Directors objected to this, fearing lest the personal work in dealing with families, the bringing about of co-operation 22

between the different societies, and the education of the public away from the Lady Bountiful idea of relief to wiser and more helpful methods of giving aid, would be lost sight of, with the result that the society would become in the eyes of the public purely a relief-giving organization. And so at a meeting of the Board and others interested, called two weeks later, the following plans were discussed and accepted: 1. That as the Associated 

Charities is not primarily a relief-giving organization, a Committee of Nine, three clergymen, three members of the Board of Directors, the Mayor, the City Marshall, and the Clerk of the Overseers of the Poor, shall be formed, who shall receive and dispense all funds which shall be placed in their hands. 2. That all investigation of applicants be made by the Associated Charities, and the information given confidentially to this Committee. 3. That plans be made for feeding tramps and beggars at the police station, or at a place designated for that purpose. 4. That all benevolent organizations and private persons be urged to report all persons whom they were aiding (a recognition of the need of what we now know as the 

Social Service Exchange). 5. That an appeal be made for an increase of membership in the Associated Charities that funds and personal service may be increased.

THE CITIZENS' COMMITTEE OF NINE 

The Citizens' Committee of Nine was organized October 27, 1893, His Honor Mayor Marsh being elected chairman, Mr. William Woodward, treasurer, and Mr. Henry L. Parker, Sr., secretary. Committees of finance and administration were appointed and contributions from the public were solicited. The Committee met each Saturday in the rooms of the Associated 

Charities, and some member of the Committee was in attendance at the rooms practically every day. That this Committee did a good piece of work is evident from the fact that the number of families dealt with dropped from 1493 in 1893-4, to 575 in 1894-5, proof positive that the relief given had not been pauperizing. How great a burden of investigation and supervision was thrown on the Associated Charities is shown by the increase of families from 242 in 1892-3 to 1493 in 1893-4 as stated above. 23

The good work done by the Committee of Nine was doubtless largely due to the splendid spirit of co-operation between the Committee and the Associated Charities, when the methods and principles of organized charity were still unfamiliar to the majority of the public. 

In going over the files of the Spy for the fall and winter of 1893-4, I found on the front page for October 24, 1893, the story of a man who had died from starvation, with an editorial starting a subscription with a donation of $50. The next day there was a front page appeal as follows: "The Associated Charities, to whom the money will be turned over, is undoubtedly better able to render efficient and timely help to the needy than any other charitable organization in the city, on account of the breadth of its scope and its far-reaching energies." In the Spy of October 27, 1893, I found an editorial on relief-giving, quoting at length from a leaflet by 

John Glenn on the evils of indiscriminate aid in Baltimore.

With the organization of the Committee of Nine, and probably because of the unwillingness of the Associated Charities to handle relief funds, the money raised by the Spy was turned over to this Committee. 

All through the winter constant appeals were made to the public through the press, often on the front pages. There are also interesting reports of concerts and entertainments of various kinds, the proceeds of which were given to the fund. Special collections were taken in all the churches and in the public schools.

AMOUNT OF RELIEF GIVEN 

The total amount collected and disbursed during that time of depression was $8,871.71. This amount seems small in comparison with the aid being given during the present depression. There are several reasons for this. The work of the Committee covered only six months, from October 

27, 1893, to April 21, 1894, when the final report was made and the unexpended balance of $348.63 turned over to the Associated Charities; the number of families aided was not large; and, third, a dollar would buy more in those days than at the present time. I recall the wife of one laborer saying in the early days, "If my husband earned $9 a week regularly, and I 24

could depend on it, I could make it go. But the trouble is losing time on stormy days." And she had three or four children. Rents in decent tenements ranged from $9 to $15 a month. One fraternal society refused to help any family paying over $12 a month rent. 

There is another factor which also ought to be taken into accountall relief workers know that the longer a family has to be aided the larger is the amount necessary per family. With long continued lack of income, shoes and clothing give out beyond repair and must be replaced; rents which in many cases can stand over for a few months, must in justice to the landlord be paid if the debt becomes too heavy; and a greater variety of food must be given to preserve health and strength. A family cannot live too long on emergency rations.

If any one is interested to learn more of the details of what was done to handle an unemployment situation forty and more years ago, he will find a full and interesting report of the Committee in the annual report of the Associated Charities for the year 1893-4. In this report there is a special expression of thanks to the Spy for securing a large contribution in the beginning of its work. 

MRS. LEE'S RESIGNATION 

Owing to the severe strain of this winter of destitution, coming as it did as an added burden to the serious problems involved in establishing the new work, Mrs. Lee's health failed to such an extent that she found it necessary to decline re-election. In accepting her resignation, the Board of 

Directors paid tribute to her unswerving fidelity, her kindly spirit towards the applicants, and to the earnest, consistent and unselfish manner in which she had carried on the work. 

A NEW GENERAL SECRETARY 

A committee having been appointed to select a new general secretary, on advice of a Boston social worker, Miss Burgess, with whom I had been associated, who had herself been a social worker in Worcester before going to Boston, my name was suggested for the position. 25

Sometime early in November, I think it was, Doctor Nichols, who was at that time secretary of the Board of Directors, wrote asking me to come to Worcester to meet the committee. You can imagine my surprise. 

I had been in Worcester only once, and then on an errand for the Associated Charities of Boston. I was not conscious of knowing any one in the city, although I was glad to find later some old college friends living here. I was at the time a district secretary in Boston, and had been under the training of Miss Zilpha D. Smith and her sister, Miss Frances.

I consulted Miss Burgess, and on her advice came to Worcester to meet the committee. The meeting was held at the home of Madam Dewey, one dark November afternoon, the clouds spitting the first snow of the season.

After the meeting, Mrs. Coes took me to the office to see it and to meet 

Miss Stiles who was registrar of the society and assistant for seventeen years. How little we thought, Miss Stiles and I, that when the end of her long and arduous life came, I would be the one at her own request to follow her alone to her resting place in the little Nashua cemetery. 

As a result of my meeting with the committee, I was asked to come to Worcester as General Secretary. As it was necessary for me to arrange my work so that it could be passed on to my successor, I was not able to come until December 10, when I began my work which was to continue for thirty-five years.

HANDICAPS 

I wonder how many appreciate the handicaps under which social work and especially family social work was carried on in those days. The fact that we had no telephone was not so serious as it sounds, because telephones were not in such common use as they are now and, therefore, we could not reach so many people through them, and not so many people expected to reach us by their use. We had no typewriter, because a typewritten letter was too business-like. We were friends of the poor, and we tried to emphasize this fact in every way possible. Because we believed that we should know what we were doing, and should not give more than emergency aid without such knowledge, we were accused of letting people starve 26

while we investigated. The fact that there was never any delay in a case of real suffering, that often food was bought at the corner grocery in order that children should not go hungry to bed, was not believed, no matter how many times we repeated the statement. Moreover, most people saw only the relief side of our work, and resented money contributed being used for "overhead," which in their estimation included salaries as well as office rent and postage stamps. We were to give and ask no questions. All questions were prying and meddling.

How little the principles and objects of modern social work are even yet understood was forcibly illustrated recently, when a prominent clergyman, not a Worcester man, in addressing an audience presumably intelligent, speaking about the relief work needed in the present time of distress, said that anyone who was hungry ought to be fed and no questions asked. 

That it was not necessary to know whether the applicant's grandfather had a cast in his left eye before aiding. It hardly seems possible that a man holding the position that this man does could have made so silly a statement. But I heard him over the radio, and heard the laugh which followed from an audience that ought to have known better. I am glad that it was not a Worcester audience.

Records, as well as investigations, were also an abomination in those early days, a waste of time and another unnecessary expense. Of course the saying "Let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth" was often quoted. But no one seemed to realize that this rule did not forbid the right hand's knowing what it was doing. At the same time the very people who criticised because time and money were spent on investigation, were often the ones who insisted most strenuously that their contributions should be used only for the "worthy poor. "But I never found any of them who could themselves give regular and systematic time to making investigations. I have always found that the volunteer visitor who gave most generously of her time and friendliness to help the destitute and unfortunate was the one who appreciated most keenly the work of the paid staff. Social work is a difficult and complicated job today, because of the demands made upon it. It was a difficult job in those far-off days, but for entirely different 27

reasons, the principal one being, perhaps, the ignorance on the part of the public as to what organized charity was trying to do. 

Another serious difficulty was the lack of specialized societies to whom we could turn for help in our work. 

The Children's Friend Society had an orphanage which did good work; and orphanages were at that time the recognized institutions for the care of dependent children. But when the Home was filled and, as I recall, its capacity was small, the Directors felt no further responsibility, no matter how dire the need. The Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children had no office in Worcester. A court official had the necessary blanks and authority from the Boston office to make complaints; but he made no investigation of the charges of neglect or cruelty which came to him. We who knew the conditions, or investigated them when reported to us, had to gather all the evidence and testify in court. Judge Utley, although always just to both sides, made this disagreeable duty as easy as possible. It is a source of satisfaction as I look back over those days, to note that we never lost a case in court. We were careful not to make trivial complaints; we learned early what legal evidence is; and the lawyers gave us generously of their time and advice. I remember so distinctly Mr. Francis H. Dewey coming to our office with law books under his arm, reading the law to me in some special case, and explaining it carefully. Such help as that kept us from making serious blunders.

To continue with our lack of co-operating societies—and you must remember that co-operation with other societies was one of the articles of our faith, especially emphasized in our by-laws-The District Nursing Society had two nurses who used our office as downtown headquarters. I wonder if one of them remembers the note she hung on the knob of the corner closet when the tricky key which always bothered us refused to work; and does she remember who found the note!

The Temporary Home and Day Nursery under Miss Emerson, who also came to Worcester in 1894, was doing excellent work in very inadequate quarters on Southbridge Street.

The Girls' Welfare Society was the Door of Hope, a refuge, primarily, for unmarried mothers. I could tell you stories of the good work this insti- 28

tution was doing under difficulties; but it was entirely different in its scope from that of the society as it now exists. 

The Employment Society was giving plain sewing, as it does now, to handicapped women. But I do not know how many were on their payroll in 1894.

And these were practically all the private societies on whom we could call for help in solving the family problems with which we were struggling. 

Mention should be made of the Board of Public Welfare, known then as the Overseers of the Poor, as that was the only group besides our own doing family relief work. From the first, Mr. Freeman Brown, who was almoner of the Board from 1891 to 1916, welcomed the new society and gave us all the co-operation which the laws and inherited customs under which he worked made possible. Their records were always open to us, which I know was not true in some cities; their advice was freely given and 

I was prone to ask advice; and best of all, there was never any duplication of relief between the two organizations. 

Such were the conditions under which the Associated Charities was working in 1894.

When I decided to accept the position of General Secretary of the 

Associated Charities of Worcester, Miss Zilpha Smith gave me one bit of advice which I am glad I had the good sense to follow. I have passed it on to young workers going to new positions many times. It was simply, "Do not make changes until you know your local conditions." And so for some time no special changes in the methods already in use were made. 

I do not deny that I wanted to make some; and the funny part of it is that many of the things I wanted to do differently because "they were not done as we did them in Boston," I afterwards found were better for Worcester, and it would have been a serious blunder for me to have made these changes.

SEWING SCHOOL 

Two new plans were carried out this winter, 1894-5. The first begun just before I came to Worcester has always seemed very practical. It was a 29

sewing school for repairing and making over old clothing which had been contributed, and was of good material, but so worn in spots that it could not be used as it was. I am not sure who suggested the scheme, but 

Mrs. C. C. Houghton was probably the originator of the plan, as she was the leader with several faithful and efficient co-workers. The clothing was ripped in the sewing rooms. If necessary, it was taken home by the sewing women, washed, pressed, and brought back to be cut and made up. The rooms were given us rent free by Mr. C. C. Houghton. During the winter of 1895-6, the Z.E.H. Club of the First Universalist Church paid an assistant to help in this work, but most of the workers were volunteers. 

Only such women as were vouched for by the Associated Charities were admitted to the class. A careful record was kept of the time which they worked, and they were paid in garments such as they needed for their families. This work went on until the winter of 1907, when Mrs. Houghton was obliged to retire on account of her health. It seemed impossible to find another volunteer to take her place, and the society was not in a position financially to employ a paid worker competent to do the work. There is no question that the sewing women enjoyed the class, both the opportunity to learn, and the friendliness of being together away from household cares one afternoon a week. I recall the delight with which one woman displayed the dress which she had made for her little girl, saying at the same time, "When I came to the class I could not thread a needle." 

During the winter of 1900, through the kindness of Miss Nellie Moore, a series of talks on practical household matters was given to the class by young women who were pupils at the School of Domestic Science at the Oread. To quote from the report of that year, "The earnest attention of the women, and the intelligent questions which they asked, and comments which they made, showed how much they appreciated these opportunities."

At Christmas or at the end of the school year, it was the custom to give the women of the sewing school a party, with music and readings, followed by simple refreshments. The 1902 report tells us that “At 

Christmas, the superintendent and teachers, assisted by their friends, 30

provided for members of the school and their families an entertainment which included a Christmas tree, with a present for each one, and refreshments of ice cream and cake. We were especially indebted to Master 

Robert Benchley for the amusing Punch and Judy show which he gave. 

Certainly if the pleasure expressed by the women and children was any reward, then those who worked so hard to make the affair a success, must have felt amply repaid."

HOME SAVINGS SOCIETY 

The second piece of new work, begun in the spring of 1895, was the Home Savings Society. It seemed an opportune time for inaugurating this particular experiment, because we felt that many people who had been obliged to apply for aid during the panic of 1893-4, had learned the wisdom of having some resources in reserve and would welcome the chance to save the small amounts which could be spared each week. The plan was not original; I had seen it in operation in Boston, and it was simple. Volunteer collectors went each week to families who already knew us and had confidence in us, collecting from five to twenty-five cents from each family. Each depositor had a card on which was entered the deposit for the week, and a careful set of books was kept in the office. 

As the money deposited belonged to the depositors, and was in no sense a part of the funds of the Associated Charities, it could be withdrawn on presentation of the deposit card and identification. Sometimes they told us what they were saving for. I recall that one woman was saving to pay for false teeth; another for lace curtains for her parlor. Another came in one morning for her money to go to the home of her sister who had died suddenly. While still another woman said with a note of tragedy in her voice, "I do not know what I should do if I did not have this money." She did not explain what the need was and we did not ask her. It was her own money. 

One of our most faithful collectors was Miss Holman, whom some of us elderly people remember as Myra M. F. Holman, who many years ago wrote charming stories for the Youth's Companion. She was especially 31

interested in our colored group, perhaps an inherited sympathy as her father, a clergyman, was one of the men who formed the "underground railroad" which helped slaves to escape to Canada. In her visiting as a Home Savings collector, she became much troubled about the little colored children left alone while their mothers were out at work. The need for some care for these children seemed so great that she finally rented one of the little cottages at the lower end of John Street, and for some time carried on alone a day nursery for the children of the neighborhood, most of them colored children.

Among her experiences was one which she told with great delight. 

When trying to persuade a colored woman to join the Home Savings Society, she told her that when she had three dollars she could have a bank book of her own. And the woman replied, "Lor, if I had a bank book I should think I was Mis' Stephen Salisbury."

The work was eminently successful, and was continued to the time of the war, when the appeals of the war committees and of the Red Cross made it difficult for us to obtain collectors. We tried to revive it later, but by that time there were so many opportunities for small savings, through the schools, the various factory plans, the vacation and Christmas funds in the banks, and the establishment of the United States postal savings, that it did not seem worth while to continue the work. We were, I am quite sure, the pioneers in Worcester in making it possible for people to save in small amounts. I believe it was a valuable piece of work. But it is one of the duties of private social work to inaugurate new efforts, and to be wise enough to relinquish them when they are no longer needed, or when the need has so increased that a separate society should be organized to meet it. 

Merely to continue certain gestures after the gestures have ceased to have a meaning, or when the transfer of certain activities to other hands is wise, is a waste of time and energy. 

Although I hesitate to speak of any of the Home Savings volunteers, because so many did such good work, yet I feel that three workers ought to be given special commendation. Our first treasurer, Mr. William C. 

Habberly, was an expert accountant and exceedingly helpful in opening our 32

books in the best form. Following him, Miss Emily Haynes carried on the work of treasurer most faithfully and efficiently for seventeen years. And

Miss Adeline M. Bisco followed Miss Haynes as treasurer, after she had for many years done most valuable work as a collector. 

That same spring, 1895, I attended my first National Conference of Social Work, or as it was then called The National Conference of Charities and Corrections, the twenty-second national meeting of social workers. 

The Conference was held at New Haven with Mr. Robert Treat Paine as president, and had, as I recall, an attendance of about eight hundred. To any one interested in the development of social work, I know nothing more enlightening than to go back and read the reports of the Conferences of the early days, and note the difference in emphasis. I have read recently some of the papers given at the New Haven Conference, and have been especially impressed with the fact that in 1895, we still feared relief almost as much as Chalmers did a hundred years before. I came home from the Conference thrilled with the experience, with all I had heard, and perhaps still more with the people whom I had met, and with whom I realized that I was working.

STATISTICS 

In our annual report for 1894-5, we began publishing more detailed statistics of our new families. I realize that the criticism of statistics is often justified; but I know that I have followed the development of some phases of our work by comparing these tables from year to year, better than I could have done in any other way. They were the beginnings of analyzing the work we were doing. Moreover, the early workers were not content with saying so many families destitute because of intemperance; they questioned the cause of the intemperance. Many of the reform movements, and much of the social legislation of the last twenty-five years have, without question, grown out of the study of individual families and their difficulties. If the pioneers had not studied "Causes of Need," or as we always said "Apparent Causes of Need," for instance, I do not see how we could have developed the present technique of investigation and treatment 33

which is so efficacious. These same pioneers would not have had the material from which to formulate the teaching for the younger generation. 

The case work and the records of the various social agencies, and especially, I think, the work of the family societies, have given what might be called the laboratory material for research, and for formulating and proving theories, looking to more understanding work in the future. If we have not furnished such material, and if we are not adding to it still more valuable data, then we are failing to do a most important work for the eventual solution of our present problems of destitution and misery. I know the statement is often quoted, "The poor ye have always with you," and I suppose in one sense this will always be true. But I believe the word "poor" in this connection is a relative term, and that the phrase should not be used as a salve for our consciences when we are reminded in some vivid way of the mass of suffering and degradation which we know exists in all our large cities, and only in somewhat less quantity in our small cities and even in many villages. And I believe that social work in all its modern developments is going to be an important factor in solving these serious human problems. But I am wandering from my subject and must return to the Associated Charities of Worcester. 

ANNUAL REPORTS 

It has always been my conviction that the annual report of a society should be just what it says it is. It should be a résumé of the year's work. 

And now in trying to write the story of these forty years, I find that the reports from year to year give me much material which I need. The reading of these reports has brought to mind many incidents and pieces of work which I had forgotten. For instance, I find that during the summer of 1899, at the request of the Tenement House Committee of the New York Charity Organization Society, we made a careful investigation of the streets where tenements could be obtained at rents varying from eight to fourteen dollars a month. To quote from the report, "We found seven hundred and fourteen houses on the ten streets, containing sixteen hundred and sixty-seven families, averaging a little over two families to a house. Two hundred and 34

fifty-three of these houses contained only one family each." We considered it a cause for congratulation that we had so little crowding of families into small, dark tenements.

And that reminds me of my first interview with Mr. James Coffey, who was for thirty-seven years, from 1884 to 1921, the clerk of the Board of Health. I reported to him conditions which I had found in one house, and asked that one of his visitors be sent to investigate. He asked me in rather a gruff tone, which I afterwards came to realize was his natural voice, "How did you know anything about it?" There was just a little suggestion that perhaps the Associated Charities had been snooping and meddling, a feeling which was probably induced on my part by his voice with which I was not then familiar. I explained to him that a woman living in the house had come to us for aid; that I had called on her in her home as we always did, and this was what I had found. My reply seemed to be entirely satisfactory, especially as I had only asked for a visit from his office. For some reason which I have forgotten, probably because the family in which we were interested moved, I did not have occasion to go into the house again. 

But not long after this interview with Mr. Coffey, I was walking through the street and stopped short in amazement. The house had not merely been vacated, it had been torn down. Not a stick or a stone of it remained. And from the day of that first interview to the day of his death, the Associated Charities had no more understanding or helpful friend than James Coffey. 

I am wondering whether it is wise to mention the names of the various officials who have given us help and counsel, lest I should inadvertently omit one, or should on the other hand fill these pages with lists of our good friends in both public and private positions. 

RECORDS 

One of the criticisms of the Associated Charities in the beginning was that we kept records of our families. The critics never seemed to realize how impossible it would be to keep track of our families without this artificial memory, the record. Nor did they realize how unpleasant it would be for our clients, and how embarrassing for us if we had to ask the same 35

questions on a second interview, in order to have the situation in mind before attempting to be of assistance. Some of our clients were more sensible than our critics. More than one coming back to the office after an absence of a year or so, and finding a new person to talk with, said to me, "The society helped us during the panic. I am sure if you will look back in the books you will find the name." They understood that some form of bookkeeping or recording was essential. 

Our records were kept first on large cards, such as the Boston Associated Charities was using. In fact, we bought our first supplies from 

Boston. We had a face card for identifying data, followed by history cards, the cards for each family being strapped together. These cards were filed in groups, according to our relation with the family. Sometimes it would be necessary to look through two or three groups to find the record we wanted. But as all the records were in one small cabinet it did not take long. 

All the records were hand written. If we had had a typewriter at the time we could not have used it for the cards because they were too heavy. 

The natural result was that all records were much briefer, just a statement of facts. Of course we carried in our memories, and in our thought of the family, much knowledge and many impressions which we did not enter on the cards, but which helped us to understand. With a card record, correspondence about the family, or with it, for that matter, had to be copied on the card or filed separately, which was inconvenient. 

During the first years of the Society, there was much duplication of record work, while we were experimenting to find out just what we needed. 

Application blanks for new families were of paper of one color; while recurrent application blanks were of another color, but the same form. 

Reports on current families were written on another form. All these reports were copied in a book of sensitized paper, and later copied on the permanent record cards. But we soon found out that all this duplication was unnecessary. Such notes as we needed could be written on plain paper, often just scraps which would later be destroyed. Moreover, this seemed to our clients more natural and friendly than filling out a printed form. 

And we were anxious to impress on our clients and on the public that we 36

were the friends of those in distress. We tried to avoid anything which would look official.

I think the real reason for so much duplication was the fact, unacknowledged but still in the back of our consciousness, that we did not quite trust the cards. We feared a card, or possibly a whole record, might be lost, or that data might go astray before it could be copied. But when the essential facts were in a book, then they were safe for reference for all time. 

On October 1, 1907, the beginning of our fiscal year, we began writing our records on the typewriter, one having been given us, and filing them in folders chronologically. This made an index necessary. But by this time our records had so increased that an index would have been needed, whatever form of filing we used. We have at different times changed the form of our face sheet, as we found the need of more identifying data, or there was a change in emphasis in our study of problems. But we have of course continued to use folders, into which can go for safe keeping correspondence and even hastily written notes, always dated no matter how insignificant the information might seem to be at the moment, as well as the history sheets on which can be copied later the notes we have kept. I must repeat that although there is temptation to write in the present tense, as I find myself doing, I am really writing of what we did previous to September 30, 1929. 

What changes have been found necessary since that time, especially to meet the dire emergency in which the Associated Charities has been working, I do not know. Without doubt many changes have had to be made to ensure prompt and efficient results.

CO-OPERATION AND THE SOCIAL SERVICE EXCHANGE 

As has been stated, one of the objects in organizing the Associated Charities was to bring about co-operation between the private societies already existing, and between these societies and public officials. We did not expect the churches to tell us what they were doing for their own members who had fallen into distress. We considered that a family matter. 

But we did ask them to report to us what they were doing for comparative strangers who were in need almost as soon as they appeared at the church. 37

doors, or as soon as the children entered Sunday School. I found when I came to Worcester a good spirit of co-operation, which increased as the years went on. At first when we had comparatively few families to deal with, we, the secretaries of the various public and private agencies, simply consulted in a very pleasant and friendly way. It was not an uncommon thing to have little unplanned conferences on the corner of the street. Two of us would meet by chance, and begin immediately discussing the problems of some family whom we both knew. Then we might be joined by a third and possibly a fourth, all of whom had had contacts with the same family at different times, or because of varying needs. Sometimes we would stop to ask whether a family was known to the other society, or we would telephone the inquiry. And all this was friendly and sociable, really much more efficient than it sounds, and without doubt led to a better understanding of each other on the part of the social workers, as well as of the family under discussion. But the time came when we could no longer compare notes in this way; we had to have a more prompt and accurate method of checking up. So we find in the report for 1909-10, the statement that we had begun asking registration from the various societies, furnishing cards to them for this purpose. And so was inaugurated the Social Service Exchange, the mechanics of co-operation. In the report for 1911-12, we said that the registration from other societies had become well established, and that we had 1378 families so recorded. I think it ought to be stated that from the beginning these blanks asked only sufficient information for identification; and that all the information was then, as it is now, held as confidential, a fact which was emphasized by the first name used, "Confidential Exchange."

I do not mean to imply that our development of the Exchange was original. Practically the same method was used in all the large cities, and was familiar to me from my work with the Associated Charities of Boston. 

At first it was difficult to convince some of the societies that there was any value to themselves in using the Exchange, or to other societies in registering their families. Then we moved to Wetherell House, and became much closer neighbors. It was easy, as the various workers passed 38

our door, for them to drop in and ask if we knew certain families, and what our experience had been. If we did not know the family, it was often possible to refer the inquirer to some society which had known it. As the habit grew, and the value of the knowledge so obtained became more evident, one particular society suddenly realized that it ought to give information as well as ask for it. The result was a deluge of registration cards. It was not an easy task to check up, report and file so many cards promptly. But weren't we glad to welcome this society and prove so conclusively the value of the Social Service Exchange. 

When the Welfare Federation, now known as the Community Chest, was organized, the question arose as to the advisability of the Federation taking over the Exchange, the thought being that possibly some societies whom we had not been able to persuade to register with us might do so if it were conducted independently of any one of the constitutent organizations. A committee was appointed to consider the matter. This committee after careful study of the function of the Exchange and the methods of conducting it, decided that such a change was not necessary and would be an additional expense to the Federation. Personally, I have always felt that for the Community Chest to take over the Exchange, besides relieving the Associated Charities of a heavy burden, might add to its efficiency and prestige, and might bring about a greater development of its work. 

STREET LIST 

During the year 1900-01, we put into convenient and permanent form the street list upon which we had been experimenting for some time, to prove its value before spending any money on it. To quote from the report of that year, "By means of this list, which is made out as a card index, it is possible to tell whether we have ever had an application from any given house in the city. It is useful in studying the different sections of the city, but it is still more useful in locating families when there has been any misunderstanding about the name. This street list will, we believe, be still more valuable as we come to deal more and more with foreigners, whose names are not easily understood." 39

WORCESTER COLLATERAL LOAN 

In addition to the definite work for which the Associated Charities was organized, the society has been instrumental in arousing interest in other projects, for which it saw the need but which did not come within its province.

Perhaps the most outstanding example of this is the Worcester Collateral Loan Association which was organized in 1896. 

At that time, among the many families in whom we were interested was one which had obtained loans on two chattel mortgages, on one of which 96% interest was being paid, and on the other 120%. In a talk which I gave at one of the churches, I referred to this exasperating condition, and suggested the need of some form of remedial loan service. Fortunately, Mr. Charles Burnham was in the audience. His attention had been called to the same abuse and he had determined that something must be done. 

Consultations followed and such information as I had was passed on to him. He interviewed the Workingmen's Loan Association in Boston, of which Mr. Robert Treat Paine was treasurer, and obtained such printed matter as was in existence. Then he went to various Directors of the 

Associated Charities for both moral and financial support. Meantime, I am sure that I must have talked to our Directors, for when Mr. Burnham put the proposition up to them, they immediately accepted the new plan and were ready to subscribe for the stock. Doctor Nichols bought the first share and sent him to Mr. Salisbury who also bought shares. The first 

President of the Worcester Collateral Loan Association was Mr. Henry L. 

Parker, Sr., who was at the time President of the Associated Charities, and the first meetings were held in our office. 

The pawnbrokers, who realized that if the new company was successful it would cut into their profits, attempted to discredit it by saying that it was simply another charity society, as no chattel mortgage company could live on the interest charged by the Collateral Loan, because the security offered so easily depreciated in value. Contrary to this statement, it is not only a thriving business today, paying dividends on the stock, but its success has made it necessary for the pawnbrokers to reduce their rates materially. 40

Of course, at first people came asking loans who did regard it as another source of relief and did not expect to repay. This group was soon disposed of, and the reputation of being a straight business organization was established. But thinking that there might be a place for charity loans which would be returned but on which no interest would be charged, the Collateral Loan gave the Associated Charities $100, with which to try the experiment. We soon found that it was just as impossible to mix business with charity as it was to mix charity with business, and the plan was dropped. There was not a sufficient amount voluntarily returned to make it a revolving fund, while the bookkeeping necessary and the effort to collect made much extra work in the office of the Associated Charities. 

We have made loans, and many times they have been returned. But 

I never advised a loan-and our loans were always small-without considering two things carefully, the character and plans of the borrower, that he intended to repay; and the fact that the need was such that a gift outright would be justifiable.

FLOWER MISSION 

I have hesitated to say anything about the Flower Mission as it was never a branch of the Associated Charities. But it was so closely related to us that it seems only courteous to give a short history of it. 

In the second, third, and fourth annual reports of the Associated Charities, there is a special account of the work done by the Flower Mission. 

Again in the fifth report there is no separate report of the Flower Mission; but it is referred to in my report, the first one which I wrote. Evidently the 

Flower Mission ceased to function for a short time. But in our report for 

1899-1900, I find that the Flower Mission had been reorganized, under the auspices of the young women's societies of several churches, each society sending two delegates. Flowers, fruits, and jellies were taken to the homes in the tenement houses where there was sickness; and flowers were sent to the City Hospital, Memorial Hospital, Belmont Hospital, and to the Home Farm. Reports of its work were published in the annual reports of the 

Associated Charities. The last report was given in our report for 1911-12, 41

in which it was stated that 409 calls were made from May 1911 to May 1912. I do not recall why it was discontinued, although I have no doubt there was a good reason for it. The Mission was, as I said, a separate society, with its own officers and monthly meetings of delegates, so that the responsibility for its continuance did not rest on our shoulders and we have no record. It used our offices Saturday mornings, and as a rule we supplied the names of those who were the recipients of their gifts. I am glad to bear witness that it did a fine piece of work during the years of its existence. 

DIRECTORY OF CHARITIES 

In 1903, the Associated Charities published a directory of the social and benevolent organizations of the city, including in it a list of the churches with the names of the various philanthropic agencies connected with each church. It also contained a list of the State organizations whose services were available for Worcester. This work was all done in the office, with the assistance of volunteers, especially Dr. George H. Haynes, who was secretary of the Board of Directors for twenty-six years. He gave us most valuable aid in reading the proof, and in making suggestions and corrections. He did this not only for the directory, but also for the annual reports, a service which relieved my mind, as well as my time, of a particularly onerous duty. The directory was used for several years to good advantage. But finally the changes in the social work of the city rendered its data obsolete. By the time revision was necessary, the legitimate work of the Associated Charities had grown so heavy that the regular staff could not give the time to it, and we did not have the resources to enlarge the staff. I wonder if it is too much to hope that after these days of stress and strain are over, the Community Chest may consider the possibility of a new edition of the "Directory of Charitable and Beneficent Organizations of Worcester.'

Doctor Merriman who, as many of you will recall, was for many years pastor of Central Church, made the criticism of the Associated 42

Charities that not sufficient effort was given to the education of the public in the principles of the Associated Charities with which he was in hearty sympathy. That we tried to do this is evident from a review of the public meetings held and the speakers of family welfare work whom we brought to Worcester.

The first public meeting, held in the first year of the society, which was addressed by Mr. Robert Treat Paine and Mr. Thomas F. Ring, has already been described. Later in the same year, Mrs. Glendower Evans and 

Miss Mason of Boston, gave talks on friendly visiting. In February 1892, Rev. Dr. Pullman, President of the Associated Charities of Lynn, gave an address before a large audience in Horticultural Hall on "Charity under

Mrs. Coes, in her report of the work of the Conference of Friendly 

Visitors for 1893-4, says, "In April, Miss Zilpha D. Smith, General

Secretary of the Associated Charities of Boston, very kindly consented to speak before the Conference, on Friendly Visiting. A large number of persons attended this meeting, and all expressed much interest in the subject so ably handled."

In his address given at our twentieth annual meeting, Doctor Nichols refers to Dean Hodges of Cambridge coming to Worcester to speak, I should judge, to the Board of Directors. But I cannot find any other reference to it or the date of the meeting.

Perhaps the most popular meeting which we ever held was the annual meeting in October, 1902, at which Rev. Edward Everett Hale gave the address. Unfortunately, Mr. Hale spoke without manuscript so that a full report of his talk could not be given, but there is an excellent summary in the annual report for 1901-2, which will well repay reading. 

At the annual meeting in 1907, Doctor Nichols gave a most interesting talk on "Some Phases of the Problem of Charity Abroad," which is also worth reading. At several meetings, representatives of other societies in 

Worcester, both public and private, gave addresses on the work of their own societies, emphasizing the value of co-operation. 

In addition to these meetings under the auspices of our own society, 43

talks have been given by members of the Board of Directors, and of the office staff in many (I came near writing, in all) of the churches of the city. 

For many years the newspapers have given us valuable assistance in "spreading the gospel" of our work. I wonder if anyone remembers 

Charlie Giblin, who was convicted in the New York courts of murder, on what seemed very slight evidence. Three times the gallows was erected for his execution, and three times the execution was deferred, until finally the sentence was commuted to imprionsment for life. Tuberculosis developed and he was pardoned. Previous to going to New York, he had been a reporter on the Telegram. When he was free, Mr. Cristy sent for him and gave him a position on the paper. Fortunately for us, the Associated Charities was one of his assignments. Many a time he has come to our office just to sit in a little rocking chair which we had, to rest for a few minutes before going on. He was a good interpreter of our work and never wrote "sob stories." Other reporters have done good work for us; but the tragedy of Charlie Giblin's life and our personal association with him has remained with me as one of my sad memories.

EDUCATION OF CLIENTS AND WORKERS 

There were two other lines of work which we carried out with, I think, some degree of success, the education of our clients, and the education of our workers, both paid and volunteer. Two plans for the training of our clients have been explained, the sewing school which did such excellent work under Mrs. C. C. Houghton, and the Home Savings Society. 

In 1912, Mrs. C. L. Smith came to us as visiting housekeeper. To quote from the report for 1912-13, "Her work is the logical following out of the objects for which the Associated Charities was organized, as stated in the by-laws, viz. 'to encourage thrift, self-dependence, and industry, through friendly intercourse, advice and sympathy.' She is simply doing intensive work where it seems to be most needed." Mrs. Smith was with us until 1928, when she was obliged to resign because of failing health. 

We did not fill her place for two reasons, the difficulty of finding a woman with just the right temperament, education and experience such as Mrs. 44

Smith had, and the necessity of adding another visitor to our staff without increasing our expenses too much.

These were direct projects which we knew were of value at the time we were carrying them out. But I was always troubled by the knowledge that we were reaching so few of those who needed our special help. The evening classes at the Girls' Trade School and the Girls' Club have gone a long way towards meeting these needs. But they do not take the place of the personal interest and supervision in the home which all three of our plans provided. The solution, as I see it, is a larger staff of visitors so that the number of assignments to each visitor could be lessened and more intensive work done in each family.

But perhaps our first responsibility in education in social work is the training of our own visitors, both paid and volunteer. In the early days, this group was so small that it was not until 1902 that it seemed necessary to put our training on any more formal basis than personal contacts and supervision. So that what I think was of less importance to the development of good social work, that is the teaching of our clients, came earlier in time than the classes for our visitors. From 1902 to 1908, we had classes for the study and discussion of social work. We had a large attendance of volunteers showing that our efforts were appreciated. In 1904, Miss Adeline M. Bisco, who for many years gave most faithful volunteer service, first as a Home Savings collector, and later as secretary of the Board of Directors of which she is still a member, wrote for our annual report, "The meetings of the Social Study Class have been of great value to the members, and their regular attendance has given proof of their appreciation of this opportunity of becoming better acquainted with the practical side of work among the poor." The business depression of 1908 brought such an increase of work that we were not able to go on with the classes.

In questioning why certain apparently good lines of work were relinquished, the fact must be borne in mind that the first responsibility of a family welfare society is to the families under its care. When the pressure of work becomes too heavy, other plans must be deferred to a more con- 45

venient time. I recall that one serious depression was called by social workers, "The year of the great interruption," recognizing that while we ought to make every effort to live up to our standards, it is not possible to do all that we want to do unless we limit our intake arbitrarily; and to me this has never seemed right. It is quite possible that the fifty-first applicant, whom we refuse to consider, may need us more and make better use of the service we can give than the fiftieth, whom we have accepted. I am not quite sure that I am right in this. Perhaps more real good would be accomplished if we limited our intake to the number which we knew we could handle with some degree of efficiency. But granting this I think the limitation should be made by groups and not by numbers. 

I have always believed that in addition to the professional training which is so necessary to meet the new problems which are constantly arising, social workers should have sufficient knowledge of allied subjects, such as law and medicine, to recognize specific problems, and to know where to obtain the advice needed to meet the difficulty. For instance, I was asked at one time by the executive of another society to go into court as a witness in a case on which I had not a particle of legal evidence. It was plain that this executive did not know what constituted legal evidence. What he wanted was the evidence of one of our visitors who did go as a witness and helped materially to win the case.

In pursuance of this idea on the training of our staff, in 1924 we asked six of our leading physicians to talk to our visitors and others who might be interested, on symptoms of disease. In explaining my thought to one of them I said "I do not expect our visitors to become diagnosticians but I do want them to recognize symptoms for which a physician should be consulted." "I know," he answered, "you want your visitors to be intelligent observers." And that was just what I was after. These lectures were eminently successful. The other social service organizations were invited, and the Public Library gave us a small hall which was packed at every meeting.

The following winter, we asked four lawyers and Mr. Becker of the 

Public Welfare Department to give a series of talks on legal facts which 46

social workers ought to know. These lectures also proved valuable and popular. At both series of talks, the number and kind of questions asked showed how keen was the interest. One lawyer who had given a lecture said to the one who was to follow him, "You would best take your law books with you. They will ask you questions you never thought of." It so happened that a question was asked the lawyer to whom this was said. He gave the legal answer, but added "You would never have such a situation to deal with." While the fact was that the visitor who asked the question had arrived, quite breathless in her haste lest she should miss the lecture and the opportunity to ask the question. She had come to the meeting directly from a family in which the problem she had asked about, and it was a serious one, existed in a particularly aggravated form. 

I want to record here my gratitude to the physicians and lawyers, all busy men, for their cordial response to my request, and for the valuable talks which they gave. Not only were they helpful on these special occasions, but in everyday difficulties which arose, both the professional men and the business men were always most cordial in giving advice and assistance to me, to the society, and to our clients. In going over our history, I have realized more than ever before, how much the Associated Charities owes to the citizens of Worcester for such success as it has had in its daily work and in laying the foundations in those early days when we had many difficult problems to deal with and often much discouragement. 

But our efforts to spread the knowledge of our work and to educate our visitors were, as I have said, subject to constant interruptions. And I want to emphasize again in answer to the question as to how much time we ought to give to education, especially the education of the public which is so essential, our first duty is the care of the needy family, whether the need is for coal and groceries, or for understanding, advice and direction. 

When through business depression, the need of material relief increases many fold, we must do our part in meeting that need first, and doing it as well as we can. No one who has not been a social worker during a period of “hard times,” can have any conception of the strain which the workers feel. To meet the needs adequately and wisely, to do it without devitalizing 47

by niggardly giving, or demoralizing by too lavish aid, is a burden almost overwhelming. 

BUSINESS DEPRESSIONS 

There have been four periods of serious business troubles and consequent suffering since the Associated Charities was organized, not including the present one with which we are struggling, by all odds the worst with which we have had to deal. 

The way in which the problem was handled in 1893-4, has already been described. It must have done much, I am sure, to establish the Associated Charities in the confidence of the public. 

The next panic came in the winter of 1907-08. That year the number of families dealt with jumped from 480 to 935. Many pitiful stories were told us of actual suffering from cold and hunger. Many times it was necessary to buy groceries from some near-by store, enough for a meal or two instead of waiting to send an order through the regular channels. A committee of clergymen was formed, the Charities Committee of the Ministers' Meeting, to assist in obtaining funds to meet the emergency. Money was also raised through newspaper appeals, and the papers gave generously of their space. The Swedish Charitable Association reported especially heavy demands, saying that there had been more requests for aid than at any time during the previous eight years. Mr. Brown of the Public Welfare 

Department, asked through the papers that shoes, rubbers and clothing be sent to the Associated Charities, from which he could also obtain supplies for his own applicants as needed. There was a generous response to this request, so much so that the owners of our building gave us an empty room in which to store the goods. 

In making the appeal, Mr. Brown said that in February 1908, there were three times as many appeals for aid as in February 1907, and that conditions were the worst he had ever known. 

There were two reports in March of children stealing food because they were hungry. Mr. Robert Northridge was so distressed at the thought of hungry children that he agreed to give bread to any one who applied. 48

Realizing how demoralizing it is for able-bodied men to ask aid, and to receive it without working for it, we conceived the plan of sending men, especially those of whom we were a little doubtful, to the Boys' Club to scrub floors in payment. We made it a definite business proposition, so much an hour, the men being paid in coal and groceries, the value of which corresponded with the number of hours worked. There were several advantages in this scheme. It weeded out the men who did not want to work. It was real work, as the men could see for themselves. And it did not take work out of the regular market. In a building such as the Boys' 

Club, there is always the possibility of more cleaning to be done than the appropriation for that purpose can cover. Some of the men tried to shirk, giving all sorts of excuses as to why they had not been able to do the work. 

But when they found that our decision was final, no more aid until work already planned was finished, it did not take them long to reach the building ready to finish their stint. On the other hand, we had men who willingly worked over time "just to finish up the job." 

There were, I think, more "sob stories" told during this panic than during any other. The newspaper publicity, although doubtless necessary, in order to obtain the funds needed, added to the difficulties of the situation because of the attitude of some of the unemployed. They read the papers as well as those to whom appeals for contributions were made. As a result, it was not an uncommon thing for an applicant to come to the office and practically demand a share of the money raised, on the score that he was out of work and so one of the people for whom the fund had been raised, and that his personal circumstances were no concern of ours. It was quite impossible to explain to some of them that the money was given to relieve actual suffering, that we were responsible for the right use of it and could not pass it out indiscriminately.

FEW UNPLEASANT EXPERIENCES 

In spite of such incidents as these, and they were sometimes very disagreeable, it is surprising how few really unpleasant experiences I had in all the thirty-five years. I recall only two cases of drunken men coming to the 49

office. One man came in late one afternoon, assuring us that he wanted to sign the pledge. Fortunately, Mr. Halleck Bartlett was in the office. He gently guided the man to the head of the stairs, saying to him that he was sure that it was the Y.M.C.A. across the street for which he was looking. 

I never thought it wise to ask the Y.M.C.A. whether the man arrived. 

The other man came when Mr. David W. Armstrong was in the office. I told the man to walk straight out of the door and down the stairs, and he went. When I took Mr. Armstrong to task for not coming to my assistance, he said calmly that he did not think that I needed any help. 

But although we did not have many disagreeable incidents, we sometimes had very funny ones. I recall one in particular. I was writing at my desk rather late one evening when the telephone bell rang. Thinking it might be one of my family calling to suggest that it was time to come home, I answered it. A woman's voice, apparently very indignant, said "There is a drunken woman on my doorstep and I want her removed at once."

I suggested that she report to the police. But in spite of the absurdity of the request, or rather demand, there was a grain of satisfaction in realizing that this woman thought of us as a social service organization and not wholly as a relief agency.

And that reminds me of another drunken woman for whom we did feel responsibility. I never knew why Miss Stiles took such a special interest in Maggie unless it was because they were almost neighbors, although the short walk between the two houses brought one into an entirely different environment.

Maggie drank occasionally. But as I have said, we were prone to ask why men and women drank. In this case we thought we knew why, and we were sorry for her. One day, when Maggie had a little infant only a few weeks old, one of the children came to Miss Stiles, crying, saying that her mother was lying on the floor drunk and there was no one to care for the baby. After careful consideration we decided on a rather radical step. We took the baby around the corner to a good woman whom we knew, explained conditions to her and asked her to take the baby. She was a good sport and accepted the responsibility. Then we went straight to the 50

Chief of Police, told him what we had done and why. He knew Maggie and he knew us, and he approved our action heartily. 

When Maggie finally came to her senses and found her baby gone, she was heartbroken, frightened and repentant. She thought she would never have her baby back. Of course the baby was returned to her. She really did reform. The experience had been too severe a lesson for her to forget. 

Sometime afterwards in speaking of the family, Mrs. Coes said "I believe Miss Stiles will take Maggie to heaven with her after all." And 

Miss Stiles' instant response was, "Oh, I hope not."

DEPRESSION OF 1913-14 

of The next business trouble after the one of 1907-8, began in the winter 1914. In spite of the fact that the number of families in distress, and the expenditures for relief increased materially, no special effort was made to raise money and the Associated Charities found itself at the end of its fiscal year, one thousand dollars in debt. When in the fall of 1914 it became evident that another hard winter was before us, the Chamber of Commerce appointed a committee, The Bureau of Employment and Relief of the Chamber of Commerce, to consider the situation, and to raise money from members of the Chamber to aid families who had never before had aid, whose distress was due to unemployment. Mr. C. G. Washburn was chairman of this Committee, Mr. Albert Stratton, treasurer, and Mr. John 

W. Odlin, clerk. All investigations were made by the Associated Charities. 

Extra visitors were employed whose salaries were paid by the Bureau. This was the only overhead expense which the Bureau had, as the Associated Charities gave the use of its office and equipment, and also supervised the work. Weekly meetings were held at which detailed reports were given of the work done and the families aided. The Bureau asked that all records of families whom they aided be destroyed, unless it was evident that there were problems other than relief which the Associated Charities should handle, or that the need for aid should continue beyond the life of the Bureau. It was evident that this request was founded on the erroneous idea that the records of the Associated Charities were public property and could 51

be read by anyone. It occasioned no little surprise when Mr. C. G. Washburn, who had been a member of our Board for fifteen years, said that he had never seen a record.

Before beginning its work, the Bureau made a brief but comprehensive survey of work being done by fifteen social service organizations and the Overseers of the Poor. The work of the Bureau was carried on from 

November 1, 1914, to April 1, 1915. By this time, the money collected was practically exhausted; and the fact that business conditions had begun to improve made it seem unwise to attempt to raise more money. The 

Bureau, consequently, closed its operations, leaving it to the regular societies to carry on the unemployment cases with their usual work. It is interesting to read in the report of the Bureau, written in the spring of 1915, the following paragraph: "It is a common complaint that public work is not wisely distributed, and that the average municipality is more ready in times of depression to pay out money for relief than to undertake work which, though not immediately necessary, must at some time be done x x x x. It is better for the city to spend its money through almost any department than through the Poor Department." This advice has a strangely familiar sound. 

I have heard the same thing during every panic since my first experience in Boston, during the depression of 1893-4. I wonder if the new Federal plans are going to teach us a better way of handling unemployment relief.

In this depression, an effort was made to provide regular work. Money was appropriated by the State for forestry work, $3000 of which came to Worcester, and was used under the direction of the City Forester, Mr. 

Neale. To quote from our report for 1914-15, "He (Mr. Neale) asked the

Associated Charities to certify that the men were Worcester residents and had dependents. This necessitated a call in the homes of applicants not already known to us, in order to verify their statements. Several times we found that men who claimed to be out of work and to have families to support were not known even by name in the houses where they told us they lived. This work, provided by the Forestry Department, was a great help, our only regret being that there was not more of it. If we sent word to ten men that they could have a few days' work, twenty-five appeared. Two or 52

three refused the work, but the number was very small; while several who were physically unable to do it were allowed to try because they were so eager to earn what their families needed rather than have it given to them."

WAR YEARS 

In going over the years of the war, both from our reports and from my memory, I have been surprised to find how little it seems to have affected our work directly. Doctor Nichols, in his report for 1914-15, referred with keen appreciation to the increased contributions which we had received in spite of the demands upon our sympathy and our pocketbooks for the nations in the war section.

My own report for 1916-17, referring to the effect of the war on our own work says, "The strain under which we are all living, the change from the ordinary routine of life to the abnormal conditions of preparing for war, has engendered a restless spirit, even a certain lawlessness, which is showing itself in our work in the lack of family responsibility. Constantly women are coming to us for aid because the husband and father has deserted. Often these women are left with little infants to care for. 

Whether the men have enlisted under false names and with false stories, we, of course, do not know. But personally, I am convinced that the increase in this cause of poverty is indirectly, if not directly, to be attributed to psychological reasons due to the war." In my report for 1917-18, I find this problem of desertion again emphasized as serious. Apparently many desertions were for two quite different reasons, for the sake of enlisting, and to escape the draft. Of course, when we could prove that the man had enlisted, the family became the responsibility of the Red Cross. But often we could only surmise that this was the case from words dropped by the man just before leaving and afterwards repeated to us. 

I wish we had a more complete statistical report on desertions for the war years. But by this time our more thorough investigations had made us so doubtful of trying to formulate "Causes of Poverty," even contributory and complicating factors, that although we considered causes carefully in 53

studying the problems of each individual family, we did not try to summarize them or to classify cases. 

Indirectly the Associated Charities suffered from the war. We lost several of our valued volunteer workers, and our assistant secretary, Miss Billings, who left us to become secretary of the Worcester Chapter of the 

Red Cross.

Our reports show that our relief expenditures increased during the war, not because of the larger number of families aided, but because of the much higher prices charged for all the necessities of life. The increase in the cost of the simplest articles of food is given elsewhere. And this was just as true for other expenses, such as rent. Wages were also higher, so that ablebodied men who could work, could meet the situation. But there were also many families who had fallen into distress from sickness, accident, death, and other causes not related to the question of employment or wages. 

Many people seem not to understand why we should have any distress to deal with when there is plenty of work. They do not consider all the other troubles which enter into life and complicate it. In fact, people have often said to me, "Of course you are not busy now that there is plenty of work for men." And one woman asked me if I ever had time in the office to do the fine needlework in which I formerly took great delight. 

INFLUENZA 

The next period which taxed our strength of body and soul as well as our resources, was the winter of 1918-19. There was much unemployment and part time work due to the business changes following the armistice, while the cost of food and rent which had increased so rapidly during the had not to any extent been lowered. war,

I think, however, that we could have met these difficulties without undue disturbance, if it had not been for the serious epidemic of influenza. 

In a large proportion of families coming to us that winter, one person or more often the whole family had influenza. And we had direct results to deal with long after the epidemic had passed. In many cases where the man of the family was the victim, there was no question that he went back to 54

work too soon, rather than remain dependent, with serious results to his health later on. It was no uncommon thing to have a woman tell us, long after the sickness, that her husband had never been well since he had the influenza.

We worked in close co-operation with the Society for District Nursing in providing food and fuel. In addition, we lent one of our visitors who had her own car, to help in the distribution of food from one of the diet kitchens established to send cooked food to families in which there was no one able to prepare it even when there was food in the house. The rest of the staff gladly worked overtime in order to release this visitor certain hours as she was needed. It was a most distressing experience; and as I said, the results later on were even more difficult to deal with. 

DEPRESSION OF 1920-22 

In November of 1920, we found ourselves in another period of business difficulties which lasted until March 1922-the longest period of unemployment we had had to handle up to that time. And again we had an interruption of our legitimate work because of the impossibility of carrying it on when we had to devote so much of our time and thought to relief-giving. But this time we had a new element to deal with which greatly lightened our burden. It was not necessary to make special appeals for money with the attendant publicity which always adds so much to the difficulties of wise relief-giving. The Community Chest, or the Worcester 

Welfare Federation, as it was then called, stood by us in a most wonderful manner.

THE COMMUNITY CHEST 

The question of a federation of social agencies of the city had been discussed before the war, but no action had been taken other than to study the experiences of other cities. After the war the subject was again considered, and the plan practically as we know it now carried through. After the State passed the law changing the name of the Overseers of the Poor to the Department of Public Welfare, it was necessary to change the name of 55

the Welfare Federation to Community Chest, because of the confusion and misapprehension on the part of the public. The new name, Community 

Chest, has the advantage of agreeing with the name used in many, I think most other cities. Although realizing the advisability of the change and voting for it, I have always regretted the necessity, for I think the old name more nearly expressed the spirit and work of our Community Chest. 

During the first few years of the Federation, I was constantly receiving letters from my co-workers in other cities, asking me to tell them confidentially how the plan worked. I was very glad to assure them of its success. I recall one meeting of the executives of the family societies of 

New England, where I was literally cross-questioned in regard to the Federation and our relation with it. One man who was present was especially opposed and critical. Afterwards he left the family work to become the executive of the Community Chest in a western city, much to the amusement of some of us who knew his attitude. One general secretary who was at the meeting, later on said that what I told them of our release from financial worries seemed like a fairy story. 

In April 1920, after the Federation was formed, each society was asked to make out a budget for eighteen months. I objected, on the score that it was impossible to foretell what the relief problem might be for so long a period. We were told to figure on a conservative budget, with the promise that if there should be business troubles and consequent unemployment, the Federation would stand for the necessary relief. When my fears proved correct, and unemployment with its consequent need for relief descended upon us, this promise was splendidly kept, not only in providing funds for relief, but also in giving the extra assistance in the office for doing the work as well as we could under pressure. 

Again we were assured that we had not pauperized our clients, by the way the applications dropped after March 1, 1922, when men were again employed. But from that time to the present serious situation there were constant fluctuations in business conditions and, consequently, in employment. For months everything would go well, then there would be an influx of applicants because men could not get work even when the country as a 56

present whole seemed prosperous. I am not sufficiently well trained in economics to explain this even if it were a part of my task in writing these chronicles to do so. But I know that the introduction of machinery which required fewer men in one operation, and the new compensation laws which made employers unwilling to take on middle-aged men who were new to their work, even if competent both mentally and physically, complicated the situation. Adjustments to the new laws could not be made immediately, and so individuals suffered. The last two years I was with the society, there was much unemployment, a forerunner, I now believe, of the condition. Mr. Adie, who made a survey of the family work in Worcester in the summer of 1929, said that there was a subtle unemployment situation all over the country which could not be explained. It seems to me that there must have been a weeding out of the partially incompetent or incapacitated in spite of the fact that there was apparently little general unemployment. It should be borne in mind in considering unemployment that, generally speaking, the first men discharged are the least valuable men, who have had the lowest wages, and are not adaptable in finding new jobs. 

Their wages have been such that there has been no margin for savings. 

Consequently, they must ask for aid sooner and be aided much longer than many of the victims of unemployment. They are also the last taken on when business improves. As a result the Associated Charities feels the beginning of business depression before conditions are bad enough to demand public attention.

The present situation I am not going to touch on, as I have had no part in handling it. I can only express my sincere sympathy, not only for the victims, but also for those who have been and still are working so desperately hard in both our public and private societies. 

CO-ORDINATION OF FAMILY SOCIETIES 

At the time the Federation was formed there were four private family societies in Worcester, the United Jewish Charities, the Swedish Charitable Association, the Swedish-Finnish Association, and the Associated Charities. 

The first three were, as their names indicate, racial societies, each society 57

confining its work to its own group-the Associated Charities taking the families which should come under the care of a private society, but for whom no special association, such as the racial societies referred to, existed. 

The United Jewish Charities had at the time an office and a paid secretary. The Swedish and Swedish-Finnish Societies did not have offices, and all work was done by the Boards of Directors. There had always been close and friendly co-operation between these societies and the Associated Charities. But after the formation of the Federation, it was felt that if a society did not have an office and an executive the work could not be done so promptly and efficiently as the Federation, of which all four of the societies were members, desired.

Early in 1925, the Federation brought forward a plan by which these societies, especially the Swedish and Swedish-Finnish, should come into co-ordination with the Associated Charities, a plan which was gladly accepted. The Swedish Charitable Association still continues this relationship; but I understand that the Swedish-Finnish Society, a much smaller organization, has turned all its work over to the Associated Charities. In the beginning, both these societies kept their own identity and continued to hold their regular monthly meetings to which all families for which they were responsible were referred for discussion and decision. All applicants for emergency relief were referred to the Associated Charities. A special visitor from our staff was assigned to take over the work of investigation and give immediate relief if needed; and each society had its own file of records. This plan is still practiced by the Swedish Charitable Society. 

A Family Welfare Committee was formed, consisting of delegates from each of the four societies, the United Jewish Charities being included on this committee. A definite arrangement was made as to just what families should be accepted by these societies and what ones should be referred to the Department of Public Welfare, although it was understood that these rules could be set aside in cases of serious emergency where immediate action seemed necessary. These rules are practically the same as those unwritten rules under which the Associated Charities had always worked, but they had never before been formulated. The plan worked well 58

rent, from the beginning; and I am sure that neither the Swedish nor the Swedish- 

Finnish Societies would go back to the old way of depending on volunteers. 

In fact, such a method would have been practically impossible with the great increase of applicants during the depression of the past few years; and any other plan would have necessitated the added expense of office equipment, and a paid staff. I am told, although I have not verified the statement, that this co-ordination, so planned that each society keeps its own separate existence and its own records, and is responsible to the Community Chest directly for its own budget and relief expenses, and yet works under the direction of the Associated Charities, is the only organization of the kind in the United States. If there are other cities trying this scheme 

I should be glad to know about them.

RELIEF 

The subject of relief in the work of the Associated Charities of Worcester has only been touched on incidentally. But its development, not only in our society but all over the country, has been most interesting. 

As was said in the beginning of this paper, relief-giving had fallen into such disrepute that at first the newly organized charities discarded it as no part of their program. At the first National Conference which I attended in 1895, one of our leading thinkers and teachers said, "We should stand firm on the rock, on which most of us have chosen to stand, of not dealing directly in material relief. If we do we shall secure the relief when it is needed all the more gladly; and, above all, we shall cultivate the habit of helping the needy to get work, and a dozen other things of greater price than alms."

Relief by work, woodyards and workrooms, was recommended. I wonder what has become of those woodyards and workrooms. There may still be some in existence, but I do not know where they are. And again the same speaker said "So-called emergencies are usually exaggerated.” 

And this was directly after the panic of 1893-4, when all of us who were engaged in social work must have seen real suffering. 

The complacency with which these statements about relief were made 59

in the early days, by delegates from the larger cities where there were old established relief societies to which they could turn and did turn, as I happen to know, in such emergencies as the speaker was willing to acknowledge did sometimes exist, was and still is occasionally exasperating. What the societies in the smaller cities, without any source of prompt relief outside their own funds and the Overseers of the Poor, could do, was not suggested. 

Compare this attitude with the statement of a New York worker at the Boston Conference in 1930, “Perhaps the first need of a worker is to discard the fear of relief."

As early as 1902, at the National Conference, some delegates were outspoken against the theory of "no relief." At that Conference Mr. Weller, of Washington, said, "Our leaders are beginning to ask whether the relation of relief work to other charitable activities is not mainly a question of expediency." And later in the same address, he said, "The fact is that a charity organization is incomplete in so far as it merely excludes or ignores relief work." I could not find any report of the discussion of this paper and after all these years can not recall it. But I do remember seeing the general secretary of one of the largest and most efficient relief societies in the country, fairly dancing in the aisle in his delight at the swing of the Conference towards his point of view and the practice of his society. Nine years later at the Boston Conference, Mr. Almy said, “Adequate relief is in my opinion the most neglected subject in modern charity and our discussion and our use of relief are alike inadequate." These quotations make it evident that our early struggle to do the right thing in giving aid was just the same as the problems and difficulties of other societies. But we accepted the responsibility of giving relief frankly and openly earlier in our existence than most of the new charity organizations. And I know that our intention was to give sufficient aid, adequate as we then understood it, at least. Moreover, these quotations emphasize the changes which have taken place in our philosophy and practice in giving aid. I am quite sure that the going into effect of the Mother's Aid law in 1913, which made the study of budgets necessary in order to plan for the best use of a minimum allowance, and the attention which was given to food values during the 60

war, helped us to a better understanding of adequate relief than we had had before.

Worcester had no relief society, no trust funds for the poor, and no resources for relief outside its own collections and the aid which the Overseers of the Poor disbursed. Therefore, the Associated Charities gave relief from its own funds, and thereby became a heretic in social work. Indeed, one fine conscientious family worker was so imbued with the belief that we should not give aid from our own funds that she suggested some camouflage which would give results but conceal the method. I did not say what I think perhaps I would say now, that the plan did not seem to me quite square; that it was a case of tweedledum and tweedledee which did not appeal to me.

That we were not always successful in giving sufficient aid, or in really ridding ourselves of our inherited fear of the dangers of relief is, perhaps, illustrated by the following experience. Not many years ago a probation officer in another city telephoned us that a Worcester girl was under arrest in that city, and asked us to look up the family. I remembered them distinctly, and the effort which the woman made to help in the support of the family, while the children were running the streets. Of course the earnings of the woman cut down the relief which the Associated Charities or the Overseers of the Poor gave, and according to the theories of the time, helped her to keep her self-respect. But what about her self-respect when her daughter was under arrest? I said to the probation officer, "If we had known as much about relief when this young woman was a child as we do now, her mother would have stayed at home and probably brought up the children properly." I say "probably," because, after all, these deductions are not provable. But on general principles we think the mother would have done a better job than the streets did. 

But in emphasizing our relief-giving or explaining why it seemed necessary, I do not want to overlook, or in any way belittle the personal work which we did for our families, and that it was done promptly and, if we can judge from results, with a fair degree of efficiency. 

For instance, one day when I was at lunch, a physician telephoned, 61

saying that a certain woman was ill with pneumonia. If she could be taken to the hospital within an hour she had a good chance for recovery, but there were six little children to be considered. It did not take me long to reach the house. Fortunately, it being at noon, the children were home from school. I told the woman what could be done and saw her start off in the ambulance. Then with the baby in my arms and five small children trailing along behind me, we went up Southbridge Street to the Day Nursery which took them in. There were no taxicabs in those days and few telephones with which to summon help, so we walked. I told the oldest boy to be sure to meet his father on his way home and explain to him what had happened, so that he would not come home to a deserted tenement. That was thirtyfive or more years ago. But I talked with the woman recently over the telephone. It would have been a tragedy if she had died and left those little children motherless. Yet because everything turned out well, I must confess that what I think of, in recalling the episode, is how ridiculous our little procession must have looked.

Two other incidents stand out in my memory which illustrate the emphasis which we placed on friendly service. 

DRAWING UP A WILL 

One of the strangest bits of social service which I ever had to do was drawing up a will.

A little cottage had been so burned that all the woodwork was charred. 

The family who owned and occupied the house consisted of a man, his wife, and his brother. The house stood jointly in the name of the man and his wife; and he was seriously ill. In fact, he died in the hospital not long after 

I first knew them. The house had been built or renovated by the man with the aid of his brother, who had contributed both work and materials. 

After the owner went to the hospital, his wife became obsessed with the idea that she had a bad heart and might not outlive her husband very long. She said if she died, following her husband's death, the property would of course go to her people, but it should go to her husband's brother. So she insisted that I should write her will immediately, which I did. I took 62

it to Mr. Henry L. Parker, who was then President of the Associated 

Charities. He told me that to be legal it should have three witnesses instead of two as I had, otherwise it would stand. So, to ease the woman's mind, I went back that evening, rewrote the will on the corner of a stand, by the light of a candle, in that half-burned house, and had it properly witnessed. I did this simply to comfort the woman, telling her that when she was better she must go to a lawyer and have her will properly drawn. 

Some years later, for the woman did not die immediately as she feared she would, her brother-in-law came to the office, asked me if I recalled the incident, and would go to the Probate Court and acknowledge my signature, as I had been one of the witnesses. Of course I went. And there was the will which I had written on a sheet of notepaper. It had been presented for probate, allowed, and is on file in the Probate Court. The brother-in-law had inherited the property as the woman had insisted that he should. 

THE SHOESTRING MAN 

Perhaps the most dramatic experience we ever had was in literally putting a man "back on his feet," the shoestring seller who had lost both feet in a railroad accident. At the request of the Survey, then called 

Charities, I wrote the story which was one of three hectographed and sent to all our societies as examples of the kind of stories they would like. 

Before sending the story, I consulted the man himself, and told him why I wanted to send it. He not only consented but added "Why can't people give a fellow a chance? They give him something to eat; and when that is gone he is just as bad off as ever. Why can't they give him a chance?"

I told this to the editor of Charities who published it as a separate item, adding that no more severe arraignment of mere relief-giving had ever been made. Later the story was used to illustrate a point in Miss Conyngton's book, "How to Help."

This is the story practically as I wrote it for Charities: "He was selling shoestrings on the corner, a cripple with both feet gone, but his face attracted attention and interest. He gave his address 63

readily, and when the secretary of the Associated Charities called, introduced his wife and child. "The investigation revealed several things: first that there was immediate need; second, that there were no natural resources from which aid could be obtained; third, that he must be helped to obtain other employment than the precarious peddling which was practically little better than begging; and fourth and best of all, it was evident that in spite of the need, the family were not beggars. "How each step towards reconstruction was taken would make too long a story. Briefly, it was something like this. A wheel-chair, for which there was no further need, was sold, and with the proceeds the rent of the damp, rear tenement in which they were living was paid. A volunteer visitor was interested who said 'The man must have artificial feet; and I will collect the money.' Then the man said a surprising thing. 'Two good artificial feet will cost one hundred and fifty dollars. Give me thirty dollars, and I will make them myself, and have the tools to work with afterwards.' The money was given him to try the experiment, and he succeeded. The first time he tried to walk any distance on his new feet, he came straight to the office to see if I would know him. While working on his feet, he had also been selling shoestrings, making enough to supply actual needs. Without any question he was making an honest effort to carry on a legitimate business, and felt humiliated when money was given him outright. But once on his feet, there was never one day of going back to his old corner on his knees. "Then began the struggle for self-support as a normal man in competition with other men. Work was obtained for him in a factory, and was bravely undertaken. But standing all day at a bench proved too hard. Discouraging days followed in which I fear there was not always enough to eat. His wife worked all she could, sewing, washing even, although a frail woman. But with a little help here and there, a loan occasionally, always repaid, charity sewing when regular sewing failed, aid a few times when absolutely necessary and, finally, the difficulties were cleared away. "Today the man is walking about like other men without even a cane. He has his own little repair shop and a comfortable home. Better 64

still, he and his wife have made many friends who will stand by in an emergency.'

SPECIAL FUNDS 

Our first year's report shows that $571.41 was contributed to a "B.I." (Benevolent Individual) Fund for temporary relief. But in the second year, no distinction was made between regular funds and relief contributions. 

That year $711.83 was spent for relief, the report of these expenditures being a part of the treasurer's report, and the bills evidently paid from the regular funds. The third year's report shows that the same plan was continued. But the fourth report, for 1893-4, states that $374.30 was paid for temporary relief, evidently for coal and groceries; and that $140.50 was contributed and spent for special aid, with the explanatory paragraph, "This has been used for the purchase of flannels, shoes, stockings, delicacies for the sick, moving families from unhealthy tenements, and in various ways which in the judgment of the Conference seemed advisable." 

The special fund was made up of contributions given directly to Mrs. 

Lee and later to me with the request that the money be spent for some family or object in which the donor was particularly interested. Often, of course, we solicited such gifts from persons who, we knew, were concerned in the family or the problem involved. So it became the practice to pay for coal and groceries, which in actual destitution must be provided promptly, from the treasurer's funds for which the Board of Directors was responsible; and to use the special fund for other aid which as a rule did not have to be given immediately, and perhaps could be refused if there was no money in hand to pay for it, and no one sufficiently interested to give us the money. 

But, although I am speaking from memory, I think it is safe to say that we never had to refuse to meet a real need because of lack of funds. I know that is true of fuel and groceries, and I am almost as sure that it was true when the need was for shoes and warm clothing. 

ADEQUATE RELIEF 

The subject which interested me more than relief methods was, and still is, the question of how much to give. We talked "adequate relief" and 65

we meant to give it, but we had, in the early days, no standards. They had to be thought out and worked out. Food values, as such, were not considered to any great extent, that is, scientifically. The first aid was practically always in food. There was "nothing to eat in the house." As a matter of economy, we gave what we knew would provide the most nourishment for the money spent. We had learned this from our home experience, and the experience of our mothers and grandmothers. We did not know about vitamins and calories. We sent on our grocery order, flour, oatmeal, cornmeal, beans, potatoes and other inexpensive and nourishing foods. Fuel also seemed a necessity and was supplied. I do not think we were extravagant or niggardly. Nor were we any more ignorant than the majority of housekeepers and homemakers who succeeded in raising their children to sturdy manhood and womanhood. We gave what families with small incomes bought for themselves. 

We felt in planning aid for a family, that it should not be so generous as to leave no incentive for effort on the part of the recipient, and should not cause resentment on the part of the hard working and thrifty neighbors next door, who, perhaps not knowing all the circumstances, might feel indignant that the children in a family who could not support itself, were just as well fed and just as well clothed as their own. On the other hand, to scrimp in relief might mean the temptation to ask more aid from some other source, a practice which might easily degenerate into outright begging. 

I wonder if it was this dilemma which brought about the custom of giving food and fuel, but refusing to pay rent. Perhaps the thought was that rent does not have to be paid daily, nor as a rule weekly in order to insure shelter; and that probably by such odd jobs as the man could pick up if he was forced to, he could 

Whatever the excuse or reason, the rent. pay the practice of not paying rent seems to have become well established, a practice which was, I must confess, often broken when the circumstances seemed to warrant such action.

In the early days, we gave relief almost wholly in kind, not in cash. 

Orders for coal and groceries were sent to the dealers for delivery, the orders for groceries varying in amounts and variety, according to the size 66

and needs of the family. One reason for giving relief in kind was to make sure that the family was properly fed on the smallest expenditure of money. 

One factor, apparently never thought of in discussions on relief, especially in planning budgets when a cash allowance is made, is that it requires a rather high degree of intelligence or training to obtain full value out of a minimum expenditure. I do not mean to imply that many of our clients do not spend money to the best advantage. They certainly do, sometimes better than we can do with all our study. But on the other hand, there are many, possibly the majority of those who fall into distress, who do not know how to spend money; and for this group, at least, I believe the grocery order, not arbitrary, but given with due regard to the number to be fed, the ages of the children, and the racial habits and antipathies, is best. 

Moreover, some of our most worth-while and self-respecting people are the very ones, who, in an emergency such as the unexpected giving out of a pair of rubbers, would skimp on the food budget already reduced to the minimum, in order to buy the rubbers rather than come to us to ask for them. This might not do any harm if it happened only once or twice in a winter. But in a large family it might happen often enough to be a serious menace to health.

Little by little, as we learned more of food values, we added to our list of articles of food, others which in the earlier days would have seemed luxuries. I recall one Director's meeting at which we had a discussion on this subject. One man, most careful in spending the money given by our contributors, said, "Your people will all have scurvy. You are not giving them fruit enough." Others followed with like comment, advising more fruit, fresh and canned. I was delighted. They were giving me just the endorsement for a more liberal diet that I needed. But I wanted them to see for themselves, and not merely to consent because I advised it. Probably the 

Board did not realize what I knew, that even a few years before, giving fruit on a charity order would have been considered rank extravagance, not only by our Board, but by any group dealing with relief.

In the winter of 1917, because of the increased cost of living, we became especially anxious about the food which our families who were just barely self-supporting were buying. In order to learn a little more definitely 68

whether there was serious underfeeding, which might result in devitalization, we asked thirteen of our families, representing five different nationalities, to write out for us their menus for each meal for a week. Twelve of them responded readily and carefully. In the thirteenth case, the woman was entirely willing, but her husband said it was none of our affairs and would not let her send it, although we had stood by the family in rather serious difficulties.

Five of these menus I have kept and they are still interesting. I went over all the menus carefully with our visiting housekeeper, who was able from them to make valuable suggestions to these families, and to others, because the lists revealed marketing tendencies when the income was small. 

Even for so few families, it was worth while doing a really constructive piece of work, according to our plan of making each family's problems individual, and also for what we ourselves learned of their difficulties. 

MOVING 

In April of 1922, we were obliged to move from the rooms which we had occupied from the beginning of the organization, because alterations were to be made in the building. As there were vacant rooms across the street in a new building at 44 Pearl Street, we moved into them. This change did not prove so satisfactory as we hoped it would. Room for storage which was promised us, we found after we moved in was not available, and with the natural increase in our work, we were exceedingly crowded. It was, therefore, with great delight that two years later we moved to much pleasanter and more commodious quarters in Wetherell House at 2 State Street. The advantages and the pleasure which our much closer association with several societies made possible by this move, have always seemed to me to balance the disadvantage of being so far from the center of the city, especially from City Hall. 

BOARD OF DIRECTORS

Perhaps the two outstanding are the confidence the public has and has always had, in the men and women who make up the Board, and the help which they as a group and as individuals personally have given the General Secretaries, in working out the problems which always have and probably still do confront the society. CHANGED STANDARDS 

One of the subjects suggested for this sketch was "The changed standards in social work." I can speak only for the family social work. But in that branch, it seems to me that there have been great changes. Three which have esepcially impressed me in my retrospect are, our different attitude towards relief work, the development of case work, and the demand for better trained social workers. 

Our relief practices certainly have changed, as has been discussed earlier in this paper. This has come about through, first, our increased knowledge of food values and of the needs of the human system; and second, because we have learned, as I think we have, that accepting relief, when it is given in the right way and in the spirit of friendliness, is not so pauperizing as we had feared. These are the reasons for the difference in our relief work, I believe, rather than any radical difference in our intentions towards our clients. It is true that in the dispensation of public relief there has been almost a revolution from the days when a family in distress was given a two dollar order of groceries regardless of the size of the family, or the ages of the children. But in the private societies, I have never known any such restriction.

Most of the early applicants came to us because of financial disaster which had deprived them of the bare necessities of life. Apparently this is still true, according to Miss Gordon Hamilton, who said at the Minneapolis Conference in 1931, "Case work's increasing preoccupation with problems of personality and behavior should not delude us into thinking that modern society has been rid of problems of poverty or that family work can evade it.”

We believed that it was the duty of the family social worker to bring the family back to the position of the best of the group to which it belonged and to raise the standard of the family, if possible, by helping it to remove such obstacles to advancement as it could not by its own exertions overcome. In order to help in surmounting the difficulties, we sought causes for the trouble, and a cure for the cause, not a cure for the symptoms, just as is done now, only our methods and our vocabulary were different. Moreover, 71

we early discarded the terms "worthy" and "unworthy"; in fact, I do not remember ever using them. We worked on the theory that everyone is worth something, the "something" in family work being the best we could do towards rehabilitation. We looked only for response to our efforts. 

Often there was long and patient waiting and working before the response came; and often in spite of our efforts it never came. 

The better understanding of psychology and related subjects, and their practical application to our problems seems to me the greatest step forward in our case work. But although we did not have the facilities for studying and treating mental conditions which we have today, we certainly recognized them and took them into account in our study of family troubles. I recall one problem with which I had to deal in Boston. A sewing woman, alone in the world, employed in dressmaking establishments, could not hold a job for any length of time because she was quarrelsome and thought that her fellow workers were persecuting her. As a result, she was constantly in financial distress and was very unhappy. I suggested a bad mental state. 

Probably I said, "I think that woman is insane." An examination was immediately planned and carried out. The diagnosis showed a state of mind so serious, and even dangerous, that she was committed to one of our 

State hospitals.

Personally, while welcoming and making use of all the help which psychology and psychiatry can give us, I have always believed that in many cases, maladjustments, especially family maladjustments, are the result of bad economic conditions which the family cannot control. The man's fear of losing his job, the cuts in hours and wages, the periods of unemployment even when short, have, must have, an injurious effect on nerves. And this situation, in turn, must have a serious reaction on the mental condition of the whole family, on the tranquility and happiness of family life, and also, of course, on the man's efficiency and ability to hold a job. I recall one very definite instance of a man who became hopelessly insane because he felt that he had failed in meeting his responsibility for the support of his family. 

This condition came about before we knew the family so that we did not have the opportunity to try, at least, to prevent it. This statement as to his 72

condition and the cause was a physician's diagnosis and not what we thought about it.

I have always believed, and I do now, that the assurance of stability in self-support, including the support of the family would go far to relieve nerve tension and its results. I rejoice in all the new resources for studying and understanding more thoroughly all forms of bad mental states and for treating them scientifically and adequately before they become serious. 

THE LONG LOOK IN OUR RELATIONS WITH OUR FAMILIES 

I have referred earlier to our growing feeling of responsibility for our families. This led, I am sure, to another change in our case work, that is to our taking a longer look forward in our relations with them. We used to feel that the sooner we could get a family to stand on its own feet and ready to work out its own salvation, the better for the family. I am quite sure that in many cases this was the right attitude. I have seen, every family worker has seen, the development of moral dependence almost as demoralizing as material dependence. But there is no doubt that often we look for results too early in our relation with the family, and perhaps sometimes hope or imagine that we have achieved our object before we have done so. Then, too, in our own society, our staff was so small that we were probably tempted to consider that we had succeeded in our efforts for a family and that we were no longer needed in their lives, because of the pressure of new work which we constantly felt. 

The longer view which we now take of our responsibilities is a step forward in raising our standards of family work. On the other hand, I have never been quite willing to state statistically that we had certain families "under care," when for any reason it had not been wise or possible to keep in touch with them for some weeks or even months. Of course, the reason for keeping a family on our list is that we know the problems are not settled, and that sooner or later, either through the family's initiative or our own, we must take it up again. I am not quite sure that my objection is valid. Perhaps we should say that the family is still under our care until the 73

difficulties are cleared away; or we throw up our hands and say that so far as we are concerned, we have exhausted our resources of helpfulness and must give up the case. It has always seemed to me that by keeping such families nominally "under care," we were claiming a larger volume of work than we were really carrying. This may be a kind of statistical honesty, when the other alternative would have been more nearly true. 

OUR RESPONSIBILITY 

I have felt very keenly that while the community might be in a large measure responsible for the present condition of a family, we as individuals, dealing with the individual family, are responsible for the past only so far as we may have had a share in allowing bad economic and political conditions to exist. In our daily work we could only take things as we found them and do our best for the restoration of the individual family. 

We early recognized the family as our unit of endeavor, and dealt with the individuals as members of the family. I felt, too, that we had no right to spend so much time on our worst families that we did not have sufficient time for those for whom there was hope of recovery. Perhaps again I was wrong. I seem to find many things in these reminiscences which I question. 

Possibly the more hopeful families could have made a recovery without other assistance than a little material aid in an emergency, and the assurance that if things did not improve under their own management, they could turn to us for friendship, understanding and further aid if needed. 

TRAINING OF WORKERS 

In the training of our workers, we are asking much more than we could in the old days, a very definite raising of standards in professional social work. But even then we were demanding just as we do now, the best material available. We did not have the fine schools of social work which we have today, nor so many college women from whom to select. 

There have been, so far, three generations of social workers: the pioneers who experimented and studied the problems for which they believed 74

there should be a solution; second, the group whom they taught what they had learned, in what has been called the apprentice system, the group to which I belong; and third, the group taught in the schools, the material for whose training was obtained through the experience and study of the two preceding generations. It should be remembered to the credit of the pioneers that it was because they themselves were demanding a high standard for those to whom they were to pass on the work that the schools were established. With all the heavy burdens they were carrying, they gave willingly of their time and thought to help in founding the schools and in teaching the apprentice group. 

CRITICISMS OF PIONEER SOCIAL WORK 

In thinking over the early work of organized charity, it is well to bear in mind that we should not criticize the social work, even our own work, done thirty or even twenty years ago by the standards and methods of today. 

Still less should we criticize the work done forty and fifty years ago. We would be a pretty poor group if we had not learned anything in the course of the years. We, who were pioneers, or close to the pioneers, can comfort ourselves with the thought that if we had not laid foundations, the present structure could not have been built. Those who are young enough to be still in social work twenty-five or thirty years from now, will probably look back on the work of today with the same feeling of criticism which we have of our early work. At least, I have sufficient faith in the possibilities of social work, and in the intelligence and devotion of the social workers of the present day to believe that the developments of the years to come will be just as great and just as important as those of the years which have passed. 

MY VISION 

In the past, I had visions which I have never wholly lost-visions of stabilized employment for every worker, adequate to meet the material and social needs of the family, decent living conditions, and the establishment of friendly relations between those whom different opportunities in 75

life had separated rather than differences in mentality or character, differences which had led to misunderstandings and antagonisms between classes. In many individual cases I have seen this vision fulfilled. I have seen families come out of the depths of poverty and despair to become self-respecting citizens.

Perhaps you who are now bearing the heat and burden of the day will see a still larger group brought back to self dependence, self respect, and happiness. And eventually my vision will be fulfilled. 76 তু
