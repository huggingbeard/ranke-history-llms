A. PREFACE 

As President of the American Welfare League, hereinafter called the “League," I submit to the Board of Directors of this League a plan for the complete solution of the emigration problems of the German refugees. I beg for a thorough examination of this Plan and for a decision on whether the League is willing to co-operate in executing it. 

I am mindful of the fact that our "League" was not organized to solve a refugee problem but rather the problem of our unemployed; nevertheless I feel myself urged to recommend to you that we proceed with the solution of the refugee problem first and later on to use the equipment of our colonies for the unemployed of the United States of America. And this can be easily done with slight modifications to our plan and still be in complete harmony with the object and purpose of the “League” and its Constitution. For the "League" has been founded, in order to give work and shelter to homeless and jobless people regardless of their religion, race or nationality. 

As a consequence of political developments in Germany and especially laws against non-Aryans resulting therefrom, approximately one million German people find themselves in greatest spiritual and material distress, because the possibility of continuing to live like human beings in what has been their fatherland up to now has been taken from them. 

They are, therefore, forced to find a new existence in other countries. 

To rescue these people, it is essential to find a rapid and complete solution of this problem. Therefore, he who knows a way whereby the problem at hand may be solved, owes it to God and to his conscience to point out this way.

Inspired by this sense of duty I have prepared a plan for the solution of the German emigration problem, in spite of the seemingly insurmountable obstacles opposing such a solution. 

The first and most difficult obstacle consists of the fact that no other country is willing to receive the German emigrants, at least in no greater number than the immigration quotas fixed by the various countries permit.

The reason for this is to be found in the great number of unemployed, for whom each country is making almost unbearable sacrifices. It is, therefore, understandable that each country seeks to prevent an increase in the number of unemployed. For the German immigrants come with empty hands, as they are not permitted to take their property with them. 

However, they must live. And so they are forced either to take jobs away from the people of the countries to which they immigrate or else to remain unemployed and so become public charges. 

To this must be added the aggravating circumstance that most refugees are Jews and that a strong anti-Jewish current exists in all countries, a fact that must neither be denied nor underrated. 

Because of these great difficulties that are connected with a settling of the German refugees, the international Committee for Refugees has suggested to settle them in an African colony. One is seeking, therefore, a sort of international “dumping place" for a million people who seem to be superfluous in this world, because nobody wants them. It would be a cruel fate for them to be degraded to a kind of "Colony for Outcasts." 

But even this plan is not feasable because of the other great difficulty, namely the question of money. For a simultaneous or reasonably rapid colonization of one million people in an uncultivated colony would require billions of dollars.

The plan worked out by me takes all the difficulties cited in the foregoing into consideration. It offers not only a complete, but also a rapid solution of the refugee problem. 

However, two conditions must be fulfilled, before the execution of the plan can be begun. 

The first condition is that the American Welfare League be willing to take over the solution of the refugee problem according to the plan described in the following. Because this plan can only become an actuality, if a welfare organization with the aims, the structures, the methods, and, above all, the spirit of unselfishness and fraternity existing in the League takes over the solution. Such a great task must never become the plaything of selfish and ambitious agitators. It must not be exposed to the dangers of impure machinations and private or political corruption, 2

nor must it become the victim of the ignorance and inefficiency of leaders who know nothing about organizing such an important welfare work. 

The second condition is that the United States be willing to fulfill the tasks which are provided for in my plan. They are tasks that can easily be fulfilled by the United States, and the fulfillment of which will bring American people advantages far greater than the sacrifices entailed. 

In presenting my plan in outline I am giving the results of 25 years of actual experience in organizing practical help. I am not describing day-dreams, but the true solution, on a very large scale, of a problem which, on a smaller scale, I have repeatedly succeeded in solving. The principles, however, remain the same, regardless of the scale on which they are applied. A very big machine is built according to exactly the same principles as a smaller machine of the same kind. 

B. THE PLAN IN OUTLINE I. Temporary settlement of the German Refugees in the United States. 

1) For the time being, the German refugees will come to the United States as guests of the American Welfare League. They will become members of the League and thus will be entitled to the aid which the League gives to its members according to the regulations of the “Constitution of the League." 

2) The German refugees will come to the United States, not as regular immigrants, but only on visitor's visas for a stay of from three to five years.

Inasmuch as the number of those who wish to enter the United States as visitors is not legally restricted, neither a change in the immigration laws nor in the quota is necessary for the entrance of the refugees. 

The only condition required for their entrance is proof that they will not become public charges during their stay in the United States. This proof will be furnished by the League, which will also guarantee that none of the German refugees will accept private employment, so that their presence can in no way have a detrimental effect upon the economic conditions in the United States. 

3) The League assumes the following obligations: 

a) Immediately after their arrival, the German refugees will be organized into so-called "Economic Communities" in such a manner that they themselves will produce everything neces- 3

sary for a decent human existence, in accordance with the regulation contained in the "Constitution" of the League which reads as follows (Article I, Section 2 and 3): 

“Section 2. The object and purpose of the League shall be: 

(a) To organize those unemployed members of the League who are able and willing to work in so-called "Economic Communities" for the purpose of local production of all staples of life, and for the creation of whatever contributes to make human existence for them better and more enjoyable. (b) To help by way of charity those members of the League who are unable to work at all." "Section 3. All work of the League is to be performed without regard to religion, race, or nationality." b) During the stay of the refugees in the United States, such Economic Communities will also be established in Canada, Mexico, Central and South America, and all refugees will gradually be transferred to these Communities for final settlement.

The question at once arises whether these countries will permit the immigration of the refugees. The answer is: These countries will not permit the refugees to enter, if they should do so singly and individually in the usual manner of immigrants, for the purpose of seeking an existence. However, they will welcome the refugees, if they are willing to settle in "Economic Communities." Because such "Communities" do not become a burden to any country. Rather, they unlock the treasures lying dormant and so enrich the country in all respects. Their exemplary work in all branches of production, especially in agriculture, as well as their scientific educational institutions, will be models for the population of the country, and their manifold welfare institutions will benefit the inhabitants of the entire district in which such an Economic Community is situated. c) After resettlement of the refugees in other countries the "League" will receive the unemployed of the United States into the Economic Communities and with them will build as many new Economic Communities as are necessary to take care of all unemployed of the United States and in this way to free the United States from the expenditure of billions of dollars which are annually used for the fight on unemployment. 

4) Before their entry into the United States, the refugees must obligate themselves in writing (a) to enter those Economic Communities to which they are assigned by the League and not to leave them without

permission of the Government of the United States, on punishment of deportation. (b) To agree to settle permanently in the countries chosen for them by the League. In this matter the League will, as far as possible, take the wishes and the needs of the individual refugees into consideration. 

5) The League will establish "Main Communities" and "Branch

Communities." 

A "Main Community" consists of at least 2,500 persons and cultivates an agricultural area of about 15,000 acres. Therefore, agriculture in all its branches will form the basis for such a Community. 

It shall be understood that the general principles and viewpoints concerning the relationship between the "League" and the Economic Communities are expressed in the following regulations regardless of whether the Communities shelter refugees or the unemployed of our country:

The Community will produce everything that can be gotten from the soil and which can be used on a paying basis, and will also transform the raw materials into all possible kinds of finished goods. 

Unlimited opportunities for work and service are therefore given in an Economic Community. Even those with the least ability will find their places. Persons of every age, sex and calling will receive their corresponding duties to perform without running the risk of losing their daily living. Unattached persons as also entire families can find refuge here, regardless of creed, race, or nationality. 

The purpose of such a Community is not merely to offer its members a scant substitute for what they were accustomed to before, but to give them something that is better in every way than anything in their past experience.

The League is to secure the moneys necessary for the purchase or lease of the land, for the purchase of all agricultural stock and equipment, for the building of Community and private houses and for the erection and the equipments of all necessary plants. 

The League is the owner of all the lands, buildings, means of production and industrial plants, as well as of the assets of the Economic Communities.

The League is the employer and the members of the Communities are the employees, and stand in the same relationship to each other as it is customary between employer and employee. 

They are to be paid according to the same principles that are usual in our present economic life. However, the social and purely human 5

relationship between the League and the members of an Economic Community will be essentially different from the relationship existing between present-day employers and their employees. For the League considers its relationship towards its Economic Communities like that of a father towards the members of his family who are working together with him in his business and whom he pays as he would have to pay strangers working for him, but who divides the profits of the business equally among the working members of his family.-The distribution of the profits will be made on the basis of the hours of work put in by a worker during the business year, regardless of the kind of work he did. 

The members of an Economic Community have their preference in the choice of their work, their living quarters and their total means of livelihood. They have the opportunity to buy anything they want in the stores of the Economic Community, and at a price which is no higher than the cost-price to the Community. Also the amount of rent for living quarters and the costs for all other kinds of services represent only the cost-price.

In all cases of sickness the members of an Economic Community will receive free medical care and nursing. 

A carefree existence is assured the members of an Economic Community for their old age or in case of total disability. 

The League will offer the children and the youth of an Economic Community the best in scientific and professional education. 

The members of an Economic Community-except the refugees, as long as they are in the United States—are free at any time to withdraw from their Community.

The members of the Economic Communities will not be paid in regular currency, but in tokens of the League which will be backed by a corresponding amount of regular money. Regular money will be used in an Economic Community only for payments to be made outside of the Community.

The various Economic Communities of a country will constitute a large entity exchanging among themselves those goods that cannot be produced in all Economic Communities and thus complementing one another. Each Community must produce food and clothing, and everything else that is absolutely essential for the running of a Community. For each Economic Community must always be independent as far as the necessities of life are concerned. 

The production in the Communities will considerably exceed the needs of the Communities themselves. Part of the production surplus

will be used for the enlargement of the Communities or for the creation of new Communities; in part it will be sold to members of the League who do not belong to Economic Communities, but who live in the villages and cities of the country; in part it will be disposed of by export to other countries.

The buildings of a Main Community will form a square block, in which all buildings will be connected with one another by means of a continuous corridor. The block will contain: a) Sixteen so-called "Corridor Houses," which will serve as living quarters only. Each Corridor House has ample room for 168 persons, so that the 16 Corridor Houses can take care of 2,688 persons in all. They will be equipped with all modern comforts. 

The rooms of a Corridor House will be exceptionally large (14 x 24 ft.). For the "League" regards it its duty to provide in its 

Communities the possibility of sheltering tens of thousands more people in times of floods, hurricanes, earth-quakes or other catastrophies and emergencies. b) Eight so-called "Intermediate Houses," which will connect the Corridor Houses with each other and which do not have any living quarters, but will serve for the following purposes:

Two Intermediate Houses will be used for educational purposes. They will have rooms for kindergarten, grammar schools, high school, adult schools, evening schools, etc. In addition, they will have a library, reading rooms and lecture halls, etc. 

One Intermediate House will serve for the proper care of the sick of the Community and the adjacent districts, as well as for other members of the League who wish to be treated there. 

One Intermediate House will be used for social purposes. It will have refreshment rooms, recreation rooms, restaurants, amusement rooms, play rooms, movies, etc. 

Four Intermediate Houses will serve as work rooms for the numerous trades and small industrial plants. c) Three Industrial Buildings for larger industrial plants. d) An Administration Building, which will contain the post office and the administration offices, as well as the living quarters of the leading officials.

The Basement of the block will be built up for housing the kitchens and dining rooms, the dairy, the bakery, the laundry and all stores. A part of the Basement will be used for garages.

A Branch Community will be established only where a special kind of production makes this necessary and where the place of production is too distant from the Main Community. The number of persons in a 

Branch Community will be determined by the amount of work which is to be done. Each Branch Community will always be under the direction. 

of a Main Community. 

6) In order to keep the costs of establishing a Community as low as possible, only as much ground as is necessary for the erection of the Community-buildings will be acquired through purchase at the beginning, which means only about 100 acres. The remainder of the necessary ground will be leased for three or five years, but with an option to buy and with a purchase price stipulated in advance. The owners of the land will have the right to become members of the Community. In such a case they can remain in their houses and earn their entire livelihood by working in the Community. The "League" will especially find out which farms the State took over because of non-payment of taxes. 

7) The refugees must build their Economic Communities themselves, as far as they are able to do so. For this reason the first refugee transports must consist only of able-bodied men who may be employed either as unskilled laborers or skilled laborers, architects, engineers or other professional workers. Any possible shortage of workers will be supplied from the unemployed United States' members of the League. 

The completion of the buildings of an Economic Community will take about six months, if 1,000 workers are employed. During this time, the workers will be housed with private families who are willing to take in a refugee for six months without pay and to give him a place to sleep and the necessary food. Upon completion of the Community, ample compensation will be made these families by giving them, without cost, products of the Economic Community, e.g., foodstuffs, cloth or clothing, shoes, furniture, etc. or by receiving sick members of these families or those in need of rest into the hospital or recreation homes of the 

Economic Community. 

For this reason the first Economic Communities will be erected in the vicinity of larger cities in the United States, so that the refugees can get to their work in the morning and back to the city in the evening without any special difficulty.

In addition, workmen camps will be constructed on the building site for those workmen who have to stay there during the nights, and also for those refugees who, for certain reasons, cannot be sheltered in private families.

The refugees who worked as pioneers in building up the first 150

Communities without wages, will be paid afterwards in the one or other way by the Communities.

From my long experience in the field of sheltering helpless and needy people, I know that among each 1,000 inhabitants of a city there will be found at least 10 families willing to shelter a refugee each on the conditions stated above and without immediate payment. This would mean that at least 1,000 able-bodied male refugees would find shelter in private families in a city of 100,000 inhabitants. 

150 Economic Communities will be required for the temporary sheltering of all German refugees; therefore 150 places for the establishment of a like number of Economic Communities would have to be found in the vicinity of bigger cities, so that 150,000 refugees could be sheltered in private families. 1) In those States of the United States that border on Canada and Mexico and which will have one or more of the first Economic Communities of refugees near these borders, such Communities will be used as the basis from which to establish Economic Communities on the other side of the border, in Mexico and Canada, at a small cost. 

2) In those States of the United States that border on Canada or Mexico, but where none of the first Economic Communities of refugees could be built near the Canadian or Mexican border, the League will first establish a number of the smaller Economic Communities near the border. II. Details of Rev. Greber's welfare work. 

1) In the year 1906 Rev. Greber undertook the task of ending, by means of an "Emergency Community," the great suffering that prevailed in one of Germany's poorest agricultural districts. The mortality there was appalling. The sick were receiving no care whatsoever. Medical assistance was unobtainable in most cases, as there were no physicians living in the neighborhood, which made the doctor's fees prohibitive for the destitute population. Furthermore, the sickness of the heads of a family increased the suffering of its other members, when there were small children, because the work about the house or in the fields remained undone in the absence of means for employing outside help. 

The welfare experts declared the plan for the solution of this problem developed by Rev. Johannes Greber, as “utopia.”

However, Rev. Greber carried out his plan. After one year the "Emergency Community" was in full swing. Trained nurses were going about their duties; a nursing equipment as complete as in the best hospital was at their disposal; the sick were receiving medical attention and the work about the house and in the fields of the sick was being done cheerfully and gratuitiously by the well. All of this was furnished by the "Emergency Community" itself, without outside contributions, only because of a special method in organizing the material help. In the second year this Community was extended to the neighbouring districts including 24 villages.

The public authorities took note of the achievement of this “Community," appreciated its value and issued over 50,000 circulars, which were sent all over Germany, recommending the plan and urging its adoption wherever necessary.

The International Congress of Social Welfare Workers in Copenhagen referred to this organization as a model for all countries. 

Professor Dr. P. Jacob of the University of Berlin, in his book "Die 

Tuberkulose und die hygienischen Misstände auf dem Lande," (p. 176179) refers to Rev. Greber's welfare work and describes it as the highest standard of practical welfare. 

2) In the very beginning of the World War, Rev. Greber decided to help the poor children of big cities. He started his work in this field already in September 1914, sheltering, without payment, 52 children from Cologne for a few months with families of his own country parish. 

In so doing he first of all wanted to find out which mistakes should be avoided in housing city children in farmer families. 

After having learned in this way all practical details in sheltering children, Rev. Greber resigned his parish and devoted his entire time to find homes for poor city children in farm districts. 14

In the year 1915 and the first half of 1916 he placed about 7,000 undernourished children with families in various country districts of Germany. At the same time he had rented a big hotel in Bad Ems in which he hospitalized daily, during the whole war, about 200 of the weakest children who needed not only food but also medical care. 

Parellelling the foregoing, arrangements were made to feed numerous hungry children twice a day in private families of their own cities. 

In the second half of 1916, when there already was a great shortage of food even in the families of German farmers, Rev. Greber determined to place the weakest German children with good families of neutral countries.

Both lay and ecclesiastical authorities rejected this plan as not feasible because of the seemingly insurmountable obstacles connected with it. 

Nevertheless, Rev. Greber carried it through in the face of all opposition and within a relatively short time he was able to find homes for 14,175 of the poorest children with good families in Holland, where they remained until their health was re-established. The Protestant children were sheltered in Protestant families, the Catholic children in Catholic families, and the Jewish children in Jewish families. 

The objections which had been made in both cases proved to be groundless, for Rev. Greber had foreseen all of the difficulties which might present themselves and had provided against them. 

These few details of Rev. Greber's welfare work indicate his mind and method, thoroughness and ability in solving difficult welfare problems. 

III. Letter of the Board of Directors of the American Welfare 

League to the President of the United States.

HON. FRANKLIN D. ROOSEVELT 

Washington, D. C.

Mr. President: 

The Board of Directors of the American Welfare League, Inc. respectfully presents to you a Plan for the solution of the problem of the 

German refugees.

The plan was originally developed by Rev. Johannes Greber, President of the League, and adopted by the League for the purpose of remedying the conditions brought about by the unemployment of millions of our citizens.

But since an international emergency has arisen that must somehow find a solution, The Board of Directors after careful deliberation, is of 15

the opinion that not only can the plans of the League be brought into use for the benefit of the refugees but should be. And since our country has basically been a haven and refuge for the oppressed we take bold to present this plan.

Rev. Johannes Greber, the author of this plan, is internationally known as a sincere, practical and very successful organizer to alleviate human suffering. As a corroboration of this statement we added as an "appendix" to the plan the testimonials concerning his welfare work. 

Because of the comprehensive welfare projects which were accomplished through him as far back as 1906 and especially during the World War, Rev. Greber was elected to the German Reichstag where he was highly esteemed and considered an expert in practical welfare work. 

In 1929 he came to the United States, first, to ascertain the possibility of getting financial aid to help the unattached aged of Germany who had lost all of their possessions as a result of the inflation, and also to make a closer study of American Social Service. But he soon found that because of the depression which had come upon us in the meantime no help for the aged in Germany could be expected from this country. He therefore devoted a large portion of his time to the study of the unemployment problem and reached the conclusion that the United States, better than any other country in the world, is in a position to satisfactorily solve this problem in spite of all its implications and magnitude. He has since perfected the plan in detail in such a way as to make possible its practical prosecution at any time.

Since Rev. Greber was only a "visitor" in the United States he returned to Germany in August 1935 and some months later came back to the United States as an immigrant. He was granted his first citizenship papers on the 28th of February 1936. During his stay here he has written several books in both German and English which were printed in the United States.

All welfare work of Rev. Greber is and always has been entirely "non-sectarian." He is pastor of a non-sectarian church, the "Church of believers in God" in New York.

The Board of Directors of the American Welfare League would be very grateful for granting to Rev. Greber an opportunity to explain his plan in all details to such Governmental officials as have your authorization to make decisions in this important matter. 

Respectfully submitted, American Welfare League, Inc.

JOHN FELSBERG, Secretary. 16

ARTICLE II of the Constitution reads: 

Any individual, firm, corporation or institution is eligible for membership in the League if willing to co-operate in the welfare work of the League according to its ability and to contribute to its organization fund a free-will contribution which shall not be less than five cents per week. 

Application for membership must be sent to the

AMERICAN WELFARE LEAGUE, INC. 

80 Fourth Avenue, New York, N. Y.
