CHAPTER I The American Spirit 

Ingenuity and inventiveness, self-reliance and hard work, courage and perseverance have always been distinguishing characteristics of the American people. These are qualities which in part constitute the true American spirit. 

Notwithstanding their many admirable qualities, many Americans have one very serious shortcoming. They are extremely careless where capital is concerned.

Capital is a precious thing; it gives to industry the power to keep civilization on the march. It

It opens the gates to pleasure, ease and contentment to those [1]

who have amassed it, and contentment is the most prized jewel in the kingdom of man. career.

Every man should strive to become a capitalist from the beginning of his Webster defines a capitalist as one who possesses capital. A man without capital is like a ship without a sail, buffeted about by every ill wind, and eventually driven high on the rocks of failure and regret, but once a man has managed to set aside a fund for working capital, however small the amount may be, his horizon begins to broaden and the land of opportunity looms in the distance. The accumulation of capital is essential to the welfare of our country. Without capital there can be no employment, and without employment there can be no wealth, which is merely another name for homes, fur- [2]

nishings, motor cars, libraries, hotels, railroads and other conveniences which add to the joy of living. 

Emerson in his essay on Wealth had this to say, "The philosophers have laid the greatness of man in making his wants few; but will a man content himself with a hut and a handful of pease?

HE IS BORN TO BE RICH . . . . 

Kings are said to have long arms, but every man should have long arms, and pluck his living, his instruments, his power, and his knowing from the sun, moon and stars. 

Man was born to be rich, or inevitably grows rich by the use of his faculties. Property is an intellectual production."

There are hundreds of thousands of people who are ambitious to make a success of their lives. They hope for some degree of comfort and pleasure as [3]

they journey along, and look forward to days of leisure, travel and enjoyment of the finer things of life. Many of these people work hard but make very little progress. The plan of living which they pursue is unsound and ineffective. Others no more favored by fortune are steadily forging to the front. 

Why not they? 

The sharpening of the wits, an inventory of one's abilities, a widening of knowledge, a more successful program and a little more determination has often provided the avenue over which men have ridden to greater success. Yes, man should reach out with a long arm of reason and wisdom if he would succeed.

Cicero gave the key to success in ten words when he said, "Man has been born for two things, thinking and act- [4]

ing." If there is a royal road to riches it surely is clear thinking and intelligent acting. No worthwhile thing, from a cottage to a transcontinental railroad was ever brought into being without thought and action. Most people have a vague idea that they would like to become wealthy, but how many can say precisely what would constitute wealth for them? How many have ever taken the time to list the things which are necessary in order that they may be happy and contented? 

Broadly speaking, man may attain wealth neither more nor less than the sum total of all the thought and action he contributes to it. 

Listen to the song of Hiawatha. “By struggle and by labor you shall gain what you have prayed for." But merely to labor is not enough. One must [5]

think his problem through to its logical conclusion and act accordingly if he is to prosper. He must have a clearly defined purpose and hew to the line. He should decide in exactly what manner he would have his wealth come to him, and then take such action as will bring it to pass.

In order to get ahead it is imperative that one provide himself with a certain amount of working capital. Those who lack the determination and integrity of purpose to do this may as well step aside at once and clear the way for those who are prepared to take advantage of every favorable eventuality which Fate has in store for them.

Much is said about the uneven distribution of wealth. Suppose it were divided equally and everyone started from scratch, each with an equal share [6]

of $3,or less, mostly in real estate and natural resources. Where would one live? What would one do? Who would start industry rolling and assume responsibility for its pay roll?

What about transportation? How about trade?

The American system is by no means perfect, but it is the best that has yet been devised. "If I had money"-how often one hears this phrase. Money is a commodity valueless except as a medium of exchange for the things one really desires. If a man can make boats, bonnets or bridges they may be exchanged for the same things through the channels of trade.

Every man is free to produce as much as he can, and to accumulate as much as he can. Because one man has more, [7]

others need not have less. Wealth is unevenly divided because one man is wise and another less intelligent. One man works and another is idle. One saves and invests and another spends all. One receives wages and another profits. Successful men supply their share of the capital and receive both wages and profits. 

Wealth of itself has little value except as it may bring contentment and happiness through its use. The constructive and beneficial uses to which it may be devoted are numerous and varied, and the greater the wealth the greater the opportunities for doing good. 

Wealth can be accumulated by most men in only one way-through their own efforts. At more or less regular intervals there comes a time in the lives of most men when surplus funds would [8]

be a tremendous help in bettering their position. 

As the years fly past, each brings its opportunities to be seized or lost. The average man can ill afford to lose a single opportunity. Each year leaves one year less of life and one year less of opportunities. 

Opportunities converted to one's use mean leisure and luxuries, holidays and happiness, but to turn an opportunity to one's account almost invariably requires a certain amount of capital, and the acquisition of capital demands character and sacrifice of trifling satisfactions.

To be able to step down a gangplank at Havre after several glorious days at sea, to visit the shores of the Mediterranean, or to tour our own country and view its mountains, lakes and water- [9]

earlier years. falls is well worth a little self-denial in 

Who can look at Mt. 

Washington from Crawford Notch, New Hampshire, gaze across the Great 

Smoky Mountains of Tennessee, or view the many other wonders of nature without a feeling of awe, a more humble spirit and thoughts of gratitude to the Almighty who created them? 

Everyone will want to quit work sometime and enjoy life in his own way. 

One need not be rich; neither can he risk being poor. Happiness is often determined by the size of one's income, and a satisfactory income can best be acquired from capital profitably employed. 

Down through the decades the voice of experience has been crying out five magic words which are the cornerstones of every financial success-work, save [ 10 ]

Would that those and invest safely. 

last two words, INVEST SAFELY, might be graven upon the mind of every individual. It is the crux of the whole business of becoming financially independent, especially for those of limited capital.

Most people work, but find it so much easier to spend than to save. Saving is difficult and requires sacrifice and self-denial. Others do save substantial sums of money, but all too often rush headlong into "get-rich-quick" schemes with a confidence which would cause a banker to weep. 

Capital is too valuable a thing to be credulously handed over to some phantom company, or to be used as a prop for ill-considered promotions. Capital must be acquired and then preserved at any cost if one is to make financial pro- [ 11 ]

gress. Patience in awaiting safe opportunities for its increase pays the largest dividends. "Opportunities have come and gone, and will come and go, but if you don't take advantage of them they never exist for you. 

No one will give you money, but they will give you a chance to make money, because they need your help."

CHAPTER II Productive Employment 

In the United States, with many waves, business rises and falls like the tides of the ocean. Seven times during the last one hundred years the American people have stood at a new crossroad confused and perplexed, and six times they have chosen the road to the right and gone ahead to greater heights of achievement and prosperity. Because this is so the future may well be considered in the light of the experience of the past without misgivings. 

With business below normal our country today enjoys an average standard of living far above that which obtains in any other country in the world. 

It was under our traditional profit [ 13 ]

system that we outclassed all other nations, and only under the same system can we hope to maintain our lead. 

A concerted drive, both singly and collectively, for a new high level of general prosperity is now in order. No nation on earth is better prepared for a general grand march to a new high peak of production and well-being. We have great natural resources, great productive capacity, millions of dollars in idle funds, tens of millions of citizens with enumerable wants unsupplied and several million men physically able and waiting to give an honest day's work for an honest day's pay.

The great fundamental need is productive employment for every man. 

To attain this end we must clear the tracks for an onward sweep of business. 

Every obstacle and every restraining [ 14 ]

PRODUCTIVE EMPLOYMENT 

influence must be removed and every incentive for placing men on pay rolls must be supplied. 

Hindrances to business will never create wealth, lucrative jobs, nor increase purchasing power.

Business is capital, management and tools for labor. What helps business benefits labor. What impedes business hurts labor. All funds, individual or governmental, come from business, and if we are to get ahead business must go ahead. 

Always the attraction of profit has been the mainspring of American progress, and without profit business will never flourish. Profit is the most powerful incentive to save, to conserve resources, and for investment of capital in private industry. As a means of producing jobs it has no rival. With [ 15 ]

work for all within the framework of industry and trade there can ensue unequalled prosperity.

This must not be a prosperity of the few at the expense of the many. It must be based upon the creation of a greater abundance of goods in which all shall share justly.

In order to attain a new high level of general prosperity there must be wider purchasing power. So long as a vast majority of the American people are obliged to spend upwards of 80% of their incomes for food and shelter production and employment are bound to lag.

As a partial remedy for this situation, millions of inexpensive homes should be built without delay, and to the fullest extent possible mass production should be applied to all food products. [ 16 ]

PRODUCTIVE EMPLOYMENT 

Denial of employment to those past a certain age is an economic blunder of the first magnitude. It is harmful to the country as a whole, and more especially so to those who produce goods for general distribution. If those over fifty years of age are no longer to be taken into industry and commerce they might do well to engage in business for themselves. 

New enterprises bestow wide benefits.

Many towns offer fine opportunities for men of means and wide experience. 

The entire nation should keep its mind upon the one important thingproductive employment for every person able and eager to work. [ 17 ]

CHAPTER III Choosing a Goal 

The mind is a veritable treasure house of ideas. Men and women everywhere, rich or poor, may conjure one or one hundred. Some ideas are sound and tremendously valuable; some are devastating in their application. Words seem inadequate to express the importance of choosing the right kind of ideas and putting them into effect. 

Edison's idea of the Mazda lamp has benefited millions of people. The idea of sending sound over the airways has brought pleasure to the civilized world. 

Endless instances could be cited of great ideas, the application of which is too intricate for the average mind. Obviously, men prosper only in proportion [ 18 ]

CHOOSING A GOAL 

to the soundness and feasibility of the ideas they seek to promote. 

Often it is a very simple idea which starts the one who conceived it on the road to fortune. The idea of a little metal ferrule which holds the rubber eraser on the end of a pencil is said to have sold for one million dollars. The hook and eye produced a fortune before snap fasteners came into use. One of the largest selling remedies of its kind in the world today, was first an idea in the mind of Mr. Linsford Richardson, who began putting up his product in the back room of a little store when he was past fifty years old.

Ideas for office equipment, food products and useful articles of many kinds, when placed on the market, have brought fame and fortune to obscure people who, except for their products, [ 19 ]

might never have become known outside of the counties in which they were born. 

Not to be forgotten are the remarkable achievements of women in various fields of endeavor. Many feats of success have been due to the determination of some woman to put her idea into effect in a big way. 

Fourscore and ten years, possibly more, perhaps less, the world is at one's feet. During early years one's will may be somewhat subdued, but for the most part life is what one makes it.

A wise Providence has ordained that into each life there shall come both sunshine and rain that life may not become monotonous, but the proportion of each to a large extent He has left in individual hands, each to be master of his own destiny, each according to his [ 20 ]

CHOOSING A GOAL 

own vision and will, free to choose his own goal and work for his own success. 

What goals justify the efforts of a lifetime? Who is successful?

Not merely the accumulation of great wealth and not those alone who build great enterprises. Many of the most successful men and women have only sufficient resources to enable them to render some service to their fellowmen, to carry on some purposeful work with peace in their minds and happiness in their hearts.

There are two kinds of success. The one most essentially worth while has to do with sacrifice and service and should have a part in every plan of life. 

A carefully chosen idea backed by intelligent action has enabled many small capitalists to attain an enviable position in the business or social world. [ 21 ]

One great American need is for more people with sound ideas and sufficient capital to put their ideas into successful operation. [ 22 ]

Confidence

History reveals that for centuries the human race has been beset with "hard times" and "evil days", after which a new high point in human progress has always been attained.

In our country several major depressions and other lesser ones have occurred in the last one hundred years, but who will say that America has not gone forward to a greater and higher mode of living after each reversal? It is as certain as anything can be in an uncertain future that we shall see high and low tides in business with ascending progress as long as civilization lasts. 

The panic of 1837 which lasted for several years dealt young America a [ 23 ]

staggering blow. In a single year six hundred eighteen banks closed their doors, hundreds of business firms went to the wall and countless numbers of people were thrown out of work. Many believed the young nation would never recover. 

In 1857 the United States received another blow, less severe but causing many failures, among which were three railroads and a great trust company.

Thousands of workmen lost their jobs or went on half pay. 

Another panic in 1873 closed the stock exchange and caused failures running into hundreds of thousands of dollars. Once more men began to say 

America was through.

The next depression began in 1893. 

In that year five hundred fifty-four banks failed, call money went to 74% [ 24 ]

CONFIDENCE 

and the stock market crashed again.

Then came the depressions of 1907, 1914 and 1920. Periods of deflation and liquidation and finally the crash of 

1929 and the slump of 1938, the recovery from which some people practically gave up hope.

The point to be emphasized is that after each setback our country has forged ahead to new highs in national income and wider distribution of wealth. 

With each recurring slump in business activity many people became apprehensive. Many have been so sure that 

America had had its day. Nothing could have been further from the facts, as time has always shown. It is safe to assume that we can confidently look forward to a day when more people will enjoy a higher standard of living than anything which has gone before. [ 25 ]

To assume a hopeful attitude and at the same time to work for our own individual success, each in his own way, is far more constructive than thinking, talking and acting as though American prosperity were a thing of the past.

Difficulties and obstacles are a part of the Divine plan. A people who know no discouragement know no defeat. Let no man fail to recognize the potentialities for labor and capital which lie in the years ahead. Notwithstanding the fact that we now enjoy a very high standard of living, we have barely scratched the surface of our possibilities.

If each man were to produce all the requirements of his family none would have an idle hour, but America has adopted the course of specialization that each may become skilled in the doing of [ 26 ]

one thing well in order to produce more goods at a lower cost, and thus produce more wealth to distribute in wages and profit. That is one feature of the American profit system. It is the system which has made this nation great. It is the system which has placed every man on an equal footing at the start with only his weakness or indolence as a handicap. It is the proven path which we shall follow as long as the beautiful stars and stripes continue to wave in the breeze.

Like an intricate machine this system sometimes fails to function smoothly.

Nevertheless, neither capital, labor, nor consumer will ever consent to having it consigned to the scrap heap. 

Each decade as the American people acquire more and better tools with which to work they become more am- [ 27 ]

bitious, more resourceful, and wealth and well-being reach down to embrace more people.

So why not be superior to every discouragement? "Grow old along with me, the best is yet to be."

CHAPTER V A Definite Purpose 

Notwithstanding the mistakes and propensity to err of our business and political leaders, America is one of the best places in the world in which to make money, but it is also a good place in which to spend or lose it. 

To many it is a place of glorious achievement with something accomplished each year; a place to enjoy life while fighting for wealth, power and position. To some it is a gay merrygo-round, while to others it is a hopeless treadmill, depending upon their own plan of life, or lack of plan and initiative. 

From Buffalo to Birmingham, from 

Passamaquoddy to the Golden Gate, [ 29 ]

the highways and byways are dotted with people, each hoping for success and each intent upon his own affairs. 

Some know exactly what they wish to accomplish and are driving straight ahead, grasping every opportunity and employing every aid which will speed them on towards their chosen goal. A vast majority, however, are simply going around in a vicious circle, earning and spending, earning more and spending more, all without plan or purpose.

Year after year the same monotonous work, the same financial worries, with nothing to look forward to except old age and dependency.

The fact that millions of hard working citizens of the richest country in the world die practically penniless is ample evidence that there is urgent need for more straight thinking in matters per- [ 30 ]

taining to thrift and profitable investment procedure.

Doubtless, a large majority of people would like to accumulate a principal sum, the income from which would equal or exceed their present incomes or salaries. How many will succeed without a definite purpose? Statistics indicate that only 5% will become independent, and the majority will leave nothing, not even a savings account or a life insurance policy.

This deplorable percentage of financial failures can and should be decreased.

In view of the fact that the writer has helped many business men acquire substantial sums of money, it is hoped that this little book, notwithstanding its limitations, may be the means of starting more people on the road to financial independence. It is intended pri- [ 31 ]

marily for men and women of limited means, but it carries a message for all thinking men.

It is more or less common knowledge that trust companies and other financial institutions having a department for the management of estates find it unprofitable to handle accounts of less than $25,000, and have little to offer the small investor other than the accommodations of a savings department. 

Every intelligent man must realize that he will never become independent on small savings earning but 32%. 

Profit, not interest, is the road which leads to financial security.

A savings bank renders valuable service. Everyone should avail himself of its facilities and deposit some part of his income regularly, until such time as he has sufficient capital to make a [ 32 ]

A DEFINITE PURPOSE 

sound, profitable investment. 

The late J. P. Morgan, recognized as one of the world's greatest financiers, once said, "The foundation of wealth is the first $100 well invested". Financial independence and business success came first from savings, then from knowing how to invest those savings well.

Several companies listed on the New 

York Stock Exchange have returned to early investors from $50,000 to $300,000 for each $500 of capital invested. 

No one is wise enough to advise all mankind what to do with his money.

So much depends on the person, his location, his circumstances, his character, his ability, his likes and dislikes. 

Each must do his own thinking.

One of the few types of investment [ 33 ]

which can be recommended to all, old or young, rich or poor, is shares in certain old-established companies which enjoy an active market. However, these stocks must be bought only when business is at low ebb and prices are correspondingly low. Anyone with the courage and foresight to buy good securities whenever prices are low need have little concern regarding his future.

Few Americans are so bound by circumstances or poverty that they cannot get ahead by saving and intelligent investing. Practically everyone earning money could save something if he were sufficiently ambitious to pay the price, which is far less than the price of being entirely thriftless and forfeiting success. 

Financial success merely requires ability to recognize real opportunities, and courage and capital to take advantage [ 34 ]

of them.

As an illustration of what can be done with very little capital:-some years ago, during a depression when prices of securities were selling at unusually low levels, a young man with family responsibilities saw a great opportunity for investment. His only asset was a life insurance policy upon which he borrowed $123. With this he bought ten shares of stock, which he later sold for $430. By repeating the process as good opportunities occurred, and by adding small savings to his account as circumstances permitted, he eventually found himself the possessor of $15,000, an amount more than sufficient to realize his ambition of owning a business of his own.

In the United States there is probably a million people who could do equally [ 35 ]

as well, or better, if they were inclined to try. By pooling their accumulations they could control a dozen corporations and a couple of banks in the bargain. 

The time has now arrived for a thoroughgoing self analysis of what each man is really trying to accomplish and how it can best be done. [36]

CHAPTER VI Getting Ahead Financially 

Men differ in character and capacity, education and environment, patience and perseverance, but all men are much alike in one particular. Each would like to be a free man, independent of pay days and another man's whim. Each in his own way hopes for a reasonable degree of success. How can it best be assured? The record would show men perform work according to their several abilities in various occupations. Some work for wages, some for professional fees and some for profit. A vast majority of successful men acquired their wealth from profits or from a combination of salary and profit.

Obviously, to obtain profits it was [ 37 ]

necessary to engage in business either alone or in co-operation with others.

This in turn required capital resources which were patiently accumulated through years of persistent saving. 

With few exceptions all great fortunes had an humble beginning. 

Andrew Carnegie made millions of dollars for himself in the steel business, and is said to have created 42 millionaires in the process. 

He began with about $1,000 acquired from small savings invested in shares of the Adams 

Express Company. 

John D. Rockefeller of Standard Oil fame built one of the world's largest fortunes in oil. His original capital had to be saved by himself from very modest earnings. 

Frank W. Woolworth, a New York farmer's son, worked several years in a [ 38 ]

dry goods business before he received $10 a week. When he married his salary was only $8.50. With $50 cash and a $300 note he secured merchandise to open his first store at Utica, New

York. The venture was a disappointment. Nothing daunted, he secured more credit and decided to try Lancaster, Pennsylvania. In June 1879 he opened his store there. The sales for his first day amounted to $127 with nothing but 5-cent items, and Mr. 

Woolworth's cherished idea was a proven success. 

On the night of April 24, 1913 another dream came true when President

Wilson pressed a button in the White 

House and 80,000 lights flashed in the sixty-story Woolworth Building on lower Broadway, New York City, which Mr. Woolworth had personally [ 39 ]

financed from penny profits. With the passing of Mr. Woolworth in 1919 an outstanding career in American business came to an end, but his great idea, now being carried out in more than 

2,000 stores throughout five countries, still lives on. 

Some years ago a Boston man decided that he was going to have an income independent of weekly wages. 

With his available cash he purchased 2 shares of stock in American Telephone 

8 Telegraph Company. For many years thereafter his dividends and savings were invested in more stock in the same company until he had acquired 

200 shares. In the meantime the dividend rate had increased to $9 a share, giving him an income of $1,800 a year from his investment.

In 1904 an engineer connected with [ 40 ]

a large steel company was persuaded by a fellow worker to purchase 500 shares of United States Steel common stock, which at that time was selling under $10 a share. In 1929 these shares sold as high as $261 per share. In the interval of passing years the dividends paid amounted to many times the invested principal.

This is the case of a New Hampshire real estate man who went to a certain city in New Jersey on a business trip. 

Adjoining the city line he noticed a large farm admirably located for a fine suburban development. After investigating certain details he bought the farm, had it surveyed and mapped out in large home sites. Through wise restrictions and careful improvements he was able to realize a profit exceeding $75,000. It has long since become a [ 41 ]

part of the adjoining city and is now one of its most beautiful sections. Here was a great opportunity which had gone unrecognized by home town realty men. 

It presents a fair example of clear thinking and intelligent action backed by a small amount of capital. 

The average man erroneously believes that financial independence can only be achieved by the "lucky" or those specially favored by having some particular advantage over the rank and file of workers in the business world. Such belief is far from the facts of the matter, although undoubtedly it has restrained many from making an honest attempt to put themselves beyond absolute dependence upon a weekly pay envelope. 

One great difficulty in the path of ambitious men lies in not having a definite aim in life. The man who aims [ 42 ]

at nothing will generally accomplish nothing, which is the result that might be expected. The one who makes financial progress is the one who knows what he wants and keeps his eye on the goal. Some years ago a train pulling into Grand Central Station in New 

York City brought a young man from a small country town in the State of 

Missouri. This young man's aim was to accumulate $30,000, invest it for an income of $1,500 a year and then enjoy life in some small country village. Finding work at meagre wages, he soon began looking for a better opportunity. 

This led him to become first, a mail carrier and later a customs house clerk, where he worked up into a very good position. He has long since passed his dollar mark, but still retains his position. [ 43 ]

Here is another young man, son of a self-made father, residing in a small city in Iowa. This lad, too, wished to break into business in the great City of 

New York. After much discussion of the matter the son journeyed to the metropolis, the father arranging with a law firm to advance him $10 weekly for a period of one year, all other expenditures and further needs to be met by the efforts of the son himself. His search for employment brought a position at $4 a week in a wholesale flour house. Diligent watch of the classified advertisements soon brought a better position as clerk with an importing firm. Next we saw him traveling in foreign countries as buyer for the firm. 

Now he is in business for himself. It is interesting to add that a certain young woman he left in Iowa is now mistress [ 44 ]

of his magnificent home in a small New 

Jersey city. 

A teamster named Casey envisioned himself with an independent income derived from real estate. True to his pur- pose, no week went by for many years that he did not deposit $2 in a certain savings bank. As time passed he built a two-family house, then another, and as rents came in he built more until eventually Landlord Casey was an independent man.

In one of our largest cities, during the depression, a great wholesale house was forced to close its doors. Among its employees were three men who had persistently set aside some of their earnings for investment. By selling these investments and combining their capital these men established a new and flourishing business on the foundation of the old [ 45 ]

and thereby became partners in a profitable enterprise. "He that hath, to him shall be given: and he that hath not, from him shall be taken even that which he hath”.

Endless cases might be cited of plain, average people who began life with nothing except ambition and determination to succeed and who now enjoy very comfortable incomes.

There are certain basic principles upon which every success must be built, certain invariable elements which it must contain. Successful men who battle their way to the front are not content with a passive life; they are ambitious, acquisitious and confident of success. 

They are never satisfied with their first step up, but continually press forward to greater and better accomplishments.

It is the easiest thing in the world to [ 46 ]

float along, spend all one earns and hope that somehow great prosperity will be dumped in one's lap. A more effective and a more intelligent thing to do is to save capital for some definite undertaking and see it through to a successful conclusion. [ 47 ]

CHAPTER VII Right Thinking 

Most people will admit that environment and circumstances are largely the result of thought. "As a man thinketh in his heart so is he". This quotation may be taken as most emphatically true. 

A man is literally what he thinks. His character, condition and whole status is the result of all his thoughts and acts. 

Man decides his own fate. In the realm of thought he creates the things which are to make him or destroy him. 

Mind is creative, but it creates only what has been suggested by one's thoughts. It creates failure and distress as well as happiness and success. All that a man does and all that he has is a result of his thinking and the course [ 48 ]

RIGHT THINKING 

he decides to pursue. Every material thing, every achievement and every possession was first an idea in the mind of someone. Like begets like. "Whatsoever ye sow that shall ye also reap" is the law of life. To think failure, to indulge in self pity, to permit hate, greed or envy to impress the mind is to invite sorrow and not success. 

It should be remembered that there are in effect two distinct departments of the mind, the conscious and the unconscious. The conscious mind thinks, decides, forgets and sleeps. The unconscious mind never forgets and never rests. It works continually twentyfour hours a day slowly grinding both the chaff and the wheat. It has the power and tendency to take every thought, every suggestion and every visualized idea of the conscious mind and [ 49 ]

through some subtle law of the universe eventually bring them to pass. Hypnotism has proved that the subconscious mind always does what it is told, and whether for good or evil begins at once to attract the thing or condition patterned for it by the conscious mind. 

Two eminent doctors speaking in the cool and calculating terms of science, based upon experience and experiments, assert that many diseases, including diabetes, stomach ulcers, high blood pressure and heart disease are the physical consequences of undisciplined thought and emotion. 

If it is true that mind controls matter, what assortment of thoughts shall we send as grist to its mill? [ 50 ]

CHAPTER VIII Knowledge Knowledge is 

One of the most essential requisites in the accomplishment of any great purpose is knowledge. power. At the beginning knowledge, either general or specialized, helps a young man to gain a foothold in the business or professional world. In later years knowledge dictates the amount of his salary or the size of his professional fee, as the case may be. Knowledge or lack of it is often the deciding factor between business success and failure. Fortunate is the man who knows exactly what he wishes to accomplish and knows how to to do it, or has the wisdom to engage those having the requisite knowledge to perform the work [51]

for him.

Many engage in business but few succeed. The mortality in business is greater than the mortality among men. Insufficient capital and inexperience are given as the two chief causes. Someone has said "It is better to have tried and failed than never to have tried at all". That may be true under some circumstances, but it is poor consolation for one who at great sacrifice has acquired capital only to see it vanish through ill-considered undertakings and preventable losses. 

No one can be confident of success who diffuses his energy and knowledge over a wide range of subjects. If a man wishes to be successful he must focus all his faculties upon the work he has set out to perform.

Aside from specialized knowledge there are two items of highly important [ 52 ]

KNOWLEDGE general knowledge which no young person can afford to overlook. To do so is to start life with a serious handicap. 

These are the ability to speak fluently and well under all conditions, and to know the art of selling, which after all, is merely the knack of influencing others to think as you do on the subject under discussion. 

As Hugh Chalmers once pointed out in Collier's Weekly, "When Christopher 

Columbus, after fourteen years of fruitless efforts, stood in the Court of Spain and convinced Queen Isabella that she should furnish him three ships and men to sail them in an attempt to discover a western route to the east, he had consummated the greatest sale in the history of the world up to that time. He had made Queen Isabella see as he did about the great idea which he had for sale." [53]

Few of us will ever aspire to influence a queen, but many may try to persuade some hard-headed business man that their name on his pay roll would be mutually advantageous. The power of persuasion is a valuable asset in the quest for success. [54]

CHAPTER IX Private Property 

Private property rests upon tradition, and tradition maintains that man is justly entitled to all that through his skill, ingenuity and industry he can accomplish. Private property is often the subject of attack, yet nothing is easier to defend. 

Men who condemn the rich are confused in mind and wrong in principle.

Men do err in the use of their riches, but so does a man who has but little. 

Broadly speaking, property is merely wealth and capital. Wealth is an abundance of those things which can be used for comfort and pleasure. All wealth not so used is capital entirely valueless except as it is converted into [55]

tools for creating more wealth. There is no limit to the amount of wealth which can be produced, provided there is no limit to capital. The more we produce the more there is to divide and enjoy. 

To produce wealth there must be capital, management and labor, and each is dependent upon the others. In American industry an investment in plant and equipment of approximately $8,000 is required for each man employed, and even in agriculture a considerable outlay for tools, machinery and upkeep must be met. Someone must supply this capital or business would languish and unemployment would rule. So long as labor does not supply its own capital management is compelled to retain a share of the profits for expansion and depreciation if the [56]

PRIVATE PROPERTY 

business is to survive.

Every man should work out a carefully considered program for acquiring property which will meet his needs in the light of his own ability and experience.

Ordinarily it would include life insurance, a home and sufficient income producing property to at least provide the necessities for himself and his family outside of his income from business or salary.

When a definite workable program of any kind has once been established it is bound to attract many dollars which otherwise might go to increase the bank account of someone else. Philanthropy is to be highly commended, but benefactions begin at home. The individual who is careful of dollars in early life is quite sure to find himself in a [ 57 ]

position to contribute liberally to the really important things in later years. 

It is well to remember that one is playing the game of Life against Time -an opponent who never rests and never returns a day or a dollar. [58]

CHAPTER X Saving Capital 

The development of America has demanded wide and persistent saving. 

Before our homes and churches were built, someone had to save; before our ships could sail the Seven Seas, someone had to save from very small incomes. Linking the East and West by great railroad systems required saving by thousands of thrifty people. At great expense manufacturing plants had to be built and equipped with machinery and tools before a job could be provided for those needing work or a profit could be made. No bank, insurance company, utility or merchandising enterprise could be founded until someone somewhere had saved the necessary [59]

capital. 

Receiving tellers' cages are by no means deserted today. In a slow but continuous stream men and women file into our savings institutions with money which, in many cases, is already earmarked for the fulfillment of some personal ambition. In New England alone more than three and one-half billions of dollars have been credited to the accounts of these thrifty people in three hundred forty-nine mutual savings institutions.

With the low rate of interest currently being paid, more and more people are saving for a purpose-a home on the West side, a business on Main Street, a trip abroad, or simply a fund for investment, which is the foundation of every success.

On the other side of the picture are [ 60 ]

Everyone knows that maintaining a home, running a car, educating children and supplying the necessities of life costs a lot of money; but what man can afford to permit his family to gamble on the future with cards which are stacked against them? There is nothing more sad in life than cases where businessmen provide every luxury for their families today and nothing for their future. A few hundred dollars carefully invested now could make such a difference in later years. 

One vital decision everyone should [ 61 ]

make is to set aside regularly some amount of capital for profitable investment. Such decisions would come quickly if the tragic consequences of leaving one's future to chance were more fully understood.

Now is the time to begin meeting this obligation. [63]

The first step in a sound financial program is to build up a cash reserve in a savings bank. This done, the investor is in a position to make his first profitable investment. By setting aside sufficient funds for one good investment each year small investors may soon become quite independent.

It is the right of every individual to have money, to own stocks, bonds and other property and to enjoy life upon this earth to the fullest extent that his own efforts can achieve. However, it is important that he reach his home port with a clean cargo. [64]

CHAPTER XI 

Investing for Profits

Investment of capital has long occupied the thought and time of many able men. What they say is interesting and helpful, but no man's pronouncement is final. Each has his own vision and his own experience; and neither can quite understand the other. Some say an investment offering a return in excess of 

5% is unsound and should be avoided.

Others maintain that every bank deposit, every bond, every business and every important undertaking carries a risk, however slight it may be. They point to the fact that while safety is a highly important factor in any investment of capital, American progress and the wealth of its people is due in large [ 65]

measure to those investors who have always demanded reasonable profits with reasonable safety.

It is easier for most people to earn money than to keep it, and in recent years the problem of investing money safely and profitably has become most perplexing. The average man or woman cannot afford to take chances with hard-earned savings. Neither can they hope to get ahead with their money earning but 2% or 4% yearly. 

Nineteen out of every twenty people seem to go to one extreme or another; they either trudge along a 4% road and acquire little or they wander off into bypaths of rank speculation and eventually lose their principal. Wisdom would seem to dictate a course somewhere between the two extremes.

CHAPTER XII Trading in Stocks 

Buying stocks for profit is usually done in one of three ways: 

First. Daily trading; trying to catch quick turns. The record is replete with the obituaries of those who tried this method and failed. 

Second. Long-pull trading; buying when stocks are low and holding for large profits. This method is used with considerable success by wealthy business men, but all short swings are missed. 

Third. Short-swing trading, trying to follow intermediate movements.

This system works well when prices move up and down without making any great progress, but long-pull profits are usually lost. [74]

TRADING IN STOCKS 

Many otherwise sensible people entertain the erroneous idea that all one has to do in order to make money trading in stocks is to buy a lot of shares and trust in the goddess of luck. They think of men who have made great fortunes buying shares in successful American enterprises when prices were low and risk was at the minimum, but they fail to consider the years of study and research which provided the background for the shrewd selection of companies in which such men invested their capital. 

These men buy almost without risk due to the character of the stocks they buy and the moderate prices they always pay. Their methods are vastly different from those of the daily trader "playing the market" on gossip, rumors and hunches, or "taking a chance" on any stock which "looks good" to his broker. [75]

The average investor in stocks will take money out of the market in an almost indentical proportion to the factual knowledge upon which his transactions are based. [76]

"There is a principle that is a bar against all information, proof against all argument, and which cannot fail to keep a man in everlasting ignorance; and that principle is condemnation before investigation".

Herbert Spencer 

One obstacle to financial success which has caused regret and often great personal loss is the haughty disdain which has crept into the character of a certain type of man. His policies, his ideas and his methods are in his opinion the acme of wisdom. He considers himself sagacious and omniscient. Anything which is new, different or unfa- [104]

miliar to him must be wrong and unworthy of his consideration. Such an attitude is very costly.

By this it is not intended to imply that every wild scheme and every "South

Sound opportunities for achievement and financial gain are missed almost daily due to disinclination to consider suggestions coming from any but the most pretentious sources. 

Individual points of view, experience and keenness of perception vary so widely that one can hardly predict the [105]

The point to be highlighted is that the man who consistently waves aside every proposition which is in any way unusual, without proper consideration, is sure to lose out on many splendid opportunities. Therefore, those who would crown their work with success had best withhold judgment of a thing until they have ascertained the salient facts concerning it.

Nearly two thousand years ago people were admonished to "prove all things; hold fast to that which is good". The same wisdom is good advice for today. [106]

CHAPTER XIX Life Insurance Life 

No successful financial program is complete without life insurance. insurance formulated to fit the individual requirements of the insured is far from a die-to-win affair. It is the first investment a young man should make.

For one whose career is accidently cut short it is often a twenty for one proposition which matures quickly. For one who lives an insurance policy is an extremely valuable document. Furthermore, money invested in life insurance enjoys a high degree of safety. 

Business houses often become insolvent; banks, trust companies, and brokerage houses have closed their doors, but rarely, if ever, has a mutual life [107]

insurance company ceased to function. 

An insurance contract issued by a great life insurance company does more than provide income protection for the family. It offers an opportunity and incentive to set aside money in small installments yearly or quarterly which soon builds up a guaranteed cash value, and also provides collateral for a loan. 

This latter feature has saved many businessmen from the bankruptcy court. 

Because loans are guaranteed in times of depression as well as in periods of prosperity many shrewd people have purposely built up loan values for the sole purpose of being in a position to buy good securities at 50 cents on the dollar in times of business uncertainty.
