CHAPTER I SUMMARY I. Origin and Scope of the Study 

Mergers and acquisitions have played an important role in shaping the market structure of and the laws that govern the American economy. 

The early pools and trusts contributed to the enactment of the Sherman Act; the great combination wave of 1894-1904 preceded the passage of the Federal Trade Commission and Clayton Acts; the wave of mergers and acquisitions in the 1920's produced the Securities and Exchange and the Public Utilities Holding Company Acts; subsequent acquisitions and mergers, especially those that occurred in the 1940's, were followed by an important revision in Section 7 of the Clayton Act. 

The pronounced rises and falls in merger activity is suggestive of a complex cyclical pattern. The disparate statistical attributes of merger waves and the particular legislation each wave has produced suggest that each period of accelerated merger activity has its own peculiar characteristics. This was the hypothesis that prompted the 

Federal Trade Commission to make a special study of those mergers and acquisitions which occurred between 1948 and 1954--a period that, from the evidence available in the Commission's files at the time the study was authorized, looked like another wave.

The present report does not purport to be comprehensive or complete in its coverage of all mergers. It is based almost entirely upon data available in the Commission's files. Chapter II deals with the total number of acquisitions recorded by the Temporary National Economic Committee and the Federal Trade Commission as having occurred in manufacturing and mining during the years 1919 to 1954, inclusive. Chapter III analyzes more intensively a selected sample of mergers and acquisitions covering a broader range of economic activities recorded by the Commission between January 1, 1951 and July 31, 1954. Chapters IV and V are based upon selected mergers that seem best suited to serve for illustrative purposes. Chapter VI deals with the statutory standards of Section 7 of the Clayton Act, as amended, and Chapter VII deals with the economic and legal problems involved in determining competitive consequences under those standards. 

It was decided at the outset that this report on mergers should be guided by two criteria: (1) it should not divert so many of the Commission's staff members from their continuing task of analyzing specific mergers that the administration of Section 7 of the Clayton Act would be impaired; and (2) it should inform the Commission, the Department of Justice, the Congress, and the public on recent mergers at the earliest possible date. For this reason the staff assigned to the merger report was given a period not to exceed four months in which the available data could be assembled. These limitations meant that some detailed information requiring a long period to assemble would

not appear in the report. It was hoped, however, that this would be a small sacrifice for which early and useful general information would more than compensate. It should be noted that before the report was put in final form for publication, it had already provided sufficient information to serve as a basis for the Commission's action on April 5, 1955, whereby the Chairman created a Commission Task Force to identify, on the basis of this report, particular corporate mergers having the greatest significance in the current merger movement and to develop methods of speeding their investigation.1/

II. Summary 

Recent trend in merger activity. Chapter II of this report brings up to date a series on mergers and acquisitions in manufacturing and mining which goes back to 1919. It also identifies and analyzes the structural characteristics of the 1948-1954 merger cycle. Although the underlying source materials do not include all mergers and acquisitions, particularly those of smaller concerns, the figures are probably correct as to trend. Since 1949 the pace of important mergers and acquisitions has been rising; in 1954 the number reported in financial manuals was three times that of 1949, and just slightly less than the number reported for each of the years 1946 and 1947 when merger activity reached a post-war peak. Nevertheless, mergers and acquisitions were occurring at a substantially lower rate in 1954 than during the later '20s. 

Nearly two-thirds of the acquisitions reported during the 1948-54 period were made by companies with assets of $10 million or more. 

In contrast, companies with assets of less than $1 million (including companies for which no asset figures were available) accounted for less than 3 percent of the total number of acquisitions during this period. The largest numbers of acquisitions were made by firms classified in the nonelectrical machinery and the food and kindred products industries in that order. The acquiring firms in these two industries accounted for 492 mergers and acquisitions, or more than one-fourth of the total of 1,773 mergers and acquisitions which were reported as consummated during this period. Merger activity was also pronounced for firms classified in the chemical and fabricated metals industries. During the 1948-54 period, acquiring companies with assets of over $10 million accounted for nearly two-thirds (65.5 percent) of all acquisitions occurring during that period. During the 1940-47 period,

The second such comparison was by industry of the acquiring firm. 

As compared to the relative distribution of the earlier period, merger activity during 1948-54 showed a pronounced increase in the proportion of all acquisitions represented by those occurring in the nonelectrical machinery and in fabricated metals industries. In petroleum and coal products, the percentage decline was even more precipitous--from 7.6 percent of the total during 1940-47 to only 2.0 percent in 1948-54. 

Nature of recent acquisitions and mergers. As already indicated, Chapter III analyzes, on a sampling basis, the general nature of acquisitions and mergers in all lines of manufacture, trade and service which fall within the Commission's jurisdiction under Section 7, as amended. The population from which this sample was selected includes trade and certain service industries in addition to manufacturing and mining industries covered in Chapter II. Moreover, while the acquisitions in Chapter II were limited to those involving whole companies, subsidiaries or divisions, those in Chapter III include acquisitions of any part of a company.1/ On the other hand, Chapter II covers acquisitions by going firms, irrespective of their legal form of organization, and by purchase syndicates, while Chapter III covers only acquisitions by corporations. The purpose of the latter chapter is to probe further into the nature of those recent mergers and acquisitions over which the Federal Trade Commission has jurisdiction under the 

1950 Anti-Merger Act.

These acquisitions were made by about 1,350 companies, not counting companies that were acquired in their turn prior to August 1, 1954. Approximately 1,000 companies made one acquisition each. A fifth of the acquisitions recorded were made by companies known to have assets of $50 million or more. Approximately 1,000 acquisitions, constituting nearly half of the total recorded, were made by companies known to have assets of at least $10 million. These are designated the large companies. Three-fifths of the remaining acquisitions were by companies with assets known to amount to at least $750,000, but not known to be as high as $10 million. These are referred to as medium-size companies. Companies with assets known to be less than $750,000 were recorded as acquiring an entire company somewhat more often than they acquired part of a company; whereas the proportion was 3 to 1 for acquiring companies in all other size categories. 

Considering all acquisitions, those recorded by the Commission and those that did not come to the Commission's attention, large companies are thought to have made less than half of the acquisitions of medium-size properties and much less than a third of the acquisitions of small properties. It also is believed that medium-size companies acquired fewer small properties than did small companies. But among the acquisitions recorded by the Commission, the large companies acquired more medium-size properties than were acquired by mediumsize companies; they also acquired more small properties than were acquired by small companies. The statistical analysis also suggests that medium-size companies were recorded as the acquirers of small properties more frequently than were small companies. However, the contrast is not great, and may have resulted from the operation of chance in drawing the sample.

A study was made of the frequency with which acquisitions recorded by the Commission conferred each of seven types of advantage on the acquiring company. 

1. Additional capacity to supply a market already supplied by the acquirer. 

2. Lengthened product lines. 

3. Product diversification. 

4. Facilities to produce goods the acquiring firm had formerly purchased. 

5. facilities to process or distribute goods the acquiring firm formerly sold. 

6. Facilities in markets not previously served, but of the same type already owned by the acquiring firm. 

7. Other advantages (empty plants, patents, etc.). By far the most frequent single type was additional capacity to supply a market already supplied by the acquirer. This type of advantage was observed in two acquisitions out of five. Also common were lengthened product

lines and diversifications, each of which may have been present in one acquisition out of four. In one instance in about eight the acquiring company procured facilities to produce goods it formerly had purchased. Facilities to process or distribute goods formerly sold were identified in roughly one acquisition out of ten. The same statement can be made concerning acquisitions of facilities of the type already owned by the acquirer, but located in a market the acquirer had not previously served. About once in ten times also the Commission noted some other advantage, such as an empty plant, patents or a valuable "corporate shell."

The relative frequency with which these various advantages were observed was very much the same for acquisitions of large or mediumsize properties, compared with acquisitions of small properties; and for acquisitions by large companies, compared with acquisitions by medium-size or small companies. One significant difference was observed. A higher proportion of acquisitions of large or mediumsize properties than of small-size properties diversified the acquirer's business.

Procedures followed in acquisitions and mergers. Chapter IV describes in some detail various plans and procedures involved in acquisitions, drawing upon actual cases for illustrations of practical application.

The first step in a corporate acquisition is discovery by an enterpriser of an opportunity whereby an apparent advantage may be gained if one firm joins with or acquires all or part of another. The term "acquisition" as here used includes all business and corporate organizational and operational devices and arrangements by which the ownership and the management of independently operated properties and businesses are brought under the control of a single management. It is, therefore, considerably broader than either "merger, or "consolidation" as these terms are used in law. 

Promotional activities are carried on largely by five distinct functionaries: (1) the parties concerned (joint efforts), (2) the acquiring company, (3) the acquired company, (4) the divesting company disposing of part of its assets, and (5) outside financial or other interests. 

The first of these types is important in certain instances but it does not appear to be common. The second, promotion by the acquiring company, appears to be by far the most important type. The third, promotion by the acquired company, is common particularly where smaller companies wish to sell out to other companies. The fourth type, promotion by the divesting company, is common when a firm finds it desirable to divest itself of a part of its property or business and goes into the market to find a buyer. The fifth type, promotion by outside parties, is important when outside financial and other interests find it to their advantage (because of stock ownership, interest in products or service to be provided, or fees to be collected for promotional assistance) to see that the combinations or acquisitions are made.

While the operating executives inside the companies involved are responsible for promoting most acquisitions and mergers, important assistance is often received from outside agencies with experience in merger promotion. These outside interests include legal and economic consultants, research and industrial engineers, banks, investment houses, brokerage firms and others. While the Commission's staff has made no quantitative analysis of this matter, the limited evidence available suggests that more and more firms representing outside interests are becoming engaged or involved in the business of promoting or playing some other vital role in merger formation. Such interests have been much in evidence in previous merger waves, but they appear to have been even more active in recent years. While these interests may not have actually promoted many of the recent mergers they have greatly facilitated their promotion. 

Acquisitions and mergers are commonly effected through acquiring stock or purchasing assets. The plans most often used in important acquisitions are the exchange of stocks between the companies, and the purchase of stock of the acquired company from individual persons or firms at private sale or in the open market. In acquiring assets, a firm may (1) purchase all of the assets of a going concern, (2) take over that part of a going firm's assets or business of which it wishes to divest itself, or (3) take over the assets of a firm no longer operating when sold at private sale or through foreclosure. 

Several forms of organization are used in acquisitions and mergers among which are the following: (1) somewhat rarely a new company may be incorporated by joint effort to take over the merging firms; (2) more commonly the acquirer, without change in name or corporate organization, may take over the stock or assets, in whole or in part, of another corporation; (3) less frequently the acquirer may change its name, its form of organization, or its capital structure in order to take over another corporation; (4) much less frequently the acquirer may organize a new subsidiary corporation to purchase the stock or assets of another firm; or (5) the acquirer may have an existing subsidiary purchase another corporation.

The most widely used method of financing acquisitions is that of exchanging the stock of the acquiring firm for the stock of the acquired firm or firms. However, other methods are also frequently employed. 

When a firm has a substantial amount of surplus cash on hand, it has both the means and the incentive to acquisition activities, and may acquire assets outright or the stock claims behind them. Bank loans and other borrowed funds are also used in many instances for such purposes. Advantages of losses for tax purposes are also an important element in acquisition financing.

All of the forms and procedures followed in making corporate acquisitions indicated by the material reviewed are normal characteristics of modern corporation organization, finance, and management. 

They may lead to socially desirable developments to the extent that

they increase efficiency in production and distribution, or simply reflect a free and competitive market for used fixed assets. But they may also lead to undesirable results in that they may restrain or eliminate competition or tend toward monopoly in particular lines of goods or services. The methods used, therefore, provide no per se basis for evaluating the effects of corporate acquisitions on the public interest.

Economic forces and motives underlying acquisitions and mergers. 

Study of specific acquisitions in the manufacturing field indicates that when a manufacturer expands his producing capacity in order to attain savings based on the scale of his operations, his first decision must be whether to build new capacity or acquire some of that already existing. 

If he builds, he creates additional capacity and competition, perhaps in an industry already well supplied; if he buys, he reaps the possible advantages of increasing his capacity, acquiring some or all of the market previously served by a competitor, and at the same time may remove or nullify some competition in his market. These competitive considerations are especially important if he is diversifying into products new to him, but in the production of which others are well established. The same is true if he is acquiring capacity in order to supply new markets or to realize transportation or other cost savings in supplying products he already makes in markets where he is already selling, but at a cost disadvantage. They also apply if the proposed expansion involves such matters as increasing his capacity to produce raw materials, balancing his operations in the utilization of raw materials already produced, supplying himself with component parts, or expanding his total capacity to produce end products. The decision whether to build or buy, therefore, involves the weighing of many economic advantages and disadvantages between the two options of acquiring or building. The analysis of the economic forces discernible in acquisitions presented in Chapter V indicates that where satisfactory existing facilities are available for purchase at a price even approximating their new construction cost, the balance often is strongly weighted in favor of purchase.

Case study also indicates that much the same forces underlie the trend toward large-scale integrated control in wholesaling and retailing. Here again, many considerations weigh the balance in favor of growth by acquisition rather than growth by competitive internal expansion. Western Auto Supply Co. summarized the matter succinctly when it stated, respecting its proposed acquisition of the West Coast and Rocky Mountain chain of stores previously operated by Gamble- 

Skogmo under the name of "Western," that: "...the Company will acquire a complete organization of retail and wholesale outlets without incurring the development, organization and promotion costs normally required to build public recognition and good will for a trade name in new territories."1/ 

1/Western Auto Supply Co. (Mo.), 46th Annual Report, 1954, p. 5.

Other cases cited cover instances in which acquisitions of existing competitors offer opportunity for quick attainment of economies of scale, diversification, and stability of operations in both production and distribution without further competitive struggle. These cases include the integration and diversification by Consolidated Foods in the production and wholesale distribution of food products; of Avco 

Manufacturing Co. by acquisitions which have added a wide variety of household appliances and farm equipment to its previous lines, and of Burlington Mills Corp. in its expansion over the years from a company engaged exclusively in the manufacture of rayon textiles until its operations today cover the manufacturing and marketing of yarns and fabrics made from synthetic fibers, cotton and wool, and numerous mixtures of both synthetic and natural fibers. 

Strictly competitive factors, however, are not the only forces underlying acquisition. When many acquisitions by larger companies are examined, it is often found that the lesser ability of smaller companies to command financial resources needed for expansion or modernization is one of a number of factors which cause the selling company to take the initiative in offering to sell. Then too, there are many instances in which companies in financial difficulties offer to sell in order to salvage something from an impending wreck. Such situations offer opportunity to the promoter-financier who becomes the initial acquirer and subsequently sells the company in whole or in part to others. Investment of available capital is a factor in such acquisitions by promoters.

The same factor is to be noted in instances in which a company having surplus cash not immediately needed in its operations invests it in the securities of other companies, either in the same or an unrelated industry. Such investments may subsequently prove to be the initial step in acquisitions carried out either as further investment and diversification of the acquirer's business, or as a means of salvaging the investments already made.

Tax savings possible under various provisions of the Internal Revenue Act granting more favorable rates on capital gains as compared with the rates applicable to operating profits of corporations and personal incomes of individuals, the provisions covering tax-free exchanges of stock, and the provisions governing the carrying forward of past operating losses as credits against future earnings are also important factors. In numerous instances, these savings have been cited by representatives of both parties as important considerations in acquisitions and mergers. Ageing owners and managers of successful closely owned concerns desiring to retire and adjust their estates often prefer to sell their companies to larger corporations in order to convert their holdings into securities having a more ready market than the unregistered securities of smaller companies. Tax savings attainable by a company that has large taxable earnings merging with one that has substantial deductible past losses, with the latter continuing as the surviving corporation, are such that those wishing to

promote such mergers have been advertising publicly their desire to acquire financially weak companies.

The Nash-Hudson, Kaiser-Willys, and Studebaker-Packard mergers have posed a dilemma for antitrust agencies, namely, is the public interest best served by permitting the independents to merge and thereby to strengthen their competitive positions, or by attempting to force them to continue to compete independently with the three dominant companies at the risk of possible eventual disappearance of at least some of them through total withdrawal or bankruptcy? Review of the facts underlying acquisitions and mergers indicates that, regardless of the size of the corporations involved, the economic forces and motives discernible are not per se different from those upon which all business judgments are based respecting the ownership and exchange of property in a free economy. The operation of these forces on a large scale, however, carries with it such adverse economic effects on third parties, and on the economy as a whole, as to bring their unrestrained operation into conflict with existing public policy and law. In policing such situations, there is sound basis for interference with the freedom of either individuals or corporations to acquire or dispose of property only where, on the basis of considered judgment, it can be shown that adverse effects on competition, and hence on the public interest, outweigh the benefits of individual and corporate freedom of action.

Determining competitive consequences under Section 7. Chapter 

VII explores the uses and limits of economic information in the administration of Section 7 of the Clayton Act, as amended in 1950, in determining the probable competitive consequences of an acquisition. 

Such consequences cannot be determined from the mere fact of acquisition itself, but must be determined from facts concerning: (a) the character of the acquiring and the acquired company and the transaction; (b) the character of the markets affected; and (c) immediate changes in the acquiring company and in the adjustments of other companies operating in these markets. 

An acquisition which substantially reduces the opportunity or the incentive of sellers (or potential sellers) or buyers (or potential buyers) to enter new markets, to experiment with new channels of sale or distribution, or to exercise choice among products and prices, may substantially lessen competition. The range of opportunities and incentives is determined by such facts as the kind and number of sources and outlets, the range of purchasing and selling prices, the extent to which buyers or sellers are dependent upon competitors for supplies or outlets, and the relative restrictiveness of long-term contractual arrangements tying suppliers and customers or groups of competitors to each other in more or less stable relationships which cannot be penetrated by other companies.

All of such facts cannot and need not be investigated in each case; only those facts which are relevant in particular market contexts, and can be obtained at reasonable cost, should become a part of the record. 

In certain cases the facts that can be obtained at reasonable cost may leave gaps in the information that would be helpful in reaching greater certainty as to the competitive consequences of an acquisition. While sufficient data to support a conclusion is required, sufficient data to provide absolute certainty as to competitive consequences would nullify the words "where the effect may be" in the Clayton Act and convert them into "where the effect is. 99 Although administration of Section 7 of the Clayton Act, as amended, requires market facts, special problems are connected with the use of such information (a) for economic conclusions and (b) as legal evidence. 

Use of the market data in reaching economic conclusions raises special fact-finding problems because relevant information is not always available in published sources. Indeed, there is no systematic publication of the mergers themselves as they occur. Since

information obtained through interviews with representatives of companies not parties to a case (third companies) may not be representative of the market in question and may contain a high degree of unverifiable opinion, new market information may have to be built up through surveys of third companies. However, such surveys may, when complete, contain the element of contingency associated with all mass economic data. 

Use of market information as legal evidence raises problems in connection with the admissibility, relevance and weight of data concerning the activities of companies which are not parties to a case. 

Such problems are particularly acute where the legitimate desire of the government or respondent to verify individual items of information must be reconciled with the need of third companies for protection from the injury that may occur if confidential information is disclosed to their competitors, suppliers or customers.

In fitting its responsibilities under Section 7 of the Clayton Act with its other administrative obligations, the Federal Trade Commission must perform a case selection and market analysis function separate from its general economic reporting and its direct trial work. 

This is so because, unlike its general economic reports, results of acquisition surveys must meet standards of legal evidence; at the same time, however, questions for investigation must be selected in terms of their relevance for appraisal of competitive consequences in the market affected and in no other terms.

Although the use of market information in the administration of Section 7 of the Clayton Act raises special problems, refusal to use such information will not solve these problems. Conclusions concerning the competitive consequences of particular acquisitions cannot be reached on the basis of rules-of-thumb; they must be reached on the basis of the market facts relevant for an understanding of such consequences.
