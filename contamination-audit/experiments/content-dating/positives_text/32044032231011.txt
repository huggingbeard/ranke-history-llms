Report to the President, Under Paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401, of Developments with 

Regard to Watch Movements, Since Modifica tion of the Trade-Agreement Concession on July 27, 1954 The tariff concession on watch movements granted in the trade agreement with Switzerland signed on January 9, 1936, was modified by the 

President by proclamation No. 3062, effective July 27, 1954. That action by the President was taken after an investigation and report to him on the subject by the Tariff Commission, pursuant to section 7 of the Trade 

Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended. 

Under Executive Order 10401 of October 14, 1952 (17 F.R. 9125), procedures are prescribed for the periodic review of a tariff modification resulting from an escape-clause action, with a view to determining whether the whole or any part of the concession that was withdrawn may be restored without causing or threatening serious injury to the domestic industry concerned. This report is the second that the Commission has made on watch movements pursuant to paragraph 1 of that Executive order; the 

Commission's first report was made on July 25, 1956. At that time, the 

President was advised that "It does not appear to the Commission that the developments to date with regard to the product in question indicate such a change in the competitive situation as to warrant institution at this time of a formal investigation under the provisions of paragraph 2 of Executive Order 10401."

Tariff duties applicable to watch movements 1/

Watch movements are dutiable under paragraph 367 of the Tariff Act of 1930. The duties, both under that act and under the subsequent modifications thereof, are specific and, in general, vary inversely with the width of the movements, and directly with the number of jewels 2/ and the number of adjustments that they incorporate. Certain features, such as those contained in self-winding movements, are subject to additional specific duties--except when they are incorporated in movements containing more than 17 jewels. Movements with more than 17 jewels, irrespective of their size, jewel count, number of adjustments, or other features, are subject to a single specific rate of duty--$10.75 each. 

The tariff schedules do not differentiate between "jeweled-lever" and "pin-lever" movements. / Although most imported pin-lever movements contain either no jewels or only 1 jewel, a considerable number of them contain 7 jewels, and some, as many as 17 jewels. The tariff provisions

Tariff Act of 1930.-Paragraph 367 of the Tariff Act of 1930 as originally enacted provided for 7 different basic rates of duties on watch movements containing at least 2 but not more than 7 jewels each. 

All of those rates were specific, and they varied inversely with the width of the movement. The rates ranged from $1.25 each for movements in the largest width bracket (more than 1.5 inches wide but less than 

1.77 inches) to $2.50 each for those in the smallest (0.6 inch or less in width). Movements with more than 7 jewels but not more than 17 jewels were subject to the aforementioned basic rates plus a duty of 15 cents for each jewel in excess of 7. Movements with no jewels or with only 

1 jewel were dutiable at 60 percent of the basic rates. All movements with 17 or fewer jewels were subject also to the following supplementary duties: $1 for each adjustment and $1 on each movement constructed or designed to operate in excess of 47 hours without rewinding, or if self-winding, or if a self-winding mechanism may be incorporated. All movements with more than 17 jewels were dutiable at $10.75 each.

Rates under 1936 Swiss agreement.--Under the trade agreement with 

Switzerland, effective February 15, 1936, the number of basic rates of duty on movements (i.e., movements containing at least 2 but not more than 7 jewels) was reduced from 7 to 4 groups; and the rates of duty applicable thereunder ranged from 28 percent to 42 percent below the rates previously applicable. For example, the duty on movements (containing 2-7 jewels) that were 1.5 inches wide or wider was reduced from $1.25 to 90 cents each and that on those 0.6 inch or less in width, from $2.50 to $1.80 each. Movements with more than 7 jewels but not more than 17 jewels became dutiable at the new basic rates plus a duty of 9 cents (previously 15 cents) for each jewel in excess of 7. The duties on movements having no jewels or only 1 jewel were condensed from 7 to 2 groups, under which all movements except the smallest-previously dutiable at rates ranging from 75 cents to $1.35 each--became dutiable at 75 cents each, and the smallest movements (0.6 inch or less in width) became dutiable at 90 cents each. Duties for each adjustment and on the 'special mechanisms described earlier were reduced from $1.00 to 50 cents. These supplementary duties applied, as before, only to movements with 17 or fewer jewels. The duty on movements with more than 17 jewels was bound against increase at $10.75 each. All other duties applicable to articles entered under paragraph 367 that were not reduced likewise were bound against increase. Thus, the very highest and the very lowest rates of duty applicable to movements entered under paragraph 367 were not changed by the Swiss trade agreement. 

The Swiss agreement provided, however, that reductions in duty would not be applicable to any movement with fewer than 7 jewels if such movement contained a bushing or its equivalent (other than a substitute for a jewel) in any position customarily occupied by a jewel.

Changes in competitive situation subsequent to 1954 tariff increases The Commission's first report on watch movements made under Executive Order 10401 described the major developments that had occurred in the competitive situation between 1953 (the year before the increased duties went into effect) and the first quarter of 1956 (the latest period for which statistical data were then available). The Commission's earlier report contains details of those developments. The present report' covers primarily the developments that have occurred since the period covered by that report.

Imports.--The volume of imports of movements dutiable under paragraph 367 was slightly higher in 1956 than in any preceding year, but the value of the imports in 1956 was considerably below the peak level reached in 1953 (see table 4). Imports in 1953 totaled 13.4 million units--the highest then on record--valued at almost 71 million dollars. Imports were considerably smaller in the 2 following years--10.5 million units valued at 54.5 million dollars in 1954 and 10.9 million units valued at 50.5 million dollars in 1955--but rose to 13.5 million units valued at 57.3 million dollars in 1956. Imports in the first quarter of 1957 amounted to 2.6 million units valued at 12.0 million dollars.

The changes in the total volume of annual imports and the accompanying changes in their aggregate value in the years mentioned above reflect primarily a major shift in the composition of the imports. The proportion of pin-lever movements rose sharply during those years, particularly between 1955 and 1956 (see table 12). Eighty percent of the rise in total imports between 1955 and 1956 was accounted for by increased imports of 1or no-jewel movements, virtually all of which contain pin-lever escapements (see table 5).

Imports of 1or no-jewel movements rose from 3.9 million units in 

1955 to 6.0 million units in 1956; imports of 2to 7-jewel movements amounted to 1.3 million units in both years; imports of 8to 15-jewel movements declined from 122 thousand units in 1955 to 57 thousand units in 1956; imports of 16to 17-jewel movements (almost all of which were 

17-jewel movements) increased from 5.6 million units in 1955 to 6.2 million units in 1956; and imports of movements with the more-than-17jewels amounted in each of the 2 years to 17 thousand units--a level somewhat higher than in any earlier year (see table 5). 

The phenomenal increase in imports of pin-lever movements since the 1954 increase in watch duties may be attributed to a combination of circumstances. Most of such movements contain either no jewels or only 

1 jewel. Such movements are generally much lower priced than jeweledlever movements, and they were subject in 1954 to a much smaller increase

in duty than were the jeweled-lever movements--most of which contain 17 jewels and none of which contain fewer than 7 jewels (see table 2). 

After the tariff increases of 1954, a number of importers--notably those not identified with nationally advertised brands of jeweled-lever watches--began importing much smaller pin-lever movements than were produced domestically and marketed them in attractively designed cases, both imported and domestic. These "high fashion" ladies' watches were well received by the public. Importers also promoted the sale of thin models of men's pin-lever watches that bore a closer outward resemblance to conventional jeweled-lever watches (domestic and imported) than to any domestic pin-lever watches. These watches likewise were well received by the public.

Increased consumer acceptance of imported pin-lever watches must be attributed not only to their comparatively low prices but also to the great variety of sizes, shapes, and styles in which they are offered and to the improvements in the quality of many of them in recent years. 

Another factor of considerable but indeterminate importance in furthéring the sale of pin-lever watches has been the mounting cost of · repairs on jeweled-lever watches.

With increasing public acceptance of imported pin-lever watches, more importers, including some domestic watch manufacturers, have entered the field. Watches containing imported pin-lever movements are no longer regarded as competing solely or primarily with watches containing domestic pin-lever movements. Many of them are now widely regarded as offering

serious competition to jeweled-lever watches, both imported and domestic. 

One of the largest importer-assemblers of a nationally advertised brand of jeweled-lever watches recently purchased a pin-lever watch factory in Switzerland and plans to market nationally advertised pin-lover watches in the United States through a subsidiary.

6.2 million units in 1956; part of the increase is believed to have resulted from larger imports of complete self-winding movements as well as of movements designed to accommodate self-winding mechanisms for attachment to the movements after importation. The separate importations of 

17-jewel movements and of jeweled-self-winding mechanisms for attachment to such movements after importation makes possible a considerable savings 1/ in duty.

Another substantial proportion of sales of watches incorporating imported movements consists of those that are either lower or higher priced than wholly domestic watches, or those--such as self-winding watches in ladies' sizes, chronographs, alarms, etc.--that contain special features not found in wholly domestic watches. Self-winding watches, a type that has become increasingly popular in recent years, contain principally imported movements. Some of the movements entered under paragraph 367 of the Tariff Act of 1930 are of the types and sizes used in clocks rather than watches, but no separate statistics are available on the volume or value of the imports of these movements. On the basis of estimates prepared by the Tariff 

Commission, however, imports of clock movements entered under paragraph 367 totaled about 1 million units in 1956, and 169 thousand units in the first quarter of 1957 (see table 4). The imports in 1955 were 1.2 million units and in the first quarter of 1956 were 210 thousand units. The tariff increases of July 1954 have had little, if any, restraining effect on imports of these clock movements inasmuch as most of them have been 1or no-jewel movements more than 1.5 inches wide, on which the rate of duty, 75 cents each, has remained unchanged since the passage of the Tariff Act of 1930.

A substantial proportion of the 2to 7-jewel movements entered under paragraph 367 also are used in clocks, but the 1954 tariff increases on most of such movements amounted to only 35 cents per unit. 

Some of the movements imported under paragraph 367 are cased in the United States in domestic cases and then reexported with benefit of drawback. The volume of such trade, however, has always been small. 

The reexports in 1956 amounted to only 196 thousand units, a quantity much below that in any of the immediately preceding years (see table 4). 

Changes in unit value of imports.-As observed in the Commission's earlier report, various importers of watch movements reacted differently to the increases in United States tariff duties in July 1954. Some continued to import substantially the same kinds and qualities of movements that they had imported previously. Others reduced the cost of their imports by lowering the quality of the movements slightly (usually in ways that affected appearance more than performance), by lowering the jewel count of their jeweled-lever movements, or by shifting from jeweled-lever movements (with 7, 15, or 17 jewels) to pin-lever movements (mostly with 1 or no jewel but some with 7 to as many as 17 jewels). 

During the last year or so, the principal developments have been an acceleration in the shift from jeweled-lever to pin-lever movements and an increase in imports of self-winding watch movements, including movements to which imported self-winding mechanisms are attached after importation. 

The Commission has never been able to ascertain in what measure, if at all, Swiss exporters absorbed the increases in United States tariff 

---

duties in 1954, either by reducing prices for identical merchandise or by granting or increasing "kickbacks" (partial refunds of purchase price).

The practice of granting "kickbacks" has apparently existed for some time, but the Commission has no way of knowing on what scale or whether the practice became more pronounced after the increase in United States duties. During the past few years, however, world consumption of Swiss watch movements has risen sharply, Swiss production has attained unprecedented levels, and virtually no unemployment has existed in the Swiss watch industry. Under such conditions, Swiss exporters would not appear to be under pressure to reduce prices, or to increase "kickbacks," to importers in the United States.

The average foreign unit value of all movements admitted under paragraph 367 declined steadily from $5.29 in 1953 to $4.65 in 1955 and $4.24 in 1956, and then rose to $4.58 in the first quarter of 1957 (see table 7). 

Most of the change during the past year or so appears to have resulted from changes in the composition of the imports rather than from changes in the foreign value of identical movements. The same observation may be made about the average unit value of the imports in each of the major jewel-count brackets.

The average unit value of 1or no-jewel movements declined from $1.82 each in 1954 to $1.70 in 1955 and $1.57 in 1956, and then rose to $1.64 in the first quarter of 1957 (see table 7). This upturn reflects primarily a decline in the proportion of movements used principally in small clocks, and an increase in the proportion used in women's wristwatches-notably those in the smallest sizes. A special analysis of individual

import entries in September 1956 shows that the unit values of 1or nojewel movements entered in that month ranged from $0.54 to $7.18. 

The average unit value of 2to 7-jewel movements declined from $2.84 in 1954 to $2.71 in 1955, but rose to $3.27 in 1956 and $3.51 in the first quarter of 1957. The decline reflected lower proportions of jeweled-lever movements, and the subsequent increase, higher proportions of such movements. The unit values of 2to 7-jewel movements ranged between $1.27 and $13.56 in September 1956.

The average unit value of 8to 15-jewel movements rose steadily from $5.37 in 1954 to $6.90 in 1955, $7.69 in 1956, and $10.72 in the first quarter of 1957. The rise was due primarily to the increased proportion of very high-priced movements used in clocks. Unit values of 8to 15jewel movements ranged between $2.93 and $36.50 in September 1956.

The average unit value of 16to 17-jewel movements changed but little between 1954 and the first quarter of 1957; the range during that period was from $6.92 to $7.11 each. This narrow range fails to reveal the considerable change that occurred during that period in the proportions of self-winding, alarm, calendar, and chronograph movements--both jeweledlever and pin-lever--in that jewel count. In September 1956, unit values of 16to 17-jewel movements ranged from $3.00 to $172.88.

principally variations in the quality of the conventional type movements imported, as well as in the proportions of self-winding, chronograph, calendar, and alarm movements. The unit values of the more-than-17-jewel movements in September 1956 ranged between $9.63 and $106.42.

Share of consumption supplied by domestic timepieces 

For many years, domestic manufacturers of pin-lever watches have felt that they encountered serious competition not only from all imported pin-lever watches but also from most low-priced watches containing imported jeweled-lever movements. Domestic manufacturers of jeweled-lever watches have long felt that they received serious competition from virtually all watches containing imported jeweled-lever movements, with little regard to the prices at which they were sold. More recently these

manufacturers have been concerned over the large and rapidly rising sale of narrow and thin watches incorporating imported pin-lever movements, mostly unjeweled or with only 1 jewel but some with as many as 17 jewels. Some of these imported watches so closely resemble highpriced, jeweled-lever watches that few laymen can readily distinguish between them. Further, since all domestic producers of jeweled-lever watches also market a lower priced line of jeweled-lever watches containing imported movements, they feel the competitive impact of imported pin-lever watches on their sales of at least their lowest priced, imported jeweled-lever watches.

Domestic consumption of watches containing domestic movements (jeweled-lever and pin-lever combined) rose from 8.4 million units in 

1955 to 9.3 million units in 1956--the highest in any year since 1951 (see table 12). Apparent consumption of all watches (domestic and imported) rose from 17.7 million units in 1955 to 21.6 million units in 

1956, the highest level for any year since 1951. The share of total consumption of all watches that was supplied by watches containing domestic jeweled-lever movements declined from 10.6 percent in 1955 to 9.3 percent in 1956, the smallest share in any year since the war period. The corresponding share supplied by watches containing domestic pin-lever movements declined from 36.6 percent in 1955 to 33.8 percent in 1956, a share just slightly below that in 1954 but somewhat higher than that in 1953. 

Domestic producers of small, spring-driven clocks supplied in 1955 an estimated 71 percent of the total domestic consumption of such clocks

plus the imported clocks most directly competitive with them (see ṭable 18). That share was the lowest for any year since the war period. In 1956 the corresponding share was 77 percent, and in the first quarter of 1957 the share was 79 percent.

Average hourly wages paid to workers engaged in the production of jeweled-lever watches have increased steadily since the war period (see table 13). The rate increased from $2.04 in 1955 to $2.19 in 1956 and to $2.24 in the first quarter of 1957.

Operations of domestic pin-lever watch producers Domestic production and inventories.--The domestic output of pinlever pocket watches and wristwatches (combined) declined from 8.4 million units in 1951 to 5.7 million units in 1954 and then rose to 7.4 million units

in 1956. Output in the first quarter of 1957, however, amounted to only 1.1 million units, a level below that in the first quarter of 1956, when output totaled 1.6 million units. 

The output of both pocket watches and wristwatches rose steadily from 1954 through 1956 but never reached the quantity produced in 1947 and 1948. The increase was more pronounced for pocket watches than for wristwatches (see table 10). During 1954-56, pocket-watch production rose from 2.6 million units to 3.6 million, whereas wristwatch production rose from 3.0 million units to 3.7 million units. The upturn in production of pocket watches marks a reversal of the preceding trend in which pocket watches were giving way to wristwatches.

Inventories of pin-lever watches in the hands of domestic manufacturers declined from 726 thousand units at the end of 1953 to 378 thousand units at the end of 1955 and then rose to 769 thousand units at the end of 1956 (see table 16). Pocket watches accounted for almost 57 percent of the total 1956 year-end inventory. Between the end of 1956 and the end of the first quarter of 1957, total inventories declined by about 

116 thousand units.

Domestic manufacturers have changed the construction and outward appearance of their pocket watches very little over the years. They continue to supply the great bulk of the domestic consumption of such watches, but imports of pin-lever pocket watches have increased somewhat during the past few years. Domestic manufacturers now make some narrower and thinner pin-lever wristwatches than they formerly did.

22 During 1957, some manufacturers expect to substitute domestically made pin-lover movements for some of the thinner and narrower wristwatches that they have been importing. None of them, however, expect to make watches in the immediate future that contain movements as narrow or as thin as those incorporated in some of the most popular imported watches, claiming that they could not do so at a cost that would enable them to compete with imported watches.

One domestic manufacturer now markets men's models of self-winding, pin-lever wristwatches, all parts of which are made in the United States. 

Sales of wholly domestic watches.--Total sales of domestic pin-lever watches declined from 17.2 million dollars in 1953 to 16.6 million in 

1954, but increased to 20.3 million in 1955 and to 22.7 million in 1956. 

Sales in the first quarter of 1957 amounted to 4.5 million dollars, whereas those in the first quarter of 1956 totaled 5.1 million dollars-a decline of almost 12 percent.

Sales of small pin-lever clocks made by the concerns that manufacture pin-lever watches declined by 22 percent between 1953 and 1954. 

Sales in 1956 were 3 percent below those in 1955 and 14 percent below those in 1953. Sales in the first quarter of 1957 were at a much lower rate than that prevailing in 1956 and 32 percent below the sales in the first quarter of 1956.

Financial experience.--Financial data of the same general type as were obtained from producers of jeweled-lever watches were also submitted by producers of pin-lever watches. Based on these data, the net.

operating profits (before income taxes) increased substantially between 1954 and 1955 but declined in 1956 to below the 1953 level. The ratio of net operating profits to net sales of domestic watches averaged 5.1 percent in 1953, 2.8 percent in 1954, 5.8 percent in 1955, and about 

3 percent in 1956.

Employment and wages.--Total employment of production and related workers engaged in the production of pin-lever watches declined from a peak of 6,081 in 1948 to 2,349 in 1953 (see table 14). Employment then increased steadily to 2,900 in 1956, but declined in the first quarter of 1957 to 2,350, compared with 3,120 in the first quarter of 1956.

Average hourly wages paid by the manufacturers of pin-lever watches to workers engaged in the production of pin-lever watches increased steadily from $1.44 in 1953 to $1.58 in 1956 and to $1.60 in the first quarter of 1957.
