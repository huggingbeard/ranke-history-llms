by 

MONA MORGAN 

The play of Hamlet has inspired hundreds of men and women to comment upon it. 

Their essays and criticisms could easily fill an enormous library. Even a cursory reading of them reveals that they differ on practically every point; they are unable to agree upon the play's meaning, purpose or its interpretation. 

One of the principal reasons for Shakespeare's success with his audiences of his day and those of our own is that he expresses himself so easily, so completely and translates the sublime into every-day language.

He is still the master playwright. His delineations of character are clear-cut, his plots full of vital interest, his men and women intensely human. In fact, Shakespeare's simplicity of style is one of his greatest charms. 1

Lovers of the theatre may well ask themselves how it happens that the play of Hamlet should be Shakespeare's one departure from simplicity, his only tragedy to be mysterious, obscure and ambiguous; in fact, this play has caused a controversy which has persisted for over a hundred years.

A literary mystery never fails to interest the mentally alive, and Hamlet is one of the most discussed plays in all the world. 

A question asked by almost every Shakespearean student is, "Was Hamlet insane?" 

Others are,-"Why did Hamlet delay avenging his father's murder?" "Did Hamlet love 

Ophelia?" etc.

But these questions are really nothing compared to what the professional critics have evolved by way of comment and explanation.

Yet, in spite of a good deal of confusion, there is no more mystery in this play than in any of the other profound and brilliant works from the same pen. However, once a

mystery has been started, well advertised and endorsed by professors, the mystery hounds will not let it rest; their speculations will go on forever.

Anyone who starts an investigation in the expectation of finding a hidden mystery is very likely to find what he seeks. If whole communities may be persistently deluded into a belief in ghosts, haunted houses and witches merely by the power of suggestion, why may not a few score actors and critics be made to seize with delight upon a dramatic mystery story, if once well advertised and properly sanctioned? What does it matter that the author himself made no pretensions to mystery; that the story of the play is derived from an old, simple, well-known legend; that it is developed scene by scene in the perfectly logical sequence essential to a successful play? . . . Still, mystery lovers, once they have set themselves in motion, will insist that the mystery is there, and they will attempt to produce it, even if they have to

tear the play to tatters in order to reconstruct it from the fragments. 

Although Shakespearean actors may not know it, they have been so influenced in this direction that they have been forced into a serious quandary by the commentators. 

The controversy on the meaning of Hamlet was started before Dr. Johnson's time and has affected every stage presentation since then. According to William Winter's account of Booth's performance in the part, he too had been misled, and it is obvious that neither of them had a thorough understanding of the play. 

Contemporary actors such as Forbes-Robertson, Sothern, Kellerd, Mantell, Hampden, Barrymore, etc., excellent in their way, had been, at the time they studied the role, misled by what the commentators had written about the part. It is safe to assert that not one of these artists attempted to play the part without "studying up" on the "authorities," and I think it is equally safe to

wager that their performances would have been a great deal better if they had read the play first.

There are two distinct and fundamental artistic forces in the theatre; the creative, which belongs to the playwright and the interpretive, which is the actor's. Many a playwright has found unsuspected virtues in his own work when it was performed by artistic and skillful players.

Therefore it is well to trust to the intelligent actor's intuition on most matters of interpretation. When, however, the playwright is dead and therefore cannot be consulted, let the player avoid the commentators, who at best, can only pass on to him the mistakes of accumulated time, and limit his own natural powers. I should prefer seeing a talented actor's mistakes than the dramatization of hundreds of scholars' confusions. 

Every prominent player who has essayed part of Hamlet in the past twenty years has, more or less, copied his predecessor, and it the

is ny purpose to return to Shakespeare, who was himself an actor, to ascertain its true interpretation.

It might be well, however, at this point, to review briefly the outstanding critics who have caused so much controversy. 

William Winter, one of the prominent dramatic critics of the nineteenth century, whose opinion swayed both actor and public, said: "Hamlet was a gentle, sensitive being, half mad with chronic melancholy, one of intermittent, compound, confusional insanity," and he adds,-"essential constituents of the part are spiritualized intellect, haunted over-wrought imagination, exquisite sensibility, sombre dignity, a wavering mentality, piteous isolation and bitter scorn, comingled with a forlorn fitful humour."

During the early part of the nineteenth century, literary men seemed to try to outdo each other in commenting upon this play. 

Coleridge, in 1808, said: 

The first thing we should ask ourselves is "What did 

Shakespeare mean when he wrote 'Hamlet?' "

A year later, A. W. Schlegel, German translator of Shakespeare, wrote: this enigmatical work resembles those irrational equations that will in no manner admit of solution. 

Then Hazlitt, about eight years later, made this surprising comment:

There is no play which suffers so much in being transferred to the stage. Hamlet himself seems hardly capable of being acted. 

James Russell Lowell thought that ""Hamlet,' among all the characters of

Shakespeare, is the most eminently a metaphysician and psychologist."

France too, had her share of commentators in this vital matter for in 1864 Victor Hugo wrote: "Hamlet' is the chef-d'oevre of tragedy dreaming." Imagine any actor depicting for three hours "tragedy-dreaming!" 

Lord Campbell observed that:

Hamlet is an ideal existence, an abstraction, impossible to tell in words. We cannot tell what Shakespeare may have meant.

Then he makes the staggering suggestion that

Shakespeare must have forgotten to properly dispose of Ophelia! And these erudite comments come from one of the finest legal minds of the nineteenth century!

Since theatrical tradition, like the gossip's tale, should, in all justice to everyone involved, be traced to its source, we cannot point out too forcibly how important these "paper bullets" from the brain are: many generations have believed them, and the modern actor is still being influenced by them.

No one can tell at this time the origin of this "profound" investigation of the play. 

Who it was who first deemed Hamlet insane, irresolute, half-witted, a deep philosopher, a symbol of life and death, and all the rest must remain forever secret. But we do know, however, whether the investigation was started in earnest or in jest, we have been suffering from the consequences ever since. 

It is very likely that Hamlet's character has been judged superficially (the learned

commentators notwithstanding), and that his delay in killing the king which persists until the last scene of the last act causes our critics some concern. According to them, when Hamlet has heard the disclosure of the ghost, he should have "flown to his revenge." 

To account for this postponement, they have twisted the obvious meaning of the lines, created a half-demented philosopher and jumbled a good play into some sort of a psychological thesis which would never have been produced by Shakespeare in his day, nor indeed (save for the fame of the author) in any day.

This is how they arrive at their conclusions: from the delay to kill the king, they assume Hamlet to be weak and irresolute, they insist there is a mystery to account for the delay, and from that they go on to their own confusion and to the confusion of their readers. The Hamlet they discuss and criticize as weak and irresolute is not the Hamlet of Shakespeare, and for this they do not place

the blame where it rightfully belongs on themselves, but on Shakespeare.

In order to get away from this confusion they have created we must dispense with all assumptions of mystery and mysticism and to realize that it is a drama intended for presentation in the theatre.

It is not Shakespeare's Hamlet which is incomprehensible, but the Hamlet of the commentator's. To contend, as some do, that

Shakespeare did not intend the play of Hamlet to be understood, or that he forgot 

Ophelia, is sheer nonsense.

Some critics have told us with an air of profundity that this tragedy is not a mere acting play; that it is something more, a deep study in psychology or ethics or metaphysics, written rather for students to muddle and fuss with than as an entertainment or boxoffice attraction.

As for the dreamy, irresponsible youth that they have created, not only is he undiscoverable in the play but he is incapable of

being the hero of any successful play whatsoever, lacking in the human quality essential to a stage hero.

Tradition on the English speaking stage is a power to conjure with; there are many producers and actors who agree with Ben Jonson's tribute to Shakespeare's genius, "He was not for an age but for all time." But it is essential to the furtherance of a deeper appreciation of his truth and art that this great dramatist be "modestly examined" for the internal merits which his works contain. 

It is not possible or logical that customs of interpretation and production should be adhered to indefinitely without variation. The mistakes of yesterday can never become the artistic creations of today. 

Any actor or actress who has taken part in any classical revival can testify that practically all the "business" and the readings are revived with the play itself. "You must cross on that line," the director will say. If the actor is sufficiently courageous, he will de-

mand the reason. The answer is usually something like this, "Booth did it that way and so must you." The name "Booth" is so awe-inspiring that any modern actor would be considered an infidel if he questioned the wisdom or efficacy of anything done by this eminent personage of the nineteenth century.

Nevertheless, the audiences of today are not the same and methods of production are entirely different. We no longer act behind gaslight. Scenery, effects and the general management of the theatre have changed, and yet, after more than three hundred years, Shakespeare has more universal appeal, more true conception of real entertainment, and there is no dramatist whose work can be more readily adapted to all ages and languages than his. 

It has been "vogue" for actors to turn "intellectual" when playing in Shakespeare.

Louis Parker described it as "getting on mental stilts." Tradition in Shakespeare's case

does not end with using some famous deceased actor's prompt book. It must go on and on, back and back into the records of time until the young actor has absorbed all of the guess work done by venerable gentlemen in the privacy of their libraries, aided and abetted by obliging printers. 

The infrequent productions of Shakespeare's plays, in spite of their charm and flexibility, can be accounted for in that very word "tradition." Modern audiences are bored by antiquated methods, certainly not by the masterpieces of his genius. 

Let us then approach this play of Hamlet unoppressed by what has been done and written about it in the past. From the very beginning of the story we find that the playwright has given to Hamlet all of the characteristics of a medieval prince of a warlike nation. As we read further we find that he is well-bred and dignified, shrewd, quick in thought and deed, proud of his name, rank and lineage; ambitious, high-spirited, dan-

gerous when opposed in matters touching him nearly; suspicious and crafty, and ready, according to the custom of his time, to take a cruel and bloody revenge for a wrong or insult. And that his outstanding characteristic is a furious, almost ungovernable temper.

If you have ever attended a modern production of this play, you will remember that this proud, fearless young prince, who might have made his first appearance in the Court scene with the boldness of a gallant of his time, probably was "discovered" or slipped on to the stage with a sort of furtive meekness and woebegone countenance, both being more appropriate to a demure old monk than to the heir of the Danish throne. 

There is nothing in the story or the language of the play to justify this strange presentation of Hamlet. When the play starts, his father has been dead some months, and the fact of his murder is not known. 

Professional critics and commentators

have considered him to be a profound thinker, a philosopher, but strangely enough no character in the play describes him in this way, nor is he so portrayed by Shakespeare.

Hamlet's first utterance of any importance, the familiar "inky cloak” speech is customarily delivered in a sad and measured cadence: 'Tis not alone my inky cloak, good mother,

Nor customary suits of solemn black, . . . these indeed seem, For they are actions that a man might play:

But I have that within which passeth show; 

These but the trappings and suits of woe. 

We have all lost loved ones, we all have our private griefs, but no one of intelligence makes a public exhibition of such a common sorrow as the death of a relative even though it is a father. Hamlet is no exception. He was the last man in Denmark to be permanently cast down by any of the ordinary (or even extraordinary) incidences of life.

The line, "I have that within which passeth

show," is popularly supposed to refer to a deeper grief for his father's death than could be expressed by an "inky cloak." But this would be very shallow and affected, for every wearer of funeral black is supposed to have feelings beyond the mere show of outward mourning. "These but the trappings and the suits of woe," spoken with a downward inflection on the last word defeats the meaning. "Woe❞ should be stressed as being the whole point of the speech. What Hamlet meant, of course, was that he had a grievance which mere mourning did not symbolize: the shame of his mother's marriage and the deprivation of his inheritance, the defeat of his ambition. 

The critics have missed the obvious sarcasm of this speech, one of the many verbal poiniards Hamlet hurls at almost every character in the play. This speech, when properly read, expresses resentment and condemnation rather than sorrow.

The play proves that Hamlet is not a fool,

a coward or a moralist. His wit is quick and intelligent, therefore the popular conception of this "inky cloak" speech is most certainly wrong: it is not a sermon on the intrinsic meaning of mourning, but an open reproach to his mother; that the wearing of black could not express all his loss.

We must never lose sight of the fact that the play was written to be played for the entertainment of an audience; that the speeches, no matter how wise and philosophic they may appear in the reading, are inserted for the purpose of furthering the story, holding the interest and unfolding the plot.

Almost immediately after this, the King, Queen and courtiers leave the stage and Hamlet is left alone. He gives us in his first soliloquy the key-note of his personal attitude toward the whole situation; his hatred for his uncle and his resentment at his mother's hasty remarriage. The Shakespearean soliloquy is a substitute for the ancient Greek chorus; it is a means of enlight-

ening the audience as to the incidents not transpiring on the stage, and the hidden motives of the characters. 

This speech which commences, "O, that this too, too solid flesh would melt," is, oddly enough, almost always delivered in a pompous monotone, intending to represent, I suppose, the product of a gentle, scholarly and philosophic mind steeped in chronic melancholy, and seriously debating a project of suicide. If this were its true interpretation the meaning of the whole play would be lost as it would be an evidence of weakness, a lack of mental balance, to say the least.

In making this speech, Hamlet had really no thought of suicide; if for a moment he wished himself dead, it was only because of his ungratified rage and spite. It is the product of the anger and spleen of a hot-headed prince, orphaned, disgraced and disinherited, all in a few months. It is little more than an attack upon the Queen, his mother, whom he likens to a beast in a coarse and violent way, even before he knows of his father's murder.

This speech is not the utterance of a philosopher. It is pessimistic, but the contempt of the world expressed in it is not that of virtuous indifference but of defeated ambition. 

A spirited young prince, disinherited, robbed of his rights to succession by his mother's marriage, her betrayal of him, her son, is surely a situation calling for grievance and resentment, not pensive melancholy.

Continuing with the reading of this scene, Hamlet is further introduced to the audience, and his conversation is characteristic of the man: 

HAM: What is your affair in Elsinore? 

We'll teach you to drink deep ere you depart. 

HOR: My lord, I came to see your father's funeral. 

HAM: I pray thee, do not mock me, fellow student; 

I think it was to see my mother's wedding. 

HOR: Indeed, my lord, it followed hard upon. 

HAM: Thrift, thrift, Horatio! the funeral baked meats 

Did coldly furnish forth the marriage tables. 

Would I had met my dearest foe in Heaven 

Or ever I had seen that day, Horatio!

In these ten lines Shakespeare displays some of the salient features in Hamlet's character,

and so plainly, that none, save those who are prejudiced by previous comments, can fail to note them.

It appears that he is jovial, quick, witty, bitingly sarcastic, proud, revengeful and with a vast capacity for bitter contempt and hatred. The last two lines are not in the language of a Christian philosopher nor the meek and chastened expression of grief under the affliction of death; they display the bitter resentment of a fierce and vindictive man whose hate pursues his foe even to Heaven!

Strangely enough, the noted commentators upon this play disregard the obvious cause of Hamlet's surliness and ill-humor in the first scene, which, of course, is his defeated ambition to be king. So blinded are they by the sensitive and melancholy Hamlet of their own making, that they ascribe his sulkiness solely to his father's death and his mother's second marriage. But his exclusion from the throne touched him more nearly than this marriage.

That a medieval man of thirty should mope merely because his father died some months before is unlikely and unnatural. 

Let us see how Shakespeare plainly indicates that Hamlet resented his disinheritance: in a talk with Horatio, he says that the King has "popped in between the election and my hopes." Another instance is a dialogue between him and Rosencrantz: 

HAM: Sir, I lack advancement. 

ROS: How can that be, when you have the voice of the king himself for your succession in Denmark?

HAM: Ay, sir, but,-While the grass grows the proverb is something musty. The end of the proverb is "the silly horse he starves."

It is reasonable to suppose that this grievance is his dominating motive throughout the play; if it were not, it would be so extraordinary that Shakespeare would have informed us of it somewhere. 

We must bear in mind that Shakespeare was a master dramatist. That he clearly de-

lineated Hamlet as an aggressive, fearless, powerful man is evidenced by his constant association with Horatio who was just the opposite. Here we find the important effect of contrast, so necessary for a good play. 

In constructing his dramas, Shakespeare uses contrasting personalities or backgrounds. In "Romeo and Juliet," for instance, the setting is a feud filled with violent hatred as strong as the lovers' own sudden passion. Hence the love story is thrown into greater relief. 

In "Othello," Shakespeare uses Iago's machinations and deviltry to counterbalance the credulity and boyishness of the Moor, whose simple earnestness proved such fertile soil for a villain's innuendos. Here again the law of contrast works to an intense climax. 

When two characters in Shakespeare's plays appear constantly together throughout the course of the drama, we may be sure it is the result of careful planning and expert play-writing.

Therefore, we find that Horatio is entirely negative; he is just as Hamlet describes him-"that man which is not passion's slave." And it is true, a common fact, that we admire most in others what we lack within ourselves. 

Had Hamlet not been "passion's slave" himself, he would hardly have cared to mention this attribute in Horatio, nor would

Shakespeare have placed them so much together. Two negative characters talking to each other throughout a long tragedy could hardly prove interesting or dramatic. 

On Horatio's first entrance, he shows clearly that he understands Hamlet's hasty temper and impatience: 

HOR: My lord, I think I saw him yesternight. 

HAM: Saw who? 

HOR: My lord, the king, your father. 

HAM: The king, my father! 

HOR: Season your admiration for a while with an attent ear, till I may deliver upon the witness of these gentlemen, this marvel to you. 

In the above sense the word "admiration" means wonder. That Horatio antici-

pated being interrupted and doubted by

Hamlet is obvious; they had been in school together.

The cross-examination of the soldiers and Horatio which follows shows that the prince is quick, suspicious and not prone to believe everything he hears. 

For courage and fortitude, Hamlet's first scene with the ghost is unique in all literature, dramatic or otherwise. There are many stories that have depicted warriors and heroes, ready at all times to face the most terrible living enemy, but quailing before the terror of spectres and phantoms from the tomb. To Shakespeare's Hamlet alone belongs the renown of having faced such an apparition with steady nerve and fearlessness.

He accosts the ghost and dares to follow where it leads. The united strength of his friends is powerless to hold him; he hurls them from him with overmastering strength and vigor. Never for a moment is he less than master of himself.

This prince of Denmark has courage; he is capable, even in the very presence of the ghost, of a fierce and ghastly play on words: "By Heaven, I'll make a ghost of him that *lets me!" With reckless daring he marches on to the dreadful midnight parley, not dazed or trembling, but manfully, with all his faculties alert.

An ordinary person would have been shocked beyond power of action by the horror of that encounter but Hamlet had a mind so strong and clear that neither death nor midnight apparitions of sheeted dead could intimidate him. 

Immediately upon the disappearance of the ghost he is ready to think and contrive; on the very scene of that dread encounter he calmly makes his plan of action; he resolves on secrecy, and denies his friends' curiosity with apt and ready jests. A moment later, when they are appalled by the mysterious * Stops, hinders.

subterranean voice, Hamlet is cool and alert; he puts the apparition to the test of its powers, even jests with the ghost: "Well said, old mole! can'st work i' the earth so fast?", and hurls his fling at Horatio's college lore: "There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, than are dreamt of in your philosophy." 

The very moment the ghost disappears he starts planning. What was to be done? First, there must be secrecy as to what occurred. 

His friends must be pledged in the most careful, deliberate manner. Next, the ghost's tale must, if possible, be verified, and if verified the murderer must be punished.

To this purpose, stimulated by hate, ambition, duty, revenge and pride, Hamlet is ready to sacrifice friendship, love and life itself.

It is at this point that Shakespeare again shows his marvelous knowledge of theatrical contrasts. Hamlet decides to "put an antic disposition on," for we all realize that truth

cuts more deeply when shown under the guise of comedy. The dramatic effect of the elder Brutus, who feigned madness in order to drive out the tyrannous Tarquins from

Rome, was a tempting and dramatic situation for the skill of a playwright; and what better place could it be introduced than at the outset of the play? Following upon the awesome interview with the ghost, Hamlet exclaims to his friends who are far more frightened than he "There's ne'er a villain dwelling in all Denmark, but he's an arrant knave." Horatio, dumbfounded at this superficial observation coming on top of so extraordinary a circumstance says, "There needs no ghost, my lord, come from the grave, to tell us this." 

Hamlet's strong mentality, clearness of vision and capacity for sound judgment are so frequently and constantly displayed throughout the play, his antics are so plainly assumed, that there is no possible room for doubt on the subject of his sanity. His man-

ner of putting on "an antic disposition" while perfectly in keeping with the reckless character of the hero of the play, is quite at variance with the attitude of the sedate and pensive Hamlet of tradition, who if he condescended to feign at all, would never indulge in the antics ascribed to him. 

Charles Reade said that "they who believe 

Hamlet insane are crazy themselves.” The insanity theory is not only without foundation but is downright nonsensical. 

However strong our conviction may be that Hamlet was perfectly sane, we cannot view a modern production of the play without entertaining some doubts. The drooping defeated attitude, the melancholy mad expression, the sad, dreary cadence of the voice, the bashful, wistful expression on the faces of our outstanding actors while interpreting this part, cannot but leave any sensible person in a quandary. 

Shakespeare's Hamlet, with his high animal spirits, his love of adventure, his soul of

wit, his delight in playing a part, in puzzling and mystifying all around him, and in the license it gives his rough and ready tongue; like a boy, he fairly revels in the practice of his jest.

There is not another author who has created a character of such boundless elasticity of spirits. Notwithstanding the full realization of the fearful task before him, he plays jests at every stage of its fateful progress. He teases poor Ophelia, ridicules and torments Polonius to his face and bandies words with the King himself, with evident pleasure in the absurdities he commits.

Not only was Hamlet sane but he was quick, he was humorous, he was witty (which melancholiacs never are); he was, in fact, worthy to be the delight and principal figure of the gayest court in Europe. 

In the delineation of King Lear, Ophelia, Lady Macbeth and others, Shakespeare shows us plainly that he understands thoroughly the working of the insane mind. Dr. Coriat

has written a most interesting and enlightening thesis on the hysteria of Lady Macbeth and he points out how unerringly Shakespeare drew this character. We must dispense with all thought that the playwright in this case was inconsistent; it is the actors and the commentators who have been confused.

The affair between Hamlet and Ophelia is another point of bewilderment to the critics; having been led astray by their mistaken preconceptions they can make nothing but confusion out of the play, being unable to account for their gentle philosopher's cruel treatment of Ophelia.

Indeed, this affair has been the stumbling block to critics and actors. Some of them say he was crazy, some that he was in a sort of no-man's land of mentality, or as Winter puts it, an "intermittent insanity." This is a very convenient invention for a muddled critic, since it enables him to escape the necessity for any consistent theory of the plot or development of the play.

However, dramatically speaking, an insane Hamlet would make a hodgepodge of the play, so we may safely assume that 

Shakespeare had no such intention, nor did he wish to leave any doubt as to the sanity of the hero.

The insanity theory is principally put forward to explain his treatment of Ophelia, but all that is needed is to understand the facts: Hamlet was no weird superman but a prince of his time, unusually able, well informed and valiant.

When Shakespeare wrote, and still more, when Hamlet was supposed to have lived, the almost universal conception of a prince was of a royal being, whose nature, rights and privileges were far superior to those of less than kingly birth.

How much he really cared for Ophelia at the time represented in the play is doubtful and is entirely immaterial to the course of the tragedy. This play is not intended to deal with the course of love, true or otherwise, but

with the operation of a sacred duty of vengeance.

Ophelia is a mere incidental victim of the tragedy; and Hamlet's affection for her, be it much or little, could not for a moment be permitted to interfere with the fulfillment of his sacred vow or the development of the main plot of the drama. The course which 

Hamlet adopts toward her would be impossible for the gentle, high-minded, philosophic stage Hamlet, but is, on the other hand, quite natural to the actual Hamlet of Shakespeare; a man, reckless, violent, haughty and imperious; cruel and implacable in revenge and perfectly capable of trampling on friendship or affection in such a cause. 

When an actor reads a play for the first time with the intention of taking a part in it on the stage his only logical procedure in determining what his interpretation will be is to observe carefully what the other characters say about him. 

It is natural to suppose that Shakespeare

knew he could convey the true character of his hero to the audience by what he wrote for the rest of the cast to speak. While Hamlet may not have been all that Ophelia imagined him, yet he must have had a good deal of charm of manner and appearance to have her say:

The courtier's, soldier's, scholar's, eye, tongue, sword, The expectancy and rose of the fair state, The glass of fashion, and the mold of form, The observed of all observers . . .

None of the characters surrounding Hamlet credited him with the loftiness of purity since ascribed to him. When Ophelia's father and brother heard of his love making they did not entertain that entire confidence in 

Lord Hamlet's cool, philosophic propriety which an audience might feel after seeing the performance of a modern actor in the part. 

They did not believe him to be sincere nor that he was to be trusted with a girl's honor. "Springes to catch woodcocks" was Polonius' comment on his vows and letters to his

daughter. Ophelia's relatives regarded him just as they would any young gallant of the court; as a dangerous companion, and his treatment of that unfortunate girl shows they were right.

Lady Ophelia dwelling at Court, accustomed to converse with the flower of its youth, does not show Hamlet as a cold, meditative, melancholy philosopher; her portrait of him is rather that of a hero, a soldier with quick commanding eye, ready tongue and an expert sword. The Hamlet of the play agrees with her description of him though the Hamlet of the stage does not.

There are two soliloquies in the play which have added to the theory that Hamlet was a super-thinker, a contemplative dealer in the abstract, and they are cited to prove that he was of a deeply thoughtful nature: the one in the first act, scene two, beginning, "O, that this too too solid flesh would melt," and the other in act three beginning "To be or not to be.' 

---

I have already shown that the first of these soliloquies is not philosophical but impatient and denunciatory. Hamlet was in an ill humor, cursed the world and wished himself dead as many an ill-tempered youth has done before and since.

The "To be or not to be" soliloquy is, indeed, meditative, but does not display much depth of thought. It is popularly supposed to represent Hamlet's ruminations on the expediency of suicide as a remedy for life's ills, followed by his negative conclusion in consideration of the dread uncertainties of the world to come.

This is certainly not profound or original. 

I must, however, do Hamlet the credit of pointing out that this interpretation of the speech is erroneous.

The question he really propounds is a more subtle, pregnant one; it has preceded every social and political revolution. Whether, being oppressed, it is nobler to endure things as they are and thus live, or to violently rebel,

and so perhaps bring upon ourselves destruction and death. Is it nobler to suffer "or to take arms against a sea of troubles, and by opposing end them?" This is not suicide. The reference to the "bare bodkin" is merely by way of illustration. 

Hamlet's argument is this: Is death such a calamity that the preservation of life should be man's principal consideration? If not, we might as well recklessly attack the world's oppressors and hazard all either to win or end everything. He is unable to answer the question because of the doubt as to what may be before us in the next world. It is, he says, the dread of the after life which makes us hesitate to risk our lives; and he illustrates this by saying that otherwise men would not endure all life's various calamities when suicide is so easy a remedy. Thus it comes, says 

Hamlet, that we fear, to become cowards, in pursuit of "enterprises of great pith and moment. 

The language of this soliloquy is that of

Shakespeare at his best and carries his marvelous rhythm and power, but the thought behind it is not supposed to be profound or philosophic; certainly it is not intended to illustrate the working of a great mind any more than a number of other equally wise and thoughtful speeches to be found in his other tragedies. 

The language of all Shakespeare's outstanding characters is full of strength and charm, but most of them are far from being philosophers. Nor was Hamlet one; he was quick, but not profound. Those who define him as a moralist or as a lunatic, or a mixture of the two, are equally wrong. Shakespeare was not the man to present problems in psychology or a demented hero as an attraction at the Globe Theatre.

As a matter of fact, Hamlet is a man of action, not of thought. Just because his mother seems to be fickle he concludes in a shallow way that all women must be the same: "Frailty thy name is woman." To

Ophelia he says, 'Tis brief as woman's love."

Shakespeare's Hamlet is resolute, practical, a typical young prince of his day and generation, rather bad-tempered and unscrupulous, but gifted with great courage and ability; not at all the mysterious being, the abstraction, the mystery of the critics, nor the moping irresolute dreamer and moral philosopher of theatrical tradition.

No more complete contrast can be imagined than that between the imaginary 

Hamlet of the Coleridge and Winter school of critics and the real Hamlet of Shakespeare.

Instead of the play being elusive, enigmatical and inconsistent, as these learned specialists have found it, I maintain that it is understandable, it is a fast moving melodrama founded on a plain, simple story.

From the very beginning of the play we see that Hamlet was revengeful by nature, and, incited as he was, to revenge his father by bloodshed became for him his sacred duty, his first and all absorbing passion. On a word

The man, capable of contemplating matricide, was not one to give a sweetheart a second thought. Hamlet is not presented to us as a lover but as a hater and avenger.

He exhibits the same vindictive spirit when he refrains from killing the King while at prayer because his death at such a moment would not be sufficient punishment to glut his appetite for vengeance which could be satisfied by nothing short of his enemy's eternal damnation. 

Hamlet was not only proud, reckless and revengeful, but he had a fierce temper, rendering him subject to violent bursts of passion. Shakespeare took great pains to display this trait; the critics and the stage have been just as careful to conceal it. 

His quick and imperious temper is first

shown in the ghost scene where his friends vainly try to restrain him from following the spectre; again in the soliloquy in the second act (scene two) where he breaks into impatient self-reproach and, as he himself says, "falls acursing like a very drab, a scullion!" To utter this soliloquy tamely (as is sometimes done on the stage in order to be consistent with the traditional, pensive 

Hamlet) is to fail to give the scene its true significance.

When he makes this speech, Hamlet's fiery and impatient soul is in a tempest of rage at the thought that his enemy, the king is still alive. He blames and curses himself furiously for his delay. Brutus, when discussing the proposed assassination of Cæsar, said, "Let's carve him as a dish fit for the gods." Not so Hamlet. "I should have fatted all the region kites with this slave's offal." This is not the language of a thoughtful, patient philosopher; it is that of a truculent medieval soldier.

This bad temper is again displayed in the chamber scene with his mother. It does not appear that she had ever given him cause for offence save in her second marriage. Yet he is pitiless to her, even in his thoughts. In this scene he shows himself absolutely merciless; giving her no opportunity to excuse herself and assailing her with coarse and cruel reproaches. His language toward her is not that which a considerate son, or indeed any man of a philosophic temper, would use toward a mother who had committed but one fault, grievous as it may seem to him. It is more than a remonstrance; it is an outburst of rough anger on the part of a fierce, passionate man.

In the last act he is guilty again of a scandalous explosion of rage in the churchyard when he grapples with Laertes in his sister's grave. At such a moment any ordinary man (much less a melancholy dreamer) would be subdued with contrite sorrow and sympathy for the twice afflicted brother.

Hamlet admits later that this gross misconduct of his was caused by bad temper at seeing Laertes' grief. "The bravery of his grief did put me in a towering passion."

Think of a man actually assaulting another because he too loudly bewails his sister's death! There is no excuse for this indecent behavior; the explanation Hamlet gives is absolutely insufficient, nor did Shakespeare introduce this line as a justification of Hamlet's conduct.

There has never been, to my knowledge, a true interpretation of Hamlet on the stage, certainly not since dramatic criticism was introduced. I have read bound volumes of theatrical reviews covering the performances of all the outstanding actors of the nineteenth century and, with one exception, all the Hamlets have done strange, inconsistent things to the part.

It is a pity that the commentators began fussing with the play before any good actor had the chance to act the part without being

biased. According to theatrical history, we have always had presented to us a pure and moral young gentleman, weak, pensive, pining because charged with a job he has not the grit to perform. 

Goethe's "Wilhelm Meister," published the latter part of the eighteenth century, is partially responsible for this idea, for he says of Hamlet-"without the strength of nerve which forms a hero, sinks beneath a burden which it cannot bear and must not cast away." Then he quotes the words: 

The time is out of joint: O cursed spite, That ever I was born to set it right! and adds, "in these words I imagine will be found the key to Hamlet's whole procedure. To me it is clear that Shakespeare meant in the present case to represent the effects of a great action laid upon a soul unfit for the performance of it."

In reading or interpreting any play, nothing is more foolish than the selection of a striking phrase or any pithy line, and out of

it creating a theory on the meaning and the scope of the play. 

Because Hamlet naturally expressed his sharp regret that he was born to the bitter task before him it does not follow that he was weak or unfit for its performance nor that he sank beneath the burden. To the contrary, these words of Hamlet show that he assumed the business without hesitation and during the course of the drama his speeches and conduct show he never flinched from its performance. 

A more modern "guesser" (where Hamlet is concerned the word "critic" is often misapplied), Professor Frederick D. Losey, who edited in 1926 a volume of Shakespeare's complete works, makes this profound comment in his introduction: ...

Hamlet . . . frankly hands us the key to his character and, through that, to the tragedy itself: "Seems, madam? nay, it is: I know not seems.' 

In this one line, the Professor builds up the whole meaning of the play, just as Goethe did from his favorite passage.

It might be an amusing experience to ask some successful playwright to pick out a passage from his own work that encompasses not only the true character of the hero, but also a key to the drama itself. Judging from the few authors I have met, I feel sure they would look at me in utter astonishment and that the reply would be (among other things) a recommendation to a brain specialist. 

Now, whether the professors, critics, commentators have misled the actors or whether the stage versions have instigated all of these fantastic theories or whether each has reacted upon the other, the result has been the establishment in the theatre of an utterly false conception of Hamlet and of the meaning of the play.

Mr. Winter has written, however, of one notable exception, a deviation from the traditional Hamlet in the performance of the renowned Italian actor, Tommaso Salvini (1829-1915), probably the greatest

tragedian of his time. He did not speak English and his performances in America, about fifty years ago, were played in Italian.

It would seem that Salvini refused to accept the conventional Hamlet of the critics and presented him as a dominant, stalwart, puissant and resolute man. Winter regrets that in doing so Salvini misconceived the part and asserts that his Hamlet was a failure. 

It is quite likely indeed that the great Italian tragedian, handicapped by his lack of English, was unable to overthrow the wellestablished stage tradition of a weak, melancholy Prince of Denmark, and that a public accustomed to that interpretation, and unable to understand Salvini's words, rejected or ignored him in the part. But Salvini, in such a matter, was more likely to be right than Winter. He was a dramatic genius who thought for himself while Winter merely followed the authority of self-appointed commentators, deficient in actual stage experience and interpretive power.

There can be no doubt in anyone's mind who has given the subject a moment's consideration, that Shakespeare intended, as always, to produce a good acting play. With the story of Hamlet dramatized in his powerful way he proceeds to fill it with life, making each character speak and act with naturalness and force as to interest and entertain his audiences. His men and women are always rational and logical and, furthermore, there is no Shakespearean play which depends for its theme upon a psychological problem.

The "critics" have created a mystery of Hamlet, not only because of his wise and cynical comments upon "this goodly frame, the earth," but because, and above all, he failed to kill the King in the first act; the postponement persisting until the last scene of the last act! 

One would suppose that fresh minds in modern times might come to this problem with a little more common sense, but no,

that particular morsel of perplexity is being valiantly carried on. 

To revert once more to Professor Losey, one time head of the English Department at the University of Syracuse; he has this ancient bit of sophistry to offer:

Had Hamlet been a less sensitive soul; had love and grief and ideals been to him only seeming, he might have retained his balance and acted instantly in revenge, thus avoiding the widespread consequences of the sin of Claudius and Gertrude.

There is a good deal of naiveté in the way this is put; I quote it only because it expresses succinctly what all the others have said in explanation of Hamlet's vaccilation. 

But let us get out of this profound literary confusion as quickly as possible and regain our mental balance.

In order to do this we must understand at the outset that the play had to be of standard length, far longer than the plays produced today, and in realizing this, we shall find no more difficulty in accounting for the delay

in killing the King than in the postponement of the denouement in any other play, be it ever so modern and commonplace. 

This is a tragedy whose main theme is revenge; it must have five acts and end with the death of the villain. Shakespeare, therefore, could not afford to have the King killed in the first act even though all the critics in the land should go distracted. 

For dramatic reasons the finale in Hamlet had to be postponed and various expedients resorted to, but in this case they need not satisfy the reader or student; they need only satisfy the audience in the theatre who are always under more or less of an illusion as to the duration of time throughout the course of the play. Even the probabilities of the plot need not be so exact as to satisfy a lawyer or a judge; fiction, poetry and drama has still the right to call upon some license.

Strange as it may seem, however, there is very little license taken in this play; the sequence of events is entirely logical and the

delay in killing the King is amply accounted for; first, by Hamlet's desire to assure himself of the truth of the ghost's disclosure, which he does by feigning madness and the "mouse-trap" play in the third act; next, by his arrest and dispatch to England immediately thereafter; followed by his escape and return, which brings us to the final scenes of the last act. 

Critics may think that these expedients are insufficient or defective, but they are, nevertheless, sanctioned by dramatic usage; they are the ones Shakespeare adopted and, if it had not been for the over-zealous examination into them, it is not likely any audience would fail to find them convincing. 

Then too, if the play were ever presented in its proper tempo, that of melodrama, such long, dreary, boresome pauses, drawling speeches, could be hastened and Shakespeare's true intention be brought forth to the complete satisfaction and conviction of the public.

One may safely assume that none of the expedients resorted to in the play to keep the King alive until the final curtain are anywhere near as improbable as the excuses and explanations invented by these very critics in their endeavor to twist a simple tale from its plain meaning.

Some other questions are raised by the mystery-monger type of professor which are equally beside the point; such as why didn't 

Hamlet raise a tumult, call on his friends for aid against the King as Laertes did? These speculations are about as useful as it would be to ask why Othello didn't consult a lawyer about Iago's stories, or why didn't someone discover Portia's disguise at the trial? If the plot of every drama had to be proof against literary examination many theatres would close their doors.

There is very little hope that such a deep rooted, long lived fallacy will be overthrown in this generation. Still, it would be very interesting to see a Hamlet on the stage which

even faintly resembles the character that Shakespeare created. If but the action were suited to the words, the words to the action, as Hamlet himself prescribes, we would then have the fierce, proud, impatient and gifted prince of Denmark.

He would not be introduced in the first act as a lackadaisical youth. His voice would be masterful, clear and strong; his attitude toward the royal pair would indicate halfconcealed resentment; his manners, walk and gestures, then and afterwards, those becoming his rank, breeding and his true character; that is to say, bold, reckless, sarcastic, selfassertive, haughty and commanding.

For instance, his exclamation addressed to 

Horatio when referring to his mother's marriage: 

Would I had met my dearest foe in Heaven, Or ever I had seen that day, Horatio! usually given on the stage in a tone of dolorous monotone would be rendered in a voice

of fierce resentment befitting the temper of a man who would be unhappy even in Heaven if he met his foe.

In the chamber scene with his mother, after he kills Polonius and discovers his body, he should show not the slightest regret. And his exclamation over the corpse: 

Thou wretched, rash intruding fool, farewell!

I have never seen an actor in the part who did not speak those words with the same sentimental pathos as though he were Romeo bidding Juliet a fond goodbye. The absurdity of giving the very opposite inflection to the meaning of the words has naturally had a puzzling effect upon the public. An audience cannot be expected to understand what is obviously incomprehensible to the actor. 

Hamlet describes himself as "proud, revengeful, ambitious," which the modern actor pronounces in tender, wistful tones! 

That Hamlet was a capable soldier, brave and warlike, is clearly indicated in the play;

the last evidence of this comes as a tribute from Fortinbras after Hamlet's death: 

Bear Hamlet like a soldier...

For he was likely, had he been put on, To have proved most royally . 

In summing up, let me add that no production of this tragedy can lay claim to accuracy which omits to show Hamlet as a man of violent and ungovernable temper when aroused. His feigning insanity is done only in the presence of those whom he believes to be his enemies. The play should be performed swiftly, not pondered over. It is a drama of situation and action; to present it otherwise is to be unjust both to Hamlet and the play. XXX
