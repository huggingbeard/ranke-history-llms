TEACHERS' MANUAL FOR BUSINESS AND LAW 

The latter portion of this manual is devoted to the answering of questions and case problems found at the conclusion of each chapter. In many instances the questions are so simple and the answer so evident that it has been thought unnecessary to include the answers in detail. In such cases reference has merely been made to the section of the text in which the answer is made clear. All case problems are clearly answered and the rule of law governing the case indicated. 

These answers are made available to give new teachers in the field a feeling of confidence and assurance in the handling of the questions and problems presented. 

Enthusiasm. In addition to the answers included it is thought advisable to include a few general suggestions for the guidance of the teacher who is inexperienced in the field of business law, with the thought that they may be helpful both to the teacher and to his students. 

To begin with, no subject is ever made interesting or to carry a sense of importance to the student in high school or commercial college unless the teacher is enthusiastic about it. Law is an interesting subject; it has an important bearing upon the everyday life and conduct of the members of society; it is a living, growing subject, vibrant with meaning and one which touches the very pulse of all human conduct. All of the relations of people with other members of society are controlled by principles of law. The subject entices us, it lures us away from other tasks that we may find the solution to some new and perplexing problem. 

Let no one undertake to teach the subject of business law unless he or she is enthusiastic about the subject, ever searching for the solution for baffling legal questions and ever desirous of pointing out to students the value of some of the fundamental legal principles which influence business conduct. The use of the 

Madison illustrations by the authors is designed to exhibit to the student the value of a knowledge of these general rules of law. 

Student Interest.--Although enthusiasm on the part of the teacher is an important factor in arousing the interest of the student in any subject, there are other considerations which the teacher should bear in mind. In the subject of business law, the use of illustrations has unusual value in maintaining and heightening the interest of students. The mere memorization of dull and musty principles of law, so that they can be returned to the teacher, is certain to kill the students' interest and to cut short any spontaneity which might exist in the class. It is only when the rules of law become alive through their application to concrete factual situations that they have significance. It is then that the interest of the student is aroused and that he begins to question. 1

Illustrations from any source are valuable but they are particularly so when they are taken from a reported case. A case which has been decided by a court is a story of the lives, or a chapter in the lives, of the litigants. These illustrations are usually available in court reports found in the law libraries of the city or in the office of an attorney whom the teacher may know. If neither of these sources is open, a few case books in his or her personal library on the major subjects being taught will prove of great value. A constant search for new, better, and more pertinent illustrations is the goal of every good teacher of business law. Many illustrations will undoubtedly come from members of the class. 

Local business or community problems, a business incident from the life of some parent or family connection is certain to be offered by students in class or after the class hour. The teacher should take full advantage of such situations and encourage the students to bring in additional problems. As illustrations are presented to the class, it is well to give the class a minute or two to think the problem over before asking for the decision in the case. 

Discussion among various members of the class may in this manner be stimulated. 

Student Questions.—One of the best criteria for gaging the interest of the class lies in the number of questions raised by the students. At first the students will be somewhat hesitant about raising questions, but if the teacher is quick to encourage, the classroom period should seem exceedingly short because of the interest of the various students as they attempt to relate the principles of law to situations in which they are interested. It is entirely possible that many questions will be raised that the teacher is unable to answer with assurance. This would be true of any practising attorney or teacher of law in a law school. At times questions are raised concerning matters upon which one must refresh his mind. Likewise many problems arise that have never been settled. In such cases the teacher should cooperate with the members of the class to work out a satisfactory solution based upon the principles that are available to them. In other cases the teacher by spending a few extra minutes sometime during the day in preparation may be able to bring the answer to class when it meets again. It is expected in a law class that the teacher will often be compelled to admit that he does not know the decision that would be reached in a certain case. 

Lesson Plan.-Some care must be exercised in working out the assignments from day to day. Although the text is broken up into a number of chapters, they are but milestones of progress. These chapters will in many cases have to be broken up into smaller sections for classroom work. Attention should be given to the summary at the close of each chapter and to the questions and review problems which follow. Case problems may be assigned to various students from time to time for report. By opening the class with the report, the interest of the other members is stimulated and class discussion is quickened from the beginning. 

No attempt has been made here to set forth a general lesson plan. Neither has it been thought advisable to indicate which subjects will be considered most important. There is more material in the text than can be handled in a semester. 

The location of the school, the type of students concerned, and their particular background and environment will all play an important part in the general lesson plan and in the material to be covered. In general it may be said that the subjects of contracts, negotiable instruments, and sales would most likely be taught to any group. In addition agency, partnership, and corporations might be

important to one group, whereas bailments and real property would be more advantageous to another. In any event the teacher should bear in mind that the purpose of the course is not to produce lawyers but to acquaint students with legal problems which abound in the realm of business relations and to aid him in thinking straight on such problems. The purpose is not merely to dole out rules of law but is, through the study of legal principles, to show the student his proper relation to society and to make him a more intelligent citizen of the community in which he resides.
