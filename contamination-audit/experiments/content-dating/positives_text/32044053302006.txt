THE CONDUCT OF COMMUNIST CHINA I. THE USE OF FORCE 

The United Nations Charter calls for the peaceful settlement of international disputes and directs all members to refrain from the threat or use of force. Communist China's aggression in Korea, its threats of and periodic recourse to force in the Taiwan area, and its use of large-scale force along the Indian border provide glaring examples of the regime's complete disregard of these fundamental principles of the United Nations Charter. As part of its ideological position, Peiping has also proclaimed, contrary to the spirit of the charter, its belief in the inevitability of war. 

A. CHINESE COMMUNIST AGGRESSION IN KOREA 

By the end of October 1950 United Nations forces in Korea had decisively routed the armies of the North Korean invaders. As the 

United Nations forces moved north, the Chinese Communists moved elements of their armed forces across the Yalu River into North Korea without notice. By November 28, 1950, according to a special communique issued by the United Nations Command, a major segment of the Communist Chinese continental armed forces in aggregate strength of over 200,000 men was arrayed against United Nations forces in North Korea. In all, Communist China sent an estimated 

1 million troops to reinforce and prolong its military intervention in 

Korea. 

Peiping's entry into the Korean conflict created an entirely new situation and shattered the hopes of the United Nations that the hostilities in Korea could be brought to a rapid close. 

As a result of this Communist Chinese action, the United Nations General Assembly on February 1, 1951, passed a resolution finding that the regime had engaged in aggression in Korea. The pertinent portion of the text of this historic resolution follows: 

The General Assembly, noting that the Security Council because of lack of unanimity of the permanent members, has failed to exercise its primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security in regard to Chinese 

Communist intervention in Korea,

Noting that the Central People's Government of the People's Republic of China has not accepted United Nations proposals to bring about a cessation of hostilities with a view to peaceful settlement, and that its armed forces continue their invasion of Korea and their large scale attacks upon United Nations forces there, 1. Finds that the Central People's Government of the People's Republic of 

China, by giving direct aid and assistance to those who were already committing aggression in Korea and by engaging in hostilities against United Nations forces there has itself engaged in aggression in Korea; 

2. Calls upon the Central People's Government of the People's Republic of 

China, to cause its forces and nationals in Korea to cease hostilities against the 

United Nations forces and to withdraw from Korea; 

3. Affirms the determination of the United Nations to continue its action in 

Korea to meet the aggression; 1

B. THREATS OF AND RECOURSE TO FORCE IN THE TAIWAN AREA 

Peiping has consistently proclaimed its right to "liberate" Taiwan by any means available, including war. In support of this position, in 1954-55 and again in 1958 the regime raised its military activities in the offshore island areas to such a pitch as to bring the specter of general war to the Far East. 

Intensified Chinese Communist military pressures against the offshore islands began on September 13, 1954, when the regime without warning initiated a heavy and long-continued artillery barrage against Quemoy. The Chinese Communist land, sea, and air assaults against the offshore islands from the Tachens to Quemoy continued intermittently through January 1955. They culminated with the armed seizure of the island of Ichiang which prior to that time had been under the continuous control of the Government of the Republic of China.

In the latter part of August 1958, the Chinese Communists resumed heavy artillery attacks against Quemoy. On August 23, 1958, the regime unleashed an artillery barrage of an estimated 55,000 rounds against Quemoy. For about 7 weeks, through the use of artillery fire and small naval craft, the Chinese Communists attempted to interrupt the regular supply of the civilian and military population of Quemoy, which totaled some 125,000 persons. The official Peiping Radio repeatedly announced that the purpose of these military operations was to take by armed force Taiwan, as well as the offshore islands of 

Quemoy and Matsu.

Beginning in the fall of 1955, efforts were made by the United States in meetings with the Chinese Communists to have both parties to these meetings renounce the use of force generally and particularly in the Taiwan area without prejudice to the right of individual and collective self-defense against armed attack. 

The United States has made clear that this renunciation would not prejudice either side in the pursuit of its objectives and policies by peaceful means.

The Chinese Communists, while stating that they accept the principle of the renunciation of force, have deprived such acceptance of its value by refusing to agree that (1) the victim of armed attack in violation of the principle has the right to defend itself, and (2) that the principle is applicable to the Taiwan area. In short, the 

Chinese Communists seem willing to renounce force only if they are first conceded the goals which they would achieve by force. 

C. USE OF FORCE IN THE BOUNDARY DISPUTE WITH INDIA 

The years following Communist China's armed suppression of Tibetan rights witnessed an intensification of the sporadic clashes taking place between Indian and Communist Chinese armed units along the disputed boundary delineating the two powers. Beginning in October 1962, the Chinese Communists touched off heavy fighting in the boundary areas and during the period October 20-24, the regime's

forces launched large-scale military attacks on the Ladakh and North east Frontier Agency (NEFA) regions. By mid-November the Chinese 

Communists had seized by armed force more than 3,000 additional square miles of Ladakh (beginning in 1957 Peiping had occupied piecemeal 12,000 miles of the sparsely inhabited Ladakh area) and had advanced its army in the NEFA region to within 35 miles of the 

Plains of Assam.

On November 18, 1963, India officially placed the number of Indian soldiers killed or missing in all sectors from October 20 to November 

16 at 1,623. Of these, 264 were known to have been killed and 155 wounded. 

Commenting on this situation, President Kennedy on November 1, 1962, wrote to the President of the International Parliamentary Union stating: 

I deplore this further instance of armed aggression by Communist China and hope that that state can be persuaded to abandon its flagrant violation of the territorial integrity of its neighbors. 

Secretary of State Rusk on November 28, 1963, further noted the extreme concern of the United States thatthe [Peiping] regime should have used violence, and on such a large scale, to settle a question which ought to be settled, if possible, by a course of negotiation. 

D. IDEOLOGICAL COMMITMENT TO WAR 

Whoever has any army has power, for war settles everything.

—Mao Tse-tung, November 1938. 

In addition to actual recourse to force as a means of dealing with international problems, the Chinese Communists in a series of ideological pronouncements have made clear their belief in the inevitability of war and their apparent willingness to enmesh humanity in a nuclear holocaust for the sake of advancing the fortunes of the 

"proletarian revolution." Representative Chinese Communist state ments on these subjects are given below. 

April 16, 1960, the regime's theoretical journal, Red Flag, wrote:

We believe in the absolute correctness of Lenin's thinking: War is an inevitable outcome of the systems of exploitation and the imperialist system is the source of modern wars. Until the imperialist system and the exploiting classes come to an end, wars of one kind or another will still occur. 

June 8, 1960, Central Committee member Liu Ch'ang-sheng stated:

Only when the Socialist revolution is victorious throughout the world can there be a world free from war, a world without armament. 

June 14, 1963, the Chinese Communists in a well-publicized letter to the Central Committee of the Soviet Communist Party noted. that while- *** Marx and Lenin did raise the possibility that revolutions may develop peacefully *** as a matter of fact there is no historical precedent for peaceful transition from capitalism to socialism.

A vivid example of the regime's refusal to allow the terrible dimensions of nuclear warfare to influence its dogmatic approach to the question of war is presented in an article appearing in the April 16, 1960, issue of Red Flag which viewed the aftermath of a nuclear war in the following manner:

On the debris of a dead imperialism the victorious people would create with extreme rapidity a civilization thousands of times higher than the capitalist system and a truly beautiful future for themselves.

II. DISRUPTIVE INTERNATIONAL BEHAVIOR 

Toward its goal of saving mankind from the scourge of war, the United Nations Charter calls for tolerance, friendship, and cooperation between nations. In the face of these charter pronouncements for peace, Communist China has proclaimed its goal to be the promotion of the "proletarian revolution" in other countries. Moreover, since its founding the regime has indulged in an official campaign of hate propaganda against foreign governments and peoples which in its massiveness and its viciousness is probably unequaled in the history of the world.

PROMOTION OF REVOLUTION IN OTHER COUNTRIES 

On October 9, 1961, at a public meeting in Peiping, Tung Pi-wu, a senior official of the regime, declared that, "in the present epoch, only under the leadership of the proletariat, and by obtaining the help of the Socialist countries, will it be possible for any country to win complete victory in its national and democratic revolution.' 

Translated from the Chinese Communist jargon, Mr. Tung's statement means that a Communist revolution, aided by external support from Communist countries, must still be fostered in the newly independent countries of the world. 

Proof that Mr. Tung's statement is not mere words was offered by the testimony of six men from the newly independent Republic of Cameroun who in 1960 attended in Peiping a clandestine 10-week course taught by French-speaking Chinese instructors in a military. academy outside Peiping. The curriculum of this course, taken from the syllabus those men brought home, included such items as these: 

Correct use of explosives and grenades, planning a sabotage operation, how to use explosives against houses, rails, bridges, tanks, guns, trucks, tractors, etc., manufacture of explosives from easily obtained materials, manufacture and use of mines and grenades, use of semiautomatic rifles and carbines, theory and practice of guerrilla warfare; ambushes; attacks on communications. 

Political lectures were also given with such titles as "The People's 

War," "The Party," "The United Front," and, of course, "The 

Imperialists Are Only Paper Tigers.' "; 

This was the fourth in a series of courses to train Camerounians to fight for the overthrow, not of European colonial rulers, but of their own sovereign African government. 

In its June 14, 1963, letter to the Central Committee of the Soviet Communist Party, the Peiping regime offered further evidence of its complete disdain for political systems other than its own and its resultant dedication to spreading the "proletarian revolution" throughout the world. The excerpts presented below from this exposition of the regime's fundamental ideology demonstrate that the Chinese Communists see the emergence of independent nations from former colonial possessions simply as steps toward Peiping's goal of world communism rather than as accomplishments in themselves worthy of the respect of fellow states. 

It is necessary [in "oppressed nations"] that the proletarian party should maintain its ideological, political, and organizational independence and insist on the leadership of the revolution. The proletarian party and the revolutionary people must learn to master all forms of struggle, including armed struggle. They must

LO defeat counterrevolutionary armed force with revolutionary armed force whenever imperialism and its lackeys resort to armed suppression. *** The proletarian party must always draw a clear line of demarcation between itself and bourgeois nationalism, to which it must never fall captive * * *.

In order to lead the proletariat and working people in revolution, Marxist- Leninist parties must master all forms of struggle and be able to substitute one form for another quickly as the conditions of struggle change. The vanguard of the proletariat will remain unconquerable in all circumstances only if it masters all forms of struggle-peaceful and armed, open and secret, legal and illegal, parliamentary struggle and mass struggle, and so forth * * *. 

Even in ordinary times, when it is leading the masses in the day-to-day struggle, the proletarian party should ideologically, politically, and organizationally prepare its own ranks and the masses for revolution and promote revolutionary struggles so that it will not miss the opportunity to overthrow the reactionary regime and establish a new state power when the conditions for revolution are ripe. Otherwise, when the objective conditions are ripe, the proletarian party will simply throw away the opportunity of seizing victory. 

III. VIOLATIONS OF HUMAN RIGHTS 

A revolution is an uprising, an act of violence whereby one class overthrows another ***. To right a wrong it is necessary to exceed the proper limits and the wrong cannot be righted without the proper limits being exceeded.-Mao 

Tse-tung, 1927.

A. TRANSGRESSIONS AGAINST THE CHINESE PEOPLE 

Communist China's failure to respect the principles of the United Nations is further evidenced by the regime's complete denial to its 600 million subjects of the fundamental human rights reaffirmed in the charter. As clearly evidenced by the kangaroo "mass trials" and executions of probably millions of landlords and counterrevolutionaries during the land reform program of the early 1950's, the regime has persecuted and liquidated entire groups of its own subjects in the name of class struggle. Moreover, as recently as in the June 

14, 1963, letter to the Soviets (see above), the regime vowed that class struggle would be a permanent feature of the Chinese Communist scene until the "transition to communism" has been completed. 

Entire portions of the citizenry are thus considered "class enemies" and deprived of the equal protection of the state. Other well-known 

Chinese Communist deprivations practiced against their own subjects include forced collectivization of rural holdings, efforts to abolish the traditional Chinese family system through the institution of the communes, and the complete absence in mainland China of political, intellectual, and religious freedoms. Convincing proof of the regime's harshness is provided by the flight from mainland China of an estimated 3 million people over the last 10 years. Following the severe disruption of the mainland economy in the wake of the commune program, the number of refugees fleeing the mainland in 1 month alone (May 1962) rose to an estimated 120,000. 

Statements from official Communist Chinese sources provide some of the best evidence of the regime's transgressions in the field of human rights. A representative selection of such statements is presented below.

Arbitrary search and imprisonment: 

It is absolutely necessary for a public security organ, under emergency conditions, to search a place or detain a person without a warrant * * *. If there are any people who consider this an infringement of personal freedom and of the

democratic rights of the people, then their views are both wrong and dangerous.

— 

People's Daily, January 8, 1955. 

The elimination of "landlords":

Only by conducting intensive and widespread propaganda on the land reform policy, freely mobilizing the peasant masses, and through meetings giving vent to grievances and differentiating class status *** could sabotage and counterattacks by the landlord class be checked and crushed. In places where land reform has been carried out in this way, it has been possible to do away with the landlord class thoroughly and to fulfill the tasks of land reforms.-Chou En-lai report, October 23, 1951.

All actions which undermine and obstruct Socialist enterprise, endanger the interests of the state and the people, and undermine the Communist Party are, of course, counterrevolutionary activity.-People's Daily, September 4, 1955.

16.8 percent of the counterrevolutionaries dealt with were sentenced to death because they had committed heinous crimes and public wrath was extremely strong against them. The overwhelming majority of these counterrevolutionaries were sentenced to death between the time of the liberation and 1952. 

These sentences were absolutely necessary at the time.-Chou En-lai report, June 26, 1957. 

Restraints on religion: *** some Buddhist practices of the past which are disadvantageous to socialism and production *** have been changed ***. B. THE CASE OF TIBET 

Faced with the reality of Chinese Communist military occupation, the Tibetan authorities on May 23, 1951, signed an agreement with the Peiping regime on "Measures for the Peaceful Liberation of Tibet." 

Terms of this agreement included confirmation of "established status, functions, and powers of the Dalai Lama"; a pledge of respect for the "religious beliefs, customs, and habits of the Tibetan people”; and a promise of "no compulsion on the part of the central authorities" on matters relating to reform. 

Systematic violations of this agreement caused the Tibetans to rise against the Chinese Communists in March 1959; shortly thereafter the Dalai Lama fled from the Chinese Communists to sanctuary in 

India. 

The 

In blatant violation of Communist China's solemn pledge of May 23, 1951, guaranteeing the Tibetans religious and political autonomy, Premier Chou En-lai on March 28, 1959, ordered the dissolution of the legitimate Tibetan Government and established Chinese Communist military rule over Tibet. Chou's order also established the 

Panchen Lama as chairman of a Tibetan puppet regime.

Panchen Lama was brought up in China, came to Tibet in the wake of the Chinese Red Army and had never been the primary religious leader of Tibet. It is clear that the replacement of the Dalai Lama was effected by foreign intervention without the consent of the 

Tibetan people.

Chinese Communist behavior in Tibet resulted in a call for respect for the human rights of the Tibetan people by the United Nations

General Assembly in 1959 and again in 1961. The text of the October 21, 1959, General Assembly resolution on Tibet is given below:

THE GENERAL ASSEMBLY, Recalling the principles regarding fundamental human rights and freedoms set out in the Charter of the United Nations and in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights adopted by the General Assembly on December 10, 1948, Considering that the fundamental rights and freedoms to which the Tibetan people, like all others, are entitled include the right to civil and religious liberty for all without distinction,

Mindful also of the distinctive cultural and religious heritage of the people of Tibet and of the automony which they have traditionally enjoyed, Gravely concerned at reports, including the official statements of His Holiness the Dalai Lama, to the effect that the fundamental human rights and freedoms of the people of Tibet have been forcibly denied them, Deploring the effect of these events in increasing international tension and in embittering the relations between peoples at a time when earnest and positive efforts are being made by responsible leaders to reduce tension and improve international relations, 1. Affirms its belief that respect for the Principles of the Charter of the United 

Nations and of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights is essential for the evolution of a peaceful world order based on the rule of law; 

2. Calls for respect for the fundamental human rights of the Tibetan people and for their distinctive cultural and religious life. 

Widespread resentment over Communist China's treatment of the Tibetan people prompted the International Commission of Jurists, an impartial and nongovernmental body made up of judges, professors, and practicing lawyers, to appoint a committee of 11 members representing 9 different nationalities to investigate and report on the 

Tibetan situation. The unanimous 1960 report to the International

Commission of Jurists by its Legal Inquiry Committee on Tibet (as the committee was named) found that: *** acts of genocide had been committed in Tibet in an attempt to destroy the Tibetans as a religious group, and that such acts are acts of genocide independently of any conventional obligation * * *. The evidence established four principal facts in relation to genocide: (a) That the Chinese will not permit adherence to and practice of Buddhism in Tibet; (b) That they have systematically set out to eradicate this religious belief in Tibet; (c) That in pursuit of this design they have killed religious figures because their religious belief and practice was an encouragement and example to others; (d) That they have forcibly transferred large numbers of Tibetan children to a Chinese materialist environment in order to prevent them from having a religious upbringing. ***The Committee also examined evidence in relation to human rights within the framework of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights as proclaimed by the General Assembly of the United Nations. The Committee came to the conclusion that the Chinese authorities in Tibet had violated among others the following human rights, which the Committee considered to be standards of behavior in the common opinion of civilized nations: 

Article 3: The right to life, liberty, and security of person was violated by acts of murder, rape, and arbitrary imprisonment.

Article 5: Torture and cruelty, inhuman and degrading treatment were inflicted on the Tibetans on a large scale. 

Article 9: Arbitrary arrests and detention were carried out. 

Article 12: Rights of privacy, of home and family life were persistently violated by the forcible transfer of members of the family and by indoctrination turning children against their parents. Children from infancy upward were removed contrary to the wishes of the parents.

Article 22: The economic, social, and cultural rights indispensable for the dignity and free development of the personality of man were denied. The

Social economic resources of Tibet were used to meet the needs of the Chinese. changes were adverse to the interests of the majority of the Tibetan people. 

The old culture of Tibet, including its religion, was attacked in an attempt to eradicate it. 

IV. VIOLATIONS OF SPECIFIC AGREEMENTS 

Other than violations of the spirit and principles of the Charter, among the international commitments agreed to and subsequently broken by the regime in its relatively short period of existence are the 

(1) The July 27, 1953, Korean armistice agreement provided that both sides shall cease the introduction into Korea of combat equipment except on a 1-for-1 replacement basis. The agreement also specified ports of entry for and supervision of replacement equipment. 

On February 21, 1955, the United Nations Command charged that the Korean and Chinese Communists had between the date of July 27, 1953 and February 5, 1955, introduced into North Korea combat Mig aircraft in excess of the replacement rate. In addition, the Communist side had failed to report its introduction of equipment, had introduced new types of equipment, and had brought this equipment into North Korea at ports of entry other than those specified in the armistice agreement. (2) Communist China also violated the Korean armistice provision providing for the expeditious return of prisoners of war desiring repatriation.

In January 1953 a U.S. Air Force aircraft on a United Nations mission was attacked 15 miles south of the Yalu River. The officers and men were captured. More than a year and a half later, and long after the conclusion of the Korean armistice agreement, 11 officers and men from the above-mentioned aircraft were brought before a Chinese Communist military tribunal and sentenced to long penitentiary terms.

Aside from representing a clear-cut violation of the Korean armis-· tice agreement, the manner and circumstances of the sentencing of these Air Force personnel was grossly contrary to the substance and spirit of all recognized international standards. 

The continued detention of these men by the Chinese Communists led to the adoption by the United Nations on December 10, 1954, of a resolution condemning, "as contrary to the Korean armistice agreement, the trial and conviction of prisoners illegally detained after September 1953." The resolution requested United Nations Secretary General Dag Hammarskjold to seek the release of these 11 men. 

With the assistance of negotiations carried on by Secretary General Hammarskjold with the Chinese Communist authorities, the 11 airmen were granted their release after almost 2 years of illegal detention. (3) Communist China has repeatedly violated the spirit and letter of the April 29, 1954, agreement with India on trade and intercourse between Tibet and India and the October 14, 1954, trade agreement between India and China. The clearest violation of the latter agreement was Peiping's insistence in 1959 that trade transactions conducted by Indian traders in Tibet be adjusted in Chinese currency instead of following the "customary practice" provided for in the agreement whereby a number of alternative media were available.

Although the agreement on trade and intercourse with Tibet provided for free transit by Indians to Hindu holy places in Tibet, in 1959 and subsequently Chinese Communist authorities harassed and discouraged such religious pilgrimages. The Chinese have also violated the spirit of the agreements by restricting the activities of 

Indian trade and consular agencies in Tibet. (4) The regime has failed to fulfill the September 10, 1955, agree ment with the United States to permit all U.S. citizens in Communist 

China who wished to leave to do so expeditiously. This commitment clearly applied to all Americans, including those in prison. By 

December 1955 they had released 16 such Americans, but thereafter no American has been freed except upon completion of the full sentence handed down by the "people's courts" or, in one case, when death in prison was imminent. Four American citizens remain in 

Chinese Communist prisons today.

V. BEHAVIOR CONTRARY TO INTERNATIONAL LAW OR TO CUSTOMARY INTERNATIONAL PRACTICES 

Even before the formal establishment of the regime in October 1949, Chinese Communist behavior toward American citizens and interests on the mainland of China was characterized by a spirit of international lawlessness and contempt for customary international practices. Examples of this type of behavior are: 

1. Beginning in late November 1948, the entire U.S. consular staff and their families at Mukden were detained for over 1 year under house arrest inside the consular compound. In that period, all communications between the staff and the U.S. Government was strictly controlled by the local authorities, and there was one period of almost 7 months when no communication of any kind was possible. 

On October 24, 1949, Consul General Ward and the four members of his staff at Mukden were formally arrested and imprisoned on trumped-up charges of assaulting a Chinese worker and incurring certain financial obligations toward the consulate's local staff. Mr. 

Ward and his staff were tried and found guilty on November 22, with their sentences commuted to deportation. 

Although consuls do not have diplomatic immunity, the international practice of civilized countries for many years has recognized that consuls should be accorded all the privileges necessary for the proper conduct of their duties, including the right to freedom of movement and unlimited freedom to communicate with their governments with respect to official business. 

The treatment accorded to Mr. Ward and to the American consular staff in Mukden was in direct violation of the basic concepts of international relations which have developed throughout the centuries. 

2. On January 14, 1950, Chinese Communist authorities in Peiping seized American consular property in defiance of protests by the U.S. 

Government. The seizure was in violation of longstanding treaty rights granted the United States in 1901 and reaffirmed in the Sino- United States treaty of 1943. This seizure of American consular property also constituted a violation of the most elementary standards of international usage and conduct and was a significant factor in leading the Department of State to order the recall of all official 

American personnel from Communist China.

3. By May 1957, American nationals imprisoned by the Chinese 

Communists numbered more than 30. In most cases, the local Chinese Communist authorities gave no explanation of the arrest or any information concerning the welfare or whereabouts of the persons arrested. Similarly, access was denied to legal counsel, to American consular and diplomatic officials, and subsequently to British officials who acted as representatives of American interests on Communist 

China following the closure of American consulates on the mainland.

By this behavior, the Chinese Communist authorities ignored the international practice of civilized countries which for many years has recognized that consuls should be afforded full opportunity for the proper conduct of their duties in the protection of their nationals. 

Closely related to this was the continued denial by Chinese Communist authorities in the early years of their rule of exit permits to certain Americans, including a number of businessmen in Shanghai, some of whom had been endeavoring for over a year to leave Čommunist China. Arbitrary refusal to permit aliens to depart from a country is, of course, a violation of the elementary principles of international law and practice. 

4. With regard to military prisoners captured during the Korean conflict, the United Nations General Assembly on December 3, 1953, expressed its-- grave concern at reports and information that North Korean and Chinese Communist forces have, in a large number of instances employed inhuman practices against the heroic soldiers of *** the United Nations Command in Korea and against the civilian population of Korea. 

The General Assembly resolution also condemned- * * * atrocious acts against captured military personnel or civilian populations as a violation of rules of international law and basic standards of conduct and morality and as affronting human rights and the dignity and worth of the human person.
