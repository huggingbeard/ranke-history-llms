INFORMATION FOR PROSPECTIVE SETTLERS IN ALASKA 

Most of Alaska lies between the same parallels of latitude as Norway, Sweden, Finland and one-third of Russia. Alaska embraces 586,000 square miles of Territory. It has been demonstrated both at the Experiment Stations and by settlers scattered over the country, that the Territory has extensive agricultural possibilities. 

In recent years considerable publicity has been given to the agricultural possibilities of the Matanuska and Tanana Valleys, and to the Kenai Peninsula. These regions have fertile soil and are well suited to the production of certain kinds of farm produce. The Alaska Railroad traverses both valleys and has made them accessible to settlers. 

Agriculture in Alaska has not been able, so far, to keep up with the demand, as is shown by the great increase in the value of agricultural products shipped to Alaska since 1932. In that year the value of agricultural products shipped to Alaska from the States was $3,600,000; in 1934, $5,800,000; in 1936, $6,800,000; and in 1940, $8,200,000. The value of fresh and canned vegetables including potatoes, shipped into Alaska from the States increased from $537,615 in 1932 to $1,060,- 819 in 1940. Meat products shipped to Alaska in 1932 had a total value of $933,496, while in 1940 the value was $2,316,- 

571. The value of imported evaporated milk (unsweetened) in 1932 was $265,471, while in 1940 it was $441,842. The average annual value of imports of this commodity for the decade 1931-1940 was $363,545. In 1932 there were 1,429,862 dozen eggs imported into Alaska; in 1940, 1,957,251 dozen.

Since 1940, due to defense activities and the demands of war, the value of imports of all kinds has increased enormously, and these figures would not give the proper perspective on normal market requirements. Now that the war is over, Alaskan development and growth will undoubtedly continue to afford great opportunity for the production of a wide range of agricultural products such as meat, vegetables, eggs, dairy products, berries, grains, wool, fertilizers and wood products.

INFORMATION FOR THE ALASKAN FARMER-SETTLER 

The growth of native trees and shrubs is not so luxuriant in the Interior as on the Coast, but more nearly approaches the normal crops of grass and grain in the States. And while a drought is unknown in the coast region, it is not unusual for the Interior to suffer from a period of dry weather. Lowest precipitation generally occurs during the late winter and early spring months and extends into June. 

It is evident that crops are not materially affected by the lack of precipitation during early spring. In fact, there is an advantage from the cropping standpoint, because seeding may progress uninterruptedly. During July and August, precipitation is on the increase. Consequently, very often when crops are ripening there is considerable rainy, overcast weather which may seriously interfere with harvesting. Usually, however, there is sufficient dry autumn weather for crops to be harvested with little or no loss. 

Continued rains in the coast region frequently interfere with farm work. On the other hand, extreme cold weather in the Interior, where the temperature sometimes falls to -66° F. or lower, restricts outdoor activities. As no field work can be done in the winter this does not affect farm work to any appreciable extent. Summers are sometimes quite warm in the Interior, with the temperature at Fairbanks occasionally reaching 96° F. But hot spells are short lived.

In the coast region, both rainfall and snow vary greatly from year to year, as well as with the locality. In Prince 

William Sound, for instance, the snowfall is usually rather heavy, at times reaching 20 feet or more during the winter. 

But in Southeastern Alaska there is comparatively little snow at sea level. The records show several winters at Sitka with less than a foot of snow on the ground at any time. 

In the Interior, the snowfall varies in like manner with local conditions. At Fairbanks, the normal snowfall is about four feet. Snow begins to fall in October and gradually accumulates during the winter until the total amount measures between three and four feet. There is an occasional year with very heavy snowfall, as, for example, the winter of 

1936-37, when the fall of snow during January alone amounted to 65.6 inches. Total snowfall for the winter was 133.5

THE ARCTIC 

North of the Endicott Mountains, on the Arctic slope, conditions are definitely adverse to crop production. South of the range, where the drainage is into the Yukon River basin, the soil and the summer temperatures are favorable to the growth of short-seasoned crops. Continuous daylight during June and July hastens growth amazingly. Wild flowers bloom and ripen seed at about the same time as they do in the Tanana Valley. It is not surprising, therefore, that there are excellent gardens above the Arctic Circle at various locations such as Shungnak, on the Kobuk River, and at Allakaket and Wiseman on the Koyukuk River. Early varieties of barley and oats have been grown and ripened in these southward sloping river valleys. 

AGRICULTURAL AREAS 

Alaska contains thousands of square miles of tillable land which can be made available for crops as a demand for new land is created by increased population and production. In addition, thousands of square miles are suitable for grazing. 

The principal farming areas are found in the Matanuska Valley, the Kenai Peninsula, the Tanana Valley, the Upper Kuskokwim and parts of the main Yukon Valley. 

A considerable body of potential agricultural land lies north of the Tanana River, between the Tanana and Fortymile, and more particularly along the South Fork of the Fortymile River. It has been estimated that this land contains 750,000 acres. Though this region may prove to be highly productive when developed, as yet it has no transportation facilities worthy of mention. Bates Rapids in the -6-

Tanana River, some distance beyond Fairbanks, are of such a nature that only small and very powerful boats can pass them. Chiefly because of these rapids very few boats navigate to the Upper Tanana.

In Southeastern Alaska there are certain areas in the river valleys and at the heads of bays which produce truck crops, and some of the grassy tide flats are utilized for grazing. The treeless islands of Southwestern Alaska and the plateaus in the mountains near Healy give promise of being suitable for range stock.

Alaska has no prairies such as characterize the middlewestern United States. Practically every foot of soil has to be cleared before it can be put under cultivation. In the coast region, as far west as Cook Inlet, there is a heavy growth of timber, and clearing for farms is generally too expensive at this stage of development. A half century hence the farmer may encroach upon the forest of this region. 

West of Cook Inlet there is comparatively little timber, but there is a wealth of small bushes and grass. This region may, therefore, be suitable chiefly for stock raising. Clearing is easier in interior valleys which are covered with timber, bushes and moss of a much lighter growth than is found in the coast region. Timber has been heavily drawn upon for fuel, lumber and mining. Fires have destroyed even larger amounts, particularly on south slope land. Consequently, much desirable homestead land is lacking in timber for building purposes and sometimes even for firewood. 

Such land is easier to clear than where virgin forests exist, but the depletion of organic matter must be taken into account. The settler must fix the fact in his mind that, wherever he goes, the good land must be cleared of a more or less heavy growth of timber and bushes before it can be made ready for cultivation. The use of bulldozers and heavy plows has greatly reduced the cost of putting land into cultivation.

Soil erosion and soil depletion are already taking a toll from the Alaska farmer. Unwise clearing and careless land use is sure to result in meager crops and abandoned homesteads. On the other hand, Alaska soils are, for the most -7-

part, of such a nature that they will respond to good management.

MATANUSKA VALLEY LOCATION. The Matanuska Valley is located between parallels 61° and 62° North latitude and meridians 149° and 150° West longitude. It is bounded on the north by the Talkeetna Mountains, on the east and south by the Chugach Mountains, and on the west by the vast level plain of the Susitna River. It lies at the head of Knik Arm of Cook Inlet about 125 miles in a direct line from the open south coast of Alaska, and is traversed by The Alaska Railroad. 

CLIMATE. The latitude of the Matanuska Valley gives it long winters, short summers and a great variation in the length of the day between winter and summer. Beginning 

April 15, there are 14 possible hours of sunshine per day.

The days gradually increase in length, with May 15 having 

17 possible hours of sunshine and June 15 about 20 possible hours. At the time of the summer solstice the sun is below the horizon for only a few hours, and during this time there is no real darkness. After June 22 the days begin to shorten, the decrease being at the same rate as was the increase before that date.

The climatic conditions in this valley are transitional between those along the Coast and those in the Interior. Grain can be ripened as in the Interior, but the Matanuska Valley has the advantage of milder winters and a longer growing season. The climate is influenced principally by the relatively warm waters of the Pacific Ocean on the south, by the Alaska Range beyond the Susitna Plains on the west, by the Talkeetna Mountains on the north and by the Chugach Mountains on the east. The moisture-laden winds from the 

Pacific Ocean come up Cook Inlet to sweep against the high mountains and determine the precipitation of rain and snow over the entire region. 

On the other hand, the effectiveness of the mountains as barriers to the passage of moisture is indicated by the veil of fog and clouds which hangs so frequently over the southern slopes of the peaks in contrast with the prevailing -8-

clear atmosphere and cloudless skies of the northern slopes of the Alaska Range.

Under the modifying influence of the surface features and the warm coastal waters, the region has temperate summers, fairly cold winters and a moderate rainfall. The climate lacks the extremes of temperature and the light precipitation that characterize the great interior valleys of the Yukon and its tributaries. The mountain ranges protect the region against the fierce blizzards from the Arctic regions. Because of the protection afforded this region by the mountains on three sides, travel is almost always easy in the Matanuska Valley.

The total annual precipitation ranges from 13 to 18 inches, less than half of which falls during the winter months as snow and rain. The spring and early summer months have the lowest precipitation, and July, August and September have the heaviest. The normal low precipitation for the early summer is not usually detrimental to crop growth. 

This region has experienced droughts of some severity since farming was begun, but none of sufficient duration to cause crop failure. The summer rainfall, while not large (from 8 to 11 inches), is ample to supply crop needs, as the rather low summer temperatures and moderate winds are not conducive to rapid drying of the surface, or to heavy loss of water by plant transpiration. Also, while daylight hours are extensive, much of the time the sky is overcast. 

In fact, the rainfall in the latter part of the summer, which comes in light showers with much cloudy weather, is often sufficient to be unfavorable for curing hay. At such times, the hay is placed on racks or piled in cocks on poles set firmly in the ground. 

The region is free from tornadoes and severe electrical storms. In fact, thunder, lightning and hail occur so rarely as to be almost unknown in the Matanuska Valley. 

However, wind storms of sufficient intensity to cut the snow from cleared and exposed places, while not common, occur frequently enough to endanger the life of exotic perennials such as ornamentals, and of legumes and bush fruits. 

During May, as the fields dry and are prepared for seeding, -9-

occasional winds cause discomfort by blowing the fine soil from the fields, a condition which may be aggravated if and when larger areas are cultivated. 

Besides the wind storms, many perennial plants are often killed or weakened during the winter by the rather frequent occurrence of warm spells, during which snow may be entirely melted or reduced to a sheet of ice. 

TOPOGRAPHY AND SOIL. The topography of the Ma tanuska Valley consists mostly of benches and lowlands with occasional irregular ridges of glacial formation. These benches and lowlands embrace a variety of soils ranging in composition from silt loam through fine sand to peat, and in drainage from well-drained benchland to watersoaked marsh. Before it can be cultivated, the greater part of the land must be cleared of timber, undergrowth, moss and any kind of other thick covering of vegetation that may be present.

Exclusive of muskeg and muck, there are two broad divisions of soil in the Matanuska Valley: the benchland soils and the stream-bottom soils. According to Bennett and Rice', the former division comprises mainly the Knik loam series and the soils representing material which evidently has undergone considerable attrition by water or glacial action, or both, mixed, especially in the surface portion, with varying quantities of volcanic matter. 

Chemical analyses show that all the soils are well-supplied with calcium; in fact, it is higher than that of the average soil in the humid portions of the United States. 

The region, consisting of benchlands varying in width from a hundred feet to several miles, constitutes a great irregular plain that is continuous, except for the interspersion of connecting bodies of water, and is essentially uniform in its principal topographic characteristics. This plain rises, step-like, from a few feet above high tide through successively-higher benches until it reaches an average maximum elevation of about 1,200 feet. From this elevation the plain

DRAINAGE. The bottom lands drain quickly, owing to the sandy, gravelly nature of the soils and subsoils. Alternate strips of deep sand and soil, underlain at shallow depths by gravel, have excessive drainage, and crops grown on these soils suffer from lack of moisture in dry seasons. On the other hand, the numerous swales and depressions require ditching to establish drainage conditions which will be favorable for any agricultural use of the land other than pasturage or hay production. Probably 75 per cent of the bottom land is sufficiently well drained to permit cultivation without ditching.

Throughout the main part of the plain, in the non-alluvial portion of the valley, are large bodies of land that vary all the way from excessively wet to excessively drained. Moisture conditions are determined very largely by the position and character of soil materials. 

The good agricultural lands, such as the benches, rolling lands and ridges are made up of Knik loam soils, most of which have well-established drainage even in localities farremoved from streams. All these soils are gravelly in the substratum, consequently showing as nearly perfect internal and downward drainage as is possible. 

Ditching is not necessary except when it is desired to give an outlet to the lower depressions. The deeper loams, such as the typical Knik loam, hold sufficient moisture, especially with proper soil management, to meet the requirements of all crops in ordinary seasons. It is estimated that 

75 per cent of the more valuable farm land of this region is well drained and capable of conserving ample moisture for crop needs. 

CROPS. Cereal crops such as spring wheat, oats and barley grow well in the Matanuska Valley. The yield per acre -11-

for the past six years has been 22 bushels for wheat, 36 bushels for oats and 25 bushels for hull-less barley. The usual time for seeding grain is about May 15, and the harvesting time is about September 1. The early wheat such as Siberian will ripen in less time and can often be harvested by August 20. This quick maturity allows time for the grain to dry and harden before freezing weather. 

Oats are grown both as a grain and as a hay or silage crop. Varieties that mature early are preferable for grain even if they are not so tall or give as high a yield as other varieties. Owing to the comparative coolness of the summer days, the harvest usually begins after the warmest part of the summer has passed. 

The main hay crop consists of oats, peas and vetch, singly or in mixtures. The crop is cut either with a binder or with a mower. If with a binder the bundles are set in shocks of six to eight bundles and dry readily in favorable weather. 

If cut with a mower the crop is generally placed on stakes set in the field. A large spike or a cross piece nailed to the stake two feet above the ground prevents the oats from settling to the ground and thus assures better air circulation than if placed in cocks without the stakes. Experiments are being conducted to mow-cure the hay by means of air chutes through which air is forced by an electrically driven fan. 

Oats and peas constitute the main silage crops. The oat varieties used require a longer number of days to mature than do the oats which are grown for seed, the object being to obtain as much forage as possible. Canadian field peas are seeded in the mixture, which yields from four to six tons of silage material per acre on average soils. This crop is always dependable because it can be harvested at any stage before maturity and taken from the field regardless of weather conditions. The cost of putting up silage at Matanuska ranges from $4.00 to $5.00 per ton.

Spring wheats of the earliest types can be matured. Difficulty is sometimes experienced in curing the shocks in the field owing to the moist fall climate. At present there is little demand for spring wheat in the immediate neighborhood other than as feed for poultry, hogs and part of the grain ration for dairy cows. -12-

Barley has proved to be valuable as grain feed. It grows well on all the upland soils and can be used as forage and as a grain crop. As grain it takes the place of corn for fattening hogs and cattle.

Winter rye can be grown successfully. It is sown the latter part of July and matures in August of the following year, occupying the field for two seasons. At present this crop is not grown to any great extent. 

Since the beginning of agriculture in Alaska, the potato has been an important crop. Unfortunately, from the marketing standpoint, due regard has not always been given to the character of the soil or to the variety. On well-drained soil, particularly with a southern exposure and properly fertilized, good crops are grown. The main varieties are 

Arctic Seedling, White Bliss and Green Mountain. Yields of excellent quality. tubers ranging from 300 to 400 bushels per acre have been harvested from the above varieties. Much of the soil in the Matanuska Valley is excellent for potato production, and the crop well deserves to be given a prominent place in farm operations here.

Root crops like mangels, carrots and rutabagas yield abundantly. These crops are used as winter succulents to supplement silage or to take the place of silage where the farmer is not equipped with a silo.

Clovers of many varieties have been tried extensively. Results have been inconclusive in regard to over-winter hardiness. In favorable years, Alsike, sweet clover (biennial) and a red clover of Russian origin have survived in good stand and produced excellent yields. At present, hope centers in the Russian variety. Seed, however, is unobtainable commercially. It devolves, therefore, upon the Experiment Stations to grow the clover and thus make the seed available. 

This is being done, but since the original amount of seed was small, several years are needed to produce an appreciable supply for distribution.

Alfalfa of the hardiest kinds obtainable has been tried with varying success. The hardier purple-flowered varieties, such as Grimm, Cossack, variegated and Ladak live over under favorable conditions. Snow cover is undoubtedly es- —13—

sential, and soil with sufficient available lime contributes to winter survival.

A yellow-flowered, sickle-podded variety of Siberian origin has proved winter hardy, but the plant has a rather spreading growth which detracts somewhat from its value as a hay crop. It is also slow in establishing itself and does not seed abundantly or with certainty. Once established in a favorable location, it is very persistent and will stand considerable pasturing.

The most important legumes now being grown by the farmers in the Matanuska Valley are field peas and annual vetches. Peas do well in the Valley and return good yields in the garden and as a field crop. 

All trials of fruit trees, such as apples, plums and cherries, have given decidedly negative results. In favorable locations, Dolga crab apples have lived for a number of years at the Matanuska Experiment Station. They have bloomed freely and ripened fruit. Many other kinds of hardiest trees obtainable have lived for a few years only. In Anchorage and at points on the Kenai Peninsula, some very nice appearing, but somewhat immature apples have been on display at fairs. At Sitka, where the climate is quite mild, much experimental work was done with a wide assortment of fruit trees. Even there, results were not such as to encourage commercial plantings.

Bush fruits, however, do remarkably well. Heavy yields of currants-red, black and white-are grown annually. 

Gooseberries do almost as well, but are subject to occasional winter injury. Raspberries-red varieties-produce splendid crops, but also suffer if planted in an exposed situation. If the canes are pressed down to the ground in the fall and held there with earth, brush, or light poles, winter injury is minimized. Strawberries are an annual crop. All plants in the Valley that have so far proved hardy come from hybrid stock that originated at Sitka years ago. Even these hardy plants are occasionally winter-killed when and if the ground is swept bare of snow. There are beds, nevertheless, that have borne fruit for a number of years with and without care. -14-

Exotic ornamental shrubs, such as Siberian pea tree, bush honey-suckle, Japanese rose and certain species of cotoneaster, are, without question, hardy and desirable. Less hardy are lilacs, artemesia, golden elder, mountain ash and May Day tree. Many herbaceous perennials are sufficiently hardy to warrant planting, such as delphinium, columbine, peonies, dicentra and gypsophila. 

LIVESTOCK. Beef production may be profitable where the ranch is located adjacent to summer range. But as yet, not enough experience with cattle has been had to give a definite answer. Winter feeding is a major hurdle. However, there is a market for fresh beef, and once the problems of livestock farming are solved, the industry should thrive.

DAIRYING. Dairying is becoming a major farm industry in the Matanuska Valley, with Guernseys and Holsteins the principal breeds of dairy cattle. Grain grown locally and fish meal produced at the canneries are used for concentrates. Oats and peas seeded in mixtures for hay and silage are the principal roughages used for winter feed. Cultivated grasses are used for hay and also for summer pasture. 

There is an excellent market for whole milk, butter, cream, cottage cheese and ice cream mix. At present the demand far exceeds the supply. Dairy products are handled cooperatively through the creamery at Palmer. 

HOGS. Most of the common breeds of hogs, including 

Duroc Jersey, Hampshire and Chester White thrive well in the Matanuska and Tanana Valleys. They are prolific and it is quite common for sows to produce from six to ten pigs per litter. They are usually bred to farrow about May 

1. The hogs are free from most diseases commonly found in farming sections in the States. They are ready for the market by the first of November and weigh between 190 and 225 pounds, providing they have been well-cared for. They are usually pastured during the summer on oats and peas, rape or permanent pasture grasses. Barley is the most common grain used in finishing pork for market since it yields -15-

well both in the Matanuska and Tanana Valleys and takes the place of corn in production. 

SHEEP. At present there are several farm flocks of sheep in the Matanuska Valley. Most of these sheep are of the wool type. Because of the growing demand for dressed lamb and mutton, the mutton type of sheep is gaining in favor. There is abundant forage on mountain slopes for summer grazing. Winter feeds consist mostly of oat and pea or oat and vetch hays grown and fed on farms in the valleys. 

POULTRY. Several breeds of poultry do well in the Mata nuska Valley. The most popular are White Leghorns, New 

Hampshire Red and Rhode Island Red. Turkeys, ducks and geese can be grown without undue difficulty. It has been found unnecessary to heat poultry houses, provided they are well insulated and properly constructed. During the winter months, most farmers use artificial lights in the poutry houses. It is possible to produce all the grain needed for poultry with a good source of protein available in locallyproduced fish meal at reasonable prices. There is a good market for eggs and all kinds of dressed, unfrozen poultry meat.

Excellent results with turkeys both at Matanuska and Anchorage show the possibilities in this phase of poultry farming. The dry weather of spring is favorable to the poults at a time when they are sensitive to wetness. As with chickens, suitable feed can be raised. In the fall there is a ready market for prime birds. 

BEEKEEPING. The earliest recorded attempt at beekeeping was at Sitka, before the establishment of the Experiment Station there, when a priest of the Russian Church imported several stands. In 1905 the Experiment Station made a trial with one stand. Neither attempt was successful, and it was concluded that the climate was unsuitable at that place. A resident of Wrangell reported in 1929 that a swarm of bees kept primarily to pollinate fruit trees produced 210 pounds of honey. At Haines, also in Southeastern Alaska, bees have been kept for several years and yields of 50 pounds of honey per stand have been reported. In nearly —16—

all of these cases the bees did not winter successfully. Either the colony was completely dead in the spring or so weakened that the bees were unable to recuperate fully in time to produce much honey.

Successful beekeeping has been carried on in the northern states and in Canada by shipping bees in package every spring from a southern point, thus eliminating the overwinter hazard. With this encouragement and example, several packages of bees were shipped from California to the Matanuska Sub-Station in the spring of 1936. Four stands produced some excellent honey and this indicates that such enterprises would be successful, at least during the better seasons, such as that of 1936. Flowers producing nectar are sufficiently abundant to allow the bees to gather a good supply. This is particularly true where fields of clover are available. One of the drawbacks to beekeeping here is the low temperature which often prevails during the working season. The season was considered ended August 24, at which time the average net weight of four stands was 106.5 pounds of comb and honey.

MARKETS. It has been amply demonstrated that the Matanuska Valley will grow the farm products discussed in the preceding pages. Marketing of crops and produce at a profit, therefore, becomes a major consideration. 

The principal market for the Matanuska Valley is the growing city of Anchorage, 43 miles distant by rail and 48 miles by road. The Matanuska Valley Farmers Cooperative 

Association delivers virtually all the fresh milk consumed by Anchorage and at most seasons of the year the supply is insufficient to meet the demand.

This cooperative association, in its extensive operations of supplying farmers with their requirements as well as handling details of storage, grading and selling of all agricultural products, is meeting the needs of established farmers and newcomers alike.

Some farmers, principally old homesteaders, handle their own marketing. But, generally speaking, the bulk of all production in the Valley is handled through the Co-op. 

The price obtained in Anchorage usually is determined -17-

by the Seattle price, plus transportation costs. The exception to this are fresh ranch eggs and fresh-dressed chicken. 

A slight premium is paid for these items. 

A large Army base adjacent to Anchorage was a big purchaser of all farm produce during the war, and it is believed that this base will be maintained during the years ahead. 

Thus it will still be a large purchaser of Matanuska products. 

The Alaska Railroad, with headquarters in Anchorage, and the Civil Aeronautics Administration, also with Alaskan headquarters in Anchorage, are substantial purchasers of local agricultural produce. The latter organization is still expanding.

A recent development has been the marketing of fresh milk, eggs, and farm produce by airplane to outlying Alaskan towns as far away as Nome. 

Thus it is safe to say that the possibilities for the profitable sale of a reasonable increase in all types of farm production in this area is assured. However, it is doubtful that a sudden large expansion in farming would be profitable. 

The total population of Anchorage and all the communities in the Anchorage marketing area is not over 25,000 persons.

The farmer must always bear in mind that the Seattle produce and wholesale houses can supply Anchorage with its normal needs in from 10 days to two weeks time. This means that there will be no phenomenal prices paid for local products. Care must be exercised in judging the annual market requirements so that an over-supply of one or more local farm products does not glut the market. If this happens, local prices will fall below the Seattle-plus-transportation costs and local farmers will not enjoy a profit. 

Now that the war is over, mining will be on the increase in the Territory and this is favorable to the Matanuska farmer. In the mountains to the north of the Valley are a number of extensive gold mining operations and these mines buy substantial quantities of all food available in the Valley. 

Also close to the farming areas are a number of coal mines which also purchase many truck loads of produce for their year-round operations. -18-

TRANSPORTATION. The Matanuska Valley is traversed by the Alaska Railroad which extends from Seward and 

Whittier, the ocean terminals, through railroad headquarters at Anchorage and on to Fairbanks, a distance of 470 miles.

An extensive program of the Alaska Road Commission has produced a network of well-graded roads. In the main settled areas, every settler has immediate access to a graded road which leads into a graveled highway. Snow plows are operated and roads quickly opened, particularly over routes traveled by school buses. A good auto road connects Anchorage and Palmer. The Glenn Highway connects the road system of the Matanuska Valley and Anchorage with the Richardson Highway lying further east. This gives Matanuska a highway connection with the coast port of Valdez and the interior town of Fairbanks during the open season. 

TANANA VALLEY 

LOCATION. The Tanana agricultural region lies 240 miles directly north of Matanuska and beyond the rugged Alaska Range. It is bounded by the parallels 64° and 66° North latitude and the meridians 146° and 149° West longitude. It covers the portion of the Tanana Valley between Big Delta near the mouth of the Goodpaster River and the confluence of the Tanana and the Yukon. Its length is approximately 

205 air line miles, and the maximum width of this section of the Tanana Valley is 70 miles. Its area of 7,000 or more square miles, or 4,480,000 acres, includes bottom or valley land, benchland, and some terrace land. Naturally, there are many acres not suitable for farming, such as stream beds, lakes, and swamps. The Tanana Valley is in the region of perma-frost, but in spite of frozen subsoil the feasibility of successful agriculture has been amply demonstrated.

CLIMATE. For the years 1932-44 inclusive, the highest temperature recorded at Fairbanks was 99° F., and the lowest was -65° F. During the growing months, beginning with 

May, the normal temperature shows a steady rise, reaching its peak in July. The growing season for hardy plants, such -19-

as grains and grasses, averages 123 days on the south slopes. 

For tender plants, such as potatoes, the growing season averages 105 days. On the Valley floor, the growing period is several days less, due to occurrence of midsummer frosts. 

The winters are cold and during the two months of lowest temperature, the normal average for 1932-44, inclusive, ranged from -4° F. to -18° F. in December and from -1° to -22.5° F. in January. The winter weather is healthful and invigorating and the air is dry, crisp, and clear. The average annual snowfall for the past eight years was 59.39 inches.

Winter winds are not common and the snow does not drift appreciably. It is usually light and feathery and remains in this condition for most of the winter. 

During the growing season the days are long. On May 1 the sun rises at 3:19 a.m.; on June 1, at 1:33 a.m.; on July 

1, 1:10 a.m. and on August 1, at 2:48 a.m. The sun sets on 

May 1 about 8:19 p.m.; on June 1, at 10:07 p.m.; July 1, at 

10:38 p.m.; and on August 1, at 9:04 p.m. During June and

July and parts of May and August, twilight is continuous throughout the short night.

Total precipitation ranges from 8.5 to 16 inches with approximately half of it coming during the growing season. 

However, because of the lower temperatures and a favorable evaporation-precipitation ratio, cereal crops can be produced with as little as 8 inches of total precipitation advantageously distributed.

Dry weather in late spring and early summer sometimes checks plant growth to such an extent as to cause low yields, or subsequent moisture may prolong the growth and thus lessen the chance of maturity and increase the danger from frosts either before or after cutting. Drizzling rains and cloudy weather are common in late summer and may cause difficulty in curing hay.

TOPOGRAPHY AND SOIL. Most of the land in the Ta nana region is hillside or old river bottom land. Hillsides with a southern exposure are considered to be the most desirable for farming because crops grown on them mature earlier than do those on river bottom lands. The lowland soils are productive and yield good crops for forage. They -20-

are prevailingly sandy, either from the surface downward, or in the subsoil, and a large proportion have sand over the surface, or from near the surface downward. 

The soil materials are mixed in diverse proportions and the surface soil is intermingled with varying quantities of vegetable matter in different stages of decay. Soils in the 

Tanana Valley are, in general, less acid than are those of the Matanuska Valley. Some of the Valley soil produces a fine quality of potatoes when it has good drainage. "The Tanana very fine sand is the most extensive soil in the Tanana bottoms. More of it was seen than all of the other bottom soils combined. It occurs through the entire extent of this great lowland area, but is relatively more abundant along the streams where there are broad belts which do not include any important bodies of other soils. 

Spruce attains a surprisingly large size on this soil, showing no more tendency to a scrubbiness over large areas than on the heaviest types, perhaps not so great a tendency. Trees 

18 inches or more in diameter near the ground are common in many places. Birch is of some local importance, but it is considerably less abundant over this land as a whole than on the more loamy soils. "The Fairbanks silt loam is distinctly a slope soil. In physical characteristics it conforms very closely with the brown loessial soil, Knox silt loam, which is one of the great farming soils in Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Missouri, Nebraska and

Wisconsin. The Fairbanks silt loam is the best all-round agricultural soil seen in the interior of Alaska. It is well drained, yet retentive of moistures, shows only a moderate degree of acidity in either the surface soil or subsoil, is easy to cultivate, and is productive."₁ 

CROPS. The most important farm crops in the Tanana 

Valley are potatoes, hay and grain. Oats and barley are grown, both for grain and for forage. Seeding is usually completed by June 1, and the crops are harvested by September 1. The yield for oats ranges from 36 to 75 bushels per acre, with an average of about 50 bushels. When early

varieties are grown for grain and are sown sufficiently early, they may be depended upon to mature every year. Early maturity is not important when the crops are grown for forage. 

On good soil, wheat yields from 18 to 25 bushels per acre. 

Early varieties, such as Siberian, Reward, and Garnet are matured without difficulty. Only spring wheats are grown in this region.

Barley yields from 20 to 30 bushels per acre, with an average of about 25 bushels. It grows well and the grain is an important crop for dairy cows and hogs. 

Hay is mainly a mixture of oats and field peas as in the Matanuska Valley. Bottom land is best suited for this crop. 

Yields up to three tons per acre have been secured. For tonnage, seeding is best done during the first 10 days in June. A general practice is to cut the crop either with mower or self-binder in late August or early September, before hard-freezing weather. If mowed, the hay is "staked." If cut with a binder, the bundles are placed in small shocks. 

Drying proceeds slowly, but the cool weather checks mold. 

Such hay generally has a very high color and is very nutritious. Dairy cows at the University Farm, fed exclusively on such hay, maintained a good milk flow. Silage made from oats and peas planted together, as in Matanuska, has proved a good source of winter succulent feed and is used on all the dairy farms in the Tanana region. 

What has been said concerning alfalfa and clover in the Matanuska Valley applies to this region. Only the yellowflowered alfalfa of Siberian origin (Medicago falcata) has shown dependable hardiness. As proof, there is a field of several acres at the abandoned Agricultural Experiment Station at Rampart, on the Yukon River, which has maintained itself for over 30 years, during which time several hay crops have been taken by a local resident. This same variety of alfalfa is giving promise of success at both the 

Fairbanks and Matanuska Stations. Ladak and Grimm al falfa have withstood the winters at Fairbanks only fairly well.

Wherever the timber is destroyed and the surface veg- -22-

etation uprooted, as in building roads, native grasses come in and flourish.

One of the chief crops of the Tanana Valley is potatoes. 

From the beginning of agriculture here, potatoes have ranked as the chief cash crop. On the market they have met stiff competition from Washington and other Western states. 

This competition has had a salutary effect, since it has emphasized the need for growing potatoes on southern slopes to take advantage of better drainage, warmer soils, and the longer growing season. Where this has been done, and where early varieties have been grown the quality has met all market requirements. Average yields run from four to seven tons, although yields of 10 to 12 tons of marketable tubers per acre have been produced commercially. The varieties commonly grown are: American Wonder, Swiss, White Bliss, and Arctic Seedling. The prices obtained range from $5.00 to $7.00 per hundred pounds.

Commercial truck gardening is being carried on successfully, particularly with such crops as cabbage, cauliflower, and broccoli; beets, carrots, celery, parsnips, turnips, rutabagas, etc. This produce is sold direct from the garden and field, or stored for later sale. Hardy vegetables for the home are produced in most family gardens. Both quality and quantity are assured where properly cared for. Tomatoes, melons, and cucumbers must be raised in greenhouses or cold frames. In both the Matanuska and Tanana Valleys corn has reached roasting ear stage in exceptional seasons, but the night temperatures are too cool for this cereal to thrive.

Peas are an assured crop. In the sandy bottom land the vines make a strong growth and produce bountifully. On the slope soils, peas produce earlier at the expense of vine. 

Seed of many of the early varieties, such as Alaska and Gradus, has been produced regularly over a long term of years. 

Currants, either red or white fruited, produce dependable crops, the white out-yielding the red. Best results have been secured on bottom land. Snow cover is essential. A bank of peat around each bush is helpful and should be left there -23-

permanently. The black currant is less hardy than the red or white. Red raspberries have been grown many years commercially. South slope land has produced excellent crops. 

A good mulch of straw is helpful to prevent too rapid drying out of the soil. Winter protection is usually given by laying the plants or bending them down and covering with coarse litter, or simply weighting them down with light poles. 

Strawberries of hybrid origin have produced excellent crops. This fruit is grown mostly in gardens for home use. 

One rancher produces strawberries on a commercial basis, selling fruit readily at a fair price. Loss of plants, due to winter injury, is of infrequent occurrence. At the abandoned 

Rampart Station a small, uncared-for field has produced crops for over 20 years. Wild fruits, such as high and low bush cranberries, red raspberries, red currants and blueberries, are fairly plentiful.

ORNAMENTALS. A number of exotic ornamentals are available for home beautification. Of outstanding beauty and hardiness are bush honeysuckle, Siberian pea tree, pink spirea, Rugosa red rose and western chokecherry.

LIVESTOCK. This region is well suited to dairying. Summer pasture is available for four months of the year; during the rest of the time livestock must be stall-fed. Oats, barley, peas, vetch, native grasses, brome grass and other cultivated grasses are all grown for winter feed and pasture. 

Because of the cold winters, dairy barns must be well constructed and insulated. Three dairy herds are maintained in the Valley, two commercially and one at the University Farm. Holsteins and Guernseys are used in commercial dairy herds.

Hogs are being raised in increasing numbers in the Tanana Valley. During the summer they may be pastured, and in the fall fattened on locally-grown barley, wheat, and peas. There is a ready market for pork. Principally, American Hampshires are raised. Properly-constructed hog houses need not be heated except at farrowing time. 

MARKETS. The thriving town of Fairbanks is the heart of this agricultural region. In years past, farmers and gardeners -24-

have experienced some difficulty in marketing their produce at a profit. One reason has been that lettuce, celery, carrots, etc. have all come on the market at the same time and with a rush. Most farmers had inadequate storage facilities and therefore were compelled to peddle their wares or take whatever price was offered.

However, the situation has improved considerably. The 

Cold Weather Test Station at Ladd Field and other Army posts in the Interior have been purchasers of large quantities of produce and have insisted on careful grading, washing, and, in some cases, packing of produce before buying.

In short, the farmers have learned considerable about merchandising. They have thus obtained a better acceptance of their products on the part of merchants and the public and have also been able to get slightly higher prices for their produce.

The farmers have recognized the benefits of a cooperative, and, at present, the Tanana Valley Farmers Cooperative is expanding its activities. Facilities are provided for grading, storage, and merchandising of a sizable quantity of farm produce. Thus the marketing season for perishables is considerably lengthened. This coop is strictly a venture on the part of the farmers themselves, and has no federal assistance or connection. 

What produce is not sold, through the Cooperative, to the Army, the merchants and the eating houses, is usually sold to local residents who make a practice of buying their winter supplies of produce and putting them in root cellars.

With the mining business picking up again, camps within a radius of 200 miles will buy considerable quantities of farm produce. Much of this is delivered by plane. Some produce is also shipped by river steamer down the Yukon to villages and trading posts, which, in themselves, are distributing points for the vast lower River country. 

With proper grading, selection of high-quality products, and improved merchandising, the prospects for selling increased quantities of farm produce is good. Local production does not meet more than an estimated half of the requirements of the Fairbanks Trading Area, and, with the ex- -25-

pected gradual growth of this region, the farm picture is conservatively encouraging. 

Again, however, it must be pointed out that quick fortunes in agriculture in Alaska must not be expected. 

TRANSPORTATION. Fairbanks is the northern terminus of the Government-owned Alaska Railroad. Trains are op erated throughout the year between Fairbanks, the largest town in interior Alaska, and Seward and Whittier, seaports of the southern coast. Three automobile highways extend out of Fairbanks. These are open from sometime in May or early June until heavy snow falls late in October. However, close to Fairbanks, roads are open throughout the year. The 

Richardson Highway, 370 miles long, links Fairbanks with 

Valdez, a port on Prince William Sound. Connection is also made on this road with the Glenn Highway, which connects with the Matanuska and Palmer road systems and with the Alaska Highway. The latter highway, at present, is open to essential travel only. Northward from Fairbanks is the Steese Highway, 160 miles long, which connects Fairbanks with Circle and the Yukon River. Northwestward is the Livengood highway, 83 miles long, which leads to the Livengood mining camp. The Alaska Highway, built by the 

Army, has been maintained and kept open throughout the year. This has demonstrated that, when the demand exists, roads in the Interior can be kept in service at all times. A network of community roads is found in the vicinity of Fairbanks and around the smaller towns on The Alaska Railroad. Year-round airplane service is provided over the vast area that Fairbanks serves as a marketing center. 

OTHER REGIONS SOUTHWESTERN ALASKA. The agricultural regions of 

Southwestern Alaska lie in the Alaska Peninsula and the treeless islands beginning with the Kodiak-Afognak group and thence extending westward and including the Aleutians. 

The lower slopes of the mountains have a heavy growth of several types of grasses and moss-like plants that offer good range for cattle during the summer. Beach rye and -26-

sedges on large areas at the heads of bays can be harvested for hay and silage. Mowing machines can be used where the areas are not cut too deeply by small streams. These grasses make dense growth and attain a height of four or more feet.

Numerous attempts have been made to raise livestock on the islands beyond Kodiak Island, but nearly all have failed, principally because of the lack of slaughter houses, cold storage, and regular, reliable transportation. Nevertheless, a considerable herd of cattle on Chirikof Island has prospered and shown a normal increase on the native grass available the year round. Also, sheep on Umnak Island have multiplied and are doing well, producing a heavy clip of excellent wool.

The distance from Seattle to Unalaska is more than 1,000 miles, and requires six days by direct route or nearly two weeks via Seward. Freight charges for provisions are almost prohibitive because of the great distance. In peace time, mail steamers beyond Kodiak arrive only once a month, and, obviously, fresh meats and vegetables are luxuries to the white people living in these localities. Now that the 

Army and Navy have large installations on the Alaska Peninsula and down the Chain, the picture may change. If these bases are maintained, and civilian farming and livestock raising are permitted, there may be limited opportunities in westernmost Alaska for hardy pioneers. At the present writing, Army and Navy policy has not been announced. If the bases are maintained, there will be markets for produce and possibly fresh meat. It is believed that the transportation problem will be solved in large measure if the bases continue.

KENAI PENINSULA. The western part of Kenai Penin sula, between Cook Inlet and the Kenai Mountains, contains some of the best agricultural land in Alaska. The climate is never severe, being neither extremely cold in winter nor hot in summer. It is tempered by the warm winds from the Pacific Ocean, which is less than a hundred miles to the south in a direct line. The same kinds of crops that are produced in the Matanuska Valley can be produced -27-

here. Although Homer is much in the limelight now, very little public land with agricultural possibilities is available within reasonable distance of the settlement. At present, Homer's most urgent problem is adequate transportation to market its produce.

Farther up Cook Inlet, at Ninilchik, Kasilof, and Kenai are large areas of potential agricultural land which will need roads and adequate transportation in order for farm produce to reach existing markets. Regular airplane service now provides transportation for passengers and freight, including foodstuffs, but the cost is high. 

SOUTHEASTERN ALASKA. Southeastern Alaska is, for the most part, non-agricultural. Its population is greater than any other section of the Territory, so the market is there, but Southeastern Alaska does not have the large agricultural acreage of the Tanana or Matanuska Valley regions.

Limited areas of untimbered land at the heads of bays are being utilized for the production of crops and livestock. 

Most of the small areas of land under cultivation are near towns and can be reached by small boats. The principal crops grown for the market are carrots, rutabagas, cabbages, potatoes, strawberries, and raspberries. There are about 

500 head of dairy cattle in Southeastern Alaska. The largest herds are located near towns, and most of the feed for the animals is shipped in from the States. Farmers in more isolated localities depend upon native tide flat grasses for forage for their livestock. The cost of clearing the heavilytimbered land of Southeastern Alaska for farm use is prohibitive, and crop growing is, therefore, limited to home gardening, trucking, and the harvesting of hay from isolated grass-covered tide flats.

Notwithstanding the small size of gardens and trucking areas, the total area under cultivation adds up to the not inconsiderable amount of 1,000 acres at present, with a possible extension to 150,000 acres, according to William A.

Rockie, Chief of Pacific Coast Region, U.S. Soil Conser vation Service, U.S. Department of Agriculture, with offices in Portland, Oregon. -28-

HEALY. An area of about 200 square miles of land on the north slope of the Alaska Range, with Healy as a center, is believed to be a potential sheep range country. Owing to its elevation, this region is treeless. No snow lies on the ground longer than three to seven days at any time. The native vegetation consists of several types of bunchgrass and sedges, native redtop, low-bush cranberry, blueberry, dwarf birch, and dwarf willow. Enough native hay can be made to supplement the forage the animals obtain on the open range in winter. It is estimated that the carrying capacity of this range is 150 sheep per square mile. Range horses have been over-wintered there for several years. 

KUSKOKWIM AND YUKON VALLEYS. There are many thousand square miles of potential agricultural lands in the Kuskokwim and Yukon Valleys, but, owing to their remoteness and inaccessibility, or because the main lanes of travel have become established elsewhere, they will undoubtedly remain undeveloped for a considerable period. 

These areas are covered with a mixture of white spruce and birch, indicating that they are able to produce crops similar to those now being produced in the Tanana Valley. 

Results like those at Fairbanks were secured at the Agricultural Experiment Station at Rampart, on the Yukon, which was operated for over 20 years but was abandoned in 1925. 

FARM COOPERATIVES 

Cooperative farming and marketing is proving a benefit to the already-established farming communities. Within the year a farmers' cooperative has been established at Homer 

(Kenai Peninsula) and Fairbanks (Tanana Valley). The cooperative at Palmer (Matanuska Valley) was organized 10 years ago and is doing business on a large scale. These cooperatives are assisting the farmers to obtain lower living costs as well as to pool their various crops and thus be able to make larger sales of produce and to extend their marketing period. The cooperatives are independent of any government aid, being entirely self-sustaining. -29-

COMMUNICATIONS 

Alaska is well supplied with adequate communication facilities, such as radio, telephone, and telegraph. The latter is handled by the United States Army Signal Corps. Commercial broadcasting stations operate in the larger cities, and a network of amateur short wave stations throughout the Territory further increases the exchange of news and information. Towns generally, have local telephone systems, but few farms have telephone service. Daily and weekly newspapers are published in practically every town of more than 1,000 persons and are distributed over the surrounding regions by well-developed and regular commercial airplane service.

SCHOOLS AND CHURCHES 

The Alaska Territorial school system was established in 1917. It is under the supervision of the Territorial Department of Education, with headquarters at Juneau. The Department consists of the Territorial Board of Education, the 

Office of the Commissioner of Education, and a Territorial 

Textbook Commission. There are schools in rural areas, as well as in the towns, and school buses are operated into most towns to and from nearby farm homes. 

Free schools are maintained by the Department of Interior Office of Indian Affairs for the Eskimos and Indians of Alaska. These schools are largely vocational. 

The University of Alaska is a Land-Grant institution, financed in part by the Federal Government and in part by the Territorial Government. The University is well equipped and offers four-year courses leading to degrees in agriculture, arts and letters, business administration, chemistry, education, general science, home economics, and pre-medics. 

Both four and five-year courses are offered in civil, geological, metallurgical and mining engineering. Short courses are offered annually in home economics and mining. Each year, mining extension courses are conducted in various towns throughout the Territory. For information concerning the University, address the President, Dr. Charles E. -30-

Bunnell, or the Registrar, at College, Alaska.

Churches of the principal denominations are located in towns and communities with a population of 1,000 or more. 

WHERE TO LOCATE 

The prospective settler should pick out his location with great care. He should locate where there are sufficient families to support schools and churches and to justify other community services and facilities. Preferably, he should locate as near a town as possible, or at least near some point on the railroad or highway. Climate, transportation, schools, and proximity to markets for his produce should be principal considerations.

THE NATIONAL FORESTS 

Alaska has two National Forests which cover the heavilytimbered coastal sections of the Territory. The Tongass National Forest has an area of 16,080,000 acres and includes nearly all of the land area of Southeastern Alaska. It extends from Dixon Entrance northward to Skagway and Icy

Strait. The Chugach National Forest includes most of the 

Prince William Sound regions and the eastern part of the Kenai Peninsula and has an area of 4,800,000 acres. The combined area of the two forests-20,880,000 acres is slightly less than six per cent of the total area of the Territory. 

The Alaska National Forests are under the jurisdiction of the Forest Service. The headquarters are in Juneau, the capital of the Territory, with responsibility vested in the Regional Forester. The administration is further localized in five divisions, with Forest officers in each division acting under the authority and supervision of the Regional Forester in Juneau.

The work in these divisions is under the administration of Division Forest Supervisors. Headquarters are as follows: 

Southern Division, Ketchikan; Petersburg Division, Peters burg; Admiralty Subdivision, Sitka; Prince William Sound 

Division, Cordova; Kenai Division, District Ranger, Seward.

The Forest Homestead Act makes possible the entry of those lands within the forests which have been classified as -33-

chiefly valuable for agriculture. However, farming on the 

Alaska National Forests, owing to the non-agricultural character of the land, rough topography, expensive clearing costs, heavy rainfall, and isolation by water from markets and schools, holds little promise of any extensive development in the near future. Home gardening is thoroughly practicable in small tracts, and many kinds of vegetables and berries are grown.

Recognizing the need for something more practical and applicable for Alaska than the general Homestead Law,

Congress, in 1927, passed an Act which permits the purchase, by individuals who are citizens of the United States, of a homesite of not to exceed five acres of public land in Alaska at $2.50 per acre. The requirements are legal residence on the tract in a habitable house for a period of three years to the exclusion of a home elsewhere. There are no stipulations as to the amount of land to be cleared and cultivated. 

This law is taken advantage of by fishermen, miners, laborers, and others, regardless of the character of their employment, who wish a home and garden outside of incorporated cities. The law, as passed, is not applicable to land inside the National Forests, but, on certain designated areas where permanent settlement seems assured, the Forest Service has made tracts available for settlement under special use permits. After complying with residence and improvement requirements for a period of three years on such areas, permitees, upon application, may have them eliminated from the National Forest and may immediately apply for patent under the Homesite Law referred to above.

Settlers on the National Forests may cut, free of charge, such timber as is necessary to clear land for cultivation and to construct the required improvements. 

Bona fide settlers, miners, residents, and prospectors for minerals in Alaska may take, free of charge, green or dry timber from the National Forests in Alaska for personal use, but not for sale. Permits (free) may be required for green saw-timber. Other material may be taken without permit. The amount of material granted under free permit -34-

to any one person in any one year shall not exceed 10,000 board feet of saw timber and 25 cords of wood, or an equivalent volume in other forms. Timber for commercial use can be purchased as stumpage in practically any quantities desired.

JUDICIAL DIVISIONS 

The Territory is divided into four judicial divisions: Juneau, headquarters of 1st Division; Nome, headquarters of 

2nd Division; Anchorage, headquarters of 3rd Division; and 

Fairbanks, headquarters of 4th Division. Federal district courts are maintained in each Division. 

TAXATION 

Land or property outside of incorporated towns is not subject to taxation in Alaska. All male settlers and employed female residents between the ages of 21 and 55 years are required to pay a school tax of five dollars per annum. 

The funds of the Territory are raised by licensing certain industries, including fisheries, cold storage plants, and mining. Any resident who owns an automobile for pleasure pays a Territorial license fee of $10.00, and a license fee of $15.00 for business vehicles.

COST OF LIVING 

The cost of living in Alaska is higher than in the States, owing to transportation charges. This is offset, to some extent, by the higher prices which the farmers receive for their products.

Capital required by the new settler should be sufficient to provide him with living expenses for a year, or longer if he settles on new land. The minimum recommended is $5,000 per family.

Settlers who purchase farms containing some cleared land should be able to receive from it an income sufficient to meet their living expenses after the first harvest. Working capital is desirable for the purchase of livestock, ma- -35-

chinery, and other equipment. Obviously, the more capital a settler has with which to get started, the better are his chances of early success.

CHANCES FOR WORK AND WAGES PAID 

In normal times, in most places where labor is employed, there are more men who want jobs than are jobs to be had. 

For the last four years jobs have been plentiful, but this condition is changing, now that the war is terminated. 

Machinery has, in many cases, displaced hand labor, and therefore decreased the camp unit number of men engaged in mining. When mining is in full swing, many men are -36-

employed in the Fairbanks, Ruby, Iditarod, Circle, Livengood and Willow Creek districts and smaller camps scattered throughout the Interior. But the influx of labor has more than kept abreast of the seasonal demand. 

In general, wages paid in the Interior are higher than those paid on the Coast. Fishing companies employ a considerable number of men during the canning season. This work is only seasonal. Usually residents of Alaska, where qualified, are given preference on labor employment in mining, fishing, and governmental projects. 

The Alaska Road Commission and The Alaska Railroad employ workmen in considerable numbers during the summer, but recruit their men mainly from residents. 

There are, as yet, but few laborers employed in agriculture. Few of the homesteaders have money to hire labor and therefore have to depend upon their own efforts for the clearing of their land and the erection of their buildings. During the crop season, some of the larger farms require additional help from time to time. 

The question is often asked: Can the homesteader who locates in Alaska find opportunities to earn enough during the period the law allows him to be absent from his homestead to support him until he can begin to market his crops? 

This will depend chiefly on the man. Pioneering is always beset with privations, and those who come must face that condition. The man who is capable and willing can ordinarily earn enough in wages to get by while proving up on his claim. However, seasonal employment in mines, on roads and elsewhere usually coincides with the agricultural season, and the homesteader must choose between wages and improving his farm.

AGRICULTURAL EXPERIMENT STATIONS 

The Federal Government established the first Experiment station in Alaska at Sitka in 1898. Later stations were established at Kenai, Copper Center, Kodiak, Rampart, Fairbanks and Matanuska, but all these Federal stations, with the exception of the ones at Fairbanks and Matanuska, were discontinued. These two stations were turned over to the -37-

University of Alaska in 1931 and since that time have been operated by the University in cooperation with the Office of Experiment Stations of the United States Department of 

Agriculture.

The main Experiment Station is located on the University campus, four miles from Fairbanks. Projects being carried on at this station include work with dairy cattle, hogs, poultry, grains, grasses, legumes, small fruits and vegetables. During recent years many homesteads have been taken up in the Tanana Valley and farmers are calling upon the Fairbanks Station for information pertaining to livestock and crop production.

The Matanuska Sub-Station No. 1 is located in the heart of the dairy region, two and one-half miles from the Matanuska railroad junction and nine miles from Palmer. Particular attention is being given to the feeding and production of dairy cattle from locally-grown feeds. Since good dairy cattle and milk producers are high in price, emphasis is being given to the economy of feeding dairy heifers for herd replacements. Investigations in pasture improvement, legume and cereal breeding, trials of cereals and forage crops, rotation systems, potato production and storage, and roughages for the economical wintering of sheep are being carried on.

In 1939, Sub-Station No. 2 was established eight miles from the town of Petersburg on the Mitkof Highway for the purpose of carrying on research work with fur-bearing animals. The Alaska Fish and Game Commission is co-operating with the Experiment Stations in financing and carrying out reasearch work at the Petersburg Station. Particular attention is being given to the use of low-priced scrap fish such as flounders, cod and herring, in the feeding of fur-producing animals. Work is also being carried on in the control of internal parasites and diseases of fur-bearing animals. 

At the present time, fur animals being kept at the Station include mink, marten, blue fox, white Arctic fox and cross fox. Plans for the future include feeding fur-bearing animals on seal meat from the Pribilof Islands, as well as on whale meat. At the present time information obtained at the Pet- -38-

ersburg Station indicates that fish can be used to the extent of between 65 and 75 per cent for the ration of furproducing animals. There are many locations along the highways in Southeastern Alaska where homesteaders can obtain five-acre tracts for the purpose of establishing fur farms. With good care and management, it is likely that fur farming along the coast of Alaska, where a good supply of fish can be obtained, has post-war possibilities. 

The results of work carried on by the Experiment Stations in Alaska can be obtained by writing to the University of 

Alaska, College, Alaska.

EXTENSION SERVICE 

In cooperation with the U.S. Department of Agriculture, the University of Alaska carries on agricultural extension work for farmers, homemakers and 4-H Club boys and girls. The main office of the Extension Service is located at the University of Alaska, College, Alaska.

The Extension Service also carries out a soil conservation program in Alaska in which payments are made to farmers who carry out certain improved practices in farming. A district office is maintained at Palmer where a fulltime agricultural home agent and secretary are employed. 

Work is carried on with farmers, homemakers and 4-H Club workers.

A 4-H Club camp is held each summer at King's Lake for club members and leaders from the Matanuska Valley and 

Southwestern Alaska, and at Harding Lake for members and leaders from Fairbanks and the Interior.

Agricultural Extension projects include gardening, livestock production, potato growing, dairying, pork production, sheep raising, and poultry. Home projects include home management, child welfare, nutrition, clothing, canning, and handicrafts. Boys and girls in 4-H Club work carry on projects of all kinds pertaining to the farm and home.

SOIL CONSERVATION 

The agricultural conservation program of the Federal -39-

Government is carried on in Alaska in connection with the Extension Service. Payments are made for certain farm practices that will improve and conserve the fertility of the soil. The 1945 program provides for the payment of $10.00 per acre for clearing land.

WILD LIFE 

Fishing and hunting in season afford settlers healthy recreation and may help reduce the cost of living. Many of the lakes and streams supply trout, grayling, and whitefish; and the larger streams supply salmon. Particularly in the Matanuska Valley, settlers have put up a large enough supply of salmon to take care of family needs. Moose are often found near settlements, and some caribou pass through the Tanana Valley during the migration period. Wild fowl, such as ducks and geese, are numerous in the early fall, but move south as winter approaches. Ptarmigan, grouse, and rabbits appear in cycles with several years between. In years of abundance, the rabbits are sufficiently numerous to do much injury to both native and other plants. 

A number of wild-life reservations have been established. 

For information regarding game laws of the Territory, apply to the Alaska Game Commission, Juneau, Alaska.

FISHERIES 

The numerous salmon canneries along the Alaskan coast have an output valued at nearly $50,000,000 per year. The agricultural interests are little affected by these canning operations. A majority of the cannery workers are brought from San Francisco, Portland and Seattle. As most of the provisions and stores are also imported, the industry offers little market for agricultural products.

TRANSPORTATION 

The Alaska Steamship Company operates ocean steamships throughout the year between Seattle, Washington, and all major Alaskan ports. The Northland Transportation —40—

Company and the Alaska Transportation Company operate to certain Southeastern Alaskan cities.

The White Pass and Yukon Railroad runs through American territory from Skagway to the summit of White Pass, 20 miles distant, and continues thence through Canadian territory to Whitehorse, head of navigation on the Yukon, 110 miles from Skagway. A tramway extends from Nome into the mining fields for a distance of 90 miles.

The Alaska Railroad is government owned and is operated under the supervision of the Department of the Interior. 

Seward and Whittier are the tidewater terminals, and Fairbanks in the Interior, is the northern terminal. Regular train service is maintained throughout the year. The railroad operates several steamboats from Nenana, where "rail and river meet," to Marshall, on the lower Yukon. Towns en route are Tanana, at the junction of the Tanana and 

Yukon rivers, Ruby, Koyukuk, Galena, Nulato and Holy Cross.

Air routes from Seattle and other cities connect with the principal cities of the Territory. Within Alaska, a network of airlines connects practically every town with the main airlines.

SUMMARY 

The Matanuska Valley offers as good opportunities for dairying, poultry, hog raising, and diversified farming as exist in the Territory. The land is covered with birch, spruce, and cottonwood in sufficient quantities to permit logging and saw-milling of lumber for local use.

The Tanana Valley is timbered with birch and spruce. 

On the north slopes the birch is more abundant, but on the south slopes spruce predominates. At present, most of the timber used by the local mills is logged in the upper valleys of the Tanana. In this valley diversified farming is also practiced. Higher summer temperatures and lighter rainfall make this region somewhat better adapted for the raising of grain than the Matanuska Valley. Dairying has also been -41-

carried on successfully in the Tanana Valley for a number of years.

Coal from Suntrana is available at the chutes in Fairbanks at $9.00 and $10.25 per ton. At the mines in the Matanuska 

Valley it is from $6.75 to $9.75 per ton F.O.B. 

The various advantages and conveniences found in rural and frontier towns of equivalent population in the States are to be had at all principal towns. Each town has its high school, daily newspaper, theater, chamber of commerce, hospital, bank, churches, general merchandise, drug and furniture stores, meat markets, restaurants, and hotels.

Doctors, dentists and lawyers are to be found in each town. 

The towns have libraries and reading rooms. Fraternal orders also maintain good buildings and reading rooms. 

Practically all homes in the Matanuska Valley receive electricity through the Matanuska Electric Association (REA), but few farms around Fairbanks have electricity. 

Farmers at Homer have telephones and individual electrical plants. Preliminary investigations have been made by a representative of the REA to furnish the Tanana Valley and Homer rural districts with electricity. 

Settlers can purchase farm machinery in Alaska at about 

Seattle prices plus transportation. Settlers coming to Alaska should make their intentions known to the railway and steamship companies, as a special settlers' freight rate is allowed on household goods, equipment, etc. 

At present there is a limited amount of livestock for sale in Alaska. Therefore, it is advisable for the prospective settler to bring high-grade dairy cows with him. There are registered dairy bulls in both the Matanuska and Tanana valleys.

If the settler brings his stock with him, he or someone must accompany them on the steamer from Seattle and must provide food and care en route. On The Alaska Railroad it is customary for the settler to travel with his stock, although the railroad will feed and water the cattle en route if, for some reason, the owner does not ride on the same freight train. Cattle must be inspected for tuberculosis and -42-

horses for glanders before they are placed on board ship at Seattle. CAUTION 

The Commissioner of Agriculture strongly recommends that every prospective settler who is interested in making his home in Alaska make a trip to the Territory first, before bringing his family, livestock and household equipment to the Northland.

Opportunities in agriculture exist in Alaska, but the need for adequate capital to get started cannot be overstressed. $5,000 is the minimum recommended for a family to get located, either on public land or on a privately-purchased farm.

The prospective settler's chances of making a successful living up here are good if he makes a trip to Alaska first, to investigate conditions, and finds them to his liking, is properly financed and has reasons to believe that his family will be happy in Alaska.
