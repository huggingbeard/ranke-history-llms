A HISTORY OF THE NEW ENGLAND 

I' 

POETRY CLUB ++ 1915-1931 

T IS impossible to write poetry which is merely poetry, or American poetry that is merely American. Poetry must be poetic in a particular way; ours must be American in a 

New England way. New England is a small corner of the 

Nation, yet it has greatly influenced American poetry. In every New England State, in nearly every part of these States, poets produce significant work. There are Yankee poets who live elsewhere, but who do their best work here, and there are poets from abroad in the land who live in New England because it represents to them not merely tradition, but a scene of mental life. However, as the centres of culture are scattered throughout the region, its poets seldom meet one another.

group of of poets with these ideas more or less in mind, sent out a call for a meeting to be held at the rooms of the Boston 

Authors' Club in Trinity Court on May 11th, 1915. The names of these poets are given under the title of Founders in the List of Members. The little rooms were crowded. Some 

Harvard undergraduates who attended have since entered the Club. Sylvester Baxter (of the Metropolitan Park Com mission, authority on Mexican baroque) presided, while many persons outlined their ideas for a Poetry Club. Some had hoped for a poetic forum; others, apparently, for poetic teas. For an hour or more, the main discussion was divided between two individuals, one of whom was a stickler for Parliamentary procedure, and the other obsessed with doubts concerning the advisability of having any Poetry Club. The untoward discussion was brought to an end by the apparently

irrelevant query of Denis McCarthy: "But don't forget the childher." Those present felt like denizens of Wonderland and most keenly so at the close of the meeting when a man, whose hair was filled with hay, admitted that he was in the right pew but the wrong church as he had intended to go to a meeting of the Poultry Association. Destiny held in store for our society similar and repeated confusion from the 

Boston newspapers.

After much disagreement, a committee was elected com posed of William Stanley Braithwaite, Edward J. O'Brien and Abbie Farwell Brown. They met soon afterwards at 

Miss Brown's, at 41 West Cedar Street, and enlarged their number to include Josephine Preston Peabody Marks, Amy 

Lowell, Katharine Lee Bates, Sylvester Baxter, and Nathan

Haskell Dole. The enlarged committee promptly called a meeting on the 18th of May at the Chilton Club. All were present but Mr. Dole, who resigned. Josephine Peabody presided.

Discussion immediately arose as to whether emphasis be made on social intercourse or on serious programmes; whether we should form a clique or a forum; whether we should hold public meetings or private conferences. It was finally determined to have the more distinguished New Eng land poets meet in private houses in Boston, (the only practical centre for a membership so scattered). Two classes of membership were created: regular members who had published books of poetry; associate members who had published notable verse in notable magazines. There was no provision at first for including critics. 

It took a long time to elect a Governing Board. Amy 

Lowell was finally made President. Mr. Braithwaite drew up the first Constitution, and during the spring the committee met to debate its clauses, at West Cedar Street, at Wellesley in June with Miss Bates, and again in June in Amy Lowell's library. When a list of New England poets had been culled

for the charter membership, Mr.

Braithwaite sent out during the summer the following invitation: 

"On May 18 a New England Poetry Club was organized in the interest of poetry and its makers in New England. It was thought advisable that such an organization should exist in order that New England poets might have freer opportunity for meeting one another than has hitherto been the case, and be able to extend hospitality to poets from a distance who may be passing through Boston. "It is proposed to hold an informal meeting on the first Wednesday evening of each month from October through June for the purpose of friendly and artistic intercourse. The first meeting will take place next October. "You are invited to become a member. Tea money for the coming year will be three dollars payable in October. It is hoped that you will join the club and thereby help to make it of interest and value. I should be very glad if you would answer before July 25.. 

Yours very truly,

W. S. BRAITHWAITE.' 

The phrase "tea money" caused misgivings.. Some poets felt that they lived too far from Boston to profit from the Club; others cared not for clubs, but enthusiastic replies to the invitation were numerous enough and about twenty came to the December meeting in the College Club. 

Amy Lowell presided. The following were present: Jose phine Peabody, Katharine Lee Bates, Grace Hazard Conk ling, Edna Dean Proctor, Charlotte Endymion Porter, Fannie 

Stearns Gifford, William Stanley Braithwaite, Edward J. 

O'Brien, Sylvester Baxter, Conrad Aiken, John Gould

Fletcher, Lilla Cabot Perry, Hannah Parkman Kimball, Kate 

Louise Brown, Anna Cleveland Cheney, Odell Shepard, Mary Farley Sanborn and Frances Bartlett.

The Board of Officers was announced and the Constitu tion declared to be in effect. Harriet Prescott Spofford and 

J. T. Trowbridge, unable to be present, sent letters. There was discussion of future plans and policies, light refreshments, but no poetry. Many who grew to be friends met for the first time. At nearly every meeting since, poems later to become known in magazines and collections have had their first hearing. The talks by members and guests have often been critically important to those who have heard them correctly. 

Foster Damon wrote, "One cannot imagine what the Club would have been without Abbie Farwell Brown. She was from the first one of the shaping spirits. We never bothered much whether or not she were an official, for we always called on her for suggestions and advice and even for more exacting service. She gave her time; she opened her house for our meetings; she steadied the Club through stormy days; she sustained it through lapses of interest. She took upon herself many arduous duties, even anticipating them when they were inevitable, so that by her energy and tact, which was really a supreme kindness, the Club ran more smoothly than Clubs have a right to run. As President she shrank from the title, though never from the duties, even when, towards her death, they became difficult for her." 

She wrote the words to many choruses and songs as well as an operetta and song cycle for children. Her poems in clude "The Rock of Liberty," a Pilgrim Ode with music by Rossiter Cole of Chicago (a cantata); and an oratorio, "The Guardian Angel" with music by Charles Skilton of 

Kansas; and the lyric for Mabel Daniels' "On the Trail," which was adopted as the Girl Scout song.

The second meeting was held in January in Marlborough Street at Mrs. Perry's who showed some autographed volumes and read translations from the Greek. In 1915 

Henry James had written Mrs. Perry from London: "I greatly applaud, by the way, your verses, which are full of feeling, force and felicity-so full that they really relieve one after the sight, inevitably more or less frequent here, of lots of invocations of the Muse to which one can't feel that she has very eagerly responded."

It was a lively meeting. Mr. Robert Haven Schauffler read his "Scum of the Earth" which launched a discussion on the immigration question. Nearly everybody read. Amy 

Lowell contributed "Patterns," John Gould Fletcher his "Red Symphony", Edward J. O'Brien read from his new volume White Fountains, Charlotte Endymion Porter read 

"Shop Flowers and Shrine Flowers."

In February, we met at Amy Lowell's house in Brookline, following her dinner for Mr. and Mrs. Louis Untermeyer and Mr. Stark Young. Mr. Untermeyer read his "Willow" poem and some parodies. Mrs. Untermeyer read a poem on clothes. Amy Lowell read from the manuscript of Men, Women and Ghosts. Mrs. Conkling gave some Mexican poems and one on the Arabian Nights. Josephine Peabody and Mr. Fletcher also read.

Most of the serious meetings were at Mrs. Perry's studio in the Fenway. In March, Stark Young and Edwin Arlington Robinson were guests, and Amy Lowell read Robinson's "Ben Jonson entertains a Man from Stratford" for him. He was elected an honorary member in 1918. 

It was at this meeting that Abbie Brown read a poem of hers on John Townsend Trowbridge, who had died on 

February 12th.

In April, the Harvard Poetry Society with which we were

to become closely affiliated, met with us for the first time, and Harriet Monroe, editor of Poetry, A Magazine of Verse, instructed us in our poetical duties, after which a few found courage to read verses. The Harvard Poetry Society was very brave. "Bravery before this pedagogy, lacking only its blackboard, was bravely indeed. We all felt as though we were going to be graded D-" writes Robert Hillyer.

In May, Josephine Peabody was hostess at her house in Lowell Park, Cambridge, with the Harvard Poetry Club. 

Among these young men there were some who became actively connected with the Club-Robert Hillyer, S. Foster 

Damon, W. L. Bullock, W. A. Norris, Royall Snow and later, Joseph Auslander, Grant Code and John Wheelwright. 

By joint invitation from Caroline Hazard, Katharine Lee 

Bates, Florence Converse and Vida Scudder, we met in June at Wellesley College and were taken for a ride. Miss Converse read a play in which Joan of Arc figures, and Mr. 

Schauffler contributed some sonnets.

In November, we met at Josephine Peabody's in Cambridge. Mr. Charles Keeler of California recited his verses with unction. Josephine Peabody read several poems from her new volume, Harvest Moon.

Joining with the Harvard Poetry Society, we arranged a lecture at the Harvard Union for Walter De La Mare, who was visiting this country to receive the Yale prize for poetry in the name of Rupert Brooke. He gave an illuminating talk about his friend, and read from his own poems. 

This was our first public meeting. 

The last meeting of this year was at the studio in December with the Harvard Poetry Society as guests. 

Speaking of the Harvard Poetry Society, Mr. Daniel

Hugh Verder was virtually legendary in his activities at Harvard. It is said that even in stern Conant Hall, he could 10

rouse business students from their couches with his query: "Do you like poetry?" and, notwithstanding their protests, would recite his verses. He subsequently became a constant contributor to the Boston Transcript. 

At a meeting where Katharine Lee Bates, as had been arranged, should read last, the polite clapping which, without distinction of merit, greeted every contributor to the symposia of those times, had been held somewhat in reserve for the finale. But Mr. Verder trained in the gallantries so lamentably lacking in modern life, determined that it was a case of ladies first, and when his turn came around, as the penultimate reader, insisted beyond all possibility of contradiction, that Miss Bates should precede him. Affable and self-contained as usual, almost laughingly she delivered the first recitation of her now famous "America the Beautiful." 

The first clap had hardly sounded before Mr. Verder was on his feet. "Now I will read my poem" he announced. We remembered only that the poem was called "Easter Morning" and ended with the frank avowal; "Birds of the morn ing I would sing with you." Immediately after the rendition, he distributed leaflets printed in Lenten purple to the gaping multitude. 

Once, Mr. Verder read before the Harvard Poetry

Society a poem on the Widener Library, and specified his reference in a rather abstract metaphor of "the denizens of earth" as "Oh, Woodchucks and you know."

While the Monthly and the Advocate had offices in the top of the Harvard Union, the Society met alternately in one and the other. Under Norris it tended to be more and more at the Advocate. Then the Monthly disappeared and the Advocate moved. The meetings continued in the Ad vocate office until certain of the undergraduate editors ob jected to so frequent a proximity to the creation of literature. 

Thereafter the meetings were in private rooms. Mr. John

At a public meeting in the Paine Music Hall, Malcolm 

Vaughan (President from 1920-1921) gave Amy Lowell an introduction which was, he thought, truthful, graceful and courageous, until she rose to her feet, corrected everything that he had said and began the reading of her poems by saying that they needed no defense.

Under Vaughan's presidency, Messrs. McLane and Aus lander objected to the criticisms made at the meetings. 

Poems were thereafter read anonymously and criticized caustically. The objecting members resigned. The New 

England Poetry Club has adopted a similar policy, but has 12

met with no such difficulty. But the Harvard Poetry Society was attacked in the Advocate. It was the college gossip that the talk there was so obscene that no decent fellow could be a member. Walcott Treat was President from 1912-1923, and John Adams Abbott from 1923-1924, the last year of the Society. 1917 

In February, Abbie Farwell Brown and Miss Mabel

Daniels attended the first performance of their joint work, "Peace with a Sword" (on a motto of Massachusetts) at the

Handel and Hayden Society concert in Symphony Hall. 

The poem seemed to many the noblest American poem of the World War. 

In February, Josephine Peabody was elected President. 

The election was turbulent, and the rivalries between groups of ladies so bitter, that the gentlemen finally withdrew to the University Club. It must be remembered that the Free 

Verse Revolt was at this time at its height, and we may ascribe the battle over the Presidency not so much to personal animus as to aesthetic principle. On the conservative side, the militants, led by Lilla Cabot Perry, desired Josephine Peabody for President. On the other hand, Amy Lowell felt that were she not re-elected, poetic experiment would seem to be repudiated by her fellow New Englanders. We freely admit that these abstract principles descended to a deplorable depth of personal incrimination. 

One grave historian remembers that a flustered little lady, obsessed with the name of her adversary, absent-mindedly recorded on her ballot the candidate she most earnestly de sired to defeat. When the votes were counted, Amy Lowell was proclaimed President, whereupon the absent-minded voter, knowing that her ballot had been decisive and realizing her slip, insisted upon having her vote changed. The succeeding pandemonium was not a fisticuff. Abbie Brown proved 13

more than the Abraham Lincoln of the situation, as she prevented secession by both sides and stopped the civil war. 

Those who were present on this occasion may refresh their memories from our hint that the obesity of felines was publicly banded as a shuttlecock between the contending criteria. "You cats" were Miss Lowell's parting words. "Anyhow, we are not fat cats" were the last words, pointed if inaccurate, which she was to hear from the society for many years.

At this meeting, the Rev. Eugene Shippen, originator of the Golden Rose Poetry Tournament of the Second Uni tarian Church, Audubon Circle, offered the award of the Rose to the Club. Abbie Brown who had served with Bliss 

Perry of Harvard and Chauncey Tinker of Yale as the Jury in the May Day Tournament, thanked him and said she would consult the Board about accepting such an honor. 

After her death in 1927 he announced to the Board that she had accepted the award of the Rose for the Club. 

This Rose, not gold but silver gilt, a naturalistic work of a French jeweler, is kept in a box on which are engraved the holders' names. Before its award was entrusted to the 

Club, the Second Church had held three competitions, imitating the "Jeux Faloreaux" which mimic the Provençal Poetry Tournaments of the Middle Ages. The spirit of the literary joust in the earlier day has been set forth by an older writer thus: "To say and recite good and remarkable words for the teaching of the ignorant, for restraining mad and foolish lovers, for living with joy and mirth, and for fleeing ennui and sadness, enemies of the gay science." We are requested by the Rev. Eugene Shippen to insert this in formation in our history.

The reader may be curious to know why the New England Poetry Club, composed of people intelligent enough to satirize themselves, should accept the award of such an emblem. The reason is simply this: there exist in Boston other associations dedicated to the Muse which do not in clude in their membership any persons known as poets be yond their own thresholds. Should the New England Poetry 

Club resign the Provençal Rose, one or other of these in- 22

stitutions would seize upon it for the attraction of a to-do, and confer it upon a recipient approximate in ability to any of their own membership. We rely on the good nature of those who receive it, and thank them for their forbearance in conferring the favor of their acceptance. 

At the seventh and last memorial session of "The 

Symposium" held in 1926-27 at the Boston Art Club, in honor of the founder, Helen Archibald Clark, a series of lectures was given called "Formative Processes." These included readings of verse by Katharine Lee Bates, Earl Marlatt, Robert Hillyer and Lilla Cabot Perry. At these meetings manuscript verse was known as "hatchery." The director and treasurer was Charlotte Endymion Porter, lifelong friend and comrade of Helen Archibald Clark, who died in February 1926. After the church rites a friend exclaimed: "Was there ever such a day for a burial, such a white day!" and Father Van Allen rejoined, remembering King Charles, "Yes, the last day of the White King." "And our Helen is the White Queen" replied Mrs. William Blake, who was later to drop the well-loved ashes into the Atlantic Ocean, while Miss Porter recited passages from Emerson and Browning.

In 1889 Helen Archibald Clark and Charlotte Endymion 

Porter, fresh from studies at the University of Pennsylvania and the Sorbonne founded a Poetry magazine "to publish the whole illuminating lore of the art and with encouragement other than the usual frown upon its personalized and emotionalized relation to Philosophy, Science and vitality." “O Lord, more brain!" was the policy of Poet Lore. The published translations of all the chief European fin-de-siècle writers, and of Americans, Josephine Peabody, Bliss Carman, Richard Hovey, Edmund Steadman. Whitman and Brown ing sent them messages with their dying breaths. Mr. Dana

Estes brought the editors to Boston. In 1903 Poet Lore was sold to Richard G. Badger, a sucker-snatcher. 23

When the Browning Society started in Philadelphia, Charlotte Endymion Porter and Helen Archibald Clark directed its studies into channels inclusive of other poets.

Library.

Denis Aloysius McCarthy died in August in his house on 

Arlington Heights, at the age of 60. He was born in Carrickon-Suir, County Tipperary, Ireland, and came to this country as a 15-year-old apprentice to a tailor. The limited education of the Christian Brothers' School in Ireland he supplemented by private study. Boston College made him a Doctor of Law, and Tufts College gave him honorary membership in Phi

Beta Kappa. He was a lecturer for the Massachusetts De partment of Education. 

His first poem was called "Where Mother Sleeps" and appeared in the Omaha World-Herald. His five books of poems were: Heart Songs and Home Songs, Songs of Sun rise, Voices from Erin, A Round of Rimes and Auld Father Toomey. The public schools used his poetry for Americani zation! He was the official poet for the City of Boston's celebration of the 150th anniversary of the Revolution. 35

While at work on "The Sowers" he wrote his "Fields of Bally-Clare" which became popular over the radio. Once and again he published uncomplimentary notices of the Poetry Club in the daily press, but when he was made Treas urer, he discharged his duties faithfully.

CONSTITUTION 

April 29, 1921 

The name of this Society shall be THE NEW ENGLAND POETRY CLUB. 

II 

Not more than seventy-five members shall be regular members of the Club. These shall be divided into two classes, active and associate. Active members shall be persons who have published one or more volumes of poetry, exclusive of books privately printed. Associate members shall be elected from among those who have published verse in magazines of standing, or who are eminent as critics of poetry, their qualifications having been approved by the Executive Council. They may vote, but shall not hold office. 

III 

The names of candidates for membership must be proposed and seconded respectively by two active members, and the qualifications of the candidates passed upon by the Executive Council. The names of candidates recommended by this Council shall then be presented to the Club for action.

IV 

The officers of the Club shall be President, Vice-President, Recording Secretary, Corresponding Secretary and Treasurer, all of whom shall be active members. From among the active members there shall also be elected two members at large who, with the officers, shall constitute the Executive Council. It shall be the duty of this 

Council to appoint members of committees and to direct the policy of the Club. Members of the Executive Council shall be elected by majority vote every two years.

The dues of all members shall be two dollars a year, payable on 39

January first. If the dues of any member who has been duly noti fied by the Treasurer remain unpaid for a year the member may be dropped by vote of the Executive Council, which has also power to reinstate members.

VI The Executive Council shall have power in its discretion to terminate the membership of any person whose continuance in the Club seems prejudicial to its best interest, provided that full opportunity has been given that person to be heard or to be represented in his own behalf.

VII 

The annual meeting of the Club shall take place in April. A quorum shall consist of six members. Notice of this meeting shall be sent to all members at least one week in advance. 

VIII 

The Constitution of the Club may be changed after the written request of at least six members other than those of the Executive Council. On receipt of such a request the Executive Council shall post copies of said changes or amendments to all members of the Club, and call a special meeting within two weeks of posting said notice. No transaction of the special meeting shall be legal unless at least nine members of the Club are present, and the proposed change or amendment shall not become law unless supported by a two-thirds vote of the members present. This Constitution, revised from the form submitted by Daniel Sargent and Norreys J. O'Conor, was adopted at the annual meeting of the Club in 1921. On December 4th, 1928, it was amended. The last sentence of Section 2 was omitted together with all the words after "Treasurer" in Section 4 up to the words: "there shall also be elected members-at-large." 40
