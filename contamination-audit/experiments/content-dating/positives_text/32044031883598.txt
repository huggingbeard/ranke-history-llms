PREFACE 

On June 24, 1964, the Government Activities Subcommittee released a preliminary study entitled "The Short Lives of the Household 

Variety Incandescent Electric Light Bulbs-A Problem Confronting the Federal Government, A Major Consumer." ¹ This study demonstrated that the standard series of household variety incandescent electric light bulbs are designed to burn out too quickly. The study suggested that the lives of the bulbs could be doubled or substantially increased to the economic benefit and convenience of the consumer. 

The study also indicated that electric light bulb usage in the Federal Government could be improved were the lives of the standard bulbs increased. Following release of this study, the Administrator of 

General Services reevaluated Federal Government bulb usage practices. Through the use of a specially selected series of "extended service" and 130-volt bulbs (in 120-volt circuits), savings estimated at $2 million annually have been achieved in Federal Government lighting costs.2

On December 12, 1965, the subcommittee requested the principal American light bulb manufacturers to advise the subcommittee of the feasibility of extending bulb life and providing consumers with information on the bulb as to bulb life and lumen output values. 

Following receipt of comments from General Electric, Westinghouse, and Sylvania, the subcommittee's earlier conclusions were reviewed. 

The accompanying report evaluates the bulb design question in greater detail and responds to the comments received from bulb manufacturers. In the appendix to this study are the bulb manufacturers' comments in their entirety, together with other pertinent information relating to bulb life. 

The technical aspects of this report have been reviewed and approved by the National Bureau of Standards. 

This report is printed for the use of the committee in a continuing evaluation of this problem.

THE SHORT LIFE OF THE ELECTRIC LIGHT BULB INTRODUCTION 

Electric light bulbs burn out too fast. This is the subcommittee's conclusion after extensive evaluation of the economic factors relating to the design and manufacture of incandescent electric light bulbs. Effi- Efficient light bulb design requires a proper balance between light output (lumens), bulb life, and electric power consumption. Inherent to the incandescent electric light bulb principle is an inverse relationship between the lumen output and the time a bulb will burn. 

In bulb design, for a constant amount of electric power, if you increase bulb lumen output or bulb life, you automatically reduce the other. 

To achieve both increased lumen output and longer life, you must use more electric power. There is no such thing as the perfect incandescent electric light bulb that will give the largest possible amount of light, burn for an extended period, and use little electric power. cient bulb design requires a proper balance between these three factors. 

Furthermore, as an additional factor in arriving at a proper balance between light output, designed life, and electric power consumption, proper consideration must be given to the convenience of the consumer. 

But the standard electric light bulbs used in the American home, from 10 through 100 watts, and generally available to the consumer, do not achieve this balance. They are not designed to burn long enough. They are improperly designed with respect to optimum economy and convenience in supplying light, as well as being a nuisance and a hazard to the consuming public. 

Bulb Life Is Shorter Now Than a Half Century Ago 

Over the 87 years since Edison perfected the bulb, there have been countless improvements in bulb design and product efficiency. Yet not one improvement ever made has been toward increasing bulb life. The standard 100-watt bulb-the most popular size in American homes-was designed to burn 800 hours in 1908. This was increased to 1,000 hours in 1910. But, on April 1, 1933, the bulb industry made sweeping reductions in the life of all standard household variety bulbs. The 100-watt bulb was reduced from 1,000 to 750 hours, where it remains today. In the decades up through the 1930's when applicable electric power costs were high and when design and production techniques were not as efficient as they are today, the accent on consistently increasing bulb lumen output and the total disregard for bulb life was perhaps understandable. But conditions have changed. These factors no longer justify a series of standard bulbs with shorter lives than those provided the public a half century ago. 1

THE SHORT LIFE OF THE ELECTRIC LIGHT BULB Frequent Replacement of Burned-Out Bulbs Is Annoying and 

Undoubtedly Contributes to Accidents in the Home Too often housewives are needlessly subjected to the dangers of replacing burned out bulbs in halls, kitchens, bathrooms, stairwells, and elsewhere in the home. These replacements often involve shaky climbs on stepladders, chairs, or tables to reach ceiling fixtures containing burned-out bulbs which should have lasted for a longer period. 

Furthermore, burned-out bulbs are not always replaced immediately. 

Delays in the replacement of bulbs in halls, stairwells, and other inaccessible places can create hazardous conditions which should be avoided. 

In government, business, and industry, bulb replacement is a considerable expense. In many instances, the sole function of certain employees is the replacement of burned-out bulbs. The cost of replacing the bulb in business or industry, as well as government, often exceeds the cost of the bulb itself.

Short-Lived Standard Bulbs 

There is scarcely an individual who has not harbored the suspicion that bulb manufacturers intentionally design bulbs to burn out quickly so that they may sell more bulbs at the consumers' expense. THE ECONOMICS OF ELECTRIC LIGHT BULB DESIGN 

Consumers are confronted with several types of expenditures in lighting their homes. First, there are the fixed costs-the installation of the wiring and the various lighting fixtures. The electric wiring and fixtures are a significant expense which can be accurately measured. 

There are also repair and maintenance costs which are subject to precise determination. In addition, there is a negligible cost of cleaning the sockets and fixtures. But, costs of these types have no relationship to the question of determining optimum bulb life. They are unaffected by changes in the lifespan in the bulbs used in the lighting system. They are in the nature of fixed costs applicable with equal force irrespective of bulb design. Therefore, they are not a proper factor to be considered in determining the most economical life of the standard bulb to be used in the home.

In a strict sense, there is a cost to the housewife or homeowner of bulb replacement. Technically, it is possible to attribute some value to the time a housewife or homeowner devotes to replacing burnedout bulbs and to compute some cost to this effort. This cost, however, is highly speculative. Furthermore, the cost of the housewife's time devoted to bulb replacement is not the crux of the problem. 

The housewife's complaint is over the annoyance of having to unnecessarily change bulbs that burn out too soon. 

This means that to the average consumer there are only two expenditures which affect bulb design, the cost of the bulb and the electric power the bulb uses. 

There Is an Inverse Relationship Between Bulb Lumen Output and Bulb Life 

The modern incandescent bulb is generically the same as described in Edison's first patent issued on November 1, 1879.3 An electric current passes through a filament encased in a bulb-like, transparent glass container from which the oxygen of the atmosphere has been 3 Edison's initial patent appears in appendix C.

removed. Either a vacuum is created in the bulb-or the air is replaced by inert gases. 

The filament offers resistance to the passage of the current and the electrical energy absorbed by the filament is dissipated by the emission of heat and light. The rate of light emission is measured in terms of "lumens." The electrical resistance of conductive materials such as tungsten filaments depends upon their diameter and length. Therefore for any given flow of electric current, the lumen output and the life of the bulb depend upon the diameter and length of the filament. 

In more precise terms, if the cross-sectional area of the filament of a lamp and also the length of the filament are decreased by the same small percentage so that the resistance of the filament and therefore the wattage of the lamp remains constant, the filament will operate at a higher temperature. The end result a brighter bulb giving more light per unit of current used, but with a shorter life.* 

Increase the diameter and length of the filament and the very opposite effect is noted. Bulb lumen output goes down. There is less light output for the current used. But, bulb life goes up significantly.

There Is a Direct Relationship Between Bulb Life and Power Consumption 

Bulb designers, however, need not choose a reduction of bulb lumen output to increase bulb life. There is an alternative. By increasing bulb wattage or, in other words, the rate at which the bulb uses electrical energy, a bulb can maintain its original lumen output and also have an increased life. This is done by increasing the amount of tungsten in the filament so that even with the increased power consumption, the filament operates at a lower temperature. It takes longer for the tungsten in the larger filament to evaporate even though additional electric power is used. This gives the bulb longer life. 

Result: A bright, longer lasting bulb-but a bulb which uses additional electric power.

The Relationships Between Life, Lumen Output, and Power Consumption Are Based Upon Laboratory Tests 

The characteristics of a bulb are interrelated and one cannot be changed without affecting the others. By means of laboratory tests, using bulbs of various designs, general values for these relationships have been determined. But, these values are not precise. They are approximations. And, they vary with changes in bulb size and design.

Laboratory tests indicate that bulb life is inversely proportional to about the seventh or eighth power of lumen output, assuming power input remains constant. This means that a relatively minor change in bulb lumen output has a pronounced effect upon bulb life. Bulb Costs Average 25 Cents 

In the June 1964 study, the subcommittee assumed a bulb cost of 25 cents. None of the manufacturers objected to this price in the comments the subcommittee has received. When sales taxes are considered, it is doubtful that many consumers purchase bulbs for less than 25 cents apiece. Most likely the average bulb cost to consumers exceeds this amount. 

Special lines of "white," "soft," "non-glare," "eye saving" bulbs produced by Westinghouse, Sylvania, and General Electric have a premium price exceeding that of the standard bulbs. "Extended service" and long-life bulbs usually sell for an even higher rate. 

Productionwise, these specialized bulbs are essentially the same as the standard.

General Electric, which predominates the market, sells bulbs through a system of consignment and agency contracts. And, by this means, is able to control the retail sales price of its product. In 

United States v. General Electric Company, et al., 272 U.S. 476 (1926), the Supreme Court gave approval to this distribution system sweeping aside the contentions of the Department of Justice that the distribution and sale of bulbs by this means was a restraint and monopoly of interstate commerce.

The impact of this decision has been modified as certain of the basic GE patents expired releasing Westinghouse and others from pricing provisions under licensing contracts. Whether this sales arrangement under present-day conditions is in fact a restraint and monopoly is a judicial question for the courts to decide.

The fact is, however, that at this time the prices of standard bulbs are about the same irrespective of manufacture. And, for the purpose of this study, 25 cents is a fair and reasonable price to use. 

The Consumers' Incremental or Marginal Power Rate Rather Than the Average Price He Pays for Electric Power Should Be Used in 

Determining the Most Economical and Appropriate Electric Light 

Bulb Design

Electric power costs, as well as electric power rate structures vary throughout the Nation. Rate structures, though different, follow a similar pattern. Consumers are charged a basic or "prime" rate for an initial amount or "bloc" of power; then lesser rates for additional "blocs" of power as consumption increases. The initial or prime rate, which in many instances is also a minimum bill, usually applies to a nominal amount of power of from 20 to 75 kilowatt-hours. The rate for this initial power is relatively high. However, after usage of this initial bloc of power, rates drop off substantially at intervals of from 50 to 100 kilowatt-hours until a minimum incremental rate is reached, usually at a point between 200 and 500 kilowatt-hours. 

General Electric and others suggest the use of an average power rate rather than this marginal or incremental rate in evaluating light bulb design. As an average rate takes into consideration the higher cost of the initial "blocs" of power, the average power rate a consumer pays is substantially higher than the incremental rate. As an example, while the incremental rate applicable to residential consumers in the United States is approximately 2 cents a kilowatt-hour, the average

rate of a residential consumer is about 2.4 cents, according to General 

Electric. With electric power costs a critical consideration, the determination of optimum bulb design requires that the proper measure of electric power costs be utilized. The proper measure is the marginal or incremental rate.

For the United States as a whole, the average residential consumer uses sufficient electric power to gain advantage of a marginal or incremental power rate of approximately 2 cents a kilowatt-hour. 

Assuming other electric usages remain constant, suppose a consumer turns off a light bulb otherwise used in his home during a particular electric power billing period. The reduction in his light bill would be computed at the marginal rate. Furthermore, assume that instead of turning off a standard 100-watt, 750-hour bulb, he were to use one of the hypothetical 109.6-watt, double life bulbs used in the subcommittee's June 1964 demonstration. Use of this higher wattage bulb would increase his light bill above the previous level. Again, the change in his light bill would be at a marginal or incremental power rate. In neither instance would the consumer's average power rate be material.

This does not mean that the average cost of electric power to the consumer is of no consequence whatever. In certain situations, the average rather than the marginal cost of power would be an appropriate measure. His electric bill, for example, in its entirety constitutes an average power cost. Also, were the consumer to rent a room in his home to a boarder with the agreement that the boarder pay for the electricity used to burn a bulb lighting the room, the proper determination of the amount he should pay would require use of the average power rate applicable to the consumer. It would only be fair as between the two parties to assume that the light in the boarder's room used a prorated share of electricity from each of the blocs in the power rate structure. As between the two parties, the average power rate the consumer paid would be the proper measure of electric power costs.

The argument favoring use of the marginal or incremental rate is basic. Those suggesting usage of the average rate in electric bulb design evaluations misunderstand the applicable principles of cost accounting. A consumer can logically attribute the initial high cost power he purchases to his television, refrigerator, and other appliances in the home. To the consumer, these power usages correspond to fixed costs of a manufacturer.

Incremental costs and profits are routine in industry. The theory applies with equal force to the electric light bulb consumer. Allocations of average costs may have their place in many types of computations, but when other parties are not involved, the consumer as the businessman can allocate costs to his best advantage. This means, as the examples outlined above demonstrate, that the proper power rate to use in bulb design evaluations is the incremental or marginal rate. If more electric power is to be used as a result of alterations in present light bulb design, any fluctuation in the consumer's lighting bill will be at a marginal or incremental rate. 

The accompanying schedule prepared for the subcommittee by the Federal Power Commission indicates that consumers in most parts of the country use sufficient electricity to have a marginal or incremental

power rate of about 2 cents. On the basis of this information, the subcommittee feels justified in utilizing a 2-cent per kilowatt-hour rate in evaluating the design of the standard bulbs. 

Were a special series of bulbs designed for the northeast section of the Nation where higher power rates generally prevail, a lower incremental rate would apply to the remaining portion of the country. 

Manufacturers of many lines of items tailor their products to meet varying requirements of different geographical areas. It would not be unreasonable to expect the bulb-manufacturing industry to show this same flexibility.

EVALUATING THE STANDARD BULBS 

Assuming a b exponent of 7, a power rate of 2 cents, and a bulb cost of 25 cents, how long should a bulb last?

The traditional approach has been to set up a formula for the cost of a given amount of light (lumen-hours), then, with differential calculus, determine an optimum relationship for bulb life in terms of bulb costs and power consumption.

A more informative evaluation can be made by assigning values in the basic formula for bulb wattage and bulb and power cost. Total light costs corresponding to varying values in bulb life would then show the magnitude of any additional cost to consumers through increasing bulb life beyond the optimum relationship obtained through the use of differential calculus. This pure mathematical approach makes no allowance, however, for consumer convenience. If the net additional cost of extending bulb life beyond the optimum is low, then the convenience of the consumer would make reasonable extensions of this kind appropriate. 

The Ratio Between Power Cost to "Burnout" and the Cost of Bulbs 

In response to inquiries concerning the economics of electric light bulb design, National Bureau of Standards scientists developed a general formula to be used as a "rule of thumb" in evaluating electric light bulb optimum economy. They determined that optimum economic balance is achieved when the cost of electric power needed to supply the bulb to "burnout" is equal to seven times the cost of the bulb. When power costs exceed this sum, bulb life is too long.

When power costs fall below seven times bulb cost, then bulb life is too short.8

This 7-to-1 formula, while an exact and concise computation, does not provide a pronounced ratio. The formula is "sluggish" in that significant changes in the rated life of the bulb and the current used reflect only minor changes in cost. 

In fact, the ratio can vary from 4 to 1 to as high as 11 to 1 without registering a significant change in efficiency. 

Despite these limitations, the formula can be of value in showing major imbalances in light bulb design. However, the ratio will be reduced from 7 to 1 to 6 to 1. The reason is that the formula as developed by the National Bureau of Standards relied upon a b exponent of 8.

For reasons previously discussed, the subcommittee has assumed a b exponent of 7 for purposes of this report. Had the 

Bureau of Standards used an exponent of 7, the result would have been a 6-to-1 formula.

Bulb savings rates can be computed. For a 750-hour bulb, the rate of bulb consumption per hour would be 25 cents divided by 750 hours, or 0.0333 cents per hour. To obtain the rates at which the consumer would save on bulb consumption were bulb life extended, the cost rate per hour for longer periods would be subtracted from this base rate. Example: a consumer would save on bulbs at the rate of about 0.008 cents per hour through extending the life of a 

750-hour bulb to 1,000 hours.

Cost rates of additional amounts of power can then be computed and compared with these projected savings. A 750-hour, 100-watt bulb would need approximately 104 watts to increase bulb life to 1,000 hours. The cost of the additional 4 watts at 2 cents per kilowatt-hour would be about 0.008 cents per hour. 

Comparison of these two costs establishes optimum break-even points that are the same as the 6-to-1 formula. Comparison of these cost rates as bulb life increases beyond the optimum shows that they are nearly equal. For the 100-watt bulb, only after bulb life reaches 

1,200 to 1,350 hours does the cost of higher power usage pull away from the offsetting savings in bulb consumption and provide a measurable net increase in cost. Comparisons applicable to 75and 60watt bulbs give similar results at correspondingly higher extensions in life.

These various tests show that the cost to the individual consumer of extending the 60-, 75-, and 100-watt bulbs beyond the optimum breakeven point would probably be so small as to be unmeasurable. But, at the extreme, the consumer would have the obvious benefit of longer lasting bulbs for his entire household at a maximum additional cost of 2.5 percent. But, what does a 2.5 percent increase mean in dollars and cents to the average consumer?

GE suggests that a 100-watt bulb lasts for about a year in the average home.⁹

Total costs associated with a 100-watt bulb are $1.75 ($1.50 for power at 2 cents per kilowatt-hour for 750 hours, and 25 cents for bulb). The additional cost per year to the consumer would be about 4.5 cents per bulb. The additional cost of extending the 

75and 60-watt bulbs to 1,500 and 2,000 hours respectively or double that of the present standards, would be even less. Neither of these bulbs are at optimum life at this time. 

This means that the consumer could have the advantage and convenience of double-life bulbs, without any reduction in initial lumen value, for an annual cost of about 5 cents for each 60-, 75-, and 100watt bulb used in the home. As an example, even a consumer with a 

10-room house, having an average of five 60-, 75-, or 100-watt bulbs per room, would have an additional lighting cost of less than $2.50 per year.

It is interesting to note than GE, in its comments on the subcommittee's study, reveals that extension of bulb life beyond the economic "break even" point for consumer convenience has been a long-standing company policy. In the appendix to the GE comments is an article • See appendix to GE comments, p. 65.

from Illuminating Engineering 10 entitled "Incandescent Lamp Design 

Life for Residential Lighting." In this article, the authors-two GE employees-point out that it is GE's policy to extend bulb life from 300 to 400 hours beyond the optimum economic life. According to this article, the optimum economic life of the 100-watt standard bulb is 350 hours. However, for reasons of "convenience" to consumers, GE, at least since 1933, when bulb life was substantially reduced, has set the life of this bulb at 750 hours.

In the ultimate sense, the reasonableness of extending bulb life at a minimal and even unmeasurable annual cost is a matter of judgment. 

The thought of increasing lighting cost by a maximum of 2.5 percent per year in exchange for double-life bulbs might not appeal to some people.

It is the considered conclusion of this subcommittee, however, that double-life bulbs would be well worth this minimal cost. 

And, it is our opinion that the overwhelming majority of consumers agree.

Westinghouse Asserts That Its Computations Are "Not Entirely in Accord With the Results" Reached in the Subcommittee's

Following receipt of Mr. Wagner's letter, Westinghouse was asked to clarify the statement quoted above-to advise the subcommittee how Westinghouse bulb computations differ from those in the subcommittee's study. In a March 3, 1966 letter from Westinghouse, this inquiry was answered in its entirety by the information contained in the following two sentences:

Experience with Westinghouse lamps discloses some differences in arriving at the cost of light, with the major one being the "b" exponent. Other variations may occur in using constant lumens versus constant watts. 

Westinghouse did not suggest whether the b exponent for its bulbs was either higher or lower than the value of 8 as used in the subcommittee's study. As this is an approximate evaluation based upon laboratory tests and which varies according to bulb design and manufacture, it would not be expected that Westinghouse computations would be "entirely in accord" with the b exponent value the subcommittee used.

After receiving this further information from Westinghouse, the subcommittee reviewed the b exponent question and decided to lower the value used in this report from 8 to 7 to avoid any possible distortion in the report's conclusions arbitrarily favoring the extension of bulb life.

Under the more exacting evaluations utilized in this report, reduction of the b exponent from 8 to 7 does have a noticeable effect upon the optimum economic life of the 100-watt bulb and to a slightly less extent on the 75and 60-watt sizes. As indicated earlier, the economic "break even" point for a 100-watt bulb, assuming a b exponent of 7 and a power rate of 2 cents per kilowatt-hour, is approximately 750 hours. If a b exponent of 8 were utilized in these same computations, the economic "break even" point would be approximately 875 hours. Because of the minimal cost factors involved in extending bulb life for reasonable periods beyond the economic "break even" point, it is not believed that the substance of the subcommittee's earlier conclusions are challenged, particularly in the absence of clear authoritative evidence from bulb manufacturers generally that their household variety bulbs have a b exponent value appreciably less than 7. Sylvania Objects to the Application of the Incremental Power Rate to the Base Power Costs 

Mr. Morse, in his letter, also stated that:

In economic studies involving the cost of electrical energy, it is customary to apply the incremental cost of power only to the incremental power consumption. 

As an example, in the calculations involving the 100-watt standard versus the 109.6-watt double-life bulb, 100 of the watts in each bulb should be computed at the base power rate, and only the 9.6 extra watts at the incremental rate. 

Sylvania's argument is easily disposed of by pointing out that the consumer is already using the "base" amount of electricity in utilizing the present series of short life standard bulbs. What the subcommittee proposes is to substitute a longer life bulb using an additional increment of electricity. The question is to balance the cost of any additional power usage needed by the longer life bulb against savings in bulb purchases. The question whether to extend the life of the bulb would be settled by determining which were cheaper, the additional increment of electricity, or the additional bulb; then, with regard to the 60-, 75-, and 100-watt bulbs, whether consumer convenience justified additional extensions in bulb life by further increasing power usage. Under this approach, it is of no consequence how much. the consumer might pay for the "base" electricity. This amount of electricity would be used under either alternative. Accordingly, it is not a relevant or pertinent factor in making a selection as between the standard or longer life bulb.

The principle Sylvania advances is correct. It is the application of this principle to this particular type of evaluation that is at fault. 

In broad terms, the point Sylvania raises supports the subcommittee's fundamental position that incremental rather than average power rates be utilized in evaluating bulb design. Essentially, it is Sylvania's position that one should apply incremental power rates to incremental power consumption. Earlier in this report it was explained why it is proper to consider the consumer's power consumption for lighting purposes as his incremental power usage even when no additional electric power is involved. As principles of cost accounting permit such an allocation, the principle that Sylvania emphasizes corresponds to the subcommittee's conclusion that incremental power rates should be used in determining optimum bulb life.

In a recent text on cost accounting, it is explained: 

In comparing alternatives, it is necessary to consider only those items of cost that are expected to differ from one alternative to another. Costs that are not affected by the choice made are not necessary in making the decision. For example, if the question is whether or not to use a more expensive grade of materials in order to reduce scrap losses, such costs as the factory manager's salary, insurance, depreciation, and property taxes on the plant will probably not be changed by the decision made and can be disregarded for purposes of the comparison. When improperly included, such costs may actually be misleading and may lead management to make the wrong decision. Hence, in computing costs for decisions involving choice between alternatives, it is essential to filter out the cost elements that are irrelevant to the purpose for which the costs are wanted and to consider only those costs that are affected by the decision in question.

The lives of the standard bulbs are too short. 

Even extension of bulb life to the optimum "break even" point would be a substantial improvement. But, in addition, the lives of the higher wattage bulbs should be doubled for consumer convenience. 

The net additional cost to the consumer per year would be low-in the average home not more than a nickel a year for each 60-, 75-, and 

100-watt bulb in use.

The bulb manufacturers' comments were generally unconvincing. 

Sylvania and Westinghouse pointed out the possibility of variations in the b exponent, but avoided any discussion as to the proper b exponent value.

Arguments were made against use of incremental rather than average power rates. These arguments cannot be supported.

APPENDIXES APPENDIX A THE SHORT LIVES OF THE HOUSEHOLD VARIETY INCANDESCENT ELECTRIC LIGht Bulbs, a PROBLEM CONFRONTING THE Federal GOVERNMENT, A MAJOR CONSUMER (JUNE 1964) A PRELIMINARY STUDY PREPARED BY THE GOVERNMENT ACTIVITIES SUBCOM- MITTEE OF THE COMMITTEE ON GOVERNMENT OPERATIONS 

A flick of the switch followed by a brief flash of light, or maybe no light at all, can bring a strong sense of annoyance and, often, suspicion. Another electric light bulb has burned out and that American consumer may well have an even firmer suspicion that the manufacturers make the bulbs to burn out quickly. 

In 1963,

In Federal facilities, bulb replacement costs are a significant item. 

Federal agencies purchased about $3.8 million worth of incandescent light bulbs of sizes comparable to those used in the average home. While specific estimates are not available, most officials agree that in the Federal Government the cost of replacing the bulb exceeds the cost of the bulb. For this reason, while a burnedout bulb in a Federal agency may not be as annoying to the individual involved as when a bulb burnout occurs at home, bulbs and their replacement cost money. 

This makes the burned-out bulb a matter of concern to the taxpayers and, therefore, to this subcommittee.

The subcommittee has reviewed the principal factors affecting the rated life of the incandescent bulb. While these studies must necessarily be implemented by extensive technical and economic evaluations, they nevertheless are sufficient to indicate: (1) The feasibility of extending the rated lives of current standard bulbs so as to reflect the significant decline in power rates over the decades since the present rated lives of these bulbs were originally established. (2) That more effective utilization of light bulbs throughout the Government would be achieved if the manufacturer marked each bulb with the lumen value and the rated life in addition to the voltage and wattage as now appear on the bulb.

In the early days following the success of Thomas Edison's electric light experiments, bulb burnouts were no real problem. Edison had conjured the magic of the electric light from a piece of carbonized sewing thread heated without oxygen. 

Undoubtedly the consumer was impressed by the magnitude of the achievement and the availability of this amazing new source of unflickering light. Early light bulbs were costly but were worth the price. Sometimes electric power companies furnished the bulbs as part of the service. More important, power rates were high and bulb efficiency was low and, as will be subsequently explained, these factors made a long-life bulb at the time unrealistic. 

But in the decades that followed Edison's successful experiments in his Menlo Park, N.J., laboratory in October 1879, the incandescent light bulb was improved until it reached a high plateau of perfection. Today, the incandescent bulb has been superseded in certain areas by fluorescent and other more efficient lighting methods. In Federal buildings, well over 95 percent of all areas are now illuminated by means of fluorescent light sources. This includes offices, post office workrooms, conference rooms, courtrooms, corridors, shops and similar areas. 

The incandescent bulb, however, is still used extensively (particularly in nonwork areas) and will continue to be a principal source of light for years to come. 

This being the case, why can't light bulbs be given longer life? Couldn't bulbs be designed to last thousands of hours rather than the 750or 1,000-hour rated life of the standard bulbs generally available today? 69-820 0-66 5 27

These questions have been the subject of increasing discussion in recent years, particularly in regard to standard bulbs versus the so-called extra-service bulbs. The latter type of bulbs are produced on a limited basis for use where bulb replacement constitutes a special problem. They have a rated life of 2,500 hours. Of the $3.8 million for general purpose incandescent bulbs the Federal 

Government purchased in 1963, $558,000 went for extended service bulbs. 

But these extra-service bulbs are not generally available to the individual consumer. In most cases they cannot be purchased at the corner drugstore or supermarket. They must be obtained from a wholesale electric supply store or other outlet serving commercial and industrial users. In recent years, these bulbs have also been offered to the individual consumer as a specialty itema "bargain." Long life is promised or even guaranteed, but at a premium price. 

Although the incandescent light bulb is an inevitable compromise between the level of illumination emitted by the bulb and the life expectancy of the bulb, usually there has been no reference to the fact that a lower illumination level accompanies the extension in the life of the bulb. 

Primarily as a result of these special sale offers, consumer groups, magazines and others have compared the standard and extra-service bulbs. Almost always, the conclusion has been that the consumer gets a better buy when he purchases the standard bulb. The studies which heretofore have been made have not been in depth and in most instances consideration of the question has been limited to a comparison of the standard versus the extra-service bulb, neither of which may present the Government or the average consumer with the best "buy."

THE STANDARD VERSUS THE EXTRA-SERVICE BULB 

The relationship between illumination level and life expectancy At a given wattage, the life of an incandescent bulb is inversely proportional to the eighth power of the bulb's level of illumination. This means that a relatively slight change in the illumination level has a far more significant effect upon the life of the bulb. As an example, a standard 100-watt, 750-hour bulb has an initial lighting level of 1,630 units of illumination (lumens). If this level were reduced approximately 8 percent--an amount few, if any, people would find noticeable the rated life of the bulb would be doubled to 1,500 hours. 

Were the illumination level of the standard 750-hour bulb cut in half to approximately 840 lumens, the life expectancy of the bulb would increase 256 times. 

It is on this basis that the light industry could construct a bulb to last a lifetime. Unfortunately, such a lifetime bulb would be of little value to the consumer as the level of illumination would be practically nil. Similarly older light bulbs "which have been burning in attics for decades" never seem to burn out as compared with more modern, efficient incandescent bulbs of the same wattage which have a shorter life but produce more light. Conversely, a far greater level of illumination could be obtained from a bulb, but at the sacrifice of life expectancy. 

The relationship between the level of illumination and bulb efficiency Instead of decreasing the illumination level to obtain longer life, another approach is possible. The decreased bulb efficiency resulting from increasing the bulb life can be taken into account by maintaining the standard light output but increasing the lamp wattage. Thus, to have an incandescent bulb incorporating both a longer life and the lumen value of the standard bulb, additional current must be used.

Taking these two factors into account, it follows that a comparison of the relative economic efficiency of the standard versus long-life bulbs primarily involves the relationship between the cost of the bulbs and the rate the consumer must pay for electric power.

The 7-to-1 formula 

This relationship between bulb prices and electric power cost has been stated in mathematical terms. It has been demonstrated that the optimum economic balance is achieved when the cost of electric power needed to supply a bulb to "burnout" is equal to seven times the cost of the bulb. When power costs are more than seven times the cost of the bulb, it would be more economical for the consumer to use a shorter life, but more efficient bulb. Conversely, when the cost of power falls below this figure, the consumer can use longer-life bulbs to his economic advantage. This "7-to-1" formula, while an exact and concise computation, does not provide a pronounced ratio. The formula is "sluggish" in that

significant change in the rated life of the bulb and the current used reflect only minor changes in cost. In fact, the ratio can vary from 4-to-1 to as high as 

11-to-1 without registering a significant change in efficiency. It is therefore of little value.

The comparison test 

But a more specific test is available. The overall cost (the price of the power and the bulb) of standard versus extra-service bulbs can be compared for a definite period of operation. On this basis, the factors affecting the relative efficiency of standard and extra-service bulbs become more apparent. 

Using 25 cents as the cost of either type bulb and 2 cents as the applicable power rate for a kilowatt hour, it would cost $2 to burn a standard 750-hour, 100-watt bulb for 1,000 hours. As a third of the rated life of the replacement bulb would also be required to complete the 1,000-hour period, total bulb cost would equal 33 cents for a total cost of operation of $2.33 for the 1,000 hours. 

A 2,500-hour bulb with an initial illumination level the same as the standard bulb (requiring 123 watts) would cost $2.46 for electric power to operate for 1,000 hours. In addition there would be a bulb cost of 10 cents (1,000/2,500 of 25 cents), or a total cost for the 1,000 hours of operation of $2.56, exceeding the 1,000 hour cost of the standard bulb by 23 cents.

In this comparison, the additional power costs needed to bring the long-life bulb up to the level of illumination of the standard bulb thus exceeds the cost the consumer would otherwise incur in replacing the standard bulbs. Therefore, assuming that the consumer could purchase the standard bulbs for 25 cents and that the applicable power rate was 2 cents a kilowatt and that the consumer, actually needed the specified level of illumination of the standard 100-watt bulb it would be to his obvious advantage to use the standard 100-watt bulb rather than a hypothetical 2,500-hour, 123-watt bulb.

But, comparisons of this type and conclusions based thereon can be misleading. 

Actually, all that the above comparison indicates is that the standard bulb in question may be a better buy than the hypothetical extra-service bulb, the wattage of which has been significantly increased so that its illumination level equals that of the standard bulb. The comparison does not prove that all present standard bulbs are a better buy at today's lower power rates than other possible bulbs designed with a greater life expectancy. 

THE FEASIBILITY OF EXTENDING THE RATED LIFE OF THE STANDARD BULBS 

Instead of comparing the present standard bulbs with those with an extended life of 2,500 hours, how would the consumer fare if the standard bulb were designed for a life expectancy of double their present rated lives? Under this approach, the 

10-watt bulb which has a rated life of 1,500 hours would be extended to 3,000 hours. The 15-watt bulb with a rated life of 1,200 hours would be extended to 

2,400 hours. The 25-, 40-, 50-, and 60-watt bulbs which now have rated lives of 

1,000 hours would be extended to 2,000 hours. The 75and 100-watt bulbs with current rated life of 750 hours would be extended to 1,500 hours. 

These more reasonable extensions in the rated lives of these household variety bulbs can be obtained without a substantial reduction in illumination levels. 

As the rated life is inversely proportional to the eighth power of the illumination level, the life of these bulbs can be doubled by a reduction in their illumination levels of approximately 8 percent. It is, of course, possible that a series of bulbs with the illumination level reduced by this small percentage might be wholly responsive to consumer needs. However, for the purposes of this discussion we will assume that the present lumen values of the standard bulbs are essential. 

Therefore, having lowered the lumen values of the various standard bulbs to double their rated lives, the wattages will be increased sufficiently to afford the new series of bulbs the initial lumen levels of the standard bulbs. We will then compare the cost of the additional current used by the longer life bulbs to the bulb replacement cost incident to use of the standard bulbs. 

Applicable electric power rates

A proper comparison of electric power and bulb replacement costs requires realistic information as to what the consumer would pay for the additional electric power used by the longer life bulbs to provide the same initial lumen value as the standard bulb. Consumers are generally charged a basic rate for power, and lesser amounts for additional units as consumption increases. Therefore, the applicable power rate in our comparison should logically be the rate at which the consumer would pay for additional power purchased rather than what

he pays as a basic or initial rate-in other words, the marginal or incremental power rate.

The Government is a large user of electric power. Therefore, the incremental rate paid for additional power needed for the higher wattage, but longer life, bulbs would almost always be applicable. In the case of individual consumers, this may not be the case, as some may not utilize sufficient power for the lowest incremental rates to apply. Neither power rates nor the method of computing them are uniform throughout the Nation. For this reason, an arithmetically exact figure cannot be used in our comparison. 

The incremental rate applicable to Government usage The Transportation and Communications Service of GSA in a study prepared for the subcommittee based upon the evaluation of selected data indicates that the incremental cost per kilowatt-hour when at least 350 kilowatts per month are used is 1.436 cents. When at least 400 kilowatt-hours per month are used the average cost per kilowatt-hour would be 1.414 cents. Therefore, for purposes of our comparison, we will use a rate of 1.5 cents per kilowatt-hour. 

Federal bulb costs

Because of volume purchases of incandescent bulbs, the GSA obtains a significant discount. Under present GSA contracts, standard bulbs from 25 to 

100 watts, with a list price of 25 cents, are obtained by the Government at 13 cents in orders of $300 or more; at 14 cents in orders from $25 to $300, and at 

15 cents in orders of less than $25. On the basis of these statistics, we will use a price of 15 cents for our comparison.

The Government would benefit from longer life bulbs Resort to the same computations as used earlier in comparing the standard versus the extra-service bulb indicates an obvious economic advantage to the Government if the life of the standard bulb were doubled. As indicated in the enclosed shedules, the comparative cost of operating all of the smaller bulbs from 10 to 50 watts for 1,000 hours indicates an advantage of the longer-life bulb. 

Furthermore, the cost differences in the case of the 60-, 75-, and 100-watt bulbs are minimal. These figures suggest that insofar as Government usage is concerned, doubling the life of the standard bulb would be doubly advantageous in the case of most of these bulb sizes. First, there would be an actual economic saving in the lighting cost in the case of the smaller watt bulbs. Second, and more important, doubling the life of these standard bulbs would be accompanied by a significant reduction in the cost of bulb replacement a cost generally conceded in Government to be in excess of the cost of the bulb intself. 

It would be of interest to make a comparable comparison as to the effect doubling the life of the standard bulbs would have on the average individual consumer. 

The incremental power rate applicable to the individual consumer In this instance, the subcommittee does not have authoritative information as to the incremental power rate the individual consumer would pay. However, it is known that electric power costs have declined considerably in the half century since bulb manufacturers originally established the rated lives of the standard bulbs in the 1,000-hour area. According to the Edison Electric Institute Bulletin, the average revenue per kilowatt-hour from residential consumers in 1913 was 

8.7 cents. This figure had dropped to 2.37 cents in 1963.

Also, according to Federal Power Commission statistics, the average typical residential electric power bills in 1962 for users of 500 kilowatt-hours averaged out at about 2.1 cents per kilowatt-hour. The incremental rate, of course, would be somewhat lower. In the District of Columbia, the average residential power rate for users of 500 kilowatt-hours per month in 1962 was approximately 2.3 cents. The rate charged in the District of Columbia for power in excess of 300 kilowatt-hours per month is 1.8 cents.

Therefore, while we do not have authoritative information in the greatest exactitude as to the incremental rate applicable to the average consumer, we do know that in many areas of the Nation this rate would fall below 2 cents a kilowatthour. In other areas, the rate would be in the 2-cent area. In some areas, the marginal rate may increase to as high as 2.5 cents. In isolated areas the rate may be higher. Therefore, two comparisons will be made one at a rate of 2 cents, and another at a rate of 2.5 cents.

Individual consumer bulb costs

Turning next to the cost of bulbs, the subcommittee will accept the list price of 25 cents apiece suggested by the manufacturers. This, of course, does not include taxes.

MANY INDIVIDUAL CONSUMERS COULD BE BENEFITED BY EXTENDING THE LIFE OF THE STANDARD BULBS-PARTICULARLY THOSE OF THE LOWER WATTAGES 

At the 2-cent rate, doubling the life expectancy of most of the standard bulbs would be to the obvious economic advantage of the consumer. Only in the case of the 100-watt bulb does the operating cost for 1,000 hours of the doubled life bulb exceed that of the standard bulb, and in this instance only by a minimal amount. 

At the 2.5 cents rate, the advantage of doubling the life of the standard bulb would be limited to those in the 10to 50-watt area. But, the differences in operational costs are minimal with regard to the 60and 75-watt bulbs, and only at the 100watt level does the bulb with the extended life cost noticeably more to operate- Bulb replacement costs of the average consumer are too nebulous a factor to consider.

The need for further evaluations These comparisons should be augmented without delay with authoritative evaluations by the GSA procurement specialists working in conjunction with the scientists at the National Bureau of Standards. It is possible that in the higher wattage bulb such studies, taking into consideration every possible factor affecting the balance between life and illumination levels, might indicate some basis for a continuation of the present rated lives. It is difficult, however, to imagine such a situation with the smaller bulb sizes. Even at an electric power rate of 

3 cents per kilowatt-hour, doubling the life of the 10through 50-watt bulbs would be advantageous to the individual consumer, for example. 

The rated lives of the standard bulb were established in most instances a half century ago when power rates were excessively high and bulb efficiency was low. 

Arthur A. Bright, in an authoritative study, "The Electric-Lamp Industry," in 

1949 suggested that these factors saved the bulb manufacturing industry from a sweeping and unqualified condemnation. But today power rates are substantially below what they were in 1913 and bulb efficiency is substantially higher. The rated lives of the bulbs, however, remain about the same. The 100-watt bulb, for example, had a rated life of 800 hours in 1908. This was increased to 1,000 hours in 1910. On April Fool's Day 1933 the industry reduced the rated life of the 100-watt bulb to 750 hours where it remains, to the knowledge of this subcommittee. The history of the rated lives of the other wattage bulbs follow somewhat the same pattern although marginal increases have been made in some instances. 

Arthur A. Bright, in his 1949 book, after justifiably defending the rated lives the manufacturers established during the early days of the incandescent light bulb, added the following comments: "Nevertheless, the emphasis which General Electric has placed on lamp efficiency at the expense of lamp life has in many instances led consumers to forget the importance of overall installed efficiency. Where lampshades or globes frequently reduce lamp efficiency by 25 percent or more, a 1or 2-percent increase in efficiency may be of less consumer benefit than the equivalent lengthening of lamp life. Greater public knowledge about the efficient use of lamps would benefit most residential users, and even many institutional, industrial, and commercial users. It has been impossible for consumers to prevail on General Electric to add long-lived lamps to its standard line so that the purchaser may select for himself the type he wants. This insistence by the industry leader that it knew best what its customers needed has in individual instances proved costly to them, for what General Electric made standard was virtually forced on 90 percent of the domestic market. Consumers have not had the freedom of choice with respect to burning hours to which they would seem to be entitled."

To the knowledge of this subcommittee, there has been no significant improvement in the 15 years since Mr. Bright made this observation. Furthermore, even the most sophisticated consumers, including Federal agencies, will continue to be hampered in the selection of proper bulbs by the lack of information as to lumen value and rated life on the bulb itself. This information could easily be added to the wattage and voltage imprinted on the end of the bulb and will give those who are interested in obtaining the optimum lighting at the most economical cost some reliable means of expeditiously determining their bulb requirements. 

During the past several months, the General Services Administration and the National Bureau of Standards have cooperated with the subcommittee in these studies. It is recommended that these efforts be continued along the lines suggested in this memorandum with the view of furnishing the subcommittee as soon as practicable with authoritative evaluations of the possible benefits of Federal procurement of incandescent bulbs with the longer rated lives.

APPENDIX D LETTER FROM SUBCOMMITTEE REQUESTING COMMENTS OF BULB MANUFACTURERS GOVERNMENT ACTIVITIES SUBCOMMITTEE, HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES, 

Washington, D.C., December 8, 1965. 

Mr. D. C. Burnham, President, Westinghouse Electric Corp., Pittsburgh, Pa. 

DEAR MR. BURNHAM: On June 24, 1964, this subcommittee released a preliminary study demonstrating that light bulb manufacturers could substantially increase the life of many household variety electric light bulbs with savings to the Federal Government and individual consumers generally. 

In this report it was pointed out that the life expectancy of the household variety bulb remains about the same as it was before World War I when the bulb industry was in its infancy and Thomas Edison was still an active, vigorous man. The report points out that the 100-watt bulb, for example, had a designed life expectancy of 800 hours in 1908; that this was increased by the manufacturers to 1,000 hours in 1910; but that, on April 1, 1933, the industry reduced the designed or rated life of this popular-sized bulb to 750 hours where it apparently remains today.

Burned-out, short-life light bulbs annoy the average consumer as well as cost him money. They also constitute a definite accident hazard in the home. To the Federal Government, with an annual bulb bill of more than $3 million and replacement labor costs estimated in excess of the cost of the bulbs themselves, a large expenditure in tax funds is involved. 

Following the release of this preliminary study, a copy of which is enclosed, the subcommittee has completed a tentative evaluation of other closely related technical and economic factors and is prepared at this time to move forward to the ultimate disposition of the matter. Before taking further action, it is our consensus that the principal members of the bulb-manufacturing industry be given an opportunity to submit their views. Any comments on any facet of this problem which you consider pertinent or of possible interest to the subcommittee would be deeply appreciated. However, our primary interest is to have the benefit of your views on two subjects: First, the technical feasibility of substantially expanding the life of household variety bulbs as demonstrated in the enclosed report; and, second, the feasibility of including information on the individual bulbs as to the rated or designed life and initial lumen value. 

The subcommittee will consider any comments or recommendations your company might wish to make and looks forward to your cooperation in the further evaluation of the light bulb problem. Meanwhile, it is our hope that the individual bulb manufacturers motivated by the public's interest and the spirit of free competition, will make bulbs of the most efficient design available to the consumer, making any further action by this subcommittee unnecessary. Sincerely, Enclosure.

For example, if media advertising directed to the residential market alluded to a bulb having a life of 5,000 hours, the copy should also include reference to the fact that this bulb will have less lumen output and/or have a higher power cost of operation. 

There was much discussion at the hearing of the advisability of issuance of a trade regulation rule requiring the marking of lumen output and life on light bulbs

or their packages. Sylvania has considered doing this on its A-line bulb package, but has chosen not to do so for various reasons including the following: 

1. Lumen and life data which might be marked on the bulb package would of necessity be average laboratory figures, not necessarily the precise lumen output or life attained by the particular bulb in the package. 

2. Consumers may be misled by such information into thinking that every bulb will have a life at least equal to that marked on the package, whereas the average life is the principal factor. Customer criticism and ill will could result if a bulb failed precisely to meet the printed ratings. 

3. Numerous external factors beyond the control of the manufacturer can have an important effect on lumen output and bulb life. Some of these factors are ambient temperature of the fixture, applied voltage, vibration or shock to which the bulb is subjected, etc. 

4. Printing of technical rating information on the package might serve to confuse the average consumer, who would not generally be able to make meaningful use of such data. This would occur as a result of there generally being only one brand of bulb in each retail store, a consequence of there usually being little consumer brand preference. A reasonably knowledgeable housewife would thus not be in the same position to compare technical bulb data as she is to compare volume and price of various brands of food products, all of which may be reposing on the shelf before her. The average consumer would certainly not remember technical data in order to compare one brand of bulb in one store with another brand in a different store. This becomes even more clear when it is remembered that the average consumer spends an average of only 25 to 30 cents a month for incandescent bulbs. 

5. Printing of the rating information on the package would result in certain additional costs to the manufacturer which might have to be passed on, in whole or in part, to the consumer.

It is for these reasons we feel that promulgation of a trade regulation rule requiring labeling of lumen and life data would be inappropriate. We believe, however, that should the Commission decide to issue such a rule, it should be limited to requiring such data solely on the packages of nonstandard bulbs intended for residential use with wattages ranging from 15 through 150. We sincerely hope that any such rule which might be adopted by the Commission will not become effective for at least 1 year following the date of issuance so that bulb manufacturers will have an opportunity to dispose of most of their current packaging and material inventory. We also trust that in the event the Commission wishes to promulgate a rule, that another hearing will be held so that the industry and other interested parties will have an opportunity to discuss the specific rule proposed.

General Comments Concerning Preliminary Study, Issued June 24, 1964, OF GOVERNMENT ACTIVITIES SUBCOMMITTEE, GOVERNMENT OPERATIONS COM- MITTEE, U.S. HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES, JACK BROOKS, CHAIRMAN, ON INCANDESCENT ELECTRIC LIGHT BULBS Preface to comments 

The above study, as its title indicates, is preliminary in character and does not state definitive recommendations or proposals as to the design of a standard line of lamps. It recommends further studies of the problem. In a few instances the study notes that lamps could be designed to produce longer life by reduction of present lumen values, but it does not advocate such reduction. Its major analysis is made upon the basis of a theoretical increase of about 8 percent in the design wattages of each type of general service lamp* of 15 through 100 watts.

This is intended to compensate for the reduction in efficiency occasioned by increasing life to twice the present design level. The resulting cost of light was calculated at varying power rates assumed to be applicable and at a constant market price for the lamps. (For Federal Government purchases the calculations are at an assumed power rate of 1.5 cents per kilowatt hour and a lamp price of 

15 cents; for the household buyer the calculations use a 25-cent purchase price and assumed power rates of 2 and 2.5 cents per kilowatt hour.)

Perhaps the major conclusion of the preliminary study is that there should be further "authoritative evaluations by GSA procurement specialists working in conjunction with scientists at the National Bureau of Standards." However, it is the tentative conclusion of the study that such evaluations might result in a finding that there is an appropriate basis for continuing the present rated lives of household type lamps of 60, 75, and 100 watts. On the other hand, it expresses the strong tentative opinion that doubling the life of the lower wattage bulbs would be advantageous to the individual consumer at an electric power rate of 3 cents per kilowatt hour.

Mr. Baynard of the subcommittee staff has advised that the subcommittee has developed no additional data or information that should be considered in connec- *The 10-watt lamp is not marketed as a general service lamp for household use. Its chief use is as a sign lamp.

tion with this matter. Mr. Brooks' letter of January 3, 1966, indicates that the subsequent tentative evaluations made by the subcommittee relate to "a series of increasingly larger bulbs of varying lumen values so as to offer the consumer the maximum and most efficient selection of bulbs to fill his own unique requirements." 

It thus appears that the further evaluations recommended in the preliminary study have not been made. Accordingly, in the absence of definite or precise recommendations, the following comments generally relate to the principles underlying or implicit in the preliminary study and the tentative conclusions expressed in it.

It is of course gratifying to note that the study, even while using a method of analysis different from that which we use, tentatively recognizes the validity of the design of our lamps of 60 watts and above. Its particular emphasis upon the advantages to be gained from extending lamp life, especially as to household usage, appears to be primarily upon lower wattage lamps which constitute a relatively small minority of the total household lamps sold. As indicated below, there are significant differences in the factors considered in determining economic life in the study and the factors we have long used. The tentative findings of the study with respect to the large majority of household lamps sold by us are, of course, welcome. It is unfortunate, however, that the opening paragraphs of the report and its title do not more clearly indicate that on the tentative analysis developed to date, the primary concern of the subcommittee is with respect to a relatively small minority of household lamps sold rather than to all of them. 

While it is technically feasible to manufacture lamps of practically any design, the preliminary study does not indicate the design life which the subcommittee believes would be the optimum for that minority of our lamps, the design of which is especially questioned in the report. (The calculations it sets out appear to be for illustrative purposes rather than tentative recommendations.) More over, it is difficult to speculate what might be the ultimate recommendation of the subcommittee following further study, in light of the premise stated on page 5 of the report. This is that design life should be calculated on the basis of the applicable power rate and should be related to "the rate at which the consumer would pay for additional power purchased rather than what he pays for an initial rate in other words, the marginal or incremental power rate. 

As the study states, rates vary widely and there was no indication as to the number of families whose power usage does not qualify them for low incremental rates. Without having a more accurate, understanding of the economic impact of a radical design revision upon our various types of customers, we should be hesitant to undertake a program which would concededly increase electrical bills of homeowners. While this effect might be minimized by designing our lamps for less light, the subcommittee does not propose this and customer purchasing habits conclusively show that homeowners desire more and not less light. Not only have we increased the efficiency of our lamps over the years but the trends reflecting customer desires have been clearly toward higher wattage lamps and the additional light which they provide.

Any substantial change in design to increase life at present light output levels through increasing electric power usage would be felt most keenly by those whose usage of electric power would not qualify them for low incremental power rates. Most of these are undoubtedly low income customers. (An analysis of electric usage patterns by customers shows that many minimum bill customers pay as much as 20 cents per kilowatt hour-or nearly nine times the average cost per kilowatt hour.) At the same time, the major beneficiaries of such redesigning would be, of course, the public utilities whose powerload would necessarily be substantially increased. A rough estimate of the increased annual cost of the additional residential power required for lighting lamps with the higher wattages used for comparison in the study is approximately $100 million annually. 

In considering the cost of electricity, especially for lower income users, we think it necessary to recognize that the low usage residential customer who uses power primarily for lighting, and who is using today the same amount of power he used 10 to 15 years ago, is paying more rather than less for such power. The fact that the average cost per kilowatt hour has been decreasing is a reflection of the increasing use of power and not the reduction in power rates, as such. The decrease in the average rate results from the increased consumption of customers who have a large proportion of their power devoted to applicances such as electric water heaters, ranges, clothes dryers, and similar high usage devices. For these reasons we doubt the validity of the premise that lamps should be designed for longer life to the extent justified by assuming that the resulting added cost of electricity will be paid for by all customers at low incremental rates.

On the other hand, the study itself appears to recognize that there are, in fact, other factors than lamp and power costs which should be considered in a precise calculation of economic life design. On page 7 of the study, in connection with the recommendation that further authoritative evaluations should be made, reference is also made to "taking into consideration every possible factor affecting the balance between life and illumination levels." Since it is not exactly clear what factors other than lamp and power costs are here referred to, it is impossible to determine the extent to which our approach varies from that which might be considered appropriate in the recommended further evaluations. 

In analyzing the cost of obtaining light, reasonable differences might arise as to the precise cost allocations to be made for wiring, switches, fixtures, etc. and their rate of amortization. However, we do not believe it appropriate to ignore such costs in the overall appraisal of the cost paid by a user for obtaining light. 

If it were sound analysis of the cost of obtaining light to ignore all but electric power and lamp costs, fluorescent light in the home would be obviously and substantially the least expensive source of home light. Yet, much as we have encouraged and promoted the use of fluorescent lamps in the home with their obvious long life and low operating cost advantages-we have not been successful in persuading the average homeowner to make the necessary investment in facilities to obtain these advantages. Thus, it seems clear that the homeowner does recognize the importance of investment costs in making his own calculations of the cost of light. Like the customer, we believe that in making comparisons of the cost of light these other necessary parts of a total lighting system must be evaluated.

The life-efficiency characteristic of tungsten-filament incandescent lamps is well-established.1-7 It is widely known that when a lamp filament operates at high efficiency, its light output is high in relation to the watts consumed, but its life is short. 

Conversely, when a filament operates at low efficiency, its light output is low in relation to the watts consumed, and its life is long. 

Hence, a design objective of long life, which means best value in most products, does not apply to incandescent lamps. The reason for this difference is that lamp bulbs are not, of themselves, the end-product sought by the user. That end-product is light. The lamp bulb is simply a device to transform electric energy into light. To obtain that light, the user pays the costs of facilities for operation and of maintenance, the cost of electric power, and the cost of the bulb itself. 

The total of these costs per unit of light flux generated is thus the primary measure of the value of a lamp bulb. Of these costs the price of the bulb itself is only a small part. Yet the amount of light produced in relation to the total lighting cost is determined by the design of the lamp bulb-by its life-efficiency relationship.

The total cost of a unit quantity of light-one million lumen-hours-may be calculated readily by using the cost-of-light equation. The various quantities in this equation have values that depend on and apply to the specific lamp bulb and field of lighting for which the cost-of-light determination is being made-in this case, the residential lighting field. The Non-Engineering Factors 

In addition to lowest cost of light, other factors of a non-engineering nature may influence the design lives of these lamps for residential service. 

One example is the inconvenience of making lamp replacements. This inconvenience is over and above the handling charge for lamp replacement and fixture cleaning, which is included in the cost-of-light calculation. It is probably safe to say that most people would be dissatisfied with a design of lamp requiring frequent replacement, even though they were aware that such a lamp design would give them the most light for their money

Another consideration is accessibility and its effect on cost and convenience of lamp replacement. 

Some bulbs are easy to reach, as in floor or table lamps. Others are more difficult to reach; for example, some are in ceiling sockets, or in enclosed fixtures which require some disassembly. Even though in the typical residence there may be relatively few such lamp bulbs that are not readily accessible, the difficulties in these cases may tend to create an erroneous impression that most lamp bulbs are hard to replace. 

Another factor is the trend toward more and more lamp bulbs per home, often accompanied by a trend toward longer average burning hours. The resulting increase in frequency of lamp replacements throughout the home as a whole may convey the impression that "lamp bulbs don't last as long as they used to." Actually, the authors know of no reductions in life ratings of general lighting lamps for more than 25 years.

Still another factor is that certain lamp bulbs in a house are used more hours than the average, and hence burn out in fewer days. As a result, the impression may again be that most of the lamp bulbs in the home must be replaced frequently. 

And still another factor is that any increase there may be in the voltage at the sockets will cause filament lamps to burn out at a faster rate. Evidence of increasing standardization on 120 volts appears in a recent report of an independent market survey service. Such standardization helps greatly to insure that lamp bulbs purchased by householders are of voltage rating correctly matching the socket voltage.

It is the judgment of the authors that these various non-engineering factors are taken reasonably into account at this time in the following "nonengineering lamp design parameters": (1) In each wattage, average lamp life ought to be in the neighborhood of a year or longer, regardless of any cost-of-light advantage for users from shorter lamp life. (2) No lamp lives ought to be shorter than those now listed and to which users are accustomed, regardless of any cost-of-light advantage which would accrue from shorter lamp life. 

These non-engineering lamp design parameters modify the values of lamp lives giving lowest cost of light, in Table I, as indicated in Table II. 

Summary 

It is the responsibility of the lamp design engineer to build into the product the best over-all value for the average conditions of use. This necessitates providing a proper balance between light output, wattage consumption and lamp life. A proper balance is particularly important in the case of lamps for residential lighting, because retailers usually can justify allocation of space for only a few lamp designs.

Longer life of filament lamps can be achieved only by reducing light output at the same wattage, or by increasing wattage to maintain the same light output.

The lives of today's general-service filament lamps are considerably in excess of those which would give the lowest cost of light under the aver age conditions of residential service. This is particularly true for the sizes of 50 watts and above that provide most of the light used in residential applications. This extra life, entailing lower efficiency, now results in an extra expense to the American householder of some 30 million dollars a year. 

Longer lives would increase this extra expense, for which no light would be received. On the other hand, reducing lamp lives to the point where they would deliver best economy would mean more frequent replacements, with attendant inconvenience.

APPENDIX C-LAMP BULB REPLACEMENT OR HANDLING CHARGE 

There is seldom an actual expenditure in payment for lamp replacements and fixture cleanings in residences. Nearly always these things are done, not by a maid or other hired help, but by some member of the household. Although this involves no cash out-of-pocket, there is a cost in time and effort, and economic calculations should include a "handling charge" to reflect the time involved at an appropriate hourly rate.

For this article determination of an up-to-date charge was made in four steps: (1) Set-up of three classifications of residence lighting fixtures from the standpoint of accessibility of the lampseasy, moderate, or difficult to reach. (2) For each lamp wattage, apportionment of presently. used residence fixtures into these three classifications. (3) Measurements of handling times for lamp replacements and fixture cleanings, covering a cross-section of resi dence lighting fixtures in service in the three classifications. (4) Determination of a proper hourly rate to convert the handling times to handling charges.

APPENDIX E-EFFECTS OF CHANGES IN VARIABLES ON UNIT COST AND ECONOMIC LIFE 

The direction in which the variables of cost factors and lamp characteristics affect cost of light and economic life of lamps is often of interest. 

The effect of each variable on unit cost is apparent from the equation. Increases in mean lumens, average maintenance factors, lamp life and annual burning hours tend to decrease the unit cost per million lumen-hours. Decreases in the other quantities-price and handling charges, watts. energy rate and annual fixed and maintenance chargesalso decrease the unit cost of light. 

The changes in economic life due to changes in these variables are less apparent. Economic life increases as lamp price, handling charge and annual operating hours increase. Also, decreases in wattage, energy rate and annual charges have the same effect. With respect to economic life the absolute value of mean lumens has no significance, but the relationship between effective mean lumens at different hypothetical lives is pertinent. For example, an unusual reduction of mean lumens or in dirt maintenance factor for a hypothetical longer life would in itself have the effect of indicating a shorter life because unit cost would tend to rise more rapidly at the longer life values.

STATEMENT BEFORE FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION BY DONALD D. SCARFF, GENERAL MANAGER, LAMP DIVISION, GENERAL ELECTric Co. 

Gentlemen, my name is Donald D. Scarff, and I am general manager of the lamp division of the General Electric Co., located at Nela Park in Cleveland, Ohio. 

The General Electric Co. has been manufacturing and marketing incandescent lamps, or light bulbs, for over 80 years. Quite naturally, then, we are very much interested in the subject of these hearings: the marking, labeling, and advertising of incandescent light bulbs. We welcome the opportunity to appear here. 

Almost everybody uses light bulbs. This seems to make the subject of light bulb design and performance one of universal interest. Discussions of the subject have appeared not only in technical publications but in many consumer periodicals. We have found from our own experience that the subject of lamp performance is extremely complex and one that is not easily understood, particularly by those who may not work with it day to day. This hearing provides another opportunity to further acquaint the public with lamp design, performance, and economics.

At the request of the Commission, our representatives have met with members of your staff on several occasions during your preliminary investigation to assist vou in assessing some of the more technical aspects of the subject. 

In connection with these prior meetings with members of the staff of this Commission, we submitted a number of documents. For your convenience we have assembled these in a notebook and I will leave them with you so that they may be made a part of the record in this proceeding. 

The notice of the initiation of the present proceeding states that it relates to certain practices in the marketing of "long-life," "extended service," and "standard" incandescent electric light bulbs. I think it might be well to spend a few moments to indicate how we in General Electric use these terms. 

We use the term "standard" in referring to our customary, regular householdtype bulb which we have been marketing for over a generation. This is what we term the A-line 15through 150-watt inside-frost incandescent lamp. Design life ratings on these lamps range from 750 to 1,200 hours, depending on wattage. 

We also use the term "standard" to distinguish these lamps from our newer line of soft white lamps. The newer line is essentially identical to the inside-frost type except for the distinctive white coating which reduces glare. I will leave with you the wrappers and sleeves we use for both of these types. 

We use the term "extended service" to describe our line of 2,500-hour incandescent lamps ranging from 25 to 1,000 watts. These are described in a publication which is included as item 8 of the documents to be submitted. I will also leave with you the sleeves we use for these lamps. You will note that the descriptive material on the packages states that, quote: "Extended service lamps produce 10 to 25 percent less light (depending on the specific type) than our corresponding standard lamp. They are designed for use only where replacement is costly or where the power cost is exceptionally low. For most applications, the General Electric standard lamp will provide more economical lighting.' end quote. These lamps are of inherently lower efficiency than our standard lamps and are particularly suited for use in commercial or industrial establishments where labor cost for lamp replacement is exceptionally high, where energy rates are unusually low, and where sockets are hard to get at. 

We in General Electric do not use the term "long life" in the marketing of our A-line incandescent lamps. The term is used by some manufacturers or marketers to describe their line of household-type lamps in the range of 15 to 150 watts. 

Published representations range anywhere from "one-third longer life," to "5,000hour lamps" to "5-year bulbs" to the extreme of "guaranteed forever."

Before expressing an opinion as to the need for the promulgation of a trade regulation rule, I believe it is important to set forth certain basic scientific facts relative to the design and operation of an incandescent lamp. To do so, I will be using a number of terms with which all of you may not be familiar but which are explained in an appendix to this statement. 

The function of a light bulb is to produce light. In an incandescent bulb light is produced by passing sufficient electricity through the tungsten filament to cause it to incandesce. In an incandescent lamp there is an inverse relationship between the efficiency of the lamp (the amount of light produced in relation to the wattage consumed) and its life to burnout. Basically, the hotter the filament, the more light it gives per unit of energy, but the shorter its life. Conversely, the cooler the filament, the less light it gives per unit of energy, but the longer the life to burnout. You can't have both longest life and highest efficiency from the same incandescent lamp.

The light bulb is only the means by which electrical energy is converted to light the end product for which the customer buys the bulb. General Electric designs its incandescent lamps generally to render to the customer the most efficient light source based on overall lighting economics as modified by special conditions of use. For example, where lamp replacement is difficult, time consuming, and costly, as in traffic signals, signs, and street lights, a longer life at a corresponding loss in efficiency is desirable. Conversely, where high concentrations of light of particular color or spectral characteristics are required, as in picture taking, a higher light output at a corresponding loss in life is desirable. 

The basic engineering factors involved in establishing design life have long been known and have been set forth most recently in an article entitled, “In candescent Lamp Design Life for Residential Lighting." This article was published in the December 1959 issue of Illuminating Engineering and is included in the material I mentioned earlier.

Application of the formula explained in this technical article is the basis for the design lives of the lamps we sell to the householder. These same lamps are also used to some extent in commercial installations for general lighting purposes. 

For the broad residential market, which is the principal point of focus for this proceeding, General Electric designs and manufactures incandescent lamps so as to render to the customer the lowest cost of light consistent with an acceptable level of convenience for frequency of bulb changing. 

To illustrate the significance of the cost of light, I would like first to define the elements in this cost and then to apply these elements in an example. To get light from an incandescent source, you must have a bulb, you must have electricity to operate the bulb, and you must have wires, socket, fixture, and switches, to complete the system. Each has a cost which is reflected in the cost of light, which is usually expressed in dollars per million lumen-hours. 

As an example, our standard 100-watt inside-frosted incandescent bulb is designed to burn an average of 750 hours. Our consumer studies indicate that with this life rating the bulb has an average in-service life of 14 months. The initial design light output of this bulb is 1,750 lumens and its average lumen output over its 750-hour life is 1,640. It is priced at retail at 25 cents. The power to operate this bulb over its 750-hour design life would, at national average residential power rates, cost about $1.75, seven times the cost of the bulb. 

On this very simplified basis which considers only bulb and power cost, and neglecting any other associated costs, the cost of light will be $1.63 per million lumen hours. 

Assuming a 10,000-hour, 100-watt incandescent lamp of the most advanced design available and selling on today's market at a price of about 69 cents, the power to operate this bulb, again at the same national average residential power rates, would approximate $23 over the 10,000-hour life. Its light output is, of course, much less than that of our standard lamp, initially 1,240 lumens instead of 1,750 with an average of 1,130 lumens over its life. The net result is that the cost of light becomes $2.10 per million lumen hours, or almost 30 percent higher than for our standard lamp. Even if the price of the 10,000-hour lamp were only 25 cents, the cost of light would still be $2.05 per million lumen hours or about 26 percent greater than for our standard lamp.

This example is a very simplified version of the calculation of the cost of light. 

A method of calculation which took into account all of the costs of a lighting system would show an even greater cost differential. 

We have recognized the need for the public to be informed on this subject. 

We have attempted to do this as demonstrated by an ad showing the balance between life and light which I will place in the record and by the distribution of well over 100,000 copies of our booklet entitled, "How Long Should a Light Bulb

Last?" which is included in the book of documents to be submitted. 

Our experience with these publications has indicated to us the great difficulty in explaining this technical story to the public. It has also convinced us that any more complex statements than these would only serve to confuse rather than clarify.

We are glad to note that an increasing number of consumer-oriented articles are appearing in newspapers and magazines which carry the facts of lamp life to the householder. Several representative articles are included as item 7 of the documents we will submit.

Now turning from this general background, we will present out thoughts with respect to a possible trade regulation rule involving incandescent lamps. 

The staff of this commission has already completed a preliminary study of this question. The fact that no proposed rule has been issued may indicate that the commission has found, as have we, that the variables affecting lamp life, light

output, and the cost of light are so many and complex as to make it very difficult to attempt to promulgate a simple and workable rule which would be genuinely helpful to the consumer in his purchase of incandescent lamps. 

The notice of this rulemaking proceeding indicates that, as a result of its preliminary study, the commission is considering whether the failure to disclose the following on markings, packaging, labeling or advertising of light bulbs may constitute unfair or deceptive acts or practices: (1) Lumen or light output; (2) Wattage; (3) Hours of useful life.

We do not believe that requiring the disclosure of design life or design lumens would be sufficiently meaningful, useful, and helpful to consumers so as to warrant the time, effort, and cost to the Commission and to manufacturers of both devising simple and workable rules and then uniformly enforcing them. 

It must be understood that, while life and lumens are expressed in numerical ratings, they lack the precision of "an ounce," "an inch," or "a pint." This is because the values given are expressions of the average performance obtained from thousands of tests made from the month-after-month production of that particular lamp type. Indeed, life ratings can be established only by destructive testing or burning out the lamp to find how long it lasted. Consequently, only as a matter of statistical chance will the life rating on a package describe accurately the specific bulb in that package, and that only under test conditions. 

Moreover, it is difficult to see how the Commission could enforce a requirement of disclosure as to design life without spelling out in meticulous detail the elaborate and costly statistical and testing procedures all manufacturers would be required to follow in determining such design life. 

Essentially, the same situation prevails with respect to lumens. Leaving aside the fact that the average consumer is not familiar with the term, "lumens" will vary from many of the same reasons which cause variations in life. Indeed, the science of photometry (the testing for light output) is even more complex than that of life testing. Approved photometric methods are described in parts I and II of an article entitled Photometry which I will leave with you for the record.

So far as wattage is concerned, I believe all manufacturers now identify the wattage of the lamp. That is, of course, the basis on which the customer now purchases the product.

From the foregoing we have concluded that a simple, workable rule requiring disclosure of life and lumen ratings is not feasible or in the interest of consumers. 

We believe that wattage representations are already made and that no action by the commission is required.

Of equal significance, the conditions under which a bulb is used can and do have a substantial and direct effect on lamp life and lumen output. These conditions include the average voltage at which the lamp is burned, the ambient temperature of the fixture, the degree of vibration of the lamp and fixture, whether or not the lamp is bumped or dropped while operating so as to cause hot shock to the tungsten filament and they are all totally outside the control of the lamp manufacturer. So that even if a lamp, when it leaves the manufacturer, is a perfectly rated lamp so far as life and lumen output is concerned, any one of a number of external variables will be introduced when the lamp is lighted which will materially affect the actual lamp life or lumen output. Required disclosure of life and lumen output, therefore, would inevitably result in the customer obtaining either less or more than he was led to expect by the disclosure. 

There could, of course, be occasions where a manufacturer may wish to compare two or more of his own lamps and in this case it may be both desirable and meaningful for the manufacturer to make comparative life and lumen ratings on the package and in the advertising. The applicability is limited here, of course, to one manufacturer comparing his own products using the same yardsticks as the basis for his performance data. 

The notice of this rulemaking proceeding indicates further that the employment of deceptive claims of savings through the use of certain light bulbs may also constitute an unfair method of competition and an unfair or deceptive act or practice. 

We are, of course, aware of so-called "long life" lamps which are being held out to the consumer as providing longer life than "standard" incandescent lamps at a cost saving to the user. 

I would like to suggest that to the extent the Commission concludes that the advertising and promotion of so-called "long life" lamps constitutes an unfair or

deceptive practice, that the Commission proceed against manufacturers and marketers employing such advertising and promotion under its normal procedures. 

However, should the commission decide that a rule is necessary or desirable, I would then suggest that any rule be limited first to the A-line or 15to 150watt incandescent types and secondly, to be applicable only to those products within this line which the manufacturer or marketer represents as being different in performance from "common," "ordinary," "regular, or "standard" lamps. 

Where such claims of performance are in the form of claims for longer life, we would suggest that any such claim be accompanied by a counterstatement that such longer life can only be obtained at a sacrifice in light output. The phrase "longer life-less light" might be an appropriate requirement. 

In the case of claims associated with longer life which also imply a cost savings through the use of such bulbs and which the commission concludes constitutes a deceptive practice, we again suggest that the normal procedures of the commission are adequate. But, if the commission decides that a rule is necessary, such a rule might provide that any claim for bulb cost savings can only be obtained at a sacrifice in light output and under average conditions, a disproportionately higher increase in the cost of electricity to achieve equal light output. 

To end where we began, this is a complex subject. I appreciate fully that, at best, my presentation is not a complete treatment of the subject. I want to assure you that our people will continue to cooperate with the commission staff. 

I will, of course, be glad to answer any questions I can, either now or in writing by July 14. 

Thank you, gentlemen.

SUPPLEMENT TO STATEMENT MADE BY D. D. SCARFF, GENERAL ELECTRIC co. 

Definition of electrical and lamp terms used in this discussion 1. Volts or voltage. The volt is the unit of potential difference between two points. When applied to incandescent lamps it is the potential difference or electrical pressure which produces a flow of electrical current through the filament. 

The operation of an incandescent lamp is critically affected by the voltage which is impressed upon it. Since practically all electric power companies in the United 

States supply a nominal 120 volts for residential service, an example will be made of a 100-watt, 120-volt lamp. Our standard inside-frosted 100-watt household lamp is designed to provide 1,750 lumens initially at an efficiency of 17.5 lumens per watt and last 750 hours. If the voltage should be raised to 125 volts, the light output will increase to approximately 2,010 lumens, efficiency increases 8 percent, but the life will decrease to approximately 440 hours. If the voltage should be decreased to 115 volts, the light output will decrease to approximately 1,515 lumens, efficiency decreases 7½ percent, and the life will increase to approximately 1,310 hours. 

2. Watts. The watt is the unit of power expended when 1 ampere of current flows between two points having a potential difference of 1 volt. As applied to incandescent lamps it is the measure of electrical power input to the lamp. Watts times hours, in thousands (kilowatt-hours) are the measure of energy consumed and paid for by the consumer. 

3. Lumens. The lumen is the unit of luminous flux: equal to the flux emitted through a unit solid angle (one stiradian) from a uniform point source of one candle. As applied to household incandescent lamps, it is a measure of the light output of a lamp as measured by spherical photometry in comparison to a group of standard lamps supplied and calibrated by the U.S. Bureau of Standards. 

Again, lumens times hours are lumen-hours, the measure of useful light produced over a period of time, the product paid for. 

4. Luminous efficacy (efficiency) of a light source. The ratio of the total luminous flux emitted by the source of the total power input to the source. 

In the case of an electric lamp, efficacy (efficiency) is expressed in lumens per watt. 

5. Lamp life. As used herein is the design life of the lamp. This will also be the average life of a large number of lamps of the same design. However, it is impossible to predict the life of any individual lamp in this group. Lamps fail in accordance with a mortality curve very much as people die. With the best presently known quality control, first failures may occur at approximately 40 percent of design life and last failures at approximately 160 percent of design life. 

6. Other lamp measurements.—In addition to the variation from average life noted above, the other measurements of lamps, watts, light output, efficiency and maintenance of light output throughout life, similarly exhibit variation of individual lamps from the mean or average of a large number of lamps of the same

design. For this reason relatively large numbers of lamps must be measured in test lots, carefully selected to statistically represent the universe from which they are drawn. For example, just for the household lamps included in this discussion, the General Electric Co. annually photometers in excess of 500,000 lamps per year. In addition, we test to destruction (life tests and quality checks which render the lamp unsalable) in excess of 400,000 lamps per year. This extensive test program is essential to ascertain and maintain the quality level of the product which we are selling. 

7. General lighting service lamps. The lamps discussed in this statement are 

15to 150-watt inside-frosted and white-finished lamps in 100to 130-volt designs. 

These lamps are predominantly used in residential lighting, are totally contained in two SIC product codes; 3641008 and 3641009, which cover general lighting 

15to 150-watt lamps, 100 to 130 volts, white lamps, and all other than white lamps.

EXCERPT FROM "ELECTRIC LAMP INDUSTRY,' ARTHUR A. BRIGHT (1949) 

The policies followed by General Electric in fixing lamp life have frequently been criticized. The company has been accused of deliberately shortening the burn-out time in order to increase its sales. It is certainly true that the lamp producers have kept down lamp life and have benefited from greater sales; but that alone is not a valid argument against the shorter-lived lamp. The validity of the 750to 1,000-hour average life depends upon its effect on lighting costs. 

In electric lighting the usual object is to obtain the desired number of lumens as cheaply as possible. Optimum lamp life depends on the complex relationships among lamp efficiency, lamp prices, lamp life, cost of changing lamps, and power rates. The cost of electric current is far greater than that of lamp replacement. 

But electric rates have declined by more than 60 percent since 1910, and yet most of the few changes in incandescent-lamp life have been downward. If the 1,000hour life was optimum in most installations in 1912, it would appear to be too short at the present time. However, from an ideal cost standpoint in many installations a much shorter life than 1,000 hours would actually have been most economical in 1912. The same is true today. The lives actually established by the lamp manufacturers have been somewhat longer than their calculations of the theoretical ideal to reduce the annoyance of frequent lamp replacement. Moreover, the rapid decline in efficiency with longer life indicates that for a large proportion of applications longer life would not be desirable. A sweeping and unqualified condemnation of present average lamp life does not seem justified, therefore.

Nevertheless, the emphasis which General Electric has placed on lamp efficiency at the expense of lamp life has in many instances led consumers to forget the importance of overall installed efficiency.
