LALLARL CIVERSITY LILLALY NOTE: 

The accompanying article was written as an original thesis in the spring of 1926 by Commander Newton L. Nichols, U.S. Navy, while a student at the U. S. Naval War College. A vast amount of research was involved, and as a result, a number of new and interesting features of Nelson's naval campaign in 1805, particularly concerning the events immediately preceding that immortal battle 

Trafalgar - were brought out. The author has consulted many authorities, notably James, Corbett, Mahan, Desbriere, Thursfield, Fraser, and the Report of the British Admiralty Board of 1913 on Nelson's tactics at Trafalgar. The latter has been especially valuable. Quotations have been injected in appropriate places, especially from Fraser. The author is greatly indebted to the above authorities.

Most of the diagrams are original, having been analyzed and drawn by the author himself.

The Author. 

INTRODUCTORY- 

The Battle of Trafalgar has been universally regarded as a classic in Naval actions of history for several reasons. First, it insured to Great 

Britain the absolute control of the sea, which she has securely kept during the 121 years that have elapsed since this epochal battle. Second, it was the culmination of the brilliant career of the greatest Naval commander of all times O Lord Nelson. Third, and most important to students of Naval warfare, it demonstrated as probably never before or since, how a wellplanned, well-conducted, and vigorous OFFENSIVE, possessed by a numerically weaker force, is more than sufficient to offset this weakness when opposed to a numerically superior force, the plans of which, however, are based primarily on MTENSIVE tactics. Moreover, it is significant to observe how truly the identical basic Principles of War applied at Trafalgar, the most momentous battle of the sail era, as they applied at 

Jutland. And this despite the lapse of 111 years, with the resultant completely revolutionized methods of conducting Naval warfare. - 1 -

SITUATION IN 1805. BRITISH AND FRENCH NAVAL POLICIES. 

In order to properly appreciate the full meaning of Trafalgar, one's mind should be refreshed, in at least a general way, with the international situation at the time, and the strategy of the contestants. Great Britain's basic foreign policy from her beginning as a nation has been the Command of the Narrow Seas the Straits of Dover and the North Sea that great centre of the world's commerce where the Scheldt, the Meuse, the Rhine, and the Thames meet; she believes that her very existence as a nation depends on this control. The two great trade routes of British commerce have always been North along the coast of the Low Countries to the Baltic and South along the coasts of Spain and Portugal to the Mediterranean. The southern route in the days of sailing vessels passed well out to sea after leaving the Channel until neer the shores of Portugal. That either of these routes should be permanently threatened by any strong continental power would in British opinion be a great threat at the security of their nation. As a consequence of this long continued and unvarying policy we have at the present day the Northern route flanked by countries like the Netherlands, Denmark, and Belgium and the Southern route by Portugal.

Napoleon learned a lesson in Egypt, and the Battle of the Nile, with its disastrous results for the French, taught him what sea-power meant. 

He was supreme on the land, but on the sea his empire ended, and Great Britain barred his path to a universal empire. From then on all his genius was bent to overcome the obstacles presented by the sea-power of England.

The conflict of British and French policies outlined above led to the outbreak of war in 1803.

NAPOLEON'S PLAN FOR INVADING ENGLAND. 

One of Napoleon's favorite schemes for some time had been the invasion of England by way of the English Channel. Napoleon was at all times handicapped by his personal lack of knowledge in Naval affairs, and by his refusal to listen to such advice as his very few competent Naval advisers endeavored to give him. It resulted that his plans of invasion were not altogether practicable and were so inefficiently executed as to eventually be abandoned.

Briefly, Napoleon's plan was to gain command of the Channel for a limited period at a suitable season for crossing with a large army embarked in a huge Flotilla of small craft designed for this purpose. 

Control of the Channel was to be obtained by drawing off the major part of the defending British Fleet on another mission in distant parts. 

With this object, Admiral Villeneuve, on 30 March, 1805, was dispatched ostensibly to attack British possessions in the West Indies. 

Admiral Nelson, with a limited number of ships, on 11 May 1805, pursued him across the Atlantic, but Villeneuve evaded him and, after an indecisive engagement with a British Squadron under Admiral Calder, enroute, returned to Ferrol, France, late in July, 1805, followed by Nelson, who returned to England. Napoleon's diversion of his Fleet across the 

Atlantic failed because the British kept the main part of their Fleet - 2

blockading French ports in the Channel, and because Villeneuve, upon his return from the West Indies, failed to effect the Naval concentration ordered by Napoleon for August, 1805, off Brest, on account of unfavorable weather. Nelson's persistence, in keeping so close on Villeneuve's trail, during this lengthy chase, is to be noted, as indicative of the characteristics of this leader later to be demonstrated. So Napoleon, greatly incensed against Villeneuve, whom he blamed for all his Naval misfortunes, gave up his plan of invading England, and demobilized his Channel Flotilla. 

On 13 August, Villeneuve sailed from Ferrol for Cadiz, where he took command of a large combined French and Spanish Fleet. 

NELSON LEAVES ENGLAND FOR CADIZ. 

On 14 September, Nelson left Ingland to take command of the 

Mediterranean Station, arriving off Cadiz on 29 September, where he joined and greatly reinforced the small British blockading squadron under Admiral Collingwood, who became his second in command, and who, as we will see, assisted Nelson so very materially in the great battle to follow. 

Nelson had been given a free hand to act by the Admiralty, with orders to: (a) Prevent the enemy from getting control of the sea. (b) Protect the British Mediterranean convoys.

Nelson attempted to draw Villeneuve out of Cadiz by concealing the strength of his forces and by a vigorous blockade of nearby ports, thus producing famine conditions in Cadiz. Villeneuve summoned a conference aboard his flagship in Cadiz of his Allied subordinates (French and Spanish) for the purpose of reaching a decision whether the Allied fleet should proceed out of Cadiz or remain at anchor inside. The conference developed into a very boisterous session. An officer who was present at this conference reported that when one of the Spanish captains present remarked that it would be foolhardy to go out because the barometer was falling, Villeneuve replied, "No, it is not the barometer that is falling. It is the courage and spirits of certain of my captains present." This resulted in several officers present drawing their swords and the conference becoming almost a hand-to-hand encounter.

In the meantime, Nelson disposed his forces so as to apparently give Villeneuve an opportunity to escape, to prevent the ships of the 

British Fleet from being blown through the Straits of Gibraltar by westerly winter gales, and to interrupt allied reinforcements. 

COMPARISON OF THE OPPOSING FORCES. 

(a) The Commanders-in-Chief. 

NELSON. 

Lord Nelson, by his remarkable Naval successes to date, had already demonstrated himself to be one of the greatest naval leaders in British history. Adored by the English populace, loved by his comrades, and holding the supreme confidence of the British Admiralty, Lord Nelson, at

Trafalgar, was indeed a God-chosen commander-in-Chief. 

Mahan writes: "It is the appointed lot of some of History's chosen few to come upon the scene at the moment when a great tendency is nearing its crisis and culmination. Specially gifted with qualities needed to realize the fullness of its possibilities, they so identify themselves - 3

with it by their deeds that they thenceforth personify to the world the movement which brought them forth, and of which their own achievements are at once the climax and the most dazzling illustration. Such was Nelson." 

VILLENEUVE. 

Villeneuve was admittedly the most accomplished officer and the most able tactician that the French Navy then possessed. His besetting defect was infirmity of purpose. He had little confidence in his own officers, and, for their part, the majority were not in accord with him. As for his Spanish allies, they maintained an open dislike for their French Commander-in-Chief, with even a certain amount of jealousy. Villeneuve could not free himself from the conviction that it was his business to save his Fleet. As we will see, his orders issued previous to the Battle of Trafalgar were in many respects excellent and anticipated Nelson's tactics. But Villeneuve's personality was such that his orders were not carried out and he received little co-operation and support. (b)

Their officers were "trained to storms, inured to sea-keeping in all weathers and matchless in their knowledge of practical sea craft." Their seamen were bred to the sea, and well trained in both seamanship and gunnery.

Allies: The French Captains were brave and willing but untrained and inefficient. The Spaniards were even less efficient. The French Captains, of course, were to a large extent the victims of fate, victims of the fortunes of war. Their crews, not bred to the sea, had to be taught the elements of seamanship on the few occasions when they got to sea. Their gunnery was neglected. It has been said that the French and Spanish ships which had taken part in the late cruise to the West Indies and back must be considered as trained in the same sense as the British. But, apart from the fact that these vessels formed little more than a half of the Allied Fleet, there is nothing to such comparison. II Allies: Villeneuve's instructions are also shown here. be noted that Villeneuve foretold Nelson's actions practically to the letter, but that he took no positive offensive measures to counteract them. His plan was primarily a DEFENSIVE one. -6.

Vice Admiral Villeneuve did not issue any written orders for the Battle of Trafalgar. Instead, he had two conferences to explain to his officers how he proposed to fight the coming battle. These instructions, as they have come down to us through the memory of his officers may be stated as follows: 

"The main battle-squadron of the Combined Fleet, the Corps de bataille, would number twenty-one sail. The remaing twelve ships were to be disposed of as a 'Squadron of Observation' or Corps de reserve, under Admirals Gravina and Magon, with orders to act independently, or take post with the main body of the fleet, as circumstances might require. "The British Fleet will not be formed in a line of battle parallel with the Combined Fleet, according to the usage of former days. 

Nelson, assuming him to be, as reported, really in command, will seek to break our line, envelop our rear, and overpower with groups of his ships as many of ours as he can isolate or cut off. "If the Combined Fleet found itself to the windward, it was to bear down and engage ship to ship, each vessel picking out her 'opposite number'. If the Combined Fleet was to leeward, it would form in close line ahead, await the attack, and do its best to beat it off. Once the battle opened, every captain must look out for himself, and trust to his own exertions and personal desire for glory. All your efforts must be to assist one another, and as far as possible, following the movements of your admiral.

You must be careful not to waste ammunition by long range firing; wait and fight only at close quarters. At the same time you must, each captain, rely rather on your own courage and ardour for glory than on the admiral's signals. In the smoke and turmoil of battle, an admiral can see very little himself; often he cannot make any signals at all. "There is nothing to alarm us in the sight of an English fleet; their 74-gun ships have not 500 men on board; the seamen are harassed by a two years' cruise; they are not more brave than we are; they have infinitely fewer motives to fight well, and possess less love of country. 

They are skillful at maneuvering. In a month we shall be as much so as they are. In fine, everything unites to inspire us with hopes of the most glorious success and of a new era for the imperial marine." 

MOVEMENTS OF THE TWO FLEETS. 

In the accompanying plates, an attempt will be made to follow the movements of the British and Allied Fleets, commencing with 0600, 19 October, 1805, when the first Allied ships succeeded in making a sortie from Cadiz, until the battle itself, some 54 hours later. This period of 54 hours had been divided into what appear to be four natural phases. The fifth phase is the battle itself. It

So far as is known, the study that follows is the first graphical account on record of the events for the 54-hour period immediately preceding Trafalgar that treats the movements on both sides simultaneously. follows, that having little recorded tactical data to guide, a great deal of careful study of the accounts by both sides has been required, with much piecing together and, in some instances, a certain amount of guesswork. Therefore, complete accuracy in every detail cannot be vouched for. 

The Allied accounts available are very general and vague as to tactical details, and the more numerous British accounts, while much better, vary considerably and are far from perfect. It is interesting to note in this connection that over one hundred years alapsed after this great battle was fought and so gloriously won by Nelson before an accurate account of even the major British tacties immediately preceding and during the battle became available. During this long period sertain misconceptions by various high authorities, including our own revered Mahan, prevailed and were commonly accepted. -7.

In 1912, the British Admiralty appointed a board to "consider the whole of the evidence relative to the tactics employed by Nelson at the Battle of Trafalgar." This board went into the subject exhaustively and submitted their report in time for it to be published in England by Trafalgar Day, 21 October, 1913, when it excited much interest. The most impressive feature of this board's report, which is confined chiefly to the battle proper, is that it proves beyond doubt the much disputed point that Nelson followed out in spirit his classic Memorandum in his evolutions at Trafalgar.

While the above Admiralty report does not attempt to cover other than the British movements, and these only for a much shorter period than I have endeavored to describe in the plates that follow, this report is the best authentic guide available, and has been made the backbone of the following account of the battle itself, to which I have attempted to add the data necessary to make the following description applicable to the operations of both Nelson and Villeneuve, commencing with the Allied exit from Cadiz. 

Also, plates will be shown illustrating the tactical lessons to be gained from Trafalgar. The following from Mahan is quoted, as it has such a direct bearing on the tactics at Trafalgar:- "The study of the sea history of the past will be found instructive by its illustration of the general principles of maritime war, notwithstanding the great changes that have been brought about in naval weapons... .it is necessary thus to study critically the history and experience of naval warfare in the days of sailing ships.". "The relative positions of two sailing ships, or fleets, with reference to the direction of the wind, involved most important tactical questions, and were perhaps the chief care of the seamen of that age…….. the distinguishing feature of the weather guage was that it conferred the power of giving or refusing battle at will, which in turn carries the usual advantage of an Offensive attitude in the choice of the method of attack. This advantage was accompanied by certain drawbacks, such as irregularity introduced into the order, exposure to raking or enfiladating cannonade, and the sacrifices of part or all of the artillery fire of the assailant, -all of which were incurred in approaching the enemy • The ships or fleet with the lee-guage, could not attack; if it did not wish to retreat, its action was confined to the defensive, and to receiving battle on the enemy's terms. This disadvantage was compensated by the comparative ease of maintaining the order of battle undisturbed, and by a sustained artillery fire, to which the enemy for awhile was unable to reply. "Historically, these favorable and unfavorable characteristics have had their counterpart and analogy in the Offensive and Defensive operations of all ages.

The Offense undertakes certain risks and disadvantages in order to reach and destroy the enemy; the defense, so long as it remains such, refuses the risks of advance, holds on to a careful well-ordered position, and avails itself of the exposure to which the assailant submits himself. "These radical differences between the weather and the lee-guage, were so clearly recognized, through the cloud of lesser details accompanying them, that the former was ordinarily chosen by the English, because their steady policy was to assail and destroy their enemy; whereas the French sought the lee-guage, because by so doing, they were usually able to cripple the enemy as he approached, and thus evade decisive encounters and preserve their ships. 11 The outstanding applications, or violations, of the Principles of

I have divided the operations between the sortie from Cadiz and the opening of the battle into four Phases. The fifth Phase is the Battle itself.

THE 1ST PHASE. 

(1st Phase 

0600 19 October to 0700 20 October.)

The morning of 19 October dawned with perfect weather, but very light wind. Preparations for getting underway were immediately made in the 

Allied Fleet, all of which activity was observed and promptly reported to Nelson by Blackwood. At 0720, Blackwood in "Euryalus" hoisted signal "370", meaning "Enemy's ships are coming out," which signal Nelson received at 0930 via Duff's Communication Squadron. Nelson at once ordered a general chase to the S. E. in order to head off Villeneuve from passing in past Gibraltar. By noon, only 9 Allied ships had succeeded in getting out of harbor, the wind having died away almost to a calm, resulting in a total of only 12 Allied ships under Rear Admiral Magon of the 33 in Cadiz, finally getting out by nightfall. Magon anchored for the night off the coast just to the north of Cadiz, as shown. Nelson fearing that the entire Allied fleet had gotten out, continued to the S.E. with the British Main Body, leaving Blackwood and his Observation frigates "Euryalus", "Naiad", "Sirius", and "Phoebe", to continue the lookout on the enemy's movements. 

By 0600 20 October, Nelson in "Victory" arrived at the position shown, just off Cape Trafalgar, in order to head off Villeneuve, as said above, and join Louis with his 6 ships from Gibraltar. But Nelson then suffered a treble disappointment, no Enemy in sight, no news or sight of Louis, and bad weather with poor visibility prevailing. The wind by this time had shifted to the SSW, so Nelson decided to avail himself of it and return to his station off Cadiz. 9.

In the meantime, the Observation Squadron had been keeping in close touch with the Allied Fleet's movements, and at 0700, Nelson received a signal from Blackwood, via Duff in "Mars", and Blackwood's "Phoebe", and linking ships, that the enemy was to the North (meaning Magon and his 12 ships). Duff was then instructed by Nelson to keep in touch with the enemy.

Attention is invited to the track of Nelson in "Victory" (British Main Body) in Plate 2, and of Duff and Blackwood, illustrating the very efficient British system of Observation and Communication, at this time. 

THE 2ND PHASE (Plate 3). 

(2nd Phase 

- 0700 to 2030 20 October).

By 0700, as we have seen, Nelson had received word that the enemy was to the North, so he stood to the Westward until 0800 to get in closer touch with the rest of the Main Body, which was pretty well scattered, and then hove to and directed the captains of all the ships in the vicinity to report on board "Victory" for a, conference. Admiral

Collingwood, Nelson's second in command, advised an attack at once. But 

Nelson wished to get to windward of Villeneuve, thus securing the advantage of the weather guage, and also in order to keep well clear of the dangerous shoals off Cape Trafalgar, with westerly gale impending and consequent risk of ships disabled in battle being wrecked on a lee shore. 

So he puraned westerly and northerly courses, as indicated in Plate 3, until 2030 that night, during which time Blackwood kept in close touch with the enemy's movements and constantly informed Nelson accordingly, still applying the principles of Security and Superiority. 

At 0700, Villeneuve, thanks to a temporary favorable wind, succeeded in getting out of Cadiz with his remaining 21 ships and stood to the west in order to clear the coast, with weather threatening. As the 

Allied Fleet went out, the shores were thronged with thousands of the populace and the churches were so crowded with anxious relatives that masses were said during the entire day. 

At 1630, Villeneuve succeeded in joining up with Magon, thus following the Principle of Superiority, and changed course to the south in the Allied Fleet's cruising formation of 5 columns, Spanish

Admiral Gravina with his support or "Observation" Squadron, to windward, thereby adhering to the Principle of Security. The Allied Fleet, due to their lack of skill and practice, maneuvered very poorly, and much confusion resulted. It is interesting to note that before the Allied Fleet succeeded in forming up they lost considerable time due to an American sailing ship that suddenly appeared on the scene, and the necessity of boarding the vessel.

The British Fleet, in the meantime, in well-ordered array, stood steadily to the southward, with little or no confusion. 

At 0400 21 October, Nelson again wore and stood to the northward in order to obtain the best possible battle position, thus applying the Principle of Movement. The wisdom of this maneuver is very evident from an examination of the relative positions of Nelson and Villeneuve at 0600 21 October in Plate 4. Nelson was in a position to square away for battle immediately. Villeneuve, as we will see, had yet much difficult maneuvering to go through which resulted in placing him under a tremendous initial handicap.

Attention is invited to the tracks of Blackwood's observatio frigates "Euryalus", "Sirius", "Naiad", and "Phoebe", during the night which have been indicated in Plate 4, as the result of considerable study, in order to illustrate the excellent British observation system. 

Nelson was at all time cognizant of the position, strength, and formation of Villeneuve's Fleet. Villeneuve knew practically nothing of the 

British Fleet. He did not even know its position until about 2030 

20 October. 

A British officer in "Euryalus" wrote: "For two days there was not a movement that we did not communicate 'till I thought Blackwood who gave the orders, and Bruce, our signal midshipman, and Soper, our signalman who executed them, must have died of it; and when we had brought the two fleets fairly together, we took our place between the two lines of lights as a cab might in Regent Street. The watch was called and Blackwood turned in quietly to wait for the morning." (Fraser)

The British through their efficient observation ships and communications constantly applied the Principle of Security up to the moment of contact of the main bodies. The Allies, on the other hand, knew practically nothing of the British movements until the main bodies were in sight and constantly violated the Principle of Security. 

THE 4TH PHASE. THE APPROACH. 

(4th Phase (Plate 5). 

0600 to Noon 21 October).

This phase represents the Approach. Great joy prevailed in both 

Fleets upon sighting each other at 0630 on the 21st. The Allies lost their dull depression after the uncertainties of the past months, and particularly the previous night, and were eager to come to close quarters. 

As for Nelson's Fleet, a British officer wrote: "The delight manifested exceeded anything I ever witnessed surpassing even those gratulations when our native cliffs are descried after a long period of distant service." (Fraser)

At 0646, Nelson signalled "Courses East. Form line of sailing in two columns and prepare for battle." (On the plate, Nelson's course actually is 69° true because of 21° Westerly variation). Collingwood accordingly formed up to leeward of Nelson, and the two British columns advanced, supported by the Observation frigates to windward, for about the center of the Allied Fleet, in accordance with Nelson's Memorandum. 

Nelson's strongest ships were in his van, the three 3-deckers "Victory", "Temeraire", and "Neptune" leading Nelson's column, and 

Collingwood leading his column in the 3-decker "Royal Sovereign".

At 0830, Villeneuve, fearing that Nelson would cut him off from Cadiz, took advantage of the Principle of Movement and ordered the Allied Fleet to wear by ship movements, thus reversing his formation. 

Due to the very light wind, with a slight shift to the southward, that prevailed during the execution of this maneuver, it took until 1030 to complete and the final Allied formation, as indicated, was convex in shape, towards the leeward and over 5 miles in length. A good many ships were out of position and in certain parts of the line were double and even treble-banked. Villeneuve's turn to the northward facilitated 

Nelson's plan as it placed Collingwood in a position to break through and engage the Allied rear. 11

During the approach the British ships carried full sail, even to studding sails. Even with this, the speed of the various ships was from 

1 to 3 knots only, resulting in many gaps, and difficult station-keeping. 

The weather column stretched out for 4 miles with "Victory" and 4 other ships closely bunched at the head, and the lee column, while somewhat shorter, had 8 ships in a group in advance, and another group a mile astern. 

"ENGLAND EXPECTS EVERY MAN TO DO HIS DUTY.' 

It was at this time that Nelson made his immortal signal, "England expects every man to do his duty."

THE 5TH PHASE THE BATTLE. 

(Plate 6). 

This plate illustrates the positions of the ships at noon, 21 October, when the battle actually was joined. An examination indicates the lack of regular formation in both Fleets and the many gaps, due to causes before stated. It was unfortunate for Villeneuve that his greatest gap occurred at the very part of the line that Nelson and Collingwood assailed and that his greatest concentration was at the head of the line, under Dumanoir, and at the rear, under Gravina, at which points it was needed.

Villeneuve and Gravina signalled incessantly all morning to various ships to get in station, which resulted in improving the line somewhat. 

But, in the center, there remained to the last a wide gap astern of the "Bucantaure".

To a certain extent, the accidental double-banking of Villeneuve's ships assisted him as it gave him some of the concentration he sorely needed. Had this been supported by the skillful maneuvering and efficient gunnery of individual ships, Trafalgar might have had a different result. 

The most disastrous defect in the Allied formation was the mistake made by Gravina with his support group of 12 powerful ships in not taking his station in column to windward, in accordance with Villeneuve's battle plan, as will be illustrated later. Gravina formed up astern despite the signals directing him to take his assigned station which Villeneuve sent him, when he saw the British Fleet headed for his center. This false move of Gravina's took away an essential feature of Villeneuve's plan and robbed him of his application of the Principle of Superiority. "There is reason to believe that Bravina had independent command of the Observation Squadron, which was to be kept as an independent body up to the moment of joining battle, when it would be available to reinforce any part of the line that might be hard pressed, to parry a threatened blow, or to strike a counter-stroke." It is well-known that Villeneuve and Gravina did not agree and that Gravina's distaste for his French commander-in- Chief had become all the more aggravated after the recent action with the British Admiral Calder's Squadron, when two Spanish ships were lost because, as Gravina claimed, of non-support by their French Allies. 

Gravina had recently made a trip to Madrid and requested of the Spanish Prime Minister Godoy that his resignation from the Allied Fleet be accepted, which request Godoy refused, stating that while he was personally sympathetic, Spain was politically bound to France. Godoy had the fear of Napoleon before his eyes. "He suggested, however, in strict confidence, that in the next battle it might be as well to let the French bear the brunt of the fighting themselves." This may account for 

Gravina's action in not taking his assigned batle station and in finally apparently running away.

THE SITUATION AT ABOUT 1500 (Plate 7). 

This plate shows the position of the ships about 3 hours after the battle started. Nearly an hour elapsed after the first gun was fired before the action became general, by which time about 22 British ships were engaged with an equal number of Allied ships. After about an hour's fighting the Allied Fleet broke into three groups, the largest being the one in the rear engaged by Collingwood. 

Dumanoir, with several ships in the van, stood serenely on for 2 hours completely out of the action, thus flagrantly violating the Principle of Co-Operation, making no attempt to return to the aid of the hard-pressed Villeneuve, and finally actually sending a signal to Villeneuve requesting instructions! Villeneuve promptly ordered him to get into action, whereupon Dumanoir laboriously tacked, being obliged to use boats to get his ships about, and with 4 of his ships, stood to the southward. This squadron then distinguished itself by shooting into several of their own ships which were by this time British prizes, as they passed down to windward, thereby killing and wounding a large number of Allied prisoners. Dumanoir then escaped to sea, to the westward, to meet his fate 10 days later when his squadron was met and defeated by a British squadron off Cape Finisticere, Dumanoir being taken a prisoner to England. The rest of Dumanoir's ships fled toward 

Cadiz, instead of tacking to the southward with Dumanoir, except the "Intrepide", which ship wore, and returned into the melee, covering herself with glory and fighting valiantly, but finally being captured.

Magon was killed, bravely fighting. Gravina received a wound, the infection from which he died of a month later. Villeneuve was taken prisoner and upon finally returning to Paris was found mysteriously stabbed. History is not definite as to whether this unfortunate man met death by his own hand, or, as rumor had it, he was assassinated by order of his enraged chief, Napoleon. 

Dumanoir, upon return to France, was court-martialed several years later for his performance in the battle, but was acquitted. His chief defence for standing on appears to have been his fear of attack when Nelson made his feint and the threat of Nelson's Observation frigates to windward, in which event Nelson's Memorandum was indeed executed almost to the letter.

A British officer wrote: "The mode of attack adopted with such success in the Trafalgar action appears to me to have succeeded from the enthusiasm inspired throughout the British Fleet, from their being commanded by their beloved Nelson, from the gallant conduct of the leaders of the two divisions, from the individual exertions of each ship after the attack commenced, and the superior practice of the guns in the English Fleet. It was successful also from the consternation spread through the combined Fleet on finding the British so much stronger than was expected, from the astonishing and rapid destruction which followed the attack of the leaders, witnessed by the whole of the hostile fleets, inspiring the one and dispiriting the other, and from the loss of the Admiral's ship early in the action."

It is conceivable that, no matter how skillfully Villeneuve had planned for the action and disposed his fleet, it would have been defeated as decisively as it was. Dumanoir's failure to tåck at once and bring his ships into battle, Gravina's flight to the rear, are indicative of that absence of morale and failure to co-operate, which inevitably brings defeat.

In this last Phase at Trafalgar, Nelson adhered to the Principles of the Objective, Movement, Offensive, Superiority, and Co-operation, whereas it is difficult to find a single one that Villeneuve and his Fleet followed. They violated to the last the Principles of Co-operation and Superiority.

THE OPPOSING BATTLE PLANS (Plate 8). 

Here, Nelson's Plan of Battle according to his famous Memorandum is shown. The numbers of ships indicated 40 for British and 46 for 

Allies are based on the maximum numbers Nelson had reason to believe might be engaged. The Plan was for Collingwood's ships to break through the line in line of bearing, commencing at the 12th ship from the rear, and to overwhelm the Allied rear by superior strength, maneuvering, and gunnery, and for Nelson, aided by the Support Group, and later by Collingwood, to take care of the balance, overwhelming the rear of this division before the van could come to their assistance. BRITISH TACTICS (Plate 9). 

Two of these diagrams illustrate the superior seamanship and gunnery developed by the British in the days of Trafalgar, which account for their remarkable successes at sea. In Nelson's day, ships were built extremely heavy in the bows, in order to endure severe weather, whereas the sterns, or "poops", were comparatively light. The naval guns of 1805 were frequently unable to pierce the heavy bow timbers, so the British maneuvered to cross the sterns of their opponents whenever possible, rake from astern, and them maneuver, as shown in the plate, so as to gain considerable superiority in gunfire in coming alongside to grapple and board, the final operation usually, in naval battles of that day. 

The French and Spanish, on the other hand, contented themselves with directing their fire, generally speaking, at the enemy's masts and sails in order to destroy his mobility, thereby enabling them to come alongside and board, to assist in which they usually carried a large number of soldiers. For instance, some 4,000 soldiers were in Villeneuve's Fleet at Trafalgar, whose duties were to man the tops and upperworks and concentrate on the enemy's personnel with musket fire. It was a bullet fired from one of the tops of a French ship alongside "Victory" that struck down Nelson. Nelson scorned such tacties, partly because of fire risks to his own ships. From the above, it can be understood why 

Nelson's columns succeeded in reaching the Allied line comparatively unscathed, despite the fact that they were distinctly capped. Nelson's evolutions would manifestly be impossible todey.
