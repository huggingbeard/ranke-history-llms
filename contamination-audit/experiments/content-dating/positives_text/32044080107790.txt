A PEOPLE'S WORLD A PLAN FOR WORLD GOVERNMENT 

Many plans have been offered for a post-war world. All are plans for a stable world, and yet most of them would result in a world of continuing conflict. In nearly all of them present nations are made the basis for post-war organization. In those that have received the greatest publicity, including the official plans of present governments, certain selected nations are given key positions and others put into positions of subordinancy. Inevitably such organizations of nations would but lead to new rivalries, new jealousies, new conflicts. 

The only stable basis for world organization is not the nations of the world, but the people of the world. The Declaration of Independence states that governments are instituted among men to guarantee to the people their inalienable rights. This applies to any government. If there is to be a world government, it must be set up for the same purpose, to guarantee their inalienable rights not to the nations but to the people of the world. A world government, to have any sound foundation, must be a government of the people of the world, for the people of the world, by the people of the world, regardless of where they were born or who their forebears were. Only such a world government has any right to exist. Only such a world government could long survive. 

In the pages that follow the basic prerequisites for such a government are sketched out. Nations are taken into account, for nations exist and cannot be ignored. But every idea presented has its roots in the concept of a people's government. The ideas need working over: they are rough-hewn only. But they grow out of the firm belief that governments exist for the people, not the people for governments. And they are presented here in the hope that the people will take them, revise them, shape them, till a workable plan for world. government has been developed, and then demand on the basis of that plan a workable government of the people of the world.

HE concept of world government is not just a dream without foundation in actuality. The cultural and technological advances of the last two centuries have been such as to make the earth a unit, whether we choose to recognize the fact or not, and to bind into one enormous superstate all the various peoples of the earth. 

Natural resources are scattered, some here, some there; but virtually all groups of human beings now find increasing need for access to those resources, regardless of their geographic location. Now we have means of transporting them, and the products developed from them, all over the earth. We have means of communication that are virtually instantaneous. Moreover, our scientific researches have proved that, however much we human beings may seem to differ from one another, we are nevertheless all members of a single biological entity, the species homo sapiens, or Man. So the concept of world government is merely an attempt to give political and practicable reality to what is actually a stubborn biologic, economic and technological fact.

But if a world government is to be a lasting and effective government, it must be established in accordance with certain definite principles.

In the first place, such a government must be from the outset the government of a majority union. Only so can it be protected from becoming simply another faction in a divided and conflicting world. 

Any attempt at world government on the part of a selected minority of the people of the world would result, to be sure, in the temporary strengthening of the people included in that minority. But it would only lead in the end to the consolidation and uprising against the minority of the rest of the people of the world. 

The standard example of the unification of independent autonomous states into a working union is the consolidation of the thirteen American colonies to form the United States of America. The fact that that union has gradually grown to a union of forty-eight states has led some thinkers to believe that a similar growth could take place from a nuclear union of a minority of the nations of the world, a growth that would culminate in a union of all nations. But the analogy is false and misleading. The thirty-five states that have been added to the American union since 1787 were, when the union was established, unexploited and virtually uninhabited virgin territory; and their incorporation was not accomplished by a politico-religious conversion of the inhabitants of that area, but by the conquest and in some cases the extermination of those inhabitants.

A truer interpretation of the unification of the thirteen colonies would be made if one limited one's view to the thirteen colonies themselves; for the growth of the American union has simply been the geographic expansion of those thirteen, so far as political realities are concerned. The framers of the Constitution did not attempt to make

a union of a minority of the population of that area.

On the con trary, Article VII of the Constitution reads, "The ratification of the conventions of nine states shall be sufficient for the establishment of this constitution between the states so ratifying the same." In other words, the Constitution did not go into effect at all until nine-thirteenths, or almost three-quarters, of the states had accepted it. It was not to go into effect until the union comprised so large a majority of the population as to eliminate any possibility of an attack upon it by the remaining states. It was not to go into effect until the union of the entire area was virtually guaranteed.

The American union has been amazingly successful because it was a majority union. A world union would be successful if it were in like fashion so inclusive a union as to obviate any outside attack, but only on that condition. Only a union that included a large majority of the human race would have any real hope of survival. Any projected world government should have as a prerequisite of its establishment, its acceptance by at least four-fifths of the population of the world.

That it would take some time to establish such a union goes without saying. But there are times when the old proverb holds true, Haste makes waste. It is better to build slowly and build soundly than to throw together something that is without solid foundation in the inherent nature of things. It is better to wait till that union can be inclusive than to build a partial union that has in it the seeds of future conflict.

A world government, if it is to be lasting and effective, must be in the truest sense a representative government. Plans for world government have been suggested which include a majority of the people of the world, but which nevertheless leave great numbers of these people without any voice in the government. Such a government could never stand. Just as a union of less than a large majority of the people would lead to attack from without, so even a majority union, if controlled by a minority of its population, would lead to insurrection from within. No one can know the specific problems of any group of people who does not live among them and himself share in those problems. 

So the actual participators in a union government should be selected in such a way as truly to represent the diversity of the population of the union.

That representative government is the only possible governmental method for achieving equity in human affairs there can be no doubt. But it is a mistake to identify representative government with a universal franchise. Even here in the United States, where universal franchise is a familiar implement of government, there is no guarantee that the people as a whole, whose attention is for the most part held by their personal affairs, will be sufficiently acquainted with the wider problems of their own region and with the qualifications of prospective representatives to choose in every instance the most suitable person for the post. How much more is this true among people

with no democratic tradition, who prefer to rely on the judgment and control of an hereditary governing class with the training and traditions of professional rulers. 

There is no basic difficulty in establishing a representative union government that includes without alteration all types of national governments. How the representatives shall be chosen and by whom is a comparative technicality, so long as they are chosen from among those people whom they are to represent, with a reasonable guarantee that they shall have the interests of those people at heart. Often the absolute monarch is as sincerely desirous of the welfare of his people as the ruler who has been selected by popular choice. The method of government does not itself create or destroy the moral caliber of the governor.

So it seems more practicable to project a union government in which representatives shall be chosen by the national governments of the various areas represented, and in which the actual method of such choice is left to the discretion of the governments concerned. Democratic governments, based on the theory of popular sovereignty, may do well to refer the choice of such representatives to a popular election. Absolute governments will be compelled by their very nature to make the choices themselves. 

The eventual ideal, perhaps, is a fully democratic government of the world. But just as the American union, when it was established, included a sizeable population of slaves, so a world union can be established which includes non-democratic populations. The details of internal operation can later be worked out and improved within the established union on the basis of actual experience. The important thing is that the government of the union from the start be truly and fully representative.

The very size of such a union as the one projected precludes war against it by any other state. But even so inclusive a union would be powerless to prevent disorder within its own borders without a strong central government. As has been pointed out in other proposals, a mere league is not enough. A union must have a unified structure, and that structure must reach down in some fashion to touch the daily lives of the people and, on some plane, to treat all of them equally, if it is to accomplish its purpose. A league creates no unity of feeling among the inhabitants of the states concerned, for those inhabitants are kept in separate compartments. The only true point of contact in a league is governmental.

On the other hand it is exceedingly difficult to create a union that shall comprise four-fifths of the human race or more, if that union is to depend on radical alteration of existing governments. Governments are the result of long processes of growth; they cannot be transformed overnight and they cannot be transformed with any real measure of success at all if the transformation is imposed upon them. So the problem to be solved in the establishment of an inclusive union is how to set up a strong central government that shall really reach the indi-

vidual inhabitants, and yet be compatible so far as possible with all existing governmental forms.

In the formation of the American union, a large proportion of the government of the people was left under the jurisdiction of the states. 

To this day a great proportion of our daily lives is subject not to federal but to state legislation. But powers were delegated to the federal government of such nature as gradually to decrease any feeling of boundary-lines between states. Common citizenship, common coinage, a central government with a visible single head-all such items of common experience tended to emphasize the unity among the inhabitants of the states and to obliterate a consciousness of such differences as existed. In the beginning the states lost very little of what they had formerly had of local self-determination; yet those few unifying elements which were vested in the central government were so strong that today everyone in the union feels himself first an American, and only secondarily, if at all, a New Yorker or a Virginian. 

Similarly a world union must be constructed with a central government whose strength lies not in its multiplicity of powers but in its power to create in the minds of its inhabitants a sense of its unity. 

Under the American Constitution it was made impossible for any state to convert its boundaries into barriers and limit the passage of either people or goods across its borders. A world union must apply the same principle if it is to be a union at all. This alone would go far towards the achievement of a true sense of unity among the people of the union, In actual fact national boundaries, except insofar as they coincide with real geographic barriers like oceans, must be emphasized to be noticeable at all. If it were not for customs and immigration officials, the boundary between the United States and Canada would, for much the greater part of its length, be totally invisible. The same applies to most of the multifarious boundaries in 

Europe. Eliminate all practical obstacles to free motion across these invisible boundaries, and they would very soon cease to have more than an academic existence for anyone.

So it should be a principle of any union government that no obstacles should be placed along internal boundaries. Individuals should have absolute freedom of motion. And any necessary control of the motion of goods should be exercised from some central point, so that even such limitation of movement as existed would emphasize not the boundaries themselves, but the unity of the whole. 

There is a fear in most people that to relinquish absolute control of necessities is to be thrust into danger. So each state today is fearful of letting go its control of its own commerce lest it suffer economically. 

The fear is not without foundation, but it is easily exaggerated; and if it be a central government of all the states concerned to whom the final control is relinquished, the advantages would outweigh the dangers. For such a central government would be desirous of the welfare of the whole, and each of the states concerned would be treated in its relation to the whole and as a part of the whole. In relinquishing con-

trol to such a central government, cach state would be freed of the constant threat of inundation by superior force or skill or ingenuity in another state. To turn over the control of international commerce to a union government would free each state of insecurity and in the end assure each state of greater economic well-being than it had ever known as an independent unit.

A large part of the world is highly conscious of money. Money passes through the hands of every individual. The superscription of the Caesar was a constant reminder to everyone from the wall of Hadrian to the Arabian desert of the existence and unity of the Roman empire. So the coinage of each land is a most immediate source of a sense of the unity of that land. 

A union government should therefore be empowered to issue its own coinage. This does not mean that the coinage of the various states within the union should be suppressed. There is no need of such suppression. Any state that chose to could continue to mint its own coins. But a coinage that was immediately usable in any part of the union would very soon displace any merely local coinage. And the union coinage that would so come into use would be a constant reminder of the unity of the union. 

The human mind, by virtue of its very nature, functions in terms of symbols. Every word is a symbol. And for most people every abstract concept needs, for clear recognition, not only a name but a concrete embodiment. Specifically, it is natural to the human mind to symbolize a state in an individual. The king is the symbol of the state, a real unity serving as an embodiment of a political unit., The king of the British Commonwealth is in only a slight degree an administrative officer. But the unity of the British Commonwealth depends upon his existence, and without him that commonwealth would very quickly dissolve.

A world union should likewise be symbolized in some one individual. 

A single administrative head is an indissoluble unit, and as such would be a true symbol of the unity of the union, visible and intelligible to every man, woman and child within the union. How that individual should be chosen, what his qualifications and duties and powers should be, how long he should remain in office—all these matters are secondary and of far less importance. For the most important function of the head of any political organization is that he serves inevitably as the symbol of the unity of the organization he heads, a constant unifying force of almost inestimable strength.

These three features alone--freedom of movement across boundaries, a common coinage and a single head—would go far toward creating a powerful central government, without requiring any radical alteration in present local governmental forms. Each of these features would bring home to the people of the union a constant sense of unity. It is but rarely recognized that in political matters true strength lies chiefly in the psychological attitude of the people. Indeed the less a government interferes with the daily lives of people, the better are its

chances of survival. There is an ancient Chinese proverb, "The Yellow 

Emperor sat on his throne facing the east, and the Empire was in order." The Yellow Emperor's mere existence created in the mind of every person in the Empire a sense of the unity of that Empire, and that sense of unity was itself sufficient to cause each person so to act in relation to others as to create unified action among them all; such is the sense of the proverb. We of the west are inclined to scoff at such a notion as impractical, to think that a government has to order us about, or at least have the power to order us about, if it is to be more than a pious fiction. But the Chinese are a more realistic people than we, and it is in fact our notion that is impractical. A truly successful union government would be a government capable of instilling a deep awareness of its unity into every citizen, and then letting that awareness control the citizens.

It is unfortunately true, however, that most people are so engrossed in their own affairs and in particular in the accumulation of wealth, that awareness of the political body to which they belong will be clouded over by awareness of purely personal desires; and that therefore some limitations must be imposed on individual freedom of action if harmony is to be maintained among men. In particular the opportunities for one person to benefit at another's expense must be rigidly controlled. It is the inalienable right of every living creature to try to keep on living, and most human beings will exercise that right. So if any individual or group of individuals, by virtue of superior intelligence or in any other way, achieves a disproportionately great prosperity, and in so doing seems to threaten the welfare and perhaps eventually the very survival of others, those others will rise up and strike down the prosperous. 1 What is true among individuals is equally true among nations. No nation or group of nations can grow fat at the expense of others without eventually precipitating war. So a union government must, if it is to be successful, obviate all opportunity for one nation to benefit at the expense of others.

It has already been said that a union government should have control of the commerce among the constituent states. But this is not sufficient. Commerce depends at last on natural resources. To be prosperous and to feel secure in its prosperity, a nation must have access to these natural resources. But natural resources are scattered. 

Not all nations have natural resources in any great amount; no nation has control and certain access to all the natural resources that it needs. 

At the present time, if one nation wishes to withhold its resources from another, that other nation must choose between doing without something it feels the need of and girding up its military loins and taking it. 

So a union government, if it is to be successful, must have the right to regulate the use of natural resources throughout its domain. This does not mean that it must own them. The actual ownership of the resources is unimportant. It is of no moment to me whether I own the bed I sleep in or not, so long as I feel secure in my right to sleep

in it. It is of no moment who owns the resources of the world, so long as those that have need of them have a guarantee that they will have access to them. And only a representative union government, which thinks of the welfare not of the part but of the whole, is in a position equitably to regulate the use of the resources needed by all. 

To insist that a union government shall have the right to regulate the use of natural resources will seem to many an almost insuperable barrier to the establishment of such a government. That nations will hesitate to give up so highly cherished a right as the control of their own resources goes without saying. But there is absolutely no point in establishing a union at all unless that union is to be given the powers necessary to its survival. A union government that had no control of resources would soon find itself in the position of the League of Nations during the past fifteen years, confronted with warfare and able to do nothing but sit on the sidelines frantically crying, Stop. I have said it before and I say it again, it is better to wait until a union can be built on a sound foundation than to build shanty union on the sands of idealistic hope. The Paris peace pact had no power to prevent war because it was merely words. A union without control of resources needed by all parts of the union would be but words also. 

Here, as in the case of international commerce, every nation would stand to gain far more in security than it lost in control by giving up its right to regulate the use of its resources. As it stands now, each nation is in constant danger of losing them anyway and of suffering great loss of life and of other property, perhaps even of political freedom, in the process. Moreover, each nation is forced to convert a large proportion of its resources into implements for the protection of those resources, a use of resources which is in itself of no benefit whatever except insofar as some few individuals are thereby given work. 

With a union control of resources, all resources could be liberated for genuinely useful purposes and none would be in danger of annihilation; with the result that every nation, even those within whose territory the resources lay, would be better off than they are under present conditions.

During the past few years personal freedom has been greatly curtailed in many parts of the world. This has brought forth a howl of resentment from other parts of the world, notably from the United States, where personal freedom is a dearly cherished right. We fail to realize that a curtailment of personal freedom in a country like present-day Germany is not a mere sadistic desire to dictate to people, but a necessary part of the governmental purposes of the country. 

For the structure of the Nazi state is based on principles so arbitrary and unrealistic that any real freedom of thought or speech would seriously undermine it.

This very fact points out a political principle of great importance in the foundation of a stable state. There are many individuals who prefer to be told what to think and say; and nearly all individuals are in some measure dependent on conventional patterns of thought. But ΙΟ

virtually no people wish to be told that they must think so and so. 

Sooner or later such an intellectual dictatorship will become too irksome to be borne, and serious internal unrest and even revolution will result. This is particularly true if the intellectual dictatorship imposes thought-patterns that are demonstrably not borne out by the nature of things, or it if enforces or forbids some particular form of religious practice.

A union government must therefore allow and guarantee freedom of thought and freedom of all such expressions of thought as speech, religion and the press. More than that, it should encourage openminded scientific study of the world and man, that the thought-patterns of the people may gradually come to reflect more and more accurately the demonstrable and enduring nature of reality. Human beings are all too prone to create their own private worlds; and the creation of unlike private worlds is a pregnant source of human conflict, as religious history makes clear. State private worlds are far more dangerous than individual private worlds, for states have more power both for good and for ill than individuals. It is incompatible with the right of free inquiry, out of which alone true knowledge of reality can arise, to impose any limitation on the right of individuals to indulge themselves in intellectual world-building. But it is imperative that no state, not even the union itself, should be given the right to create and impose on individuals any arbitrary concept of things or any compulsory pattern of thought. Truth is not created by man. 

It is self-existent and must be discovered. Inevitably it will be discovered and in the long run will triumph. So it is sowing the seeds of its own disestablishment for any state to attempt to limit or control the freedom of human thought or expression. 

Not only in thought and expression, but in behavior as well, human beings should be left as nearly as possible completely free. The founders of the American republic spoke often of government deriving its just rights from the consent of the governed. So worded this sounds like a moral privilege. But it is far more than a privilege. In the final analysis no government can exist at all except by consent of the governed. A government, to be stable, must be merely the brain of the people it governs, an instrument for the achievement of the satisfaction of the fundamental needs' and desires of the governed. And since individual needs and desires vary, a government, to be successful, must leave each individual as free as public safety permits. Governors will have, no doubt, their own notions of how people ought to act, but governors are merely undermining the government they administer if they attempt to force people to act that way. Except insofar as the welfare of the total group demands the curtailment of personal liberty, each individual must be left free to do as he sees fit. 

At the moment there exist within virtually every state stringent laws controlling human behavior. In many instances these laws are but the modern forms of primitive taboos. Some of them at least do serious violence to the basic impulses of individuals, without being 

II

in any sense necessary to the true welfare of the society. But it happens that these are the very laws that are most deeply ingrained into the ethical consciousness of the people concerned. They are in fact a very real source of social unrest and of destructively criminal actions; but for a union government to attempt to repeal or annul them would be to create equally great social unrest of a different sort. It is unwise therefore for a union government to attempt to interfere with the internal governments of states in this matter of human behavior. 

Freedom of religion, speech and the press will inevitably tend to lift taboos that limit freedom of action; and insistence on this more subtle type of freedom must suffice. On the other hand, no union government should entrench such taboos by passing laws of its own curtailing human freedom of behavior.

One more feature of a union government remains to be mentioned. 

The union government must have the power to prevent the taking of human life by other human beings. Laws forbidding murder are virtually universal; and since such laws have from time immemorial been enacted by individual states, there is no reason to vest the right to make and enforce such laws in the union government. But hitherto states have reserved to themselves the right to commit mass-murder on other states. The American congress has the power "to declare war," and also necessarily the right to raise and maintain armed forces for the prosecution of war. In a union government to accord such a right to the constituent states would be analogous to giving to individuals within a state the right to commit murder when they chose. A union government must therefore forbid to the constituent states the right to raise or maintain armed forces or to declare war. 

The laws of virtually all states allow to the individual the right to kill in self-defense. Most plans for world government allow to states an analogous right to defend themselves against invasion. This is a dangerous right, for the difference between attack and defense is not always easy to determine; and the existence of forces armed for defense is a constant subtle temptation to aggressive action. A properly organized world government need grant no such right. No state can attack another without an armed force. A world government need only prohibit the raising of armed forces and enforce the prohibition. A world government should make this prohibition absolute; and to give that government the power to stop any breach of this prohibition at its source, it should also have the right to regulate the manufacture and distribution of firearms and implements of war. 

A government such as that projected here, which shall include from the outset at least four-fifths of the human race, would not, if properly administered, have need of armed forces at all in the usual sense of that term. For such a union would be virtually impregnable from attack; and a proper administration of the internal affairs of the union would eventually give such great satisfaction to all groups and individuals within the union as to preclude any concerted rebellion or insurrrection. Nevertheless it is doubtful if a union government I 2

would survive long enough to achieve so satisfactory an internal condition unless it were prepared at the outset to wield force. At the beginning at least there would be local dissatisfaction and local resentment of unfamiliar limitations of economic autonomy. The notion that force is an effective means of achieving desired objectives is deeply ingrained in the human mind; and it would take generations and perhaps centuries of experience with other methods to eradicate the notion. Inevitably groups within the union would be tempted from time to time to try resorting to force and to bootleg the implements for using force, unless they were confronted with the prospect of a counterforce too great for them to overcome. 

So a union government must have the right to raise and maintain armed forces that can act as a police patrol of the entire union. Such forces must be centrally controlled. Moreover they must be so distributed through the union as to make impossible their seduction into local feats of insurrection. Specifically, no armed force should ever be posted in the area in which it was raised. For in the event of local dissatisfaction, an armed force native to the locality would be naturally sympathetic with the disaffected group, and worse than useless in keeping such a group in order. Rather, each armed force must be posted in an area where its members will have no more than general human sympathy with the inhabitants, so that their primary loyalty to the union will not be in danger of being undermined. They then will be able to act as impersonal upholders of the law and of the union to which they all belong.

Finally, it goes without saying that the armed forces of the union. should never be used for aggressive action against states outside the union. Indeed it should be an ironclad law that armed forces of the union should never, as armed forces, pass beyond the boundaries of the union for any purpose whatsoever. A union of four-fifths of humanity would be too strong and too fully supplied with raw materials to have any real need of such a right. The possibility of the invasion of the union would be almost inconceivably remote; and if it should by chance occur, the right to repel the invasion is the only right the union would need for its full protection. The right to take its troops beyond its own borders might encourage thoughts of aggression against the weak but conceivably rich nations outside the union. And any actual aggression would create undying resentment among the victims of the aggression, and also, more likely than not, sharp criticism and even insurrection within the union itself. seem.

The ideal to be achieved is a condition under which armed forces of any sort are unnecessary. This is not so utopian an ideal as it may 

Individuals or groups of individuals pit themselves against the society in which they live only when some deep internal dissatisfaction, a sense of oppression or want, drives them to a form of desperation. 

A skilful government will so coordinate the factors of human experience as to make possible a virtually universal sense of satisfaction among the governed. This sense of satisfaction is the only secure 13

foundation for a stable state. In some respects all individuals are alike. 

For example, they all want enough to eat and an equal opportunity with others to acquire the material satisfactions. A stable government must have the power to guarantee them this. In other respects individuals vary. And a stable government must leave them comparatively free to seek these individual satisfactions, each for himself. A government which has learned where to control and where to leave free, so that each may achieve a full sense of satisfaction, will be unshakable and have virtually no need of force to maintain order among its inhabitants.

The ultimate goal of a union such as that projected here is the unification of the entire human race. So every effort should be made to encourage any nation outside the union to come in, and to come in voluntarily and gladly. It behooves a union government therefore to treat such outside nations with the utmost cordiality and hospitality, respecting their rights and privileges and possessions scrupulously, and setting up no barriers to free intercouse and travel between itself and them. A union of four-fifths of humanity need have no fear, on any count, of the other fifth. And if the union made a success of its own internal economy and created no feeling of antagonism among those outside, the outsiders would very quickly find that their own best interests demanded their incorporation in the larger whole. 

To summarize the features of this projected union government: it shall be representative; shall recognize and so far as possible leave unaltered existing national governments; shall not be established until it shall comprise at least four-fifths of humanity; shall allow the free movement of people and goods across all internal boundaries; shall have a common coinage; shall have a single administrative head; shall guarantee freedom of speech, press and religion to all its inhabitants; shall, so far as is possible without gross interference with national governments, allow freedom of action to all its inhabitants; shall have centralized control of international commerce and of the exploitation and conservation of raw materials; shall prohibit to the constituent states the right to raise armed forces; shall regulate the manufacture and distribution of firearms and implements of war; and shall itself raise and maintain an armed force with centralized control which shall be so distributed as to serve as an effective police patrol of the entire union. Finally, this union government shall treat all states remaining outside the union with absolute benevolence and cordiality, that the union may grow at last into a unified commonwealth of man. 14

Many people conceive of government as restraint. They look upon a ruler, whether that ruler be one man or a representative body, as being concerned primarily with saying, Don't. Laws are thought of as prohibitions. And in general, government is felt to be a bridle and a whip, limiting and directing the freedom of individuals. 

Such a concept of government has deep cause for its existence in the experience of humanity. All too often rulers have been drivers. 

The most famous body of laws in the western world, the Mosaic decalogue, is almost entirely a series of sentences beginning "Thou shalt not."

At some time or place nearly everything that it is possible for human beings to do has been forbidden. And hardly a government has existed that has not concerned itself very largely with forcing one group of the governed, by direct means or by subtle, to serve the interests of another group. The world is nearly free now of overt slavery. 

But subtler and less honest forms of slavery are in existence in most parts of the so-called civilized globe. 

But government of the whole for the benefit of the part, concerning itself largely with restraints and prohibitions, is not the ideal government. Indeed, in a deeper sense, it is misgovernment. On the contrary a true government would have very little need of restraints and prohibitions. True government is not primarily restraint at all, but coordination. A true government would not content itself with the all too easy process of telling the governed what they must not do, but would endeavor to show to the governed how to go about doing what they wanted to do. There is a deeply rebellious streak in human beings: tell them that they must not do a thing, and often that will be the one thing they feel impelled to do. But human beings will feel gratitude and love for the agency that shows them how to win satisfaction for the desires that plague them. A government of prohibitions and restraints is a government with the seeds of death at its inmost core. A government that guided men to their own objectives would be unshakable. A government approaches the ideal in direct proportion as it is able to give to the governed a sense of complete personal freedom.

Governments must however be made up of individuals. Individuals have specific desires and will tend always to take the nearest road to the fulfilment of those desires. Individuals to whom the government of a people is entrusted will be sorely tempted to use their position to achieve by short-cut methods their own personal objectives. And so subtle and devious is the human mind that even the most sincere and unselfish individual will find ways and means of achieving his own ends by acting in what he firmly believes to be the interests of others. 

Rare indeed is the human being to whom his own motives are an open book.

So any government, if it is to have a fair chance of being a true and successful government, must be so organized as to make impossible IS

its perversion to the unrecognized ends of the individuals that comprise it. It is conceivable that an absolute monarch might be so truly depersonalized, so completely an embodiment of the people he ruled, as to be able personally to carry out a true government of his people. The 

Chinese sage Lao-tze wrote, "The wise-man's heart is not his own heart, it is the hundred families' heart." And that some few such individuals actually exist there can be no doubt. 

On the other hand, human history has a hundred examples of the tyrant ruler for one of the wise-man. So it is imperative, if a world government is to be established and maintained, that the opportunities for tyranny in that government be as nearly as possible eliminated. A system that makes tyranny impossible will not seriously impede the wise-man, if good fortune should place such a person in a governmental position. But a system that has no inherent check on tyranny will be all too likely to result in a governmental control that bars the wise-man or even executes him. For no one is so implacably the enemy of tyranny as the man who thinks and feels, not for himself, but for the multitude.

So a world government should be a government of checks and balances. It should be elaborate enough in structure to make impossible the growth of any one part into a tyrannous minority. Moreover it should be so constructed and so managed as to attract the type of individual that naturally thinks in terms of other people besides himself and to repel those who see any occupation as means to their own ends.

A world government should also be one which tended increasingly to think in terms of humanity rather than in terms of lesser divisions of humanity. Hitherto our superpersonal loyalties have had but a comparatively narrow range. Nations and churches have been the widest units intelligible and emotionally real to most human beings. 

As a consequence, human antagonisms have been encouraged, often quite unintentionally, as the other side of these wider loyalties. 

Patriotism is usually more than love for one's own nation; more often than not it includes a latent and easily excited hatred for other nations and a tendency to think of other nationals as something less than human. Religious loyalties have had much the same result. The 

Christian crusaders of the middle ages thought of all Mohammedans as servants of the fiends of hell.

So a world government, to be successful, must have a means of checking and gradually obliterating the tendency to think of human beings in compartments. To do this it must recognize the existence of those compartments, lest by attempting to suppress them by fiat it should merely intensify their self-awareness, as the persecution of the Jews has intensified the Jewish race-consciousness. On the other hand, a world government should treat all these multifarious groups alike and in such fashion as to make a sense of the organic oneness of humanity penetrate past their isolationism.

The means of achieving a true government for all humanity can- 16

not be discovered overnight. It requires a careful study of governmental forms and of the results achieved by those governmental forms all over the earth. We of the United States are familiar with one type of government in which a system of checks and balances is a principal foundation-stone. But the American Constitution has not been completely successful even among Americans. To build a government for the world, one must also make careful study of such other systems as the imperial Chinese government, the absolute communistic monarchy of the Incas, the Russian soviet system, and the many and often highly successful systems of tribal government among primitive peoples of both past and present. Each may have its own particular feature to offer. And it is well to consider very seriously any factor that is common to them all.

In making such a study, however, it is necessary to bear in mind that in the past two centuries radical changes have been effected in the conditions of human life. These changes have not, of course, altered the basic elements of human nature. But our methods of providing for our material needs and our understanding of the structure and functioning of the environing universe necessarily have profound effects on our patterns of behavior. So a study of systems of government that have their roots or their whole period of existence in the past must take account of the conditions under which they originated and operated; and all elements that owe their existence to past conditions must be recognized and discounted. A world government is of the future. It must be built to accord with environmental conditions that are only just beginning to take clear shape. 

Meanwhile I am offering, as a suggested system for world government, the Union Constitution in the following pages. It has, I have no doubt, many serious faults. For one thing, it follows rather too closely the pattern of the American Constitution. I do not expect or wish that it should be more than a basis for discussion. But it does throw into specific and more or less concrete form, and so makes more intelligible, the abstract governmental principles and propositions here presented.

It will be noted that I have followed the American Constitution in predicating a tripartite government, setting up the legislative, administrative and judiciary functions as independent but mutually limiting bodies.

I have also predicated a bicameral legislature, but one which is essentially different in certain respects from the American legislature. The power to originate legislation is vested in one chamber only, what I have called the Legislative Council; and the other chamber, the Advisory Council, is given the veto power that under the American Constitution is vested in the President. 

The Legislative Council is conceived of as strictly representative and based on numerical population. The purpose of this, and of vesting the power to originate legislation in this body only, is to throw the emphasis on humanity as an aggregate of individuals rather than on 17

any existing compartmentization of humanity. So it is hoped that the welfare of all humanity, rather than of any faction of human beings, will be the primary concern of the Council; and this is further guaranteed by stipulating that laws enacted by the Council must be by a two-thirds vote of the total body. 

The existence of national groups is recognized in the Advisory Council, and each group is set on a par with every other. Veto power is given to this Council, that it may be impossible for the Legislative Council, by mere force of large units of population, to impose on the entirety legislation that would be inacceptable to a majority of smaller national or racial groups. On the other hand the very existence of the 

Council is made optional, and the governmental structure so conceived as to make possible the eventual dying out of the Advisory Council, so that national groups may not be perpetuated by having a necessary function in the government. It seems safe to assume that if the time should ever come when the self-awareness of national groups had so died away as to eliminate this voluntary Advisory Council, the equal representation in the Legislative Council, together with the discretionary powers of the World Court, would be sufficient to ensure just legislation.

The administrative powers are vested in a single individual and his appointees, primarily that the union may have a visible single head to symbolize its unity. The right to elect this individual is given to the 

Legislative Council, because the function of the Administrator is to carry out the will of that Council; and it is stipulated that the election must be by a three-quarters vote, in order that the Administrator chosen may be surely acceptable to a large majority of the union. 

The somewhat elaborate structure/predicated for the administrative department is given simply as a specific statement of one possible method of administration. Underlying it is the principle that an administrator can act justly and wisely in the degree that he is himself impersonal in his attitude toward the group over which he administrates. This is the underlying principle of the city-manager type of city government, a method which has been found extremely successful. 

Certain it is that a world government must be guarded as strictly as possible from any danger of local favoritism in its administration. 

The system predicated for the support of the Legislative Council, the Central Administrator and the World Court out of a common fund is intended to discourage from governmental aspirations such individuals as would be inclined to see in governmental positions a means to self-benefit, and to emphasize a sense of community and singleness of purpose among the principal governmental functionaries. This objective, I am convinced, is sound; but there might well be discoverable other more effective means of achieving it. The maximum limit set on expenditures for self-support is designed for the same purpose.

It must be admitted that even the most skilfully designed system of government is not immune to perversion. In the last analysis a 18

government is only as good as the men and women that comprise it. 

No mere piece of paper can guarantee the morality of human beings. 

Humanity must depend at last for its welfare, its harmony, its very survival on its own integrity. There are laws built into the nature of things that will inevitably be obeyed, from which there is no reprieve. In the last analysis government is no more than humanity adapting its own behavior to these inviolable and inescapable cosmic laws. Living intelligence and the will to survive in human beings. themselves-it is these rather than any system or theory on which human government must finally depend. There are moments when thinking persons despair of human beings; but the moments pass. The very despair grows out of a dream of the good life which guarantees the survival-and eventually, in some sense, the transfiguration-of humanity.
