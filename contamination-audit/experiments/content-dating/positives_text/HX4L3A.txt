CENTURY and a hal ago the inhabitants of North 

America spent much time in forecasting the weather but little in recording its vicissitudes. Nevertheless, it was generally reported that the winter of 

1779-1780 was a bad one. Oldest inhabitants of the Jerseys spoke of it as the "Great Freeze." The snow lay four and five feet deep. 

The paths of Morristown, New 

Jersey, were pink with the blood of shoeless Continental soldiers.

Congress, of which great things were expected then as now, seemed to be unable to supply the small force wintering in Morris county with even the essentials of existence. Washington writes that for several days the men would be without bread, for several days without meat, and often without either. Poorly fed and clothed the Continental force found what shelter it could in quickly constructed huts. Snow sifted on the sleeping soldiers at times.

In New York Town the British had a substantial community to live upon. Yet the terror of the winter was felt when the day after Christmas the British commander, Sir Harry Clinton, embarked with 8.000 troops for the southern campaign. Storms battered the fleet; ships foundered.

Of General Knyphausen 

When Clinton sailed away he left in charge of the British forces in and about New York one Gen eral, the Baron Knyphausen. The 

Baron had come over in June, 1776, at the head of 12,000 German troops purchased by England for export to the North American colonies. He was just a decade short of three score years and ten, a seasoned traveler on the Continent, having fought in various 

European wars for value received.

In 1776 impassioned patriotic platitudes were the rule. It is refreshing to learn from Lafayette that on the voyage to America Knyphausen was astounded at the distance. Looking up from his cards he frankly asked the ship's captain, "Captain, ain't we hab sailed past America?" La Fayette has preserved for us a bit of the human side of the German general.

During the winter of 1779-1780, however, La Fayette's interests

were chiefly in exterminating Knyphausen's mercenaries and their employers. La Fayette was at the 

Court of France trying to persuade young Louis XVI to send

French troops to the aid of young 

America.

Young America was in need of reemorcements. When the spring opened there were but 7,000 Continental troops between Canada and the Chesapeake. In June, Washington had under his immediate command only 3760 fit for duty.

In addition of course there the militia, an unstable body of troops that fought magnificently when gathered together and organized, but unless they heard shooting they often evidenced a distinct lack of interest in the 

Revolution. When it came time to fight they had to be assembled from the valleys and from over the hill and far away-tar barrels must be lighted and signal gune fired. Staunch farmers were delighted to get down benind the stone walls of their farms and take a pot shot at the British, but they were not enthusiastic about getting too far from those farms. 

However, America was a land of magnificent distances full of locally belligerent farmers. These factors were as important as drilled troops 

Currency Unstable 

Perhaps it is fortunate Washington was unable to assemble larger forces. As it was, his armies exhausted. the neighboring larders and the War Department could not procure provisions to forward to him. The Continental currency had so depreciated that "a waggon full of money would scarce buy a waggon full of provisions." The men's pay was in arrears. To add to the confusion the British issued counterfeit Continental money which served the double purpose of obtaining credit where it was honored, and casting discredit and suspicion upon all Continental currency where its falsity was discovered. In May, 1780 two Continental regiments stationed at Morristown mutineed. Poor pay and worse food, sickness and cold had ruined their morale. The soldiers declared that provisions must be forthcoming or they would hunt them at the point of the bayonet. Their officers prevailed upon them to return to their quarters. The balance of the Jersey Brigade was at Connecticut Farms, East of the Passaic River. The stage was set for a pleasant June day in Colonial America. Came the dawn. 

As the sky brightened the British moved on Elizabeth Town. 

Colonel Dayton had placed a guard of a dozen men at a junction of the road before that place. 

As the dark mass of the British troops loomed in the half light, the Jersey guard challenged, fired one volley and fled. They had probably been ordered to "retire," but "fled" probably describes their withdrawal more accurately. When a dozen shoot at thousands there is little time for strict observance of phraseology. New Jersey now took a hand, seven hours being lost bridging a marsh. Colonel Dayton withdrew his 300 men from Elizabeth Town.

The British entered in shining array and in full force they paraded the streets of the town. We can sympathize with Governor Livingston: "I thought Elizabeth Town would be an improper place for me. I therefore retired towards

The British were led by guides along the main road to Connecticut Farms, the old Galloping Hill road. 

Colonel Dayton fell back on the 

Farms, fighting in good order as he withdrew. The countryside was alarmed and astir. The militia were gathering. About a quarter of a mile in front of the Farms sixty militia made a stand on the rising ground just beyond the West 

Branch of the Elizabeth River.

The marshy ground prevented the British from turning this point of irate resistance. They had to force their way straight through. and this they could do only after some delay during which field piece proved superior to muskets. Arriving at Connecticut Farms, Day-

save ton made no attempt to stem the red tide there, but hoped to the village from destruction by retiring immediately. On the high ground beyond the Farms Maxwell waited, and there the Jersey Brigade was reunited. 

Meanwhile, Captain Dayton's letter to General Washington had had its effect. As the news of the 

British move reached Springfield, the alarm was given to the countryside: on Prospect Hill in the rear of Springfield the eighteenpounder signal gun was fired and a tar barrel on a pole blazed, to create a pillar of smoke to lead the militia to the battle. When the dispatch bearer reached Morristown Washington put the army in motion towards Springfield. At "½ after P. M." (the scribe wrote "A. M.", then realized that it was past noon and changed it to "P.M.") Washington wrote to an officer nearby to spread the intelligence and then to march "as expeditiously as you can (consistent with the men's health) with the detachment under your command and will all the militia that will join you for Chatham." Washington has in mind the broken health of the soldiers after their hard winter; the militia are to be assembled as the ball of soldiers rolls along. 

While the troops were gathering, Maxwell and Dayton held the 

British "near three hours" at the high ground just west of Connecticut Farms. The British finally brought up 1,500 re-enforcements and were then able to resume their advance. The Jersey troops crossed the East Branch of the 

Rahway River into Springfield. A field piece covered the crossing. 

The British learned that Washington had put his forces in a strong position on the Short Hills beyond Springfield. The long, tiring, sultry day was drawing to a close. 

The expected horde of mutinous deserters from the Continental side had failed to materialize. The 

British started their retreat. 

Falling back to Connecticut 

Farms the British let loose the dogs of war upon that insignificant hamlet. "Eight or ten dwellings besides stores, shops and outhouses and then the church edifice" were pillaged and burned.

The Farms as a village was wiped out. During the conflagration OCcurred the unhappy Caldwell incident.

The Caldwell Incident 

Rev. Caldwell was Chaplain of 

Dayton's regiment. His sermons stressed both Faith and Works. 

He had and taught Faith in the cause of freedom. In the language of the day he encouraged the Continentals to give the King's men "the Works." When the fighting drew near the Farms that moruing Caldwell besought his good wife to retire within the American lines. True to the home she so ably managed she determined to remain in the little house. She felt that her presence would be a protection to the house and its contents. She looked to Providence and chivalry for her own safety.

Report had it that she served refreshments to several British officers who appeared. In the course of the fighting, however, a redcoated soldier jumped over the back fence and ran towards the house. Mrs. Caldwell was in a back bedroom with her three small children. Caldwell's little boy ran to the window crying "Let me see him!" The man shot Mrs. 

Caldwell twice. She died. The man ran off. Just before the house was burned her body was removed. An unhappy incident. Some say that the red coat was a former servant of the Caldwells and took this opportunity to settle a private grudge. 

At any rate it was a madman's act. 

What can be said for the burning of the little village? The British claimed that the villagers had shot at them. This is not unlikely. 

Any man who had or could bor-

row a gun was a soldier. The 

Minute Man who single-handed takes pot-shots at the foe is a hero in the eyes of some, a guerilla in the eyes of others. The destruction of innocent lives and property will always be condemned. Yet it is hard to believe that it will ever be unusual in time of war. In this case the King's men, both tried and true and bought and paid for, had had little sleep, no rest; had marched miles thru dust and swamps, under fire; had seen their popular young leader mortally wounded; and now, unsuccessful, they were retracing painful steps thru a sultry Jersey 

June afternoon. Washington wastes no time arguing the propriety of the British action. In his report to

Congress he merely states that the 

British retired “*** having burnt in the course of the day, according to their common mode of warfare, a Meeting House, several dwelling houses and barns."

The Second Engagement 

At five o'clock in the afternoon of the 22nd of June a spy returned

from Elizabeth Town and reported to General Greene at Springfield as follows: 

"General Clinton with the whole

British army was to be in motion that evening; 3,000 were to embark and sail up the Hudson; the rest of the army was to march up the west side of the Hudson, starting out through Elizabeth 

Town and Newark, thus cutting 

Washington off from West Poin..

The British had learned of Washington's march towards Pompton.

An advance party of the British had already been thrown out towards Elizabeth Town and 2 of its men captured." 

Immediately Greene wrote this information to Washington, saying that while it was clear that a move was to be expected the route could not be known. He adds, "P. S. I am afraid the ac of the prisoners being taken is premature." 

In his letter to Washington 

Greene says, "The Gentleman (the spy) left the enemy at 3 o'clock this afternoon and appears to be in great trepidation." The spy's profession has never been known as a restful one. The "gentleman" had just returned from the heart of the enemy camp with its dangers still in mind. He felt a bit weak. He returned to a sceptical commander. Note the post script to the letter. The "gentleman" probably put in a bad afternoon and evening, for in another letter to Washington at 10 o'clock that night Greene says he doubts the word of Mr. the spy, and has told him that treachery means death. However, the spy's words were vindicated to a certain extent: the two prisoners captured according to the spy's report were brought in but were "so uproa iously drunk and filfthy that they could not or would not give any information." 

Apparently the horsemen carrying Greene's letters had some difficulty in locating the Commanderin-Chief for Washington's reply to
