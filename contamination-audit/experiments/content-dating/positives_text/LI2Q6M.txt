September 4, 1958 

Dear Governor Clement: 

The year 1957-58 has been a milestone in Tennessee State Government. A complete report on the financial activities of State Government reflects clearly that Tennessee's progressive policies have been soundly based and that the taxpayers have received a full measure of services for each dollar paid into the State Treasury.

Significant among the facts brought out in this report are the following: 

Over $100,000,000 of State tax revenue-more than one-third of the total collections-was spent in our educational program. 

A highway program was placed under construction equal in magnitude to any combined two-year program previous to your administration.

Realized savings from appropriations, resulting from economical operations, were sufficient to permit transfer of $4,125,000 to the Bond and Construction Fund in lieu of issuing Mental Health Bonds in a like amount.

A $5,000,000 increase in revenues despite a general recession which caused financial problems in most of our sister states. 

I have attempted herein to present in a simplified manner the financial record of State Government for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1958. I trust that this report will acquaint interested citizens with the financial requirements of conducting state programs and providing services.

Income of Tennesseans Increasing.. 

During the past twenty years, personal income of Tennesseans has increased over 300 per cent. 

Per capita income in the year 1937, a reflection of the economic condition, was only $328.00. 

Data for 1957, the latest available, places per capita income of Tennesseans at $1,387.00. 

Taxes, as a per cent of income, have shown little change during this period. In 1937 Tennesseans paid 5.3% or 5-3/10 cents of each dollar earned in state taxes. In 1957 Tennesseans paid 

5.6% or slightly over 51/2 cents of each dollar earned for the support of state government.

How We Compare With Our Neighbors 

State taxes amounted to $271 million in 1957-58-approximately $78.00 for every person in Tennessee. In only four Southern States do the citizens pay a smaller per capita tax than do Tennesseans. 

Tennessee's revenue collections for the past fiscal year represent a $5 million increase over 1956-57. More Than 60% of Revenue

COMES FROM TWO SOURCES 

The largest single producer of state revenue is the Retail Sales Tax-amounting to one-third of all collections. The Gasoline and related motor vehicle taxes, combined, accounted for another one-third of the collections-leaving all other sources to produce only one-third of state revenues. 

Tennessee does not levy either a personal income tax, real property tax or a severance tax, which accounts for a large portion of the tax receipts in many states. 

In most states an individual's tax is based on a combination of three theories: 

What one earns 

What one spends 

What one owns

Taxes on individuals in Tennessee, to the contrary, are largely based on what one spends.

CW and Counties SHARE IN STATE TAXES 

In excess of $40 million of the tax revenues paid to State government during the past year were immediately returned to local governments. 

Under a sharing program, cities and counties receive a pro rata portion of collections from the sales, gasoline, motor fuel, alcoholic beverage, beer, income and gross receipts taxes. Payments to cities and counties amount to 15 cents of each dollar of taxes collected. 

Taxes shared in this manner are paid to local governments either monthly or quarterly, and except for the gasoline tax may be used for any purpose desired by the city or county. In addition to these direct payments, Tennessee aids local governments in the support of educational, highway, health and other programs.

Than One-Third of Jares GO TO EDUCATION 

Educational expenditures from state taxes amounted to $110,000,000 in 1957-58-consuming in excess of one-third of all taxes paid into the State Treasury. An additional $8,000,000 was expended from federal aid and other sources, allowing for a total education program of $118,000,000. 

Financial support for education has been greatly increased under the leadership of this administration. The expenditures in 1957-58 represent an increase of $41,000,000 over the expenditures for 1952-53. Indicative of the support given to this program is the fact that more money has been appropriated for education during the past six years from the state level than was spent from 1900 to 1950-the first half of this century. 

Educational programs may be generally divided into two categories: (1) Public School education, grades 1-12; and (2) Higher and special education-our colleges, universities and schools for the handicapped. Traditionally state government has assumed full responsibility for financing higher and special education. Within recent years an increasingly larger share of the cost of public school education has been borne by the state. GRADE

More than $100,000,000 was expended for highway improvements during 1957-58-encompassing a highway construction program larger in magnitude than any two-year program prior to 1953. 

Tennessee's program revolves around the nation-wide interstate program of highways but recognizes the need for secondary roads to move traffic to and from the interstate routes. 

Tennessee's construction program, therefore, can be classified under two general categories: (1) 

Rural and Secondary Roads, entailing the expenditure of $21,500,000; and (2) Primary and Interstate Roads where in excess of $70,000,000 of new construction was instituted in 1957-58. 

Over and above this program, Tennessee shares 3¢ of the 7¢ gasoline tax with cities and counties amounting in 1957-58 to $27,800,000, which was available for highway construction at the local level.

HIGHWAY SERVICES

Jove Bey Services 

State government, unlike private enterprise, does not operate on a profit motive. With notable exceptions-Game and Fish

Commission, Hotel and Restaurant Inspection, etc.-there is little relationship between the level of services and any given revenue source. 

PUBLIC EDUCATION 

There are, however, certain tax sources which are dedicated toward the support of certain services to insure their maintenance at minimum levels. Probably the best known dedicated revenues are the sales and tobacco taxes which partially support the educational system, and the gasoline and motor vehicle taxes primarily earmarked for highway purposes.

Because there is little or no relationship between the needs of welfare recipients and the amount of collections from a tax source, for example, available funds are budgeted in an attempt to provide relative support to all services. 

HIGHWAYS AGRICULTURE CONSERVATION 

The fiscal program, as transmitted to the 1957 General Assembly, recognized unmet needs in certain services, and additional funds were made available toward meeting these needs. Teachers' salaries were increased, continuing the effort to provide adequate remuneration for educational personnel; more highway funds were provided to keep pace with the nationwide program of interstate highway construction; and greater emphasis was placed on treatment of the mentally ill and programs for inmates of correctional institutions.

Financial Administration 

Tennessee's financial policies established during this administration has brought recognition to our State and resulted in savings to the taxpayer. The early resolve of this administration to engage in bond financing only according to a planned schedule with adequate funds being set aside for debt retirement, coupled with other progressive financial policies, earned for our State a “AA” bond rating. This rating has broadened the market for our bonds and thereby permitted their sale at a smaller interest rate.

The passage of legislation during the 1955 General Assembly creating a single State Building Commission and the 1957 General Assembly's permission to use General Fund Surplus in lieu of authorized bonds have proven to be important financial advances. 

The single state authority charged with the responsibility of supervision of State construction projects allows for more uniform building standards and elimination of unnecessary frills in new Close supervision of all work to insure compliance with specifications permits the Building Commission to insure that buildings and structures meet high standards of construction. structures. 

During the past fiscal year $9,125,000.00 of authorized bonds were cancelled and a like amount of surplus was transferred to the Highway and Bond and Construction Funds. The savings from this transaction were made possible by economies in operation of various state programs and an increase in revenues over and above the amount budgeted. 

Tennessee's outstanding debt on June 30, 1958 amounted to $114,368,500.00. The accumulated Sinking Fund Balance amounting to $7,700,396.03 reduces this total to a net debt of $106,- 668,103.97.

Tennessee's debt, compared with tax receipts, reflects a most favorable picture. The net debt of $106 million represents only 39% of one year's revenue from taxes. Close adherence to sound financial policies may soon earn a higher financial rating for our State.

Revenues & Expenditures 

State government is probably the largest single business in Tennessee. In two programs of government-education and highways-expenditures for each exceeds $100,000,000.00 annually. In another program-welfare-expenditures exceed $50,000,000.00 annually. 

For the most part, however, state government is not composed of large functions; rather services are provided on a broad scale to every citizen of the state. Our judicial system, our legislative system, the problems of labor, conservation of natural resources, economic development, public and mental health and establishment and operation of correctional institutions, all form a part of state government. 

State government has grown in some proportion to the growth of Tennessee and with technological advances of science and industry. The responsibility of the executive is not only one of maintaining existing services on an adequate level, but also of developing new programs as the situation demands. This is perhaps best illustrated by the advances made in public health and education during this administration and the concurrent establishment of a Department of Mental Health in 1953 and an Advisory Commission on Atomic Energy in 1957. 

Among the levels of government-federal, state, local—there are cooperative programs of service and plans for sharing of revenues. During the past year Tennessee received $100,000,000 from the Federal Government in partial support of the highway, welfare, health and other programs. 

During this same year, Tennessee disbursed approximately $100,000,000.00 to local governments for support of education, highways and other services while at the same time returning to cities and counties $40,000,000.00 of the tax collections as a direct grant. More than 50% of state tax collections, therefore, are expended for programs conducted by cities and counties.
