The Significance of Printing as a Social Force BY GLEN U. CLEETON Head, Department of Printing Carnegie Institute of Technology PITTSBURGH, PENNSYLVANIA MCMXL

The five-hundredth anniversary of the invention of printing from movable type is being celebrated in 1940 by international precedent. Perhaps we should be celebrating the thousandth anniversary of printing from separate characters, an art invented by Pi-Sheng in China about a thousand years ago. Or, perchance, it is the invention of the printing press that we should be celebrating. At any rate, the five-hundredth birthday of printing, as we now understand the art, is being celebrated. 

No one knows precisely the events that transpired five hundred years ago, for the historical evidence is obscure. But we do know that somehow the mechanics of bringing movable type, paper, and ink together to produce multiple records became known sometime before the middle of the fifteenth century. 

We know, also, that Johannes Gutenberg played an important part in the initial stages of a movement that spread rapidly over the entire continent of Europe before the discovery of America. Today, therefore, we pay tribute to the discovery of a means of record and communication which has, more than any other tool of civilization, changed the lives of men. 

This booklet is a contribution to the celebration of the fivehundredth anniversary of printing. In its pages are recorded the words of an address presented before the members of the National Graphic Arts Education Guild, assembled in their annual convention in New York City, in July, 1939. The address has been reproduced in trade and professional journals, both in 3

America and abroad. It is being printed in its present form as a means of stimulating further thought on the main theme of the address; namely, the significance of printing as a social force. 

No attempt has been made to present the history of printing; others have performed that task too well to warrant repetition. 

Instead, emphasis is placed on the question: What part has printing played in the social development of our country? The story presented is sketchy, but sufficient evidence is offered to sustain the conclusion that no other invention has had the long and socially significant record as has the printing press. 

Much additional evidence supporting the contention that printing has gone hand in hand with social progress in America could be adduced. Not mentioned specifically in the following pages, but highly significant, is the fact that one of the first acts of the New England colonists was the procuring of printing equipment from England. Thus it was that the Stephen Daye 

Press, which was honored by the issuance of a commemorative stamp in 1939, arrived in Cambridge in 1639. Interestingly enough, the initial products of that press were the Freeman's Oath and the Bay Psalm Book-titles which in themselves testify to the social importance of printing in early America. 

Social changes of world-wide importance have occurred since the address herewith presented was delivered in July, 1939. 

Likewise, industrial and commercial changes in methods of recording and communicating are being made. These facts are mentioned because some of the statements contained in the address were prophetic in nature. No part of the address as originally delivered has been changed. The prophetic statements must stand or fall in the light of recent events and those yet to come. But the foresight, good or bad, therein displayed will in no way modify the main contention that printing has been a basic and powerful force in the world. That printing has served the ends of peace and democracy in the past is usually admitted. 

That peace and democracy will be capably served in the future by the art of printing is the hope of every sane mind in the world today.

THE SPINNING JENNY, the steam engine, the cotton gin, the telephone and telegraph, the gas engine, the electric motor, radio, and other inventions that you can readily name-all have had marked influence in modifying the mode of life of mankind and have indirectly had an effect on the culture of the periods following their introduction to general use. But no other major invention has had the long and socially significant record as has the printing press. Not only did the invention of the printing press inaugurate the movement which led to westward expansion and the colonization of America, but it continues to exist as a basic and powerful force in the world of today and it will maintain this place of importance in the world of tomorrow. 

Let us look at a few of the outstanding social movements in which printing has been a prime force. First of all, it is generally acknowledged by historians that the whole pattern of thought and action in Western Europe changed as a result of the introduction of printing in the fifteenth century. It is difficult to obtain a true picture of this fact by reading the average history of printing written by students of printing. The drama and romance in the story of this newly discovered process is obscured by tedious efforts to trace the footsteps of the founders-the Gutenbergs, Costers, Jensons, and Caxtons.

Admittedly, the spread of the printing press was no mushroom growth measured in terms of modern social and industrial movements. But consider for a moment the fact that in spite of 5

its introduction into a world almost wholly illiterate, more than a thousand printing presses were operating in Germany by the end of the fifteenth century. Little wonder that the influence of printing was felt in the sixteenth century and those which followed even down to the present.

It is quite possible that the failure of the public to rush pellmell to the New World which Columbus discovered and even the fact that the New World was named after another man came about because Columbus did not publish his experiences. 

Amerigo Vespucci did. When he returned from his voyage to what is now known as South America, he wrote an account of his experiences which got into print. Commenting on this incident as a major historical turning point, Roger Burlingame, in his challenging and informative book-March of the Iron

Men-states: 

"Forty years before, a strange, new machine had come out of

Germany. In 1464, it had entered Rome. Five years later there was one in Venice. In the fourteen seventies it had invaded 

France, Spain, Switzerland, the Low Countries and even outlandish England. Into one of these machines had gone the letters of Vespucci; out of it had come a multiple record. "In the Vosges mountains, there was a little monastic college called St. Die. It specialized in geography. In it had been written the Imago Mundi of Pierre d'Ailly which had turned the thoughts of Columbus westward. At the turn of the century, this college had acquired one of the new machines, a useful appurtenance to an educational institution. "Round the printing press in St. Die were grouped a number of ambitious men. We may find their counterparts in any modern publishing house: editor, publicity man, scout. The scout, Ringmann, picked up-in Italy, perhaps, or Paris for Ringmann was a traveling scout-copies of Vespucci's popular letters. 

They were in Italian which is to say the Tuscan dialect of Dante. He brought them back and translated them into Latin. 

This gave them standing as well as international circulation. 

Then the press agent, Waldseemuller, whose vocation was pro-

fessor of geography, wrote a startling blurb for the author which was included as a preface. "I do not see,' he wrote, 'what is rightly to hinder us from calling it (Vespucci's discovery) Amerige or America, i. e., the land of Americus, after its discoverer Americus, a man of sagacious mind. . ' "Vespucci died before this appeared. We have no record of royalties going to his estate. Through it, however, his name achieved immortality, an honor which belonged to Columbus who had no publicity man."

Used first as an agency of the Church, printing became the most powerful weapon in the battle to break the stranglehold of feudalism on culture and wealth. The Reformation with its hero in the person of Martin Luther would not have been possible if a new means of communication had not been made available. Religious malcontent, initiated by the leaders of the 

Reformation, was spread by the agency of the printed word, took new form as a rebellion against the established Church, and motivated emigration to the New World. This accounts for the settlement of the northern colonies, but the southern colonization was carried forward through what today would be recognized as promotional tactics.

Printed tracts done in the best advertising style of the times were used to recruit emigrants for the Virginia settlements. 

One of these documents, printed in London in 1609 and bearing the promising title "Offering Most Excellent Fruits by Planting in Virginia," is in the possession of the New York Public Library. Emigrants enticed by this pamphlet to cast their lot in the New World were so thoroughly "sold" by its contents that they persuaded a group of survivors (150 out of 900), who had boarded ship to return to England, to disembark and gamble their lives for unknown stakes. Says Burlingame: "The tired planters, with the smell of death still upon them, were induced to return to their miseries by a lot of ignorant adventurers whose sole information came from high-powered printed salesmanship. In all history, there can be no more arresting

evidence of the new force which had come into the world." 

The part played by the printing press in colonial development is too well understood to require more than passing comment. Its force has been immortalized by the work of the great printer and public leader, Benjamin Franklin. "If we were required to point out a precise moment at which our special American culture began. we could hardly do better than to mark the appearance of Franklin. If we must give credit to an inventor for our American civilization, it must go to him." "He was a product of the unspecialized age we have just examined in that he was a jack-of-all-trades. He violated the proverb by becoming master of at least half a dozen. Some of them he created. In the course of his life he was a printer, journalist, columnist, writer, salesman, advertising man, promotor, publicity expert, civic organizer, fireman, postmaster, librarian, military architect, quartermaster, soldier, legislator, diplomatist and statesman. He was also a scientific discoverer, a practical inventor, an amateur mathematician, a philosopher, an innovator in navigation, transport, hygiene and sanitation, city planning, street lighting, paving and cleaning, hospitalization and higher education. He was a wit, satirist, humorist and hoaxer, a political agitator, a maker of slogans, maxims and wall mottoes, a promoter of clubs, associations, secret societies, lodges; a good mixer, a vulgarizer of the arts and sciences, a lobbyist and a politician. He made considerable contributions to meteorology and medicine, he introduced an ideal of cleanliness into an era when bathing was still a lick without a promise and he was an athlete in a sportless age." Burlingame, March of the Iron Men. pp. 68-69.

Drawn together by the printed word, the American colonists rebelled successfully against the parent nation. Second only to the Pennsylvania rifle (developed by the then backwoods German settlers living in and around Lancaster, Pennsylvania -and having greater velocity, range, and accuracy than the crude muskets of General Gage's men) the printing press was the most effective weapon against Britain.

Time does not permit a discussion of great length on the printing press and the assistance it provided in the progressive expansion of the Colonies into a great nation. But we should not lose sight of the fact that printing made such monumental documents as Webster's dictionary and McGuffey's readers available to the masses. It requires no stretch of the imagination to recognize the probability that the envisioning and realization of a free public school system also depended on the art of printing. 

Nor may we overlook the possibility that the Civil War that resulted in a final Union of still somewhat independent commonwealths, which later became our nation, would not have come about so early in our history had there not been the rapid development of newspaper printing that preceded the Civil War. The telegraph, the cylinder press, and commercial imagination gave America cheap newspapers in the 40's and 50's. Sectional hates fanned by these new instruments burst into flame as a result of irritating incidents and the personality of a thoroughly misunderstood man. The powerful social influence of the newspapers directed by more calm-minded men might easily have brought Union without war. We know that today; a few far-sighted men, including Abraham Lincoln, knew it then.

But I dwell too long on printing in the world of yesterday. 

Hastily, then may I point to the development, with the aid of printing, of later nineteenth and early twentieth century phenomena such as widely circulated, low-cost books, public libraries, mass circulation magazines, and widespread periodical and direct mail advertising. The influence of each of these forces is evident to socially minded observers. 

You need only look about you to see clearly the significance of printing in the world of today. Imagine a world without printing if you wish to recognize its significance more clearly. 

Neither you nor I would wish to live in such a world.

Let us consider briefly printing and the world of tomorrow. 

Because of printing, the world of tomorrow will avoid the

collapse of civilization-come a war of world-wide proportions or any other equal catastrophe. The printed record of the elements of modern culture and technology exists in too great quantity, is too widely distributed and duplicated to make destruction possible. Reconstruction on the basis of printed records would be rapid even though half the world's population and its wealth in material form as found in great inventions were destroyed.

Printing will perpetuate the accomplishments of the world of today in the world of tomorrow. The fall of past civilizations and their disappearance in successive ages have been cited by dreary prophets as the cycle which the world must again eventually follow. But these dire prophets fail to take into account an important fact; namely, that printing, the agency of record and communication, was not a part of the Egyptian, Hebrew, Phoenecian, Greek, Roman, or Aztec civilizations. Nor do they recognize the startling fact that the world of today was founded on the very means of recording and communication that printing provides. 

Some of you who have followed me so patiently thus far are thinking, "But what of competing means of record and communication which are present in the world of today and forseeable in the world of tomorrow." These instruments in the form of photography, radio, television, (and I suppose we should include facsimile broadcasting although that is a form of printing) seem to me to offer little threat to printing in so far as it serves as a means of recording. The phonograph, micro-film, and photo-sound track are the most likely competitors here. As yet these are technically less adaptable than printing, and I see no likelihood that they will ever approach the flexibility of printing, especially for mass use.

The telephone and the telegraph extended the use of printing as a means of communication. Those who have watched radio develop assure me that it accentuates the need for direct mail and supplementary commercial forms and in these fields creates new needs for printing. That radio is affecting the functions of ΙΟ

newspapers in the modern world, only the newspapers themselves deny (this they do publicly, but admit privately). Here the answer, as I see it, is; newspapers must sooner or later undergo a change to admit of this new competition. Newspapers will, in the future, concern themselves less with spot news and will take on more of the character of daily news magazines-more analysis and discussion of the news, more features, more pictures. In fact, those changes are already taking place. Television has less to offer in the way of competition than radio, and facsimile appears to me to be only a spot news instrument. 

The greatest force that printing has to fear today is the force of armed dictatorship. Printing flourishes best in a democracy. 

This is not surprising in that it helped create democracy. That autocratic dictatorship fears the power of print is clearly shown by the modern dictator's efforts to control, stifle, censor, and otherwise restrict printing. The modern dictator recognizes printing as his natural enemy and well he may for when the history of the world of tomorrow is written it will reveal that printing was one of the weapons used to return the world to democracy. And wherever democracy exists, printing will play an important part just as it has during the past five centuriesthe most fruitful five hundred years in technical and cultural progress the world has known.
