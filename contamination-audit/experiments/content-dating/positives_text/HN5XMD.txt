The Laboratory Book 

1. Use, for drawings, heavy linen ledger paper, of standard size, provided with punch holes for fastening into manila cover. Always have on hand a clean cover into which drawings and notes may be bound. For descriptive writing and for answering of questions, an ordinary grade of writing paper may be used but it must be of standard size and also punched for binding. 

2. The drawing pencil should be HHHH or else No. 4. Keep it sharp with the pencil sharpener and have the lead pointed with sandpaper. All lines in the drawings are to be sharp and clear; no "broad gray lines" are permitted. Shading is to be done only with dotting. 

When putting in the dots avoid hammering the pencil. Do not press hard with pencil when drawing. 

3. Have a good red rubber eraser. Corn pith (art gum) erasers are not to be used. 

4. Always leave an inch margin at top and left side of page. 

Print proper general heading at top of page before beginning to draw and also put your own name in the lower right-hand corner of the page. Have name of object above, not below, each drawing. State the amount of magnification below the drawing. Make printing of reasonable size; not too large. 

5. Drawings should be so arranged on page that there will be no crowding. 

6. Labels are to be printed, not written. Answers to questions may be written. Be sure to form your printed letters correctly. Look in any book to see proper form of letters. Do not put capital letters into the middle of a word. Remember that capital I and capital J have no dot. 

7. All labeling is to be done directly, not by means of reference letters or numbers. Use a ruled dotted line (not a row of dashes) to extend from the printed word to the particular part of the drawing to which it refers. Every printed or written word is to be horizontal on the page. Have labels, unless it would be awkward, at the right of the drawing and not over 2 cm. away. Initial words of labels are to be aligned in a vertical row. 

8. The printed directions sometimes describe more work than the class will have time to do. Do what is called for by the instructor. 

If you find time for additional work, some can always be furnished. 

Students must, in no case, leave the laboratory before the closing bell rings. The last few minutes, when there is not time to undertake a new piece of work, may be profitably used in going over drawings, improving lettering, or in studying classifications. 

9. When books are to be handed in for inspection be sure to go over them carefully for spelling, lettering, and other matters. Have pages arranged in proper order. Clean up pages with eraser. Have cover clean. Bind loosely with string; have sheets fastened in so loosely that they can be readily turned over without tearing at the punch holes.

Use of Microscope 

1. When you lift or carry the microscope please handle it with extreme care. If it does not have curved handle arm you should take hold below, not above, the stage. 

2. Always return the instrument to its proper place and with low-power lens down. This is an absolute requirement. Students' grades will be reduced when this requirement has not been met. 

3. Use low power before using high power. Never use high power when low power will suffice. 

4. Be sure that the mirror is properly adjusted. Use the concave mirror, not the plane. Have the diaphragm open about one-eighth of an inch (3 mm.). Keep microscope at your left and book at your right (unless you draw with the left hand). 

5. Look down the tube with left eye (unless you draw with left hand). Keep both eyes wide open; do not squint or half-close one eye. 

6. Focus with the fine adjustment while you are looking down the tube. The left thumb and forefinger should be on the fine adjustment screw all the time that you are using the high power. Gently focus up and down to see clearly at all depths in the specimen. Examine the entire preparation and use the most typical part for your drawing. 

7. If the lenses are not clean they should be wiped with a clean cloth or lens paper. If water or other liquids get on the stage, wipe the stage dry. 

8. Do not put a slide on the stage or take one off when the highpower objective is down. The high-power objective should not be down, except when you are actually using it. Failure to follow this rule often means injured specimens or a smeared-up objective. 

9. When you put away the instrument at the close of the hour have low-power objective down. 

10. Push your chair or stool under the table and put away all materials before leaving the room at the close of the hour. Brush off any eraser-crumbs from the table. 

N. B. Every one of these rules is important. They should be read every day for the first two weeks of the course and occasionally thereafter. Students' grades for laboratory work will depend considerably upon the proper use of the microscope.

LABORATORY DIRECTIONS Preliminary Studies 1. CELLS OF EPIDERMIS OF ONION SCALE. Scratch, with a needle, a small square of 6 mm. in the skin of one of the scale leaves of an onion. Use the inner (concave surface) of the scale. Peel off this small piece of skin without getting any of the underlying substance. Place the material at once, outer surface up, in a drop of water on the center of a clean slide and cover it with a clean cover-glass. 

Read "The Laboratory Book", page 3, and "Use of Microscope", page 4 of this manual, before beginning your work. (a) Make drawing of a small part of the section, low power, showing 20 or more cells. Have the drawing accurate, i. e., make the cells correct in shape and fitted together just as they really are in the specimen; long diameter of cells is to be vertical on page. Only the cell walls are to be shown in the drawing, not the protoplasm. 

Make average length of cells about 20 mm. (b) Before making the next drawing lift up cover-glass, add a drop of iodin solution. The iodin stains the protoplasm. Draw accurately, under high power, a typical cell; long diameter to be vertical on page. Make the cell about 8 cm. long. Show cytoplasm by fine lines and dots; indicate the nucleus by fine dots close together. Do not hammer with the pencil to make the dotting, as the noise is very disturbing. Use an even, continuous, line for the nuclear membrane. 

Make a rather thick, continuous line for the cell-wall. Label all parts. 

Submit fully labeled drawing to instructor before considering it finished. Be ready to re-draw if your work it not neat and accurate. 

2. CELLS OF MOSS LEAVES. Put the upper, leafy, part of a moss plant into a drop of water on a clean slide. Separate the leaves from the stem and from each other by means of needles. Cover with a clean cover-glass. (a) Draw outline of a single leaf, low power, without showing any of the cells. Have long diameter of leaf vertical on page and apex up. (b) Draw ten or more cells, high power, with the leaf placed as before. Make the separate cells large, at least 8 mm in diameter. 

Show chloroplastids correctly. Note that the cell-wall is relatively thick. Be sure to give it the proper thickness by means of a heavy line. 

Do not use double lines for the cell-walls. Label: cell-wall, chloroplastids. Submit completed and labeled drawing for criticism.

1. CORN STARCH, (MAIZE STARCH). What shape are the grains? 

Do they cling together? Are they angular or rounded? Is the hilum (pronounced hye-lum) in the center or off at one side? Are there any lines of growth, or any radiating lines? If the specimen has been mounted for a time it will begin to go to pieces, numerous breaks and cracks appearing; do not draw specimen in this condition but make a fresh mount.

Under low power, the grains should appear about 1 to 3 mm. in diameter; make them this size in your drawing and have the high power drawings larger, about 9 mm. Put proper title above drawing and state magnification below. Show completed and labeled drawing to the instructor; do not submit unfinished work. After making drawing, treat specimen with iodin solution (a drop at edge of cover-glass) and watch the result, using low power. Note down in writing how the iodin affects the starch.

Write down answers to all questions in these directions, unless answers are shown by drawings. If you have only a little to write, put it on the same page with drawings; if there is much to be written, use a separate sheet (writing paper, not drawing paper).

7. WHEAT FLOUR. Make two mounts; one unstained, and the other stained with rather strong iodin solution. Flour, of course, contains other materials besides starch. The study is to be made under both low and high power but the drawings should be made with high power. Notice the portions of cell-wall, occasional parenchyma cells containing either starch or protein grains, pieces of bran, bits of hair from the "brush" at the end of the wheat grain, perhaps some fibres from the bolting cloth or from the flour sack, etc. In the iodin-stained section any protein grains will be colored yellow in sharp contrast to the blue coloration which the starch takes. Bran is yellow in the unstained specimen as well as after staining. In your drawing be sure to show all the various things which occur in addition to the starch; but draw more starch grains because these are the most numerous objects to be seen. Leave starch grains clear and shade the other things. This study is an important one and must be made with care. 

Label all of the various structures which you draw. Submit drawings to the instructor but be sure that you have first found and drawn all parts. Do not present for criticism a half-finished or an unlabeled drawing.

1. MICROSCOPIC STUDY OF TEXTILE FIBRES. Each of the following is to have thorough study under the microscope. Mount a small quantity in water, spreading out the material with needles. Study the section under low and high power, determining all the following points before you make any drawings. Write down the answers to the questions unless the answers will be shown by the drawings. Start this work on a new page. 

Are the fibers straight or twisted? Are they ribbon-shaped or cylindrical? Are they single or in parallel groups or strands? Do the fibers have a smooth edge or ragged? Are they composed of a number of short cells like shingles on a roof, or is each fiber a single long, narrow cell? Is the fiber hollow, i. e. with a lumen (cavity) and wall, or is it solid? If solid, is it or is it not composed of cells? 

If a lumen is present in the fiber is its diameter about the same as the thickness of the wall, or is it greater, or less? Does the fiber have any cross markings?

5. WOOD PULP. Mount a small bit of commercial wood pulp, or some paper towel which has been soaked in water for a few hours. 

Study as you did the textile fibers. Find the various structural elements of the wood such as fibers, ducts, tracheids, short parenchyma cells, etc. There will be complete fibres, broken fibers, irregular cell masses. Look for tracheids with bordred pits; these are a certain indication of coniferous wood such as spruce or pine. (If you do not remember about these tracheids with bordered pits, look them up in any college text-book of botany.) Cotton or linen fibers may be present if rags or paper were mixed with the wood at the paper mill. 

Draw representatives of the various structural elements; make the drawings large and distinct. Label each; state magnification. 

6. ANILIN SULPHATE TEST FOR LIGNIN. Apply a drop of anilin sulphate solution to material to be tested. If lignin is present a yellow color appears. Paper containing wood pulp does not always give the reaction because the chemical treatment in the process of papermaking may have altered the lignin, or a large amount of sizing or loading may interfere. A more accurate test for wood pulp in paper can be made by examination of teased bits of paper with the microscope. Use anilin sulphate test on the following; note down results: (a) Paper towel, (b) Quiz paper, (c) News paper, (d) Ledger or writing paper, (e) Shingle or board or match.

Cereal Grains

Cereal grains are the one-seeded fruits, or grains of plants of the Poaceae, or Gramineae, Grass Family. The grains are the most important foods of human beings and their cultivation is one of the chief industries of man. The leaves and straw, sometimes the grains as well, are fed to domestic animals. Other grasses are used for hay, forage, and grazing. Buckwheat, although a member of the Smartweed Family, may be considered with the grains, since its seeds are used for flour. 

Among civilized peoples, grains furnish a very large part of the food supply. In densely populated countries, as India, China, and Japan about 90 per cent of the food calories consumed are derived from grain, chiefly rice. All grains have about the same fuel value per pound, viz.: 

1600 calories. Wheat contains approximately 10 per cent protein, 1 per cent fat, 75 per cent carbohydrate,—the remainder being indigestible matter and water. The bran layer has valuable mineral salts and the water-soluble vitamine "B". The chemical composition of rice, barley, and rye is not greatly different from wheat. Indian corn has somewhat less protein and a little more fat than the other grains. *WHEAT. Triticum aestivum (pronounce Trit'i-cum es'tiv-um); original home in Syria, whence its cultivation spread in earliest times throughout western Asia and eastern Europe. Wild wheat had large kernels but produced only a small yield, because the plants did not "stool" freely. Cultivated wheat has larger heads, hence produces more grain. The spikelets of both wild and cultivated wheats have four or five flowers; the glumes are about the same width as the lemmas. Wheat is now cultivated in cooler parts of north temperate regions, also in Argentina and Australia, and is grown to a considerable extent in the highlands of India. Wheat and barley are the grains referred to as "corn" in ancient writings. Wheat is used chiefly as flour: white, whole-wheat, graham, gluten. Various "breakfast foods", or "breakfast cereals" are prepared from wheat, such as rolled wheat, cracked wheat, flaked wheat, gluten meal, middlings, shredded wheat, puffed wheat, etc. Wheat starch, wheat bran and shorts are among the other products. Wheat flour because of its gluten content, makes a light bread. The gluten is sticky, or tenacious, so that the gas bubbles of carbon dioxide formed by the yeast or the baking powder are held

Roots, Tubers, and Bulbs used as Food

These underground parts of plants furnish chiefly carbohydrate food but all of them contain a certain amount of protein as well. 

This ranges from 1 to 2 per cent. They contain very little fat, and are deficient in vitamines. Hence, persons on a "vegetable diet" need to include leafy vegetables which furnish some of the substances which these underground parts lack.

Common Edible Fruits 

Fruits contain carbohydrates, water, organic acids, and salts. They are well supplied with the water-soluble vitamine "B". Few fruits have any great number of calories per pound. A person would need to eat a great number of apples or oranges in a day to supply the necessary energy for body activity. The following have rather more calories than most fruits: banana, plum, peach, fig, olive. Some of the fruits named in this section are regarded commonly as "vegetables" rather than fruits; examples are the egg plant, tomato, peppers, squash, and pumpkin. 

DATE; a one-seeded berry, the fruit of the date palm, Phoenix dactylifera, a member of the Palmaceae, Palm Family. Native of 

Arabia, northern Africa and other warm regions in the vicinity of the 

Mediterranean Sea.

The banana is cultivated in all tropical lands. Many varieties are known. In India the term "plantain" is often used for the banana.

Moraceae, Mulberry Family.
