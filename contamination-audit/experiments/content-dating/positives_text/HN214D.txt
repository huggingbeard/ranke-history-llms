PREFACE The purpose of Our Animal Books is to motivate in the growing citizen, from his pre-school days to junior high school, an intelligent regard for his own pets and for the animals of his city, state, and country. He, however, rather than the animal, is the chief factor in each book.

The sequence of the series follows the child's developing interest in pets and in America's birds and mammals. Each book is a unit, but though it is complete in itself, it contributes to the progress of the series. The Primer, Fuzzy Tail, is devoted to the kitten, telling in story form just how a kitten should be fed, handled, and properly played with. 

Book One, Sniff, gives youthful owners similar instruction in the feeding, housing, exercising, and general up-bringing of a puppy, again through story medium. Book Two, Pets and Friends, is an introduction to other pets attractive to a little child. 

Book Three, The Pet Club, as its title implies, is given to the care and consideration of animals used for instruction purposes in elementary classrooms and nature laboratories, the child, again, the chief factor in the narrative. Book Four, On Charlie

Clarke's Farm, tells the experiences of a boy and his family and friends in their daily contacts with animals of farm, field, and forest. Book Five, Our 

Town and City Animals, gives in dramatic narrative what the child should know regarding the animal dwellers of his town or city. Book Six, Paths to 

Conservation, points toward participation in the iii

Miss Wright arranged for the trip. At the club meeting that week, she gave them a little talk to prepare them for the visit. 

She said that just a little over one hundred years ago there was not an animal protective society in the whole world. In 1824, the first society was organized in London, England, and was called The Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals. There was no society in the United States until 1866, when The American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to 25

Animals was organized in New York City. Soon other societies were formed in other cities, such as Boston, Philadelphia, Chicago, and San Francisco. Today there is an animal society in every city in the United States, and in nearly every large city in the world.

TRAINING WILD ANIMALS 

Anti-cruelty societies are opposed to performing animal acts. 

First, the animals may have been cruelly treated while they were being trained. Animals, especially wild animals, will not do naturally what the trainer wants them to do. So he uses his whip and club and blank-cartridge revolver and other painful means to make his performers go through their acts. 

Second, when the animals are in the circus ring or on the vaudeville stage, the trainer never lets them forget that they must obey his commands.

They know if they disobey their trainer, they will be punished during the act in a way the audience cannot see or they will be punished after the act or both during and after the act. 

Third, animals, like men, women, and children, do not always feel like doing what they are expected to do. They may be sick. They may be tired. 

They may want to play. However sick or tired or unhappy they may be, they must perform. Animal acts, like all other acts in a circus or in a theater, run on schedule, and are shown two or more times every day. 

Fourth, in the largest circuses and in the best shows, the animals are fed properly to keep them well, and in some instances are housed properly. 

This is not true in all circuses and animal shows.

The monster curtains open, The fiddles creak and croon; 

And then I bow to the people

A lumbering baboon. 

I wonder why I do it? 

Why do the humans stare 

From even rows of shadow 

Behind the footlights' glare? 

Why do I go through my weary tricks 

On a table and a chair? 

They laugh and clap and giggle, They never seem to tire, For I am quite amusing 

As I dance upon a wire, Or leap, at my master's signal, Through golden hoops of fire. 

I cannot smile, like the people, I cannot speak at all; 

I pirouette insanely 

In the foolish carnival; 

Yet could I laugh, oh I would laugh

For I wonder why those people 

Sit in such even rows, And smile at my useless knowledge, Laugh at my mincing toes, And dream that they have wisdom! — 

How little a human knows! 

And why do they always gather 

In houses bright and hot,

When they might be out in the open 

In a place I've never forgot? 

Why do they live in a shell like this, And bid me share their lot? 

And why is my life a schedule, Run by rote and rule? 

I was not meant for theaters, I was not made for school;

I was not meant to caper here, A thing of ridicule! 

I was not meant to be the slave 

Of a man in a shiny suit, Or bring the golden dollars in, To stand up and salute; 

The good God put me in the world 

To be a happy brute! 

But at eight o'clock each evening, And at two in the afternoon 

The monster curtains open, The fiddles creak and croon; 

And I bow to the senseless people 

A sensible baboon!

She said that running horses have been tripped by wire, and have been thrown over cliffs and killed; that lions have often been injured by a wire-meshed floor charged with electricity to make them ferocious; that wild animals are shot at with blank cartridges and prodded and whipped into doing unnatural stunts; that animals have been drowned, shot, and otherwise killed to make a "thrilling" scene. She said that movie audiences did not seem to enjoy screen performances that were dangerous for helpless animals, that many persons left motion-picture theaters in protest to cruel acts, and that more and more prominent persons were condemning the use of animals in unnatural and cruel acts in motion-picture plays. 

Then Howard called on Daniel to read his paper on rodeos, roundups, and wild-west shows. "I am going to begin by reading you what a judge of the Circuit Court in Alabama has to say about rodeos," said Daniel, and then he read what Judge Walter B. Jones, the Circuit Court of Montgomery, Alabama, had written for a

Montgomery newspaper when a rodeo tried to give a performance in his home city. Here it is: 

From what I have read, and the scenes that I have witnessed on the screens of the motion picture theaters of the country, I don't see where it takes any great amount of courage to engage in a rodeo. 

Those who glory in their ability to rope a calf, bull-dog a steer, hog-tie a frightened animal, never impressed me as heroes at all. I can never find it in my heart to admire a man or a woman who is unkind to God's dumb animals, who finds pleasure in beating, abusing, and cutting poor, helpless animals.

I can't conceive for a minute that any outstanding sportsman would have any pleasure or satisfaction in taking part in a rodeo. Just now three 

Americans, gentlemen and sportsmen, are occupying a warm place in the affections of the American people. I refer to Charles Lindbergh, Bobby Jones, and Admiral Byrd, and they won, and retain, the love of the American people because they possess in marked degree the characteristics which denote the man. Imagine one of them, if you can, appearing before the public in a rodeo.

The animals of the world, particularly those we have domesticated, have been faithful and useful servants of man. They cannot speak for themselves, and good men and good women should make it a point to speak for them and voice their needs and their wrongs, and enlist the sympathy of the public in condemning every exhibition where animals are tormented and tortured, and caused agony and suffering.

I don't believe that rodeos are exhibitions worthy of the patronage of people who believe in a fair deal for our dumb friends of the animal kingdom. 

Then Daniel said that in their large city a set of eighteen rodeo rules had been agreed to by the head of the S.P.C.A. and the manager of the rodeo that performed in the city each year. Here are eight of the eighteen rules: 

1. No sharp or taped spurs. 

2. No wide wood paddles. If horses need starting, use snap of whip or strap. 

3. No sore, lame, or injured animals permitted in any performance. 

4. No cruel bits permitted. 

5. Clowns not to ride calves or haze calves or fire pistols. 

6. Clowns not to carry cats or other animals into arena or release them. 

7. No bull-teasing or annoying of animals by waving colored cloth or by performers in matador costumes. 

8. No busting of calves accidentally or purposely will be tolerated.

He told the club that at all performances of the rodeo in their city there are four uniformed officers of the S.P.C.A. Two of the four are stationed at the chute, a narrow place through which the animals enter the arena. Every horse that is used is examined before he enters the chute. Daniel closed his remarks by saying: "Though our city has rules strictly controlling rodeo performances, and though England and other European countries have outlawed the rodeo, there are places right in our own country where the rodeo acts are cruel."

Then Joan, who had attended the circus, gave her report. "I was told by the head of our S.P.

C.A.," she said, "that the owner of the large circus I attended gave up all wild-animal acts over ten years ago. In that circus you cannot see lions and tigers and apes and monkeys and ba-

boons and snakes doing cruel stunts as they used to do years ago and still do in cheap little shows today. Why, just last summer an animal trainer of a circus was fined in Pittsburgh for his brutal treatment in a lion act. The fine was the largest the court could impose. I think it was wonderful for the people of Pittsburgh and the Pittsburgh S.P.C.A. to take that animal trainer into court." Joan stopped for breath. "The only performing animals I saw at the circus," ," she continued, "were animals whose intelligence is very high dogs, horses, seals, and elephants. They seemed to know what they were doing and to enjoy it. The horses waltzed beautifully to music. Why, I have seen horses waltz on the street when the band played, or even when a hurdy-gurdy ground out some old tune. 

The dogs did nothing dangerous. The elephants did nothing difficult. They looked very wise. 

The seals bounced a large, light rubber ball from one to another, catching it on their noses. At the zoo, they enjoy playing with a large ball. I could not see anything cruel in that act. As soon as they had finished, they ran across to their keeper, who fed them bits of fish. "There are inspectors from the S.P.C.A. at each circus performance. They examine daily all animals used in any way. The owners of this large circus do not want their stock abused. All animals are fed, housed, and exercised properly. 

Small circuses, like small zoos, that cannot af-

ford to feed and care for their animals or that have cruel animal acts should be put out of business. That is what the Royal S.P.C.A. is doing in England. It is closing all such side shows and circuses by asking the public not to attend them." 

As Joan took her seat, Miss Wright said, "The place for wild animals is in jungle sanctuaries or in the world's largest zoos, where they can enjoy large comfortable dwelling places, good food, and care that will keep them well and happy. Unnatural and cruel use of animals should be abolished in the movies and in vaudeville, side shows, circuses, rodeos, and roundups." 

Then Howard said, "All who would like to become members of the Jack London Club, stand."

Chapter XXV BE KIND TO ANIMALS WEEK 

"Be Kind to Animals Week" was being celebrated throughout the country. Famous actors and actresses, motion-picture stars, and lecturers were speaking over the radio on city programs, and on coast-to-coast hookups. Opera singers and concert musicians were making appeals before or at the close of their radio numbers. 

Clergymen had referred to animal week in their sermons, the Sunday before, which is known as "Be Kind to Animals Sunday."

In shop windows and in departments of the large stores there were special displays in honor 213

of the week. A hardware store had fitted up one of its windows to look like a garden. In the center was a fountain with water spraying lightly into the lovely basin where toy birds seemed to be bathing and drinking. Above the flower beds that bordered the garden were bird houses with more birds perched before the doors or on nearby branches of artificial shrubs and trees. There was a feeding station where other birds were feasting.

One of the largest department stores, covering a city block, had a window that displayed everything to make a dog happy and healthy. In one corner was a large, roomy doghouse, raised above the dampness of the ground, with the doorway facing the sunshine. Overhead, wire was stretched from one corner of the window diagonally across to another corner. On this was a ring that could slide easily along it, and on the ring the dog's leash had been snapped. He stood beneath, his leash fastened to his comfortably fitting harness. He looked as if he were ready to romp from one end of the long trolley to the other. He wore no collar to hurt his neck and throat when he strained at his leash. Near the doorway of his house were his large food bowl and drinking bowl. His brush and comb and bathtub were in another part of the window. And in still another were his dog biscuits and other food intended to keep him physically fit.

There was also a drama committee, which staged a play, assisted by members of the school drama club and its adult counselor. The play was given during the Wednesday morning assembly, with all the pupils and teachers present, and as many parents and other relatives and friends as could come. The play was in two acts and told the story about the founding of the first animal society in the United States by Henry 

Bergh, and about his early struggles. Before the curtain went up on the first act, Mr. MacDonald gave a fine talk on the value of animal week, and Howard spoke briefly on the different activities of the club. Then David played his violin. His beautiful music touched the heart of everyone. 

That was David's lovely service during Animal 

Week. When the applause died away, the curtain slowly lifted for the first act of the play. 

Excitement ran high after school that after-

noon when every pupil who had a radio tuned in to hear three members of the animal club speak on the S.P.C.A. radio program. They had been chosen for their excellent English, and because their voices seemed suited to radio broadcasting. 

The speakers were Howard, Russell, and Marie.

Howard repeated the talk on the club activities that he had given in assembly. Russell talked about the books on his book list. Marie told what her school was doing that week. Each speaker had three minutes. You can say a great deal in three minutes, if you say it as briefly and as briskly as these speakers did.

As Miss Wright listened she thought of the wonderful work the members of her club were doing, and the wonderful work that hundreds of other members, as the years passed, would do for city pets and for America's birds and animals.
