Message From the President of the Council of Ministers 

Among the numerous stamp collectors in the world, Royalty and Presidents of Republics have been renowned. Reading the biographies of Franklin D. Roosevelt who during so many years had such great influence on the destinies of the United States and the World, I was struck by the fact that amongst his perhaps less important personal hobbies while President, is mentioned his interest in the collection of stamps. 

In spite of the overwhelming number of decisions he had to take daily and the responsibilities which rested on him he still found time and a mind to complete, rearrange and study his collection. This interest in a seemingly insignificant subject may appear strange for a person of his stature. However, this is not so. In stamps the geography and political groupings of the world are pictured and they reflect the history of the nations. All political changes during a century of succession of monarchs or presidents, change of regimes, revision of frontiers, reunion or division of states, war and occupation, all these things can be retraced in these small pieces of paper. Besides, in their execution, one finds the changes of techniques and conceptions of art. 

The idea to introduce the stamp for the dispatch of letters has turned out to be most fruitful. It has helped immensely to stimulate the exchange of correspondence within and between the countries of the world. Further the pace of postal traffic has accelerated continually. The airmail stamps of today bear witness to this most recent development in this respect. 

It seems to me a happy thought to organize exhibitions of stamps from the countries which celebrate the centenary of their first stamp. I highly appreciate that now a collection of stamps from the Netherlands is being shown. 

The visitors will find evidence of our history, not only of the last hundred years but also of previous centuries as laid down in stamps marking jubilees and commemorations. 

Many also will be interested in the graphic work of Netherlands artists.

During 1850, The Netherlands, following the example of other countries generally and of England particularly, instigated important reforms in postal service. 

By drastic reductions of postal rates, and by increasing communications facilities and postoffices, this reform was intended to transform postal services into a public utility to a much greater extent than they had ever been before. 

It is interesting to note that at first the Government had not planned to include the issue of adhesive postage stamps as part of this reform although other countries had already used them, and without which no modern postal system could be possible. Netherlands Stamps in Our Time By W. F. Gouwe Aesthetical Advisor of The Netherlands Postal and Telecommunications Services

When J. F. van Roijen, as Secretary General of The Netherlands Postal and 

Telecommunications Services, during the first decades of this century, began to set standards for typographical form of postal printed matter, he demonstrated that he was a man who knew what he wanted. In all nations where civilization was advanced, architecture, applied arts and typography had established new principles. He himself was a typographic arts student with no mean reputation-even beyond our borders-by reason of the many excellently produced books he printed on his private press. He had offered many important suggestions for the improvement for the production of printing forms and postal publications, so it was inevitable that he felt the need of modernizing stamp manufacture.

The first step involved the staging of a contest with prizes offered for such designs as van Roijen felt were necessary. Artists like the architect, De 

Bazel; the typographer S. H. de Roos, and the famous painter, Willem van

Konijnenburg were especially invited to participate in this contest and apprised of the artistic planes this leader wished for new Netherlands postage stamps. 

New stamp series, like the Children's stamps, introduced in 1924, and whose premiums was used to benefit youth in need of care and protection; and the summer stamps to aid social and cultural progress, all offered young, aspiring artists (as well as veterans who participated) the opportunity of suggesting new ideas for the subtle graphical work that is so necessary for postage stamps. In the beginning artists confined their ideas to symbolical and historical designs. A specific Netherlands character in modern treatment Iwas not achieved from the outset. When postage stamps were authorized in The Netherlands, through the law of April 12, 1850, the Government encountered a question of selecting an appropriate design for its initial adhesives. 

In those days an adhesive stamp was exclusively a certificate to show that a postal fee had been paid in advance of the service it promised, was rendered. 

But what should such a small-sized certificate look like? What ought to be printed upon it? Stamps had been in use only less than a dozen years; only a handful of nations had begun using them by 1852. 

The Dutch considered all stamps then in existence, and paid particular attention to those of Great Britain, which introduced this new form of postal service-the "Penny Black," of May, 1840.

Holland's Monarchy In A Changing World By Jerome L. Heldring Director, The Netherlands Information Service 

The Netherlands represents one of the few examples of a country which started as a republic and became a monarchy. For more than two centuries it was called the Republic of the United Netherlands, which was a loose federation of seven provinces. These two centuries, the 17th and 18th, were the most glorious of Dutch history-if power and wealth are the marks of glory. But Dutch culture also attained in this period heights unsurpassed in history.

So it was not because of lack of success that the Dutch decided in 1814 to accept a monarchy for their country just liberated from a twenty-years' domination by the French. Nor did they turn to some foreign dynasty as so many newly-founded states did in the 

19th century. The House of Orange- 

Nassau to which they turned had been connected with The Netherlands since the nation's birth in the 16th century.

In fact, it was the founder of that dynasty, William the Silent, who led the rebellion of the seven Dutch provinces against the King of Spain. Once liberated from Spain, The Netherlands, though a Republic whose sovereign was the States-General (Congress), kept its close ties with the House of Orange- 

Nassau. 

The function of Stadhouder, chief executive officer of the Republic, was reserved for members of that dynasty, seven of whom fulfilled that post in the period of 1580 to 1795.

Printing Netherlands Postage Stamps By James H. Baxter * Managing Editor National Philatelic Museum 

Designed principally by artists of the Dutch school, Netherlands postage stamps, with their distinctive characteristics, have been produced by recess, lithographic and typographic printing processes. Although several issues bear elaborate and intricate designs, most of the postage stamps of this country have been modeled for utility rather than for aesthetic reasons. In some cases designs themselves have determined what particular process should be used in printing. Because of their very nature, these designs could be printed by no process except that which was chosen. The 

Orange-Nassau Jubilee issue of 1913, for instance, which was composed of intricate and delicate lines, was intaglio engraved in steel because the reproduction of such a design could be produced effectively by no other method. is also true of Netherlands printed by photogravure, offset and typography. In almost every instance the particular type of printing employed in producing the issue was determined by restrictions of the design itself.

Two types of photogravure presses are in current use in Holland: sheet-fed 

(Fig. 4) and web-fed (Fig. 5) presses. 

Both print from cylindrical plates which revolve in an ink trough, where all the cavities are filled with pigment. 

Before passing under the impression cylinder, the surplus ink is scraped from the surface of the intaglio plate with a thin steel doctor blade. The ink, held in the wells below the surface, is transferred to the paper by contact. 

Offset Methods 

Offset printing is an adaptation of the lithographic principle which is based upon the fact that water and grease will not mix. Designs on such plates have an affinity for greased-based, lithographic ink, while the blank areas of the plate have an affinity for water. 

Usually composed of thin, flexible sheets of zinc, offset plates (Fig. 6) are produced photo-mechanically. A plate is curved around a plate cylinder and is printed on a rotary press. possible to print offset plates in the flat.

As the plate revolves in the printing press, it is dampened with water rollers, which moisten the unprotected surface of the plate so that these areas will repel ink. When the ink rollers pass over the plate, the pigment adheres only to the lines of the design and is repelled by the dampened surface of the plate. The image is imprinted onto a resilient rubber blanket, and in turn is offset to the paper as the sheet passes between the blanket and impression cylinders (Fig. 7). 

Typography 

Many Netherlands stamps have been printed by typography, the philatelic term for book or letter press printing. 

When a design is printed without any change whatever, it is possible to produce typographic printing plates by photo engraving. Since stamps printed from photo engraved plates are more easily counterfeited than those printed by other processes, the Netherlands

PTT produces original steel engravings in relief to forestall the production of spurious issues.

Engraving steel dies in relief is considerably more difficult than engraving recessed dies because it is the colorless parts of the design that the engraver cuts or etches in the die, the impression being obtained from the raised, or untouched, relief surface. Despite the difficulty involved, Dutch engravers have mastered the problems of relief engraving and have developed a distinctive technique. Typographic cliches of engraved relief designs are produced by stereotyping. 

In stereotyping, a counter impression of the original die is made in a wood pulp matrix. After drying, the mold is placed in a specially constructed casting box and molten stereotype metal is poured upon the matrix, producing a replica of the original die (Fig. 8). 

Enough of these cliches are cast to compose a printing plate (Fig. 8 and 9). 

Each cliche is then carefully trimmed to exact dimensions with margins one half the perforation gutter width all around the design. When trimmed the cliches are locked in a printing chase. It is from this form that stamp impressions are actually produced.

Gumming Methods in Holland 

The success of a postage stamp depends upon its ability to stick to the task until the job is finished. That calls for the application of an adhesive to the back of sheets to facilitate the affixing of stamps to mail matter. Gumming must follow printing on engraved stamps printed by the wet process, but on stamps printed by other methods the paper usually receives an application of gum before printing.

In the Netherlands, gum is applied to webs of unprinted paper (Fig. 10) by a direct rotary process. As the web travels around a cylinder, blank paper comes in contact with a gum-saturated roller which transfers the sticky substance to the back of the web. The paper then travels through a drying chamber where the moisture is removed from the adhesive. The gummed web then is wound automatically into rolls.

To correct the tendency of gummed paper to curl or buckle, the paper is run through a machine equipped with an angle bar which breaks the gum when the web of paper is drawn diagonally across it (Fig. 11). After receiving this treatment, the paper is either rewound in rolls or is cut into sheets.

Comb Perforating 

With a comb perforator, such as those used by the Netherlands PTT (Fig. 12), it is possible to make twoway perforations with a single passage of a sheet through the machine.

The perforating head on the comb machine 74

consists of a long straight line of perforations with a number of perforations, one stamp deep, set at right angles. 

Sheets of stamps are held in register during perforating by pins that pass through perforation markers in the margins on opposite sides of the sheet. 

In the case of 100-subject sheets, only 11 strokes are needed to completely punch the perforations. Completed sheets of stamps are inspected, cut into postoffice panes, counted, made-up into suitable parcels and then are placed in a storage vault ready for requisition by postoffices throughout the country.

The Netherlands Postal System By Burton E. Bauder Member of the Netherlands and Colonial Philatelist

Netherlands, it is appropriate to review the evolution of the Dutch postal system. It is an interesting history, building up to today's efficient postal system, a model of operation for others to follow.

The excellent courier services organized by the Romans in the Lowlands at the start of the Christian era were primarily intended for military intelligence. But until the end of the sixteenth century there existed no organized service. Important cities and merchants sent special couriers to the sessions of the States-General, to other countries for political information and even to the battlefields to await the outcome of conflicts. From these couriers a sort of postal system gradually developed as the art of writing became more widespread. The couriers then, instead of traveling, employed others to do the traveling for them, and set themselves up as postmasters, opening regular routes to various cities in the country. 

The couriers originally were not allowed to carry letters on their return journeys. However, in the first half of the eighteenth century someone had the inspiration of letting the couriers meet halfway to exchange their mail, thus cutting both time and cost in half. 

This was followed subsequently by the setting up of a number of exchange offices.

Soon the profit motive of these postmasters started to cause trouble. Only the desirable routes were contracted for; the rural areas were totally ignored. This caused the cities to take administration of the letter deliveries into their own hands, although as long as the profits remained modest, the postmasters were left in charge, a small part of the profits going to the cities. 

The postmastership then became the favorite objective for nepotism as profits increased out of all proper proportions.

In 1754 a uniform for couriers was adopted but even with the new administration only the important centers were included in the postal service. The first dated postmark was used in 1829, not for the cancellation of stamps which were still unknown, but for the control of prompt delivery. Letter boxes were soon placed in the larger cities and mail deliveries were increased in some cities to eight or ten a day, subsequently a cause of complaint from some business men.

Inno- vations introduced by the Postal Law of 1870 aroused deep interest in other countries. It fixed a uniform postage rate, graduated according to weight alone. A charge of five cents was made for unstamped envelopes. Special delivery made its first appearance as did the postal card and money order.

Rural areas now were no longer isolated. Even the smallest hamlet had its postal connection. The network of postal routes increased from 12,000 miles in 1860 to 35,000 miles in 1890.

The Classic Stamps of the Netherlands 

By Philip H. Ward, Jr. 

On January 1, 1852, just a century ago, the Netherlands issued their first postage stamps. This was almost eleven years after postage stamps, as we know them today, appeared in Great Britain.

This series of 1852 consisted of three varieties: a 5c blue, 10c lake and 15c orange all similar in design with portrait of the reigning monarch, King 

William III. These stamps were designed and engraved on steel plates by J. W. Kaiser of Brussels and printed at the Government Mint at Utrecht. They were produced in sheets of 100 consisting of four blocks of 25 with wide margins around each block. Of the 5c five plates were used, ten plates for the 10c and one only for the 15c denomination. The stamps were issued imperforate on paper watermarked posthorn. 

Some years back mint blocks of this issue were to be had with little hunting but today they are seldom found and can truly be ranked as scarce. Really fine mint singles are not common and used multiples in choice condition are by no means plentiful.

New impetus was given to the redevelopment of Surinam by the discovery of rich bauxite deposits which were subsequently operated by an American and a Dutch company. Surinam is now the largest producer of bauxite for the American aluminum industry. Although the bauxite-mining has helped considerably to raise the standard of living in Surinam, it would be unwise to continue to make this the sole basis for the territory's economy. 

Therefore the Netherlands Government has initiated an important agricultural program through which it hopes to give 

Surinam greater economic security. By means of its own Point Four Program

Holland is aiding Surinam to establish itself as a producer of rice, tobacco, citrus and lumber. 

Since the middle of the 19th Century political relationship between the Netherlands and its American territories has undergone a fundamental change that reflects the evolution of a hundred years of political thinking. Both the 

Netherlands Antilles and Surinam have emerged from their colonial status to become autonomous, self-governing, entities. As was the case in many other parts of the world (Indonesia being a striking example) the last war precipitated the movement towards selfgovernment in the West-Indies. In 1942 Queen 

Wilhelmina promised a constitutional relationship in which the Netherlands, Surinam and the Netherlands Antilles were to take part and in which each of them was to manage its domestic affairs independently and relying on its own power, but animated by the will to assist each other. This constitutional reform came into effect for Surinam in 1950 and for the Antilles a year later.

It has been said that the Dutch invented genre painting. The claim is perhaps exaggerated, but they certainly excelled in it. They delighted in portraying the intimate aspects of life, scenes in taverns, shops, church interiors, women doing humble household tasks or grooming themselves. 

There was a lot of jolly humor in these scenes, much gaiety, bourgeois happiness and evident self-satisfaction. But besides and above all, it was excellent painting, done with technical skill, dignity and restraint.

Having absorbed the glory of their comfortable homes, the loveliness of their women, the somewhat pompous solidity and ostentation of their men, the Dutch masters turned to the landand seascape. Ruysdael, Hobbema, later van de Velde and others portrayed all this: they rendered the silvery light of Holland, unique in the world, the wide flat horizons, the trees fighting the constant wind.

The development of commercial aviation dates from 1919. Among the airlines who pioneered in this development of commercial aviation was K. L. M. 

Royal Dutch Airlines, the oldest operat ing airline in the world.

Transportation in Europe has been completely dislocated as a result of World War I, and as people had acquired more confidence in the use of aeroplanes from experience gained during the war, the idea of using air transportation for long distance travel evolved.

Within the Netherlands, the foundation for the development of commercial aviation was laid one evening in the club-house at Soesterberg Airport, when Lieutenant Albert Plesman conceived the idea of organizing the 

World's first Air Exposition. The aim of this exhibition was to acquaint the public with the progress which had been made in aviation during the War. 

Once the plans for this exhibition had been formulated by Lt. Plesman and a Lt. Hofstee, they together arranged for financial banking through the interest of a number of prominent Dutchmen.

The world's first Air Exhibition was held in Amsterdam on August 1, 1919.

It was a tremendous success. There were half a million visitors, of whom some 4,000 went up for joy rides. This exhibition provided the stimulus for the formation of an air transport company and on October 7, 1919, the "Konink lijke Luchtvaart Maatschappij, N. V." (K. L. M. Royal Dutch Airlines) came into existence with Albert Plesman as its president-director. 

K. L. M. now is the oldest airline in the world and is one of the few air transport companies which originated as a private enterprise. This was made possible by the cooperation of a number of commercial, shipping and banking concerns, and additional support from the Dutch Government. 

K. L. M. had its primitive beginning after six months of study and preparation when on May 17, 1920, it inaugurated the Amsterdam-London service using an open DeHavilland DH-9A "Airco" military aircraft which had been converted to carry two passengers. 

The passengers were garbed in the flying clothes of the day, leather jackets, flying helmets, and fur-lined boots. The flight ceased to be an event and in a little more than a month became a matter of daily routine between the two cities.

The first of a series of six charter flights between those two points were conducted starting in February 1946. 

Inasmuch as Government permission to fly this route via New York had not yet been obtained, a much longer southern route via Dakar and Natal had to be followed. Douglas C-54s were used for these flights and the passengers consisted mainly of personnel for the Royal 

Dutch Shell Company.

K. L. M. was ready to conduct a series of familiarization tests between Amsterdam and New York prior to inaugurating their long awaited service to the New World. These flights were carried out with C-54's and were used for pilot training and to haul badly needed aircraft spare parts to the Netherlands.

The first eastward flight with the 

American pilot, Thomas O. McClure, Jr. in command had the distinction of being the first commercial aircraft ever to make the Ground Control Approach to 

Prestwick Airport in Scotland. The return trip to New York was made via

Santa Maria, in the Azores, and Moncton, Canada. On arrival in Santa

Maria, the crew found the former American Ambassador to Great Britain, John Winant stranded on the island and gave him a lift as far as Moncton. 

In May 1945, to be exact on the 21st, a DC-4 named the "Rotterdam" made the first scheduled flight between Amsterdam and New York. At the controls was Evert van Dijk who 16 years before had accompanied Kingsford- 155

Smith in the Southern Cross on the first westward crossing of the Atlantic. 

Lockheed Constellations have since replaced the DC-4s on the North Atlantic route and Holland is now but a half day from the City that was once known as New Amsterdam. 

K. L. M.'s network was gradually extended and another milestone in the swift post-war development was the inauguration of service to South America.

The route taken on the first flight by Captain J. Frijns in a DC-4 on August 

6, 1946, was Lisbon, Dakar, Recife, Rio de Janeiro, and Sao Paulo to Buenos

Aires. This flight was the first of a number of special flights to South 

America. Then K. L. M. obtained permission from the Uruguayan Government and operated service between Montevideo via Lisbon, Dakar, Natal and

Rio de Janeiro. 

As K. L. M. gradually got back on her feet, she inaugurated service to 

South Africa. In spite of the regularity with which flights were made to South Africa, they could not be referred to as schedule services at first, in view of the fact that permission to operate such a service had not yet been obtained from the South African Government. About the end of July, 1947, an air agreement was signed and since August 16th of that year there has been a schedule weekly service between Amsterdam and Johannesburg. 

Although the rip-snorting swash- buckling frontier days of rapid expansion are over, K. L. M. will keep abreast of new aviation developments and continue her phoenix rise to fame.

At this time we think it is incumbent upon us to review the activities of your Museum. After all, the future success of the National Philatelic Museum depends upon your support and upon your ability to arouse interest among your stamp collecting friends.

The year 1951 was a period of most unusual activity. In cooperation with various organizations, special exhibitions were held and the Museum played an important part in many national and international exhibitions. The major activity centered around the issuance of the new stamps of the United Nations. The writer was appointed a member of the Committee for the Approval of Designs and collaborated closely with 

Mr. Bertil A. Renborg, Chief of the

United Nations Postal Administration, and Mr. Jan Juta, Chief of the United

Nations Visual Information Center.

Toronto. It was on a comparatively small scale with the stamps flown especially for the exhibit. The continuous research on United Nations stamps was entrusted to our Managing Editor,

Mr. James H. Baxter, and during the 

A. S. D. A. Exhibition in the Armory in 

New York City this exhibit occupied a large space in the center of the exhi.its with 54 frames (16 pages each) in three rows, beautifully decorated with 

4 rows of the flags of the 60 member nations of the United Nations. The exhibit, using the blue color of the United Nations, was artistically turned out and attracted wide attention.

Speciale Catalogus van de Postzegels van Nederland en de Overzeese Gediedsdelen, 11th edition, 1951. pp. 336, paper bound. Published by the Nederlandsche Vereeniging van Postzegel handelaren, Postbus 111, 's-Gravenhage, Netherlands. Price, f 2.00. This detailed catalogue of the stamps of the 

Netherlands and its colonies will answer many of the usual questions of specialists in these issues. Because the information is presented in standard catalogue form and accompanied by many illustrations, it is easy to understand in spite of the fact that the catalogue is written in Dutch. Only a few sections, such as the extended discussions of the first issue, are likely to baffle English readers. This catalogue lists a great many minor varieties, such as those due to variations in gauge of the perforations, which do not appear in Scott. It lists prices for many stamps on cover and in blocks and describes proofs, specimens and plate numbers where they exist. For most issues it lists the pertinent facts relating to printers, sheet size, and quantities printed and sold. It describes and illustrates the more important types of cancellations, and refers collectors to sources of further information in the literature of philately. In addition to ordinary postage stamps, this catalogue lists internees' stamps, telegraph stamps and the numerous handstamps of the Japa nese occupation of the Dutch Indies. 

This is a very handy and comprehensive catalogue, and interested collectors are indebted to the Dutch stamp dealers who publish it.

DR. JOHN FREEHAFER 

A native Philadelphian, John Freehafer became a stamp collector (of sorts) at the age of ten, and also collects maps and fine books. 

During two years of service in the quartermaster corps of the U. S. army his collecting activities were temporarily set aside, but they have now been fully resumed. 

Educated in the public schools of Philadelphia, he is a graduate of the University of Pennsylvania, Class of 1945, and holds the degrees of M.S., A.M. and Ph.D. from that school, the last two being earned in the field of English literature. He has taught English courses at the University of Pennsylvania and Temple University, and philately courses at Temple University. He has served the National Philatelic Museum as librarian, Editor of the Museum Magazine, and a member of the Museum Exhibition Committee. His writ ings include numerous articles and reviews in the fields of philately and English. He is book review editor for the Philadelphia Forum.

As an active philatelist, he is a member of that well-nigh extinct species, the true general collector, although his collection is semi-specialized in Belgium, Brazil and such exotic countries as the Indian native states. His collection also shows an increasing tendency to include such specialists' items as multiple pieces, proofs and unusual covers. expresses a laudable desire to write his book "in a lighter vein" so as "to popularise the stamp of India amongst the general collectors," but the volleys of exclamation points and slang expressions which he uses to carry out this 191

end become astonishing at times. Even such an objective statement as "This printing is now known as Die III!" requires the spice of an exclamation point, and Mr. Cooper advises his suffering reader, whenever the explanation of a philatelic point becomes overinvolved, to ask for "an ice-bag from his missus!" Mr. Cooper is spoiling for a fight, too, if we may judge from his demand that Mr. Harilal Banker "come out with the true version" of his finding a unique sheet of the half anna 

92 arches, or the "juicy (sic) fireworks" which resulted from his attacks on the "so-called experts" who questioned the standing of certain Indian errors. Aside from such outbursts of overenthusiasm, however, Mr. Cooper has much material of serious philatelic value to present. Besides giving thorough treatment to the regular varieties of Indian stamps, he covers an astonishing variety of other subjects, including the history of India, postal history of India, early handstruck stamps, overland route to India, Scinde dawks, essays, proofs, specimens, postal stationery, telegraph stamps, revenues, official reprints, forgeries, early cancellations, India used abroad, rocket mails, meter stamps, and philatelic literature relating to India. One of Mr. Cooper's most interesting chapters is that with the unpromising heading of "Miscellaneous." It includes such famous or infamous items as the Minto Fete 

Stamps, the Mt. Everest label and the "Jai Hind" issue. Another set of items brought to light and illustrated by Mr. 

Cooper is the set of essays prepared for an Edward VIII. issue for India. Mr. 

Cooper also explains the "Postal Service" stamps, which have puzzled so many collectors. Under the heading of "Philately in India" he observes that "there are hardly 1,000 active stampcollectors in India, a country of 350,-* 

000,000 souls!" He explains that "Illiteracy, coupled with abject poverty of the masses, has kept out stamp-collecting from the lower and middle class Indians."

Stamps of India contains many excellent illustrations. On the jacket is pictured the "Four Anna 'Head Inverted,'

India's great rarity," now in the collection of "Mr. H. C. Gibson, of Pennsyl vania, U. S. A." Stamps of India will interest the general collector and prove indispensable to the specialist in India stamps.

Theodore Sizer, The Works of Colonel 

John Trumbull, Artist of the American 

Revolution. xviii & 117 & 40 pp. of plates. Yale University Press, $5.00.

Colonel Trumbull was the "official" painter of the Revolution, and many of his portraits and historic group paintings have been reproduced on stamps.

Beside presenting large reproductions of the original paintings from which the designs for the 24c 1869 and Burgoyne stamps were taken, Professor Sizer provides complete keys for the identification of all the persons appearing on them. There is also a full page reproduction of "Gen. Washington at

Trenton," which has been used as the basis for five stamp designs, beginning with the 90c blue of 1860. The De 

Neufville portrait, which was used as the basis for the 7c Bicentennial, is discussed but not reproduced. Professor 

Sizer is the author of an article on "Trumbull's Paintings on Postage

Stamps," published in the American Philatelist in 1949. His present work is an outstanding contribution to our knowledge of Trumbull. Professor

Sizer has done a remarkable job of separating the numerous forged paintings which have been passed off from time to time under the name of Trumbull from the genuine paintings, which he lists in detail. The Yale University 

Press may well be proud of a book which, besides possessing great scholarly value, was handsome enough to be judged one of the "Fifty Books of the 

Year" 1950 by the American Institute of Graphic Arts. Now in its second printings, The Works of Colonel John 

Trumbull should interest many students of United States stamp designs.

F. George Kay, Royal Mail: The 

Story of the Posts in England from the 192

Time of Edward IVth to the Present 

Day. x & 198. Macmillan (import), $4.50. Mr. Kay's retelling of the story of the English mail system from its beginnings down to the present time is provided with a spotty bibliography which omits many sources that philatelists and postal historians rightly regard as important. Nevertheless, Mr. 

Kay has produced a lucid and entertaining account of his subject, enlivened by a number of remarkable ancedotes. In order to show the deplorable state of the English roads, for example, he quotes from a fifteenth century court record. "It appears that an Aylesbury miller in 1499 dug a pit in the highway, 10 feet long, 8 feet broad, and 8 feet deep, in which a travelling glove merchant was drowned one night. The local jury acquitted the miller of all blame, on the grounds that he had nowhere else to get the particular clay he needed." The mail service was scarcely better than the roads in those early days, but gradually the notion that the mails should provide a service to the general public caught on, until England became the great pioneer in postal reform and introduced the postage stamp.

At times Mr. Kay has little to say on subjects that vitally concern philatelists. He devotes but a single sentence to the "Bishop mark," for example.

His work is of chief value to the postal historian who wants a clear account of the historical background of the English postal system, or to the general reader. As one would expect from Mr. 

Kay's membership in the Royal Society of Arts, Royal Mail is a handsomely printed book, with endpapers in color, maps and 46 pages of illustrations, including quite a few excellent ones connected with the issuance of the first postage stamp. 

Franklin R. Bruns, Jr., Fell's Profitable Stamp Guide. pp. 128. Frederick 

Fell, Inc., $1.50. Besides being a handy and inexpensive guide to the fundamentals of philately, this volume by the Curator of Philately of the Smithsonian Institution includes quite a few features that are new or unusual in books of its kind. Its twenty-five chapters include presentations of such basic subjects as the origin of posts, stamp pioneers, stamp impressions, paper, watermarks, perforations and accessories. The more unusual items include a listing of foreign monetary units with their values; a chapter on the United Nations and its stamps; and a complete list of U. S. commemoratives (with quantities printed), taken from the official publi cations of the Post Office Department. 

There are useful chapters on philatelic literature and philatelic organizations. 

Mr. Bruns' experience as Philatelic Advisor to the Republic of Liberia stands him in good stead when he comes to describe the designing and production of stamps. Over a hundred illustrations appear in the Stamp Guide. Most of these are based on stamps, but there is also an excellent page of photographs showing steps in the actual production of the Four Chaplains stamp. At its very low price, Mr. Bruns' book may be heartily recommended to anyone desiring to review the subjects covered in it, or as a gift to a beginning or moderately advanced collector. covers
