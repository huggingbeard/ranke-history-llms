PREFACE 

This publication provides information on certain legal provisions relating to labor, gathered from labor codes, laws, decrees, regulations, and constitutions of 13 Latin American countries. The material is limited to brief summary statements of certain basic legal requirements and is not intended to indicate the degree of effectiveness or enforcement of the particular provisions.

The material in this publication was prepared by Windsor W. 

Stroup in the Bureau's Division of Foreign Labor Conditions.

NOTES ON TERMINOLOGY 

All summary statements in this publication relate to legal obligatory requirements unless otherwise indicated. 

The terms "law" and "legislation" are used interchangeably; they carry no legal technical distinction, including generally all legal forms such as legislative enactments, acts and laws, executive decrees, decree-laws, and administrative rules and regulations. Court rulings are so indicated. 

Unless otherwise indicated, the terms "employee" and "worker" as used in these statements are synonymous and interchangeable. 

The labor and social security laws of some countries---notably Chile and Peru---differentiate between two distinct classes of employed persons, obreros (workers) and empleados (employees), and generally contain separate provisions for each class. 

Definition of these categories is not always clear, but generally an obrero is a wage-earner whose work is predominantly physical and manual, such as a laborer. Empleado is the more exclusive term, referring generally to a salaried employee whose work is predominantly mental and intellectual, including most office workers and technicians, and sometimes certain skilled workers. iv

LATIN AMERICAN LABOR LEGISLATION COMPARATIVE SUMMARIES OF SELECTED PROVISIONS INTRODUCTION 

Demand in Latin America for labor and social legislation resulted largely from the impetus given industrialization in the last 40 years, particularly by World Wars I and II, and the consequent growth of the industrial labor force. The greatest advances in legislation establishing minimum wages, maximum hours, holidays with pay, and similar benefits were made in the 1930's. The recommendations and conventions of the International Labour Office stimulated much labor legislation. The ILO also gave technical assistance in the drafting of legislation to such countries as Venezuela, Bolivia, and Mexico. By 1940, 8 of the 20 Latin American countries had adopted comprehensive labor codes. 

In the 1940's, the adoption of a "floor" for wages gained favor throughout the area, and special attention was paid to greater job security through restrictions on the employer's right to dismiss employees and through severance pay requirements. Laws requiring severance pay, or dismissal compensation, are prevalent throughout Latin America today. On the other hand, unemployment compensation is almost nonexistent, although many countries have provided in their laws or constitutions for the institution of unemployment compensation legislation. Laws and constitutional provisions recognizing the right of workers to participate in the profits of their employer are found in many countries, and have been implemented in a few.

Labor legislation in Latin American countries is in some respects significantly different in character, administration, and effectiveness from that in the United States. For example, many of the provisions selected for inclusion in this publication have no legislative or regulatory counterparts in the United States---provisions such as those relating to severance pay and 7 days' pay for 6 days' work, and "nationalization of employment." In the Latin American countries---and this may be observed of many of the less industrially developed countries of the world---considerably more emphasis is placed on legislative action and administrative regulations for solving problems of working conditions and industrial relations as compared with the situation in the U. S. where many of these matters are handled within the sphere of collective bargaining. This publication presents information regarding the characteristics of legislative provisions in the labor field, but makes no attempt to evaluate their effectiveness. - 1 -

HOURS OF WORK, OVERTIME, AND RATE DIFFERENTIALS 

Brazil

The normal workday and workweek are fixed at 8 and 48 hours, respectively. Overtime not exceeding 2 hours daily is permitted in most cases. 

Special regulations, relating chiefly to working hours, apply to workers in dangerous or public interest activities. Overtime pay for most occupations must be at the rate of time and a quarter to time and a half, but may be waived if there is a corresponding reduction in hours worked in another day of the week. Nightwork (10 p.m. to 5 a.m.) is prohibited for women and calls for a 20-percent premium to be paid to men. Chile

Maximum hours generally are 8 per day and 48 per week; upon workeremployer agreement, the workday may be extended to 9 hours in order to provide a half holiday on Saturday. Hours over the maximum are considered overtime and must be approved by the Directorate General of Labor, must not exceed 2 hours a day, and require payment at time and a half rates. 

Overtime is generally prohibited on Sundays and holidays. Farm, home, and domestic workers are generally exempted from the above provisions. Colombia

Maximum daily and weekly hours are 8 and 48, respectively, except for those employed in agriculture, stockbreeding, and forestry in which occupations maximum hours are 9 and 54. For work performed on a shift basis, the 8 and 48 hour maximums may be exceeded and not considered overtime, as long as the average hours worked over a period of 3 weeks or less does not exceed 8 a day and 48 a week. Work performed between the hours of 6 a.m. and 6 p.m. is considered daywork. Nightwork requires a premium of 35 percent. Overtime pay for daywork must be at the rate of time and a quarter; for nightwork, at time and three-quarters over normal daywork rates. 

Costa Rica

Maximum daily and weekly hours for daywork (that performed between 5 a.m. and 7 p.m.) are 8 and 48, respectively; for nightwork (generally prohibited for women), 6 and 36; a "mixed" day (day and night work) may not exceed 7 hours. Hours for work neither dangerous nor unhealthful may be extended, by contract, up to 10 hours for daywork, and up to 8 hours for "mixed" work, provided weekly hours do not exceed 48. Hours beyond these limits are overtime and must be paid for at time and half over the minimum rate or higher if agreed upon. Overtime is prohibited in dangerous or unhealthful work, or more than 4 times a week; hours including overtime must not exceed 12 a day.

Cuba 

Basic maximum hours are 8 per day and 44 (with pay for 48) per week. 

Weekly hours may be greater in certain seasonal industries: e.g., a 56-hour week is authorized for sugar workers during the crop season. Greater weekly and monthly hour limitations apply also to a variety of occupations and industries. Under special circumstances, with Ministry of Labor approval, daily hours may exceed 8, provided 48 hours a week are not exceeded; otherwise, work exceeding the 8-hour daily limit is considered overtime and must be paid for at time and a quarter rates. The law is generally silent regarding nightwork, the law basically stating that the manner of distributing the 8 hours of daily work "so that a worker may be idle on Saturday afternoons" is left to agreement between employer and workers. 

El Salvador 

For nonagricultural workers generally, maximum hours for daytime work (that performed between 6 a.m. and 8 p.m.) are 8 per day and 44 per week; for nightwork, 7 and 39, respectively. Nightwork requires a 25-percent premium over daytime wage rates. Overtime at time and a half above basic rates is required for hours exceeding the above maximum up to a 12-hour-aday total for daytime work and up to a 10-hour-a-night total for nightime work; up to 48 hours a week for daytime work, and 42 hours a week for nightime work; hours over these shall be compensated for by double-time rates. (Hourly basic remuneration for piece or task work is calculated by dividing the average daily remuneration during the week preceding by the number of ordinary daily working hours for daywork.) Guatemala

For daywork (that performed between 5 a.m. and 8 p.m.) "normal and maximum hours... at the disposal of the employer" are specified as 8 per day and 48 per week; for nightwork, 6 and 36 hours, respectively, when by law, custom, or contract, employees work less than 48 hours a week, they have the right to receive a full week's (48 hours) pay. By agreement between employees and employers in operations of a dangerous or unhealthful nature, an additional 2 hours daily may be worked provided that weekly hours do not exceed 48 per day or 36 for nightwork (or 42 for mixed daynight work). Including overtime, hours shall not exceed 12 per day; overtime is prohibited in work of a dangerous or unhealthful nature. Overtime must be paid for at time and a half rates in commercial and industrial undertakings, and agricultural undertakings employing more than 500 workers; otherwise, at time and a quarter.

. 

Mexicó 

Maximum hours for daytime work (that performed between 6 a.m. and 

8 p.m.) are 8 per day; for nightwork, 7; for mixed day-night work, 7½ 2.

By worker-employer agreement, these limits may be exceeded to permit workers Saturday afternoon rest or an equivalent arrangement. Double time on the amount paid for normal hours is stipulated for work exceeding such hours, but such overtime must not exceed 3 hours a day, nor occur 3 times consecutively or oftener than 3 times a week. Nicaragua

Maximum hours are 8 per day and 48 per week; in unhealthful places, the daily limit is 6 hours. By worker-employer agreement, daily limits may exceed 8 hours if the weekly 48-hour limit is observed, so that the worker may be free on Saturday afternoons or on other days. Night shifts (between 8 p.m. and 6 a.m.) are limited to 7 hours; mixed day-night work is limited to 7 hours. Nightwork requires double-time overrates for daytime work, unless performed in periodic shifts in turn. Overtime, permissible "in special cases" not to exceed 3 hours per day or more than 3 times in 1 week, must be paid for at double-time rates. Panama

Daywork is that performed between 5 a.m. and 7 p.m. Maximum hours for daywork are 8 per day and 48 per week; for nightwork, 7 and 42; for mixed day-night shifts, 7 and 45. Overtime is permissible up to a total of 11 hours per day or shift provided the average hours during 3 consecutive weeks does not exceed the above maximums, and that there is an agreement regarding such overtime between the workers and employers, approved by the Office of the General Labor Inspector. Overtime is prohibited in dangerous or unhealthful occupations. Daytime overtime must be paid for at time and a quarter; nighttime overtime at time and a half over day rates; and time and three-quarters must be paid over night-shift rates when overtime is prolonged into day hours. Peru 

Maximum daily hours are 8 per day; maximum weekly hours are 48 for men, 45 for women and minors. (Empleados (employees) in practice work a maximum of 7 hours daily). Overtime pay is compulsory; the rate being at least time and a quarter (time and a half is frequently paid). (Empleados are not entitled to pay for overtime devoted to the preparation of semiannual or annual balance sheet statements.) No general rule seems applicable to nightwork (between 8 p.m. and 7 a.m.), but women and minors under 21 are barred from most nightwork.

Uruguay 

Maximum hours generally are 8 per day and 48 per week. Saturday hours are limited to 4 (with pay for 8); in commerce, the maximum weekly hours are 44. The 8-hour day may be exceeded without limitation, provided the workweek does not exceed 6 days or the above weekly hourly maximums. 

Hours may be distributed unevenly; normally, where nightwork is permitted, hours are limited to 7; nightwork in unhealthful activities may not exceed 

30 hours a week. For unauthorized overtime, the law imposes fines on employers and loss of wages for workers. For authorized overtime, there is no general specification that the worker is entitled to greater remuneration. 

However, for work performed on a day of rest, the law expressly states that compensation shall not be less than double the regular pay; and for work performed under special authorization on Saturday in excess of the legal maximum of 4 hours, as during harvest seasons, such extra hours must be paid for at double-time rates. There are few legal precedents: where the law is silent, the courts have been inclined to decide on payment of double wages for overtime. Venezuela

For the purpose of fixing maximum hours, the labor code differentiates between workers (obreros) and employees (empleados). Maximum daytime hours (those between 5 a.m. and 7 p.m.) for "workers" are 8 daily and 48 weekly; for nightwork, 7 and 42. For "employees," maximum hours for daytime work are 8 and 44; for nightwork, 7 and 40. Shift work may exceed 8 hours daily and 48 weekly, provided average hours worked over a 3-week period do not exceed these limits. If authorized by the labor inspector, overtime is permitted, not exceeding 2 hours daily, on not more than 100 days in a year. 

Overtime must be paid for at time and a quarter over the (basic) wage. The regular wage for nightwork must be 20 percent over the regular day scale for the same work.

PAID VACATION 

Brazil

Following 1 year's service with the same employer, workers are entitled to annual paid vacations according to number of days worked the preceding year, as follows: 20 working days' vacation for those who worked 250 or more days with 6 or fewer absences; 15 days for 250 or more days worked; 11 days for 200 to 249 days worked; 7 days for 150 to 199 days worked. The vacation is non cumulative; employers who refuse to grant the leave must pay double time for the nongranted vacation. Chile

Obreros (workers) are entitled to an annual vacation of 15 weekdays with full pay for those who have worked for the same enterprise more than 288 days in the immediately preceding year, and 7 days for those who worked between 220 and 288 days in the year. Empleados (employees) have the right to 15 workdays of paid vacation after 1 year of employment, though this may vary downward in accordance with the number of days actually worked during the year. Empleados in certain areas of the country (provinces of Tarapaca, Antofagasta, Alacama, and Magallanes) receive an annual paid vacation of 25 days. The vacation may not be compensated for in money. Colombia

Following 1 year's service, employees are entitled to annual vacations with pay of 15 consecutive working days. Upon authorization of the 

Ministry of Labor, a cash payment may be made in lieu of the vacation for a period not to exceed one-half of the full vacation period. Upon agreement of the parties, vacations may be accumulated for a period not to exceed 2 years. 

Costa Rica

Employees are entitled to an annual 2 weeks' paid vacation after every 50 weeks of continuous service to the same employer (absence due to illness, leave without pay, etc., does not interrupt continuity). Employers in agriculture or stockraising need not grant vacations, but must, in lieu thereof, pay workers for all days (14) designated by law as holidays, except Sundays (the law stipulates only 5 paid holidays for workers generally). If a worker terminates his employment for any reason prior to taking an earned vacation, he is entitled to payment in lieu thereof; otherwise, vacation may not be compensated for. Vacation payment is equivalent to the average normal and overtime pay earned during the preceding week or longer period as provided in the Regulations (according to a 1952 Superior Labor Court decision, workers regularly receiving certain perquisites in addition to monetary wages, are entitled to the monetary equivalent of the perquisites during vacation periods).

Cuba Employees and workers are entitled to 1 month's paid vacation for each continuous 11 months worked, or a shorter vacation in proportion to time worked. Payment (in advance) is the average salary earned during the preceding months; if the employer normally deducts for food or lodging, the worker may ask for the equivalent cash value in the vacation payment. The law is applicable to farmworkers also, and to workers in the sugar industry who, by virtue of seasonal piecerate labor, shall be paid a bonus of 9.09 percent of wages earned, to be settled at the final pay, in lieu of the paid vacation. The law states that its purpose is to create the possibility of providing employment for substitute personnel during vacation time as well as afford rest to workers, and so prohibits employees from working for any employer during vacation. When employers cannot dispense with the services of individuals within the time limits fixed by law, the Ministry of Labor may waive the time limit, in which case the employer is required to pay a cash compensation of 50 percent of the employee's vacation wage and the vacation rights are made cumulative to the next year. A tripartite National Commission for the 

Use of Paid Vacations is charged with the development of vacation facilities such as transport and resort areas. 

El Salvador 

Following 1 year's continuous service, office workers are entitled to 15 days' paid vacation; for others when working for the same employer or in the same enterprise or establishment, paid vacation entitlements are for nonoffice commercial workers, 15 days; for nonoffice workers in industrial enterprises which employ more than 10 workers, 10 days; other nonagricultural workers, 6 days. Vacation rights are conditioned by a minimum of 200 days worked during the year, and if unjustified absences total 15 days or more, there is no entitlement; if they number between 5 and 14, half vacation is due; and if less than 5, the complete vacation is due. Vacation payment per day for time rate workers is the regular daily wage on the day beginning vacation; for other than time rate workers, the daily vacation payment is equivalent to the average daily regular wage received on worked days of either the first or second 6 months, whichever benefits the worker more. If, by contract, housing or food is considered remuneration and such perquisites are not furnished during vacation, 25 percent additional remuneration is paid in lieu of each. If a work contract is terminated by the employer, the worker is entitled to proportional vacation remuneration. Vacation payment is in advance. The vacation must be taken; it may not be replaced by a cash payment.

Guatemala A worker is entitled to an annual paid vacation after every year in which he works at least 150 days in the service of the same employer, as follows: 15 days in the case of commercial undertakings; 10 days in the case of industrial undertakings, or agricultural or stockraising undertakings which employ permanently not fewer than 500 employees; and 5 days otherwise. The number of days specified for annual vacation are calendar days, thus including weekends and any holidays falling within the vacation period. Vacation pay is based on average wages, including overtime, earned during the last 3 months if employed in an agricultural or stockraising undertaking, or during the last year in other cases. be in advance. Mexico 

Payment must

The law provides that the labor contract shall determine vacations, but stipulates a minimum annual vacation of 4 workdays for nonfarm employees who have worked more than 1 year, and 6 workdays for those in employment over 2 years. In case of unjustifiable absence from work, the employer may deduct such time from the vacation period. Basis for payment for piece-rate work is the average previous month's wages. Nicaragua

Employees are entitled to 15 days' vacation with pay in advance after each 6 months of continuous service to the same employer. Obligatory rest days, absence due to illness, etc., does not interrupt work continuity for vacation purposes. One week of the vacation must be taken; the remainder may be worked and compensated for by double wages upon employer-employee agreement. Vacation payment for piecework, etc., is the equivalent of earnings on the corresponding days of the previous month. Panama

Employees are entitled to annual vacations with pay of 30 days for each continuous 11 months of work. Vacations are accumulative up to 2 vacations, if agreed upon between employer and employee; employees may forego the vacation "in exchange for a pecuniary or economic compensation" upon Ministry of Labor authorization. Payment, required 3 days in advance of vacation, is 

1 month's salary for monthly rate employees; 4 1/3 weeks' pay for weekly rate employees; and for hourly or day rate employees, payment is calculated on the total pay received during the 11 months, divided by the number of regular work shifts served, and this quotient multiplied by the corresponding days of rest to which the worker is entitled. Bonuses and other extra compensations are not considered in determining vacation pay.

Peru

Following a minimum of 260 days worked in a year, obreros (workers) are entitled to a vacation with pay of 15 consecutive days; empleados (employees), 30 consecutive days. Vacations are obligatory, irrenunciable, and noncumulative. However, by agreement with employer, empleados may work 

15 of their 30 days' vacation, in which event they are entitled to double pay for those 15 days; obreros have no such option. Uruguay

Basically, workers are entitled to 12 days of paid vacation annually (plus 3 paid national holidays). Employment need not be continuous, nor with the same employer. The vacation may be split into two 6-day periods. 

Payment is in advance. Proportional payment for an earned, but unused part of vacation, is due at the termination of a labor contract. A number of special laws apply to specific industries and occupations: e.g., rural workers are entitled to annual paid vacations of 8 consecutive days. 

Venezuela 

Nonfarm workers are entitled to 15 workdays of paid vacation for each year of uninterrupted service. Farm workers are entitled to 4 days' vacation at the end of any year in which they have worked more than twothirds of the workdays. Workers (except "employees") whose services are terminated prior to completing 1 year's work shall be paid 1 day's wages for each month of service, in lieu of vacation. Payment for piece-rate workers is based on the average pay received during the preceding 3 months. 

Vacation payment must be made at the beginning of the vacation period.

Brazil

SICK LEAVE In cases of sickness insurance or assistance, workers are considered as being on leave of absence during the period of such benefits. For workers contributing to health. insurance funds administered by a pension institute, employers must pay two-thirds pay for the first 15 days of illness. From the 16th day, the Institute pays for a period of either 12 or 24 months, 66 or 70 percent of the minimum wage applicable in the area. 

Following that period, the worker is entitled to an invalidity pension for up to 5 years, then a life pension if still disabled. Chile

Empleados (employees) who have served the same employer for 1 year or more are entitled to sick leave with pay for certified illness up to 4 months; for 6 to 12 months' service, up to 1 month's paid sick leave; for less than 6 months' service, 1 month's sick leave but without pay. Minimumpay requirements for sick leave rights are: first month, full salary; second month, 75 percent of salary; third month, 50 percent; fourth month, 25 percent. The sick employee is "entitled to an advance during each month of his illness." Colombia

Illness constitutes a justified cause for absence from work. For nonoccupational illness, workers are entitled to leave with two-thirds pay for the first 90 days of illness, one-half pay for an additional 90 days. (As with workmen's compensation obligations, employers usually purchase insurance to take care of this contingency.) 

Costa Rica

No provision. However, illness is not deemed to interrupt continuity of work for purposes of vacation and severance pay. Cuba

All workers except domestics are entitled to 9 days' paid sick leave annually.

. 

El Salvador 

Absence due to illness attributable to gross negligence (established by a labor judge) on the part of the worker is cause for suspension of contract; otherwise, sick and convalescence leave with pay at 75 percent of basic wage is due for 60 days a year for workers having 300 or more days' service, 40 days for 150 to 300 days' service, and 20 days for 30 to 

150 days' service. Also, the employer must grant leave of absence to a worker whose relative's sickness may reasonably require his presence, but is not bound to pay wages for more than 2 days in 1 calendar month or more than 15 days a year in these cases. If any absence is attributable to the employer, wages must be paid. Agricultural workers are generally exempt from these provisions. Guatemala

Employees are entitled to sick leave at half wages not to exceed 3 months for employees of over 9 months' service, 2 months for those with 

6 to 9 months' service, and 1 month for those with between 2 and 6 months' service. Employees with less than 2 months' service are not entitled to sick-leave benefits. Employer must hold job open for 3 months in cases of sick leave by employees with over 2 months' service. Wage payment is calculated on the basis of the average wages earned for ordinary and overtime work during the last 6 months, or fraction thereof if employment has been for a shorter period. Mexico 

No general provision. Nicaragua 

No general provision. Mine workers with over 6 months' service are entitled to 60 percent of their salary during absence due to common sickness, up to 30 days in each 6 months. Other provisions are: (1) For temporary incapacity for work due to an employment-connected accident or illness, the worker is entitled to 60 percent of his wages; (2) an employer must reinstate within 1 year a former employee whose absence is by reason of professional accident or disease, provided the employee has not been indemnified for total permanent disability; (3) an employer must pay wages for time lost through fault of the employer; and (4) no deductions may be made from the salaries or indemnizations be paid to workers who are suffering from accidents, occupational sickness, maternity or other similar conditions, or who are on vacation.

Panama 

Workers are entitled to sick leave with pay at the rate of 12 hours for each 26 working days, or 144 hours per year, which is accumulative up to 2 consecutive years for use during the third year. Employees who have exhausted sick-leave rights may take such leave deducted from earned vacation time; and if employee has over 3 years' service, he is entitled to additional sick leave up to 30 days without pay. Workers suffering illnesses not incurred in the course of employment may suspend the work contract for a period not exceeding 3 months. Peru

No general leave provision. The following stipulations are indicative of the right and practice of sick leave. Verified illness is not deemed to interrupt years of service in the calculation of severance indemnity to discharged employees. All workers who contract certain occupational diseases, or suffer work accidents in certain industries, are entitled to compensation and medical aid from their employer. Employees suffering occupational illness must be granted leave with full pay for the first 2 months of illness; for the next 4 months, 80, 60, 40, and 20 percent of pay, and if the disability is permanent, 20 percent for life. The sickness allowance benefit under the social security program consists of 70 percent of average earnings from the third day of sickness to a maximum period of 26 weeks, which may be extended to 52 weeks in case of long-term diseases or convalescence (the wage percentage is reduced to 35 percent if the insured is admitted to a hospital and has no dependents). Uruguay 

No provision. Venezuela

No provision beyond the stipulation that illness is deemed a justified cause for absence from work.

SEVENTH DAY PAY 1/ 

Brazil 

Workers are entitled to a paid weekly day of rest following 6 days' continuous work (unless absence is justified). Payment is calculated as that for a normal day of work, excluding overtime; for piece-rate workers, payment is the equivalent of weekly earnings in normal hours divided by the number of days actually spent in the employer's service; for homeworkers, payment is calculated on the basis of total weekly production divided by 6; employees engaged on a monthly or bimonthly basis are deemed to receive seventh day pay implicitly. Chile

Sunday rest is obligatory for all workers generally; exceptions include workers in seasonal undertakings dependent upon natural forces, who are entitled to a rest day every fortnight. Obreros (workers), if they have worked a full day "on all days on which the undertaking or section of the undertaking was in operation during the week in question," and if time lost by reason of lateness or leave of absence does not exceed 2 hours in 1 week or 4 hours in a calendar month, qualify for base pay (normal cash remuneration, excluding any other additional or special remuneration) in cash for the Sunday (or statutory holiday) not worked. Piecework remuneration is reckoned according to the average earnings during the week in question, but must not be less than the minimum base pay in cash which the undertaking pays to wage earners employed on timework. Hours worked on 

Sundays (or statutory holidays) are considered overtime and must be paid for as such (time and a half), provided total weekly hours exceed the maximum limits established by law (or those stipulated in the contract, if they are less). Colombia

Nonsalaried employees who work 6 consecutive workdays (or whose absence is for just cause, such as illness) are entitled to pay for the unworked seventh day; if he works that day, he must receive 8 days' pay or a compensatory day off. Payment is on the basis of the normal daily wage; for work on a piece-rate or unit basis, payment is based on the average wage paid in the course of the preceding week.

Costa Rica 

Workers are entitled to a day of rest after each week or 6 days of continuous work. Commercial employees are entitled to pay for this day off; in other cases, only if so stipulated. Upon agreement, a worker may work on the rest day if the work is not difficult, dangerous, or unhealthful and is performed in agricultural or stockraising operations, or in industries by their nature requiring continuous operation or involving obvious public or social interest; remuneration is at time and a half rates in agriculture, otherwise at double time. In industries involving public interest, if a worker does not agree to work on the rest day, the employer may grant cumulative days of rest by the month, following Ministry of Labor authorization, after hearing the worker and deciding against him. Cuba

No provision. However, employees who have worked the full maximum workweek of 4 hours must be paid for 48 hours' work. 

El Salvador 

Nonagricultural workers are entitled to a weekly paid day of rest, unless there has been unjustified absence. Payment is the basic remuneration; daily basic remuneration for piece or task work is calculated by dividing by 6 the total regular remuneration received the week previous; monthly rate workers are understood to receive seventh day pay implicitly. 

If the rest day is worked, employees are entitled to both (1) compensation at time and a half rates (if overtime is performed on this day, overtime rates (see Hours of work, etc., p. 2) are applied on the time and a half rates), and (2) a compensatory day of rest in the same week or the next (in special cases approved by the National Labor Department, rest days may be accumulated up to 4 in 1 month, taken consecutively). Guatemala

Workers are entitled to a rest day with pay after every week of normal work or after every 6 days of continuous work. Payment is equivalent to the average wage for ordinary hours and overtime earned during the week immediately preceding the rest day. For fortnight, or monthly rate employees, payment is deemed to be implicitly included, but in these cases if work is performed on the rest day, payment shall be made, calculated as the daily average of wages earned for ordinary and overtime hours during the last preceding fortnight or month. Mexico

Workers are entitled to a paid day of rest for each 6 days worked.

Nicaragua No requirement. Obligatory rest days are all Sundays and 8 specified holidays a year (or substituted for in certain types of work). The 8 unworked holidays are with pay. If rest days are worked, double time shall be paid. Payment for piecework, etc., shall be an amount equal to the earnings on the corresponding day of the previous month. Panama 

No provision. However, a weekly day of rest (preferably Sunday) is compulsory, and if work is performed on Sunday, payment is to be at time and a half rates. Peru

Obreros (workers) are entitled to a paid day of rest on Sunday following 6 continuous full days' work. Payment for day rate obreros is 

1 day's wage; for piece-rate obreros, one-sixth of weekly average earnings; for obreros paid monthly or fortnightly, one twenty-fifth of 1 month's remuneration. The Sunday pay must include the daily average of the cost-ofliving bonus and food subsidy payment, where these are paid; overtime pay, output bonuses, tips, etc., are excluded from the pay calculation. Uruguay 

No provision. A weekly day of rest is obligatory, but without pay. Venezuela

Workers are entitled to a weekly day of rest on Sunday with regular pay. One day's absence from work during the week does not change this right. For piece-rate workers, seventh day pay shall be the average earned in the corresponding week.

PROFIT SHARING 2/ 

Brazil

The constitution lists among precepts to which labor legislation must adhere, the "Obligatory and direct participation of the worker in the profits of concerns, under the terms and in the form determined by law." Implementing legislation, however, has not been enacted. Chile

Obreros (workers) who are members of a legal union and are employed by any industrial or commercial concern with at least 25 obreros are entitled to a share in the profit of the enterprise. The companies concerned must set aside 10 percent of their annual net profits for this purpose. Each share paid is determined by the wage of the individual and the number of days worked, but it may in no case exceed 6 percent of the total wages paid the obrero during the year. Half of the profit shares accruing to obreros are paid to them and half to their unions. If the enterprise is incorporated it may grant the unions stock shares equivalent to 6 percent of paid-in capital instead of paying the 10 percent of profits; this option is not widely practiced, however. 

Empleados (employees) of industrial and commercial undertakings are entitled to an annual bonus for which the company must set aside 20 percent of its net profits. One-half of the amount set aside is to be distributed among the empleados according to their annual salaries and the other half according to the number of years of service. The maximum sum payable is 6 times the monthly minimum salary set for the particular type of work in Santiago. The employer has the option of complying with this requirement or of giving the empleado a bonus of 25 percent of his annual salary up to a specified maximum.

However, bonuses of one sort or another--sometimes referred to as "Christmas," "annual," or "year-end" bonuses--are customary or decreed in many Latin 

American countries.

Colombia The 1950 labor code provides that an annual "service bonus" be substituted for profit shares required under previous legislation, in the amount of 1 month's wages for workers in undertakings capitalized at 

200,000 pesos or more, 15 days' wages when capital is less. (Previous profit-sharing legislation was as follows: A 1948 decree required that industrial and commercial enterprises capitalized at 100,000 pesos or more; and agricultural, forestry, and stockraising undertakings capitalized at 200,000 pesos or more, must distribute a portion of profits to their workers according to a graduated scale ranging from 5 percent of that profit which exceeds 12 percent but is less than 15 percent of the capital, to 20 percent of that profit which exceeds 35 percent of the capital.

The individual worker's share was to be determined in accordance with a points system based on wage or salary earned, family responsibilities, seniority, attendance, efficiency, and honesty. ALIEN EMPLOYMENT RESTRICTIONS 3/ 

Brazil

Two-thirds of all employees of commercial and industrial firms must be Brazilian nationals, and two-thirds of the payroll must be paid to Brazilians. Chile

At least 85 percent of persons classed as empleados (employees) of any one employer must be Chilean nationals, and at least 85 percent of the empleado payroll must be paid to Chilean nationals. Technical employees are excluded from these percentages when technicians are not available among the Chilean population. The law does not apply to obreros (workers). Colombia

At Least 90 percent of unskilled workers and at least 80 percent of skilled workers, specialists, and managerial employees of an enterprise must be Colombian nationals. These percentages may be reduced for strictly technical and indispensable staffs for such time as is required in training 

Colombian nationals. 

Costa Rica

At least 90 percent of all workers in an enterprise must be Costa Rican nationals, and at least 85 percent of the total payroll must be paid to Costa Ricans. 

3/ Described variously as laws on nationalization of employment and as proportional employment legislation, these requirements specify minimum proportions of nationals which must be maintained by each employer. Sometimes the same or similar percentage is applied to the employer's payroll. This section provides summaries of this basic requirement without regard to special provisions of limited applicability, e.g., those regarding employment in public works or the practice of certain professions. Coupled with the basic proportional employment provisions is sometimes the stipulation that nationals shall not be paid less, nor afforded poorer working conditions, than aliens doing equal work; when not so included, a requirement calling for equal pay for equal work without regard to nationality as well as age, sex, race, etc., is usually found in other basic labor legislation or in the national constitution.

Cuba The 1933 Law of Cubanization of Labor required that at least 50 percent of workers in each craft and employee grouping in an enterprise must be Cubans by birth, and at least 50 percent of total payroll must be paid to native Cubans; vacancies and new positions must be occupied by native or naturalized Cubans, and reductions in force must start with foreigners, if any. Technical positions requiring special skills may be excluded from these requirements when appropriate Cubar technicians are not available.

El Salvador 

Except in agriculture and homeworking, at least 90 percent of the workers in an enterprise must be Salvadoran nationals, and at least 85 percent of the payroll must be paid to Salvadorans. These percentages may be reduced by 10 percent under special circumstances and for the purpose of employing persons whom it is difficult or impossible to replace by Salvadorans, on condition that the employer train Salvadoras for such positions. For the calculation of these percentages, all persons of Central American origin are considered Salvadoran. Guatemala

At least 90 percent of the workers in an enterprise must be Guatemalan nationals, and at least 85 percent of the payroll must be paid to Guatemala ans. 

Under special conditions these percentages may be reduced by 10 percent, in which case the employer must institute training programs for the purpose of qualifying Guatemalans to replace alien workers. Mexico

At least 90 percent of workers in each technical and unskilled category utilized by an employer must be Mexican nationals. Nicaragua

At least 75 percent of the workers in an enterprise must be Nicaraguan nationals, technical employees excluded. Panama

At least 75 percent of the workers in each personnel category of an enterprise must be Panamanian nationals, and at least 75 percent of the payroll must be paid to Panamanians. Technicians are generally excepted, and the percentages may be reduced temporarily in cases of shortage of specialized workers.

Peru

At least 80 percent of all persons employed by each employer or enterprise must be Peruvian nationals, and at least 80 percent of the payroll must be paid to Peruvian nationals. Technical personnel are excluded from these percentages when Peruvian technicians are not available. Uruguay 

No basic provision. Venezuela

Except in agriculture, at least 75 percent of all persons employed in an enterprise must be Venezuelan nationals; this percentage applies separately to empleados (employees) and obreros (workers). Under special circumstances, a temporary reduction in this percentage may be permitted for technical reasons.

PERSONNEL ROSTER (ESCALAFON) / 

Brazil

Employees not holding positions of trust or whose labor contracts do not involve, implicitly or explicitly, the condition of transfer, may not be transferred without their consent to a locality other than that to which their labor contract refers if the transfer necessarily requires a change of domicile, except in the event of the extinction of the establishment in which the employee works. Despite this restriction, an employee may be transferred to a different locality in cases of "necessity of work," in which event he is entitled to a 25-percent salary increase for the duration of the transfer. The employer must pay the expenses of a transfer. 

The requirement of equal pay for equal work regardless of sex is deemed inapplicable in cases of promotion by seniority, when work is organized on a career basis. Chile

No requirement. However, an empleado (employee) receiving wages less than or equal to one and one-half times the "living" (minimum) wage established annually in his area of the country by a tripartite commission is entitled to an increase of 3 percent each time a year's employment is completed, provided there has been no change in his income. Those receiving more than one and one-half times the "living wage" are entitled to a 10-percent increase each time they complete 3 years' service with the same employer, provided there has been no voluntary increase; if the 10-percent increment exceeds 40 percent of the "living wage," the increment shall be limited to that equaling 40 percent of the "living wage." Colombia

Among the 19 items which must be covered in the rules of employment which employers are required to provide and which become an integral part of the employment contract, is the requirement of indicating "the conditions of admission, apprenticeship, and probationary period." Rules of employment must not contain "rules of a purely technical or administrative nature" nor any rules not enumerated among the 19 referred to above. 

Escalafon refers to a roster system which governs promotions, transfers, and other personnel changes on the basis of such factors as seniority and merit, as for example, was required in Guatemala prior to 1955. (See Guatemala section.) Although in none of these countries is there now a comprehensive escalafon system legally required, there are summarized in this section legal provisions generally relating to such personnel changes.

Costa Rica 

Such internal work regulations as the employer may prepare (and requiring approval by the Labor Inspection Service) should include worksalary categories, disciplinary provisions, and special standards by type of work pertaining to age and sex. A 1955 administrative decision of the 

Ministry of Labor specified that seniority must be observed in layoffs and discharges. Cuba

No provision other than the stipulation that pay must be equal for equal work performed under identical conditions, unless differences are based on legitimate seniority, capacity, speciality, or other pertinent peculiarities.

El Salvador 

No provision other than rules of employment which must be drawn up by commercial and industrial undertakings and establishments employing permanently 10 or more workers, and approved by the Director of the National Department of Labor, must cover a number of specified matters. These include time, day, and place for wage payment, rules for maximum performance of work, and "such other conditions and rules as the Director of the National Department of Labor may consider necessary"; payrolls must be submitted to labor inspectors on request. Guatemala

A 1948 amendment to the labor code, but annulled in February 1955, had obliged the employer: "To establish a roster which governs promotions and other personnel changes, taking into account fundamentally the capacity and efficiency of the worker and, other things being equal, length of service in the enterprise. The capacity and efficiency of the workers must be appraised by committees composed of workers and employers with the intervention of the Ministry of Labor and Social Welfare when they are unable to agree." Mexico

No provisions regulating escalafon, but among those referring to it are those which require employers (1) to make readjustments, in the absence of stipulations in a collective contract, observing the right of seniority; (2) to prefer Mexicans and organized workers over those not, under similar circumstances; and (3) to consider physical capacity, efficiency, and seniority in the promotion of railwaymen.

DISCIPLINARY DISCHARGE AND SEVERANCE INDEMNITY 5/ 

Brazil

An employee serving the same firm less than 10 years may be discharged for just cause, as defined by the law, without receiving any indemnity. In defining "just cause," the law includes disloyalty, dishonesty, habitual drunkenness, insubordination, etc., but the law is strictly interpreted and decisions are generally in favor of the employee. An employee of 10 or more years' service may be discharged for just cause as defined by the law, but only after an administrative inquiry by the Ministry of Labor.

An employee with less than 1 year's service may be discharged without just cause without indemnity; employees with from 1 to 10 years' service are entitled to indemnity; employees of over 10 years' service may be discharged on proof of "incompatibility" with the work, in which case double indemnity must be paid. 

For indefinite-term contracts, indemnity is 1 month's remuneration for each year of service, each fraction equal to or in excess of 6 months (after the first year) being reckoned as a year. Basis of indemnity is for dayworkers, 25 days; for hourly workers, 200 hours; for commission or percentage workers, average earnings of last 3 years; for piece or job workers, average time involved by piece or job, calculating the value of that which would be accomplished in 30 days. For fixed-term contracts, employees are entitled to half remuneration to the expiration of the contract, amount to be calculated as in cases of indefinite-term contracts.

Chile

Empleados (employees) may be discharged, without liability for indemnity, for desertion for 2 consecutive days without due cause, fraud, or breach of trust, or for operations carried out by the employee within the business which are prohibited by the employer in writing in the contract of employment. Obreros (workers) may be discharged, without liability for any indemnity, for a number of causes, including dishonesty, violence, immoral conduct, property damage, and absence without sufficient reason for 2 consecutive days, 2 Mondays in the month, or 3 days altogether in a month.

Empleados discharged for any reason except those noted above, who do not voluntarily resign, and who have been in the service of the same employer at least 1 full year, are entitled to 1 month's salary for each full year of service, taking into account the entire salary up to a maximum of 1,000 pesos. The payment is based on the average salary and/or commission during the last 6 months worked, and is paid from a salaried employees' fund to which the employer contributes 8.33 percent of payroll; the employee nothing. An obrero discharged without fault, or with fault if employed for more than 1 year, is entitled to certain contingent traveling expenses to his former residence. Discharged obreros in cases of collective dismissal of 10 or more or in the event of total stoppage of an undertaking, are entitled to compensation equal to 15 days' wages for every year or fraction of a year exceeding 9 months in the undertaking concerned, provided advance approval for the dismissals or stoppage is secured from either the Minister of Economic Affairs and Commerce or the Minister of Labor. (Virtually all collective labor agreements provide for a lump-sum severance payment for obreros, usually specified as 2 weeks' pay ment for each year worked.)

Colombia Offenses and disciplinary sanctions must be indicated in the rules of employment which employers are required to provide and which become an integral part of the employment contract. If sanctions include suspension from work, such suspension must not exceed 8 days for first offenses, and 2 months for repeated offenses. Sanctions not provided for, are illegal. The law provides that workers may be discharged, without indemnity, for felonious acts, serious intentional property destruction, or detrimental revelation of business secrets. Trade union officials may be discharged, transferred, or reduced in grade only for just cause as determined by the Ministry of Labor.

The employer is required to pay a severance indemnity of 1 month's pay for each year worked, or proportionately for fractions of a year's service. Payment is based on employee's last monthly wage, except that any increase received after the effective date of the labor code will affect only the part of the severance pay for the 3 years preceding the dismissal. Employees of certain undertakings of limited capitalization are entitled to only 15 days' wages for each year of service.

In addition, if an indefinite-term contract fails to contain a "reserve clause" providing for 45 days' notification or payment of salary in lieu thereof, the contract is presumed to be for successive periods of 6 months, and the discharged worker is entitled to a terminal payment equivalent to his salary for any unworked portion of a 6 months' period.

Costa Rica

Such internal work regulations as the employer may prepare (and requiring approval by the Labor Inspection Service) are expected to include disciplinary provisions. Disciplinary suspensions may not be imposed (1) if not based on such provisions in the work regulations, (2) before granting the worker a hearing, (3) for more than 8 days. (Labor court decisions have maintained that suspensions and other disciplinary sanctions cannot be imposed if internal work regulations do not exist.) The law provides that employees may be discharged, without indemity, for a number of causes, including immoral conduct, property damage, revelation of secrets, unjustified absence without permission for 2 consecutive days or for more than 2 days in the same calendar month, etc.

When a worker under indefinite contract is discharged without just cause as so enumerated in the law, or because of redundancy, or resigns for just cause (similar to above but imputable to employer), and unless he becomes entitled to retirement pay or old-age pension granted by the Government or Social Security Fund, he is entitled to severance compensation not to exceed the equivalent of 8 months' pay, after continuous work, as follows: For 3 to 6 months' service, an amount equivalent to 

10 days' wages; for 6 to 12 months' service, 20 days' wages; for service over 1 year, 1 month's wages for each year of service or fraction not less than 6 months. Payment amount is based on average wages earned during the last 6 months, or for a shorter period if such period was not completed.

In job or fixed-time contracts, contract termination without just cause may be made by either party, subject to any indemnity a labor court may impose; if the employer terminates the contract, the employee is entitled to 1 day's wage for each 6 days of continuous service with a minimum of 3 days' wages, or if the contract was for a period of 6 months or more, a minimum of 1 month's wages.

--  Cuba

Specified just causes authorizing an employer to discharge workers include such offenses as negligence, habitual drunkenness, divulgence of secrets, unjustified absence for more than 2 consecutive days or more than 12 days in the year, etc. In all cases of dismissal or suspension, the employer must present a statement of charges to the employee, his labor organization, and the Ministry of Labor or provincial labor office. A 

Ministry of Labor official then hears evidence and conducts investigations until the end of the agreed upon time for proceedings, whereupon the entire case is turned over to the employer who must issue a resolution withdrawing charges, imposing sanctions, or dismissing the worker. This resolution is sent to the worker, his labor organization, and the Ministry of Labor or provincial labor office with the case file. The employer's must also inform the worker and his organization of appeal procedures. 

Upon appeal, the Ministry of Labor studies the case and issues a resolution confirming the employer's action, modifying the terms, or reinstating the worker; this decision may be appealed to the President of the Republic whose decision may in turn be appealed to the courts. Agricultural workers and temporary workers of less than 6 months' service are exempt from these provisions. (There has been occasional evidence of some relaxation in the interpretation and administration of the law in recent years.)

There is no severance-pay requirement. The Labor Ministry in early 

1954 prepared draft legislation providing for indemnified dismissal, but no action on this measure has been taken as yet.

El Salvador 

Employees may be discharged without liability to the employer for a number of causes, including immoral conduct, repeated or gross negligence, property damage, divulgence of secrets, and unjustified absence without permission for 2 whole consecutive days or for 3 days in 1 calendar month. Commercial and industrial undertakings and establishments employing permanently 10 or more workers must draw up rules of employment, approved by the Director of the National Labor Department, which must contain disciplinary rules and sanctions; workers may be suspended for not more than 1 day for each breach of discipline under the respective rules. 

For dismissal without just cause, workers under indefinite-term contracts are entitled to compensation equal to (1) 15 days' wages if in the service of an employer 3 months or less; (2) 30 days' wages for 

3 to 12 months' service, (3) 90 days' wages for 1 to 5 years' service and (4) thereafter, 90 days' wages for every period of 5 years or fraction thereof exceeding 2 years. For unjust dismissal under a definite-term or job contract, workers are entitled to compensation equal to the wages to the end of the contract, provided it does not exceed what would be required under an indefinite-term contract. 

Payment amount is based on average wages earned for normal hours of work during the preceding 30 working days, or over the 12 months preceding if commissions form all or part of remuneration.

An employer may not discharge a worker for inefficiency in a post of greater responsibility to which he has been promoted within 3 months but rather must reinstate him in his former position; if the worker considers himself unsuited for the new post and within 3 months requests reinstatement in the former position, the employer shall oblige. worker is entitled to reinstatement at the cessation of any of 14 specified circumstances which may have permitted contract suspension. Agricultural workers generally are exempt from these provisions.

Guatemala

Employees may be discharged, without liability for indemnity, for a number of causes, mostly involving immoral conduct, property damage, divulgence of secrets, etc., and including absence from work without permission for 2 whole consecutive days or on 3 occasions in a month. 

Under indefinite-term contracts, in cases of unjustifiable dismissal, or justifiable resignation, workers are entitled to severance pay at the rate of 1 month's wages for each year of uninterrupted employment, or proportionate compensation if the employment was for less than a year. Payment amount shall be based on average wages earned during the entire period of employment subsequent to March 1, 1956 (previously, on average wages of last 6 months of employment). Under fixed-term contracts, damages deemed sufficient by a labor inspector or by the labor courts must be paid by the party terminating the contract, if terminated without good reasons; if premature termination is ordered by the employer, however, the damages he is bound to pay to the discharged worker must be at least 1 day's wages for every month of uninterrupted employment. Changes in the labor code in February 1955 removed all workers' rights to petition for reinstatement, and canceled the right of courts to order reinstatement of workers deemed unjustifiably discharged.

Mexico

Obligatory work rules (made in accordance with collective contract provisions, or in lieu thereof by an employer-worker commission) must contain disciplinary provisions and sanctions; however, a disciplinary suspension may not exceed 8 days. Employees may be discharged, without liability for indemnity, for a number of causes, including dishonesty, violence, property damage, immoral acts, revelation of trade secrets, intoxication, unjustifiable absence without permission for more than 3 times during 1 month, etc.

For dismissal without just cause, workers are entitled to either contractual compliance or compensation equal to 3 months' wages, at the worker's option. Employers are so liable also in cases of voluntary quits by reason of employer dishonesty or ill treatment. In cases of disputes, if the employer refuses to submit to arbitration or to accept an arbitral award, the arbitration board shall decree that the worker be compensated with 3 months' wages, plus in cases of fixed-term contracts not exceeding 1 year, an amount equal to wages earned during one-half of the time worked; in cases of fixed-term contracts exceeding 1 year, an amount equal to 6 months' wages for the first year and 20 days' wages for the remaining years of the contract; in cases of indefinite-term contracts, 20 days' wages for each year of service, provided these latter indemnities do not exceed that to which the worker would be entitled for death due to an industrial accident. These wage indemnities are calculated to include wages, bonuses, profit shares, and any other economic advantages stipulated for the worker. Nicaragua

Employees may be discharged, without liability for any indemnity, for a number of causes, including dishonesty, immoral conduct, violence, property damage, divulgence of secrets, abandonment of work, negligence or manifest unskillfulness, and absence from work on 3 working days in any 1 month.

In cases of dismissal without just cause under indefinite-term contract, daily and weekly rate employees are entitled to 4 weeks' notice and fortnightly rate employees to 2 months' notice; these notice periods are increased by one-fifth for each year of service, and may be compensated for by lump-sum payment of wages for the period (which is the usual practice and hence creates a form of severance pay). Workers under definite-term contract who are discharged without just cause are entitled to pay for the remainder of the contract period. An employer must reinstate within 1 year a former employee whose absence is by reason of professional accident or disease, provided the employee has not been indemnified for total permanent disability.

Panama 

A worker may be discharged, without liability for any indemnity, for a number of causes, including immoral conduct, property damage, divulgence of secrets, absence from work without permission or justified cause for 2 consecutive days or ↳ Mondays in the course of the same year, or for more than 2 alternate days in the same month; for "manifest incompetence or inefficiency," for "loss of employer's confidence" in carrying out tasks of supervision or direction, etc.

The labor code includes no severance pay provision. Employees must be reinstated if not completely incapacitated and if not more than 1 year has elapsed since incapacitation. Peru

An obrero (worker) or empleado (employee), may be discharged without employer liability for severance indemnity for stealing, unjustifiable absence for more than 3 consecutive days, serious lack of respect toward the employer, habitual intoxication, or dealing in the employer's business for his own account.

Obreros are entitled to severance pay at the rate of 15 days' pay per year of service if employer's capital exceeds 500,000 soles, 6 days' pay if the employer's capital is less. Empleados are entitled to 1 month's pay for each year of service.

Uruguay 

No disciplinary provisions. 

Except for dismissal for "serious misconduct," workers generally are entitled to 1 month's pay for each year of service; workers on a piece-rate basis, or on daily or hourly wages and permanently employed in industry, are entitled to 25 days' pay for each year during which the worker has worked 240 days. Workers who have worked between 100 and 240 days are entitled to 2 days' compensation for every 25 days of work. Severance pay is limited to 3 months' pay if the worker is pensionable, 6 months if not. Venezuela 

Without liability for payment of either termination pay or compensation for length of service, employers may discharge workers for just causes including lack of honesty, acts of violence, immoral conduct, property damage, unjustifiable absence from work for 3 days in 1 month, gross failure to comply with the terms of a labor contract, serious lack of respect due an employer, harmful revelation of trade secrets, and abandonment of work. 

For unjustifiable dismissal, for contract termination for any reason beyond a worker's control, or if the worker resigns for a justifiable reason as determined by law (similar to above, but imputable to the employer), he is entitled to "compensation for length of service" equivalent to one-half of the wages earned during the last complete month of service for each year of uninterrupted work or fraction over 8 months (for farm workers, 6 days' pay for each year in which work has been performed during two-thirds of the work-days in that year). For task or piecework, payment shall be one-twelfth of all wages earned during the 6 months preceding work cessation. 

In addition, under the same conditions but if the work contract is for an indefinite period, he is entitled to severance pay (in the nature of unemployment compensation) based on continuous employment, as follows: 5 days' pay for 3 to 6 months' service; 10 days' pay for 

6 to 12 months' service; 15 days' pay for each year of service or fraction thereof greater than 8 months. Severance pay may not exceed the equivalent of 8 months' pay; it may not be claimed if the worker is protected by unemployment insurance or old-age or retirement insurance; and, unlike length of service compensation, it is not payable upon death of the employee or upon the closing of business through no fault of the employer.

MINIMUM-WAGE MACHINERY 6/ 

Brazil

Minimum-wage rates are decreed by the President after decisions of regional minimum-wage boards are submitted to him by the Minister of Labor, Industry, and Commerce with such amendments as the Ministry may have made. The boards are separately responsible for the 22 States and regions into which the country is divided; the boards consist of from 5 to 11 members, comprising equal members of representatives of employers and employees and a chairman; chairmen are appointed by the President; members, by the Minister of Labor, Industry, and Commerce. 

Rates apply to all workers in the region, zone, or subzone, and are for 3-year periods (but have remained unaltered for longer periods). 

Besides this legal minimum-wage system, there are in Brazil other provisions concerning wage regulation. Worthy of particular note is the compulsory observances by all members of an industry of collective labor agreements entered into by at least three-quarters of the total number of workers or employers, and the provision for compulsory settlement of industrial disputes by official minimum-wage fixing. Chile

There are 4 different systems governing payment of minimum wages: (1) Industrial wage-earning workers, (2) private salaried employees, (3) nitrate workers, and (4) agricultural workers. In addition, the re are other forms of wage regulation: (1) For special groups of workers, such as a 1952 act which fixed special remuneration for waiters in hotels and restaurants; (2) profit sharing; (3) special bonuses for private employees with family dependents, through a family allowance system; and (4) decree percentage wage and salary increases for all workers and employees.

For industrial workers, minimum wages are determined by area employerworker boards, presided over by local government officials, for periods of 1 year or less; such determinations are sent to the General Directorate of 

Labor, which issues a final decision. For private salaried employees, minimum ("living") salaries are determined annually in each province by 4year term, employer-worker boards, presided over by the governor and under the supervision of the Central Joint Salary Board. For nitrate workers, minimum wages are determined by area worker-employer boards, presided over by a government official, for periods of 6 to 12 months, separately for 8 specified categories of work. For agricultural workers, minimum wages are determined annually by 3-year term, provincial boards comprising the local labor inspector, a local agronomist, the head of the local social security office, and workers' and employers' representatives, through recommendations to a central board.

Costa Rica

A tripartite National Wage Board, appointed by the President, recommends minimum day and piece rates every 2 years, for executive approval by industry, occupation, and geographic area. The general policy of the 

Board has been to simplify awards as much as possible so that, eventually, the only wage fixed by the Board shall be a basic wage, and all other wages shall be freely arrived at by negotiations between employers and workers. Cuba

The Board consists of 3 government officials, 7 representatives each of employers and workers in the sugar, stockraising, coffee-growing, tobaccogrowing, commerce, and railway industries, all appointed by the Minister of 

Labor who acts as chairman. 

El Salvador 

The constitution states that workers shall be entitled to a minimum wage, to be fixed at intervals, paying special attention to the cost of living, etc., and to be sufficiently high to satisfy normal needs, etc., but as yet there is no minimum-wage legislation or machinery in 

El Salvador.

Guatemala

The minimum-wage system as set forth in the labor code calls for tripartite minimum-wage boards to be set up in departments and in economic areas for the annual determination of rates. Some boards have been established but difficulties precluded the fixing of rates. 

As a result of a technical assistance agreement between Guatemala and the International Labor Office, minimum-wage boards were set up in the textile industry, and a Presidential order in February 1953 put a minimum wage into operation in that industry. Mexico

Municipal minimum-wage boards, consisting of an equal number of workers' and employers' representatives (not less than 2 in each case) and chaired by a municipal official, are convened each odd year to set local wage minimums for 2-year periods, following approval by the Central Conciliation and Arbitration Board in the State. Rates fixed are usually for rural and urban work, and usually include several for urban workers by occupations. 

In addition to this general system of minimum-wage fixing, there are other methods of wage regulation. To be noted particularly is the compulsory extension to all workers in a given branch of industry of the wages stipulated in collective agreements entered into by more than twothirds of the employers and workers in that branch. Characteristic of this form of wage regulation is that detailed wage rates for different occupations are specified in the contracts and the worker's skill and productivity are taken into consideration. Nicaragua

The labor code provides for the establishment in the capital of each department of a minimum-wage board consisting of 2 workers and 2 employers from the area and chaired by the local magistrate. These boards are to recommend minimum time, piece, or job rates for each branch of economic activity to the Minister of Labor (through the General Labor Inspectorate) who is to decree rates for 1-year periods. Payments in kind are considered part of the minimum wage. Where no specific minimum is fixed, the minimum wage is considered to be the wage paid to workers with equal qualifications and under similar conditions, decreased by one-fourth. (No specific minimum wage has as yet been set in Nicaragua.)

Panama 

The labor code provides that minimum wages shall be fixed and wages in general regulated for all workers except in agricultural undertakings employing fewer than 5 workers, or stockraising undertakings employing fewer than 3. The minimum-wage schedule is to be fixed by areas by the 

Minister of Labor on the basis of recommendations by the National Minimum Wage Board, consisting of 3 Government officials and 1 representative each from a national employers' and a national employees' association. 

Board recommendations are scheduled every 2 years, and the Board must prepare a report on rate revisions whenever requested by at least 10 employers or workers within a given branch of industry. Peru

Although the constitution calls for the statutory regulation of all wages, Peru as yet has no general legal minimum-wage fixing system. There is, however, an extensive series of acts, decrees, and orders respecting minimum wages in specific industries and occupations, and limited, usually, to specified geographical areas. A 1936 decree providing for the establishment of conciliation boards prescribes that labor disputes which cannot be settled by these boards must be referred to arbitration courts which, when the disputes concern wages, are competent to fix minimum rates. Uruguay Minimum-wage rates are fixed by special tripartite minimum-wage boards established occasionally for occupational groups within specified industries. If the Council of Ministers considers the rates too low, and the board does not suitably amend the award, the Government may fix the minimum. For certain industries and occupations (e.g., rural workers), minimum wages are fixed directly by legislation. 

Venezuela 

The labor code provides that the Government, when it considers it necessary, may appoint minimum-wage boards to fix minimum wages by industries or branches of industries, and that on these boards "insofar as possible, the interests of employers and of workers shall be represented." 

Other legislation calls for board membership to include 3 to 6 employers' representatives and an equal number of workers' representatives "where this is deemed necessary, in accordance with the law." The boards fix both piece and time minimum rates, and report them to the National Labor Office which forwards them to the Government with its own comments and any other comments it has received. Upon Government approval and publication, the rates become effective. In practice, however, this minimum-wage legislation has been rarely applied.

Guatemala

Nicaragua

UNION DUES CHECKOFF AND FUNDS CONTROLS 

Brazil

The main source of union income is the "trade union tax"--a compulsory annual payroll deduction of 1 day's pay for every worker (except those in agriculture, government, or domestic service), whether a union member or not. The Bank of Brazil collects these "taxes" and credits 59 percent to the appropriate local union, 15 and 5 percent respectively to the federation (usually regional) and confederation (national) with which the local union is affiliated, and 20 percent to a special Social Fund administered by the Minister of Labor, Industry, and Commerce (through a committee representing employees, employers, liberal professions, and other contributing groups). The remaining 1 percent is retained by the 

Bank of Brazil for expenses incident to administration of its tax funds. (If there is no local union, the share that would ordinarily go to the local union goes to the federation; if there is no federation, the share that would ordinarily go to the federation goes to the confederation; and if there is no confederation, the share that would ordinarily go to the confederation goes to the Social Fund.) The unions may spend these funds only on employment services, cooperatives, medical and legal aid, schools and libraries, and recreation (projects similar to those for which workers' contributions to the Social Fund are used). Any other activities must be financed by the local unions' monthly dues, reportedly a very small part of union revenue. The Government must approve each union budget annually. Chile

The employer must deduct union dues from the payroll if the union so requests. Funds controls include the following: (1) Mutual benefit, pension and insurance funds established by the union are subject to authorization and supervision by the General Labor Inspectorate; (2) funds must be deposited in the nearest branch of the National Savings Bank; (3) sums kept in union cashboxes must not exceed 500 pesos in cash; (4) investment of union funds received as profit shares is charged to a committee consisting of the union chairman and a representative each of the undertaking and the President of the Republic; (5) union fund expenditures exceeding 

2,000 pesos must be authorized by the labor inspector for the locality; (6) the competent labor inspectorate must be sent a copy of the union treasurer's balance sheet every 6 months; (7) union funds may not be used to provide loans to members.

Colombia 

Checkoff of union dues is permitted but not required. Although union functions are enumerated in the labor code, no specific restriction on the use of union funds is provided. However, no funds or property of a union may be used for purposes other than those for which the union was formed; certain safeguards with regard to proper budgeting and budgetary checks on fund outlays, deposit of funds, and treasurer's security are provided; and it is stipulated that "the accounts of every industrial association shall be managed in accordance with the provisions to be issued in this respect by the National Department for the Supervision of Industrial Associations." 

Costa Rica

The employer must deduct ordinary union dues from the worker's pay at the request of the union and with the consent of the worker. No provision relative to funds controls other than that unions must keep account books "duly stamped and authorized by the General Labor Office." Cuba

A number of law-decrees in late January 1955 made compulsory a checkoff of 1 percent of earnings for union dues in a large number of industries. A 

March 1954 decree required the checkoff in the sugar industry, where mills must withhold 1 percent of each worker's pay and deposit it as follows: 45 percent to the local union, 31 percent to the National Federation of Sugar Workers, 13 percent to the Confederation of Cuban workers, 9 percent to the latter's special Committee on International Policy, and 2 percent retained for employer expenses incident to the checkoff. There are no specific controls on the use of union funds; unions are prohibited from engaging in any commercial activities.

El Salvador 

Checkoff of union dues is compulsory, on penalty of fine, provided the union has communicated the names of member employees through the departmental labor inspector. The Ministries of Labor and Economic 

Affairs are charged with supervising the financial administration of all unions. Among specified functions of unions is the establishment and subsidization of mutual benefit societies, cooperatives, clubs, etc., for members, and the acquisition of necessary property. Required rules which must be government-approved cover dues, including percentage set aside for a fund guaranteeing union obligations, and which regulate expenditures and percentages of income for various activities, restricting the percentage earmarked for administration from "becoming excessively large" in relation to other expenditures. Union management must keep account records, authorized and sealed by the Industrial Associations Section of the Ministry of Labor, and must deposit union funds in banks, "while maintaining liquid funds in its own office for petty expenses." Unions are prohibited from dividend distribution from union funds and from any lucrative activities or any investment of union funds not associated with subsidized mutual benefit societies, etc. Guatemala

If the employee or his union so requests, the employer must deduct union dues from wages.

One of the many provisions of the labor code dealing with trade unions charges the President with the development of a national policy for the protection of the labor movements in conformity with certain principles, including exercising the strictest possible supervision over unions to see that they conform to statutory provisions, particularly regarding the satisfactory management of their finances. One code provision requires unions to fix administrative expenses "at a reasonable percentage; " otherwise, "registration, authorization, and grant of the status of body corporate shall be refused."

Mexico

Upon the request of the union, the employer must deduct union dues from wage payments; in such cases, the union must prove that the amounts deducted are those fixed in its bylaws. The same applies to contributions for cooperative societies and savings funds. The enterprise may demand compensation from the union or from the union's cooperative or savings fund for expenses incident to the checkoff. As to union funds controls, provisions are restricted to these stipulations: (1) Union bylaws must state manner of dues payment, amount, and method of administering such dues; (2) unions may acquire goods but only buildings "immediately and directly intended for the object of their institution"; (3) unions must furnish labor authorities with reports requested, provided they "refer exclusively to their actions as such unions"; (4) unions are prohibited from "exercising the trade of merchants for lucrative purposes"; and (5) the union's directorate must "render to the general meeting of its members, at least every 6 months, a complete and detailed account of the administration of funds of the union". Nicaragua

No provision regarding dues checkoff. CONCILIATION AND ARBITRATION 

Brazil

All individual and collective grievances and disputes must be referred to a highly developed system of labor courts, a specialized branch of the judiciary in which Government, labor, and management are represented. The system includes local (temporarily convened) conciliation boards, or in their absence, ordinary law courts; regional labor courts; and the Supreme Labor Court which has national jurisdiction and is the third-instance organ. The courts' benches are presidentially appointed. Legal procedures are stipulated by a wide variety of special regulations. There is also a National Mediation Service in the Labor 

Department of the Ministry of Labor, Industry, and Commerce. Chile

A conciliation attempt is compulsory. Arbitration is generally voluntary; it is compulsory in public services. Arbitral awards are binding for the period stipulated which must not be less than 6 months. Colombia

Arbitration is compulsory for collective disputes in public service enterprises which fail to reach settlement by direct agreement or by conciliation. All arbitral decisions are compulsory and form the basis of the new labor contract. 

Costa Rica

Conciliation must be employed to settle problems which might lead to a collective conflict. Arbitration is voluntary except in public service activities. Arbitral awards are binding on all parties for the period stipulated which must not be less than 6 months. Cuba

Conciliation procedures are compulsory prior to calling a strike. 

There are no arbitration provisions in the law. 

El Salvador 

Conciliation is compulsory. Arbitration is compulsory only in public services. All arbitral awards are binding.

· Guatemala

Prior to the calling of a lawful strike, the detailed conciliation procedure set forth in the labor code must be exhausted. If settlement under conciliation is not achieved, employees must call a strike; if they fail to do so, they are bound to submit to compulsory arbitration. 

Compliance with arbitration awards is compulsory. Mexico

A system of tripartite boards of conciliation and arbitration operate to settle labor disputes, both individual and collective. The constitution appears to make arbitration compulsory, but Mexico's basic labor law does not. The civil code states that a party to any dispute is not required to arbitrate, and a 1936 presidential pronouncement states that competent courts have ruled that the arbitral procedure established in the law is for the purpose of guaranteeing a recourse in those cases in which there is a lack of unity among workers. Nicaragua

Conciliation is compulsory for conflicts of a social-economic nature. 

Arbitration is compulsory only for disputes in public services or in enterprises serving the public interest (e.g., agriculture). Arbitral decisions are compulsory for the period stipulated which must not be less than 6 months. Panama

Conciliation must be employed to settle problems which might lead to a collective conflict. If the conciliation tribunal reaches a unanimous decision, such decision is binding upon all parties. Arbitration is voluntary. An arbitral decision is binding on all parties for a period to be not less than 6 months which the court shall decide. Peru

Conciliation and arbitration of collective labor disputes is of a compulsory nature, through machinery established by law. A 1926 Presidential 

Decree stipulates that both worker and employer organizations insert a clause in their constitutions and rules to the effect that it is compulsory to submit collective labor disputes to conciliation and arbitration. Arbitral awards are binding.

- Uruguay Conciliation and arbitration are not compulsory. A number of means of conciliation and arbitration exist for enterprises holding public service concessions; the basic organism, permanent in character, is an employer-worker board which is empowered to act at the request of any interested party.

Venezuela 

A conciliation attempt is compulsory; arbitration is voluntary, but the Labor Ministry may order arbitration. Arbitral awards are binding on all parties for the specified period which must not be less than 6 months.

STRIKES 

Brazil

To be legal, a strike or lockout must be authorized by the appropriate court on the failure of the opposing party to obey a labor tribunal award. 

Strikes in essential industries, in public works or services, in Government establishments, and in Government-owned enterprises such as shipping, docks, and railroads are expressly prohibited. The law provides penalties for illegal strikes and lockouts. Chile 

A strike may be lawfully called following (1) agreement by an absolute majority of members voting at a ballot in which at least two-thirds of the members take part; and (2) exhaustion of the conciliation procedures provided in the law. Strikes in the Government, public utilities, public undertakings, and semipublic institutions are prohibited. No provision with regard to payment of wages for a strike period. Colombia

Strike declarations must be approved by secret ballot of a majority of the employees in the establishment or undertaking, or by a general meeting of the basic industrial association with which more than half the employees concerned are affiliated. Strikes are illegal (1) in public services; (2) if for purposes other than occupational or economic; (3) if lawful procedure for direct settlement and conciliation is not carried out; (4) if declared more than 2 months after termination of the conciliation stage; (5) if not peaceful; (6) if for the purpose of influencing or intimidating the authorities; and (7) if declaration is invalid. The employer is not required by law to pay wages for time lost during a strike. 

Costa Rica

Before a legal strike may be called, the workers must (1) represent at least 60 percent of the persons employed in the enterprise, locality, or business affected and (2) have exhausted all conciliation procedures provided in the law. If a labor court declares that the causes of a legal strike are chargeable to the employer by reason of noncompliance with a labor contract, unjustifiable refusal to sign a collective agreement, or mistreatment of or violence against workers, the employer shall be required to pay wages for the period of the strike. Strikes in public services and in agriculture are generally prohibited.

Cuba A strike may be lawfully called only if (1) the detailed conciliation proceedings provided by law are exhausted, (2) a majority of the members of the labor organization or trade section, or (in the case of unorganized workers) of those who authorized the statement of demands, are in favor of the strike, and (3) the decision to strike was adopted by secret ballot. 

General strikes in certain public services and solidarity strikes with dissimilar unions are unlawful. There are no provisions with regard to payment of wages for the period of a strike. 

El Salvador 

A strike may be lawfully called only if (1) the strikers constitute an absolute majority of persons employed by the business, (2) conciliation procedures provided by law are exhausted, and (3) the strike is for a purpose enumerated in the law, such as to force contract compliance or revision, or to reconcile worker rights with those of the employer. If the cause of a lawful strike is deemed imputable to the employer, he is required to pay wages for the period of the strike. Strikes in public services and solidarity strikes are prohibited. Guatemala 

A strike may be lawfully called only if the strikers (1) constitute at least two-thirds of the persons employed in the undertaking concerned, and (2) have exhausted conciliation procedures. If the labor and social welfare tribunals declare that the reasons for a lawful strike are imputable to the employer, they shall order the employer to pay wages for the days during which the employees were on strike. 

In such cases, workers are entitled to double pay if they work in clinics, hospitals, public hygiene and cleaning services, power supply and lighting services, and water supply services. Strikes in public services other than those noted above, are unlawful; strikes in the public services noted are also unlawful if the employees fail to provide staff for the continuation of services sufficient to prevent serious or immediate injury.

Mexico

Before a strike may be called, the following requirements must be satisfied: (1) Its sole purpose must be to force the employer to make, enforce, or revise a collective labor contract, to support another lawful strike, or to harmonize the rights of labor and capital; (2) a majority of the employees of the undertaking or business concerned must be in favor of the strike; and (3) the strike must not be declared illegal by a conciliation and arbitration board which conducts conciliation proceedings following receipt of union demands and the employer's answer to them. If the strike is lawful, the employer is liable for the payment of wages for the days during which the workers are on strike, except in the case of sympathy strikes. Nicaragua

Before a legally declared strike can be called, the strikers must (1) represent at least 60 percent of the total number of workers of the company, and (2) have exhausted the conciliation procedures provided in the law. Strikes are unlawful in public services or in works of a collective public interest, including agricultural activities; they must be peaceful and for purposes specified in the law, such as defense of common economic and social interests, forcing compliance or revision of labor contracts, correcting unfair treatment, etc. If the courts decide that the causes of a strike are chargeable to the employer, he will be ordered to pay wages for the strike period. Panama 

A strike may be lawfully called if (1) the strikers constitute 60 percent of the persons employed in the undertaking concerned, and (2) conciliation procedures have been exhausted. If the labor courts decide the causes for a legal strike are attributable to the employer, the courts "shall sentence the employer to the payment of the salaries due."

Peru

A 1913 Supreme Decree permits industrial strikes following failure of employer to designate arbitrators to meet with arbitrators representing workers, or delay of more than 4 days in rendition of arbitral decision. This is conditioned upon a favorable secret majority vote by at least 75 percent of the active workers in the enterprise (the vote to be renewed every 4 days during the strike). However, a 1942 Supreme 

Decree abolished the provisions for the naming of arbitrators by employers and workers and established machinery for conciliation and arbitration under Government auspices. If a strike is in accordance with all legal requirements, workers are entitled to remuneration for the strike period upon their return to work. Uruguay The constitution guarantees the right to strike and states that "Regulations shall be made governing its exercise and effect on that basis." No general regulations exist, however. Interruptions in public services are unlawful; if such occur, and the Arbitration Tribunal finds the employer responsible, the penalty imposed is payment of wages for the time lost; if workers are found responsible, the penalty is rescission of the labor contract without the right to compensation for discharge. Venezuela

A strike may not be called before the conciliation procedure provided by law is exhausted, nor until 120 hours have elapsed from the time the labor inspector has received a summary of complaints from the workers. 

The chief civil authorities of the district must be notified prior to calling and terminating a strike, so as to facilitate police in their mission of preserving public order, etc. Sympathy strikes are legitimate if the workers belong to the same unions, trades, crafts, or occupations, and if they are under the jurisdiction of the same labor inspection office. 

There is no provision regarding payment of wages for strike period.

Translation, Imprensa Nacional, Rio de Janeiro, 1944. The International 

Labor Office also published an English translation in Legislative Series, Geneva, 1943 (Braz. 1), and issue in the Series from time to time English translations of amendments to the code and other legislation bearing on labor. Another source in English for most of the provisions of Brazilian labor and social legislation is A Statement of the Laws of Brazil in Matters Affecting Business in Its Various Aspects and Activities, Inter-American Development Commission, Washington, D. C., 1948, available from the Pan American Union, Washington, D. C. Chile

The basic source of Chilean labor legislation is the Labor Code of 1931. An English translation of the code was published by the International Labor Office in Legislative Series, Geneva, 1931 (Chile 1);

English translations of amendments to the code and other legislation bearing on labor have been issued in the Series from time to time. 

Another source in English of most of the provisions of Chilean labor and social legislation is A Statement of the Laws of Chile in Matters Affecting Business in Its Various Aspects and Activities, Inter-American Development Commission, Washington, D. C., 1946, available from the Pan 

American Union, Washington, D. C. 

8/ In addition to the main codes and laws indicated for most of the countries, there is often a myriad of executive decrees, administrative rulings and regulations, and court opinions and decisions which implement and interpret the basic legal provisions. Where possible, these have been considered in the preparation of the summaries in this document. Also, the national constitutions of most of the Latin American countries contain labor and social guarantees and principles to which subsequent legislation is required to adhere and implement. These constitutional provisions in some instances include detailed, specific requirements, such as concerning maximum hours of work. English translations of the constitutions may be found in The Constitutions of the Americas (as of January 1, 1948), Russell

H. Fitzgibbon and others, University of Chicago Press, Chicago, 1948. 

English translations of the constitutions also are available from the Pan 

American Union, Washington, D. C.

. Colombia

The basic source of Colombian labor legislation is the Substantive Labor Code of 1950. An English translation of the code was published by the International Labor Office in Legislative Series, Geneva, 1950 (Col. 1); English translation of amendments to the code and other legislation bearing on labor have been issued in the Series from time to time. 

Another source in English of most of the provisions of Colombian labor and social legislation is A Statement of the Laws of Colombia in Matters 

Affecting Business, Pan American Union, Washington, D. C., Second Edition, 1953.

El Salvador 

Labor legislation in El Salvador is found in the form of acts, laws, decrees, and regulations. English translations are published from time to time by the International Labor Office in Legislative Series, Geneva.
