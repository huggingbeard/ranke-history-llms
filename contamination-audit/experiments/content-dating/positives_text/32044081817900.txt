JOHN BURNYEAT 

of CRABTREEBECK One of the smallest bodies of water in the English Lake Country is Lows-water, some ten miles south of Cockermouth and fifteen miles inland from the Irish sea. The English Lake Country, famous for its scenery, is south of Scotland on the west coast. Into an area the size of Rhode Island is crowded some of the most fascinating country in the world; the rambling ground of hikers, bicyclists and tourists; the home of many 

English poets and writers; the vacation land of many city dwellers. This is a land of entrancing names. There is Saddleback, a mountain that looks its name; and 

Bootle and Boot which are towns; and Cockermouth which is not a kind of spaniel but a village. Coldbeck and Troutbeck are rivers, and Ambleside and Windermere are lakes. But not all the lakes have names ending in mere for there is Coniston Water, Ennerdale Water and Lows-water. Even the gaps in the mountains bear

picturesque titles, for there is Kirkstone Pass, and Sty Head Pass, and Honister which is almost straight up and down. In addition to the name mere for lake, which we do not use in America, there is the Screes, a slope covered with loose stones, and the fells which may be either a mountain or a moor, or a moor on a mountain. 

A moor is a stretch of land covered with purple heather and inhabited chiefly by sheep who pay no attention to purple blossoms about them.

In this maze of lakes, mountains, moors, twisting valleys, and rushing streams, John Burnyeat was born about the year 1631. This was long before the tourist trade brought prosperity to the country inns, and when those who made their homes in the Lake Country had to earn a living from its thin soil. We know nothing about John Burnyeat's parents except the statement that they "were of good repute, and his education was according to his parentage." Nor do we know anything for certain about John Burnyeat's youth, except that he lived on a small farm called Crabtreebeck in the parish and county of Lows-water in Cumberland, which is the northeast end of the English Lake Country. His own 

Journal does not begin until 1653, the year in which he became a Quaker. He evidently felt like the Old 

Testament prophets Jeremiah and Isaiah who did not consider of any importance what took place before they began to teach as "servants of God." But in the

Cumberland Testimony, written by his friends after his death, this is said of John Burnyeat, "the Lord visited him in his young and tender years, and inclined his heart after good things; he gave himself to reading the 

Scriptures... and going from one man to another who were accounted men of experience." Evidently the experience of John Burnyeat was much like that of 

George Fox who was about seven years older. Like Fox he had devout parents, little formal education, and spent long hours studying the Bible by himself. Like 

Fox he was much disturbed by religious questions which rose in his mind and to which he could not find answers among the wisest men of Cumberland. 

John Burnyeat assisted his parents on the little farm of Crabtreebeck, which he later owned and tilled. Like many New England farms in this country there was little level land in the farm, only a five-acre field by Lake Lows-water, the rest being hillside pasture. 

The stone house, which still stands as it did in John Burnyeat's time, is set into the hillside. The main house is two rooms long and a single room wide, and a story and a half high. Against one end there is a low stone shed, and beyond the main house, like a string of cars, there is an open wagon shed and a two-story barn now used as a sleeping place by the boys of one of the English Friends' schools when on vacation. We would think the main room very dark for only small, deeply

recessed windows admit light. There is a quaint stone entryway about four feet deep with a peaked roof and with a stone slab bench upon which John Burnyeat must have rested many an evening after his day's work was done.

Over the fireplace in the main room there is a huge panel reaching almost to the ceiling on which is carved the words JOHN BURNYEAT 1672. A singular feature of both rooms on the first floor is the heavy beams darkened by the smoke of many winters' fires. 

These beams curve at least two feet out of plumb. A narrow stairway leads to the second floor where a tiny hall gives access to two or three small bedrooms. The doorways of these rooms also are curved which gives the doors the appearance of barrel heads. The bedrooms themselves are so low that the floor had to be built on a slant and the beds propped up on blocks of wood to keep them from sliding into the eaves. The mystery of the curved beams used throughout the house is explained by the fact that Lows-water is only fifteen miles from the Irish Sea, and the original proprietor must have gone to the sea and brought back beams from the hull of a wrecked ship to put into his house. Back of the stone house is a little brook, or beck, with water foaming past the house, and on its bank grow the crabtrees which give the name to the house, Crabtreebeck.

This was the home of one of the greatest Quaker preachers of the first generation of "messengers," a friend of George Fox and William Penn, a man who traveled far and wide, returning from time to time to rest and refill his slender purse by working on his small farm beside the tiny lake. Friends in America, and especially those in Maryland, owe him a debt of gratitude; and of him, his contemporaries wrote "many an untrodden path he traveled, and passed through great dangers both by sea and land... passing through wilderness places and dangerous waters." 

Before George Fox ever visited Cumberland, a group of men and women whom we call "Seekers" were meeting together at a place called Peter's Head of 

Pradshaw. Fox met with this group in 1653 and a

Friends' Meeting was established. It was so large that no house in the neighborhood could hold the gatherings of Friends and they were compelled to meet outdoors on Pradsaw Crag, which was a broken ridge of rock that rose so steeply that Friends could be sheltered from the windy weather in the lea of it. In the winter the meeting was divided into four groups so that they could gather in as many houses; for twenty years all met together on Pradshaw Crag until a meeting-house was built at the foot of the ridge. 

John Burnyeat met with the group of Seekers at Pradshaw Crag, but he continued to be much troubled

about religious matters until the coming of George Fox who gave him peace of mind. John Burnyeat now felt that any evil he had committed in the past was forgiven, and that the light of Christ in his heart would henceforth guide him into right living. 

John Burnyeat found himself in the midst of a marvelous fellowship and of the group which met at 

Pradshaw Crag he wrote, "Then as his children and blessed family, we still continued to meet together twice in the week, or oftener; . . . and he was in the midst of us, and did honour our assemblies with his heavenly power and presence; and that was our great delight, and the sweetness of it did wonderfully engage our souls to love him, and our hearts to wait upon him . . we were knit together in the perfect bond of love, of fellowship and membership. So that we became a body compact, made up of many members." "We met together in silence, it may be, sometimes, not a word was uttered in our meetings for months; . . . .. but everyone that was faithful, waited upon the living word in our own hearts."

To John Burnyeat the meetings held at Pradshaw 

Crag were a wonderful experience, and he wrote in his Journal, "I did in my spirit long for the next meetingday." At first he took no vocal part in them but gradually he began to share with his fellow worshipers the thoughts which went through his mind. He was

anxious that his outward activities should conform to the high ideals he expressed in his messages, and this he found easier to do if he took considerable time for prayer and worship. "I found that as my heart was kept near the power, it kept me tender, soft, and living. And 

I found a constant sweet stream, that ran softly in my soul, of divine peace, pleasure and joy."

John Burnyeat desired to remain an inconspicuous member of his home meeting, and for four years he did not go beyond the bounds of his own parish except to visit individual Friends who had been put into prison for teaching Quakerism. But one day he felt that he must go to the neighboring town of Aspetry and there preach to those he would find gathered in the parish church. When he got to Aspetry, he was much relieved to find that the church service was already under way and the minister was speaking. After the church service was over Burnyeat started to go home and then he realized that he had not fulfilled his mission; rather, that he had remained silent for fear of being sent to jail.

So he returned to the church and as soon as the minister had finished his afternoon sermon John Burnyeat began to speak in the churchyard. When he had finished saying all that was on his mind he returned home "in peace, and my heart was filled with unspeakable joy... then 

I saw it was good to be faithful to the Lord."

This was the beginning of John Burnyeat's travels. 

Soon he went to nearby Lorton and here he was driven out of the church and beaten by the attenders. On another occasion at Denton the minister struck him repeatedly over the head with the pulpit Bible and the churchmen drove him out with their canes leaving him, "sore with the blows." It was lawful at this period in 

England for a layman to ask questions of the minister, or to speak following the regular service of the Church of England, but Burnyeat was soon arrested for disturbing the peace and put into jail at Carlisle for twentythree weeks.

In 1658 John Burnyeat began the first of the many wider journeys which were to take him over a large part of Great Britain and beyond. He spent three months traveling and speaking in Scotland, mentioning in his Journal such places as Aberdeen, Edinburgh, Hamilton and Ayr. Then he went to the north of 

Ireland where many Protestants had been settled and visited Dublin, Lisburn, Kilkenny and Cork. Through 

Ireland he traveled with Robert Lodge of whom he wrote, "we became concerned in one work, service, and travel together, and were truly united in spirit . . . I do not remember that we were ever angry or grieved one at the other in all that time." The partners met with some rough treatment in their travels. At Londonderry the mayor ordered them out into a boat "and not

to come back," and in four towns in Ireland they were put into jail. But in spite of "diligent labor and hard travel, often in cold, hunger, and hardship in that country, which was in many parts uninhabited" they stayed in Ireland for a year and made many converts to 

Quakerism. 

John Burnyeat records that he first desired strongly to go to America in 1662, and the urge was so powerful that he traveled up to London to place the matter before George Fox; but for some reason arrangements were not made for him to go and he had to return to Crabtreebeck with his wish unfulfilled. On the way home he visited Friends who were in jail for publicly preaching Quakerism, and at Ripon he was arrested and put into prison for fourteen weeks. Here his jailer was a rough fellow who ordered John Burnyeat not to talk to his fellow prisoners about his religion, and when he heard him praying aloud he put him into a dungeon without any light, in solitary confinement. 

Two years later Burnyeat was able to fulfill his desire to go to America, but first he spent a summer in Ireland, and then, in July, 1664, he took ship at Galway and sailed to the Barbadoes. In sailing days the Barbadoes were the gateway to North America, and most Friends spent some time there before taking other ships for the mainland. John Burnyeat spent seven weeks on the trip to the Barbadoes and after he arrived he spent three or

four months meeting with Friends in the islands before sailing to Maryland.

Quaker messengers had been traveling in the American colonies since 1656 when Mary Fisher and Anne

Austin landed in the Puritan colony of Massachusetts. 

These messengers were harshly treated in many of the colonies. In the Puritan colonies of New England, for example, where the leaders wanted no new ideas introduced that were contrary to those they had established, the Quakers were fined, expelled, beaten, and a few put to death. In the southern colonies they were often fined and put in jail.

In Maryland, where John Burnyeat first landed, Lord Baltimore had established toleration for all religious faiths, but for a time his laws were a dead letter as far as the Quakers were concerned as they were considered to be revolutionists and dangerous to the political government. This was due to the fact that the Friends would not take the oath of allegiance to the king, since they were opposed to any oath-taking, and to the fact that they refused to take off their hats before the magistrates, being unwilling to perform this customary act since, to them, it was a recognition that men were not equals. Fines were levied against the Quakers, a number were put in prison, and those who gave shelter to them were apt to be sentenced to prison also or fined for their hospitality. This feeling lasted from 1656,

when Elizabeth Harris first preached Quakerism in

Maryland, until the powerful messages of John Burnyeat beginning in 1665 changed the feeling of the magistrates towards the Quakers.

On his first visit to America, John Burnyeat spent two years in traveling from Virginia to Massachusetts, being in Maryland on three different occasions for visits extending over a period of several months. Today it is difficult to appreciate the hardships and sufferings involved in colonial travel. When John Burnyeat came to Maryland there were no towns in that province, and only a few scattered hamlets, the entire population being less than ten thousand people. These were scattered along the river banks or settled in clearings on the edge of the forests. There were few, if any, roads and in going from one tiny settlement to another one traveled by boat up and down the rivers, or on horseback along ancient Indian trails. The early Quaker ministers went hundreds of miles through the dense forests where they were constantly exposed to attack by Indians and wild beasts. A picture of the ordinary adventures can be gathered from these quotations from 

John Burnyeat's own Journal: 

"We met with strong winds, and rough weather, and some danger . . . on second-day we began our journey through the woods to go over-land to New England. We took horse at John Pitt's house at the

head of Tredaven Creek, and went through the woods above the heads of Miles River and Wye River, and also headed Chester River, and lay two nights in the woods. On fourth-day we came to 

Sassafras River, and swam our horses, and went over ourselves in boats or canoes. We then rode on to Bohemia River, and there also caused our horses to swim, and went over ourselves in canoes." "we got to Delaware and Newcastle, and there got lodging, for we were wet with rain.” "We supposed we traveled nearly forty miles. In the evening we got to a few Indian wigwams, which are their houses; we saw no man or woman, house or dwelling that day, for there dwelt no English in that country then. We lodged that night in an Indian wigwam, and lay upon the ground, as the Indians themselves did: and next day we traveled through several of their towns; and they were kind to us, and helped us over creeks with their canoes; we made our horses swim at the sides of the canoes, and so traveled on. Towards evening we got to an Indian town; and when we had put our horses to grass, we went up to the (Indian) king's house, who received us kindly, and showed us very civil respect. But, alas! he was so poorly provided, having got so little that day, that most of us could neither get to eat nor drink in his wigwam; but it was because he had it

not. So we lay, as well as he, upon the ground; only a mat under us, and a piece of wood, or any such thing under our heads. Next morning early we took horse, and traveled through several Indian towns; and that night lodged in the woods." 

Often the Friends spoke to the Indians and John Burnyeat casually mentions in his Journal that when he was going to Maryland in 1673 with George Fox as a traveling companion, Fox spoke one evening to the assembled Indians by means of an interpreter for the space of "four or five hours, I suppose it was nigh five hours; and they were very still and quiet, and very attentive."

On his first journey to America John Burnyeat spent much time in Maryland, traveled widely in Virginia, and then turned northward, being the first Quaker to visit Manhattan Island. His travels included Salem and 

Boston, and he spent a winter in Rhode Island because, "there was no going off the Island to the main (land), the snow was so deep." The second summer he visited in the Barbadoes and aften a ten weeks' voyage landed in 1667 in England.

Although he traveled extensively for the next three years in England, Wales, and Ireland, John Burnyeat's mind was still on America as is indicated by the letters which he sent over to his friends in Maryland and other parts of the colonies. He might have returned sooner

but he was hindered by the law which was being rigidly enforced prohibiting any gathering of religious groups other than the Established Church. In London soldiers were placed at the doors of Friends' meeting-houses to keep Friends out of their places of worship or to arrest those who worshiped within. John Burnyeat was speaking in Devonshire House in the spring of 1670 when soldiers entered and carried him off to the terrible Newgate prison. He was fined one hundred dollars, and when this was paid and he spoke again at Devonshire House, he was rearrested and confined in Newgate for several weeks.

On his release from Newgate Burnyeat set out on his second trip to America. This time it took him three months to sail to the Barbadoes, and he spent another six months visiting Friends in the islands. Arriving in 

New England he was present at the Yearly Meeting of 1671, and then traveled slowly south as far as Virginia giving his time especially to organizing business meetings so that the Friends might conduct their affairs in better order. 

In April of 1672 he planned to return again to New

England and was desirous of seeing once again as many of his friends in Maryland as possible, so he called a general meeting to gather at West River, "that I might see them together before I departed, for I was determined to go as soon as I could after that meeting." This

gathering proved to be the birthplace of Baltimore Yearly Meeting, the second Yearly Meeting to be established in America, for George Fox and several other prominent Friends landed from Jamaica on the opening day and before he departed the Yearly Meeting was definitely organized. John Burnyeat describes this historic event in these words: "There were Friends present from all parts of the province, and we had a very large meeting, which continued several days; in order that men's and women's (business) meetings for the settling of things might be established in the province 

George Fox did wonderfully open the service thereof to Friends, and they with gladness of heart received advice in such necessary things, as were then opened unto them; and all were comforted and edified. 

When all was over, and we clear, and all sweet and pleasant among Friends, we departed, and went down to the Cliffs, some by water and some by land; and there we had a large meeting of both Friends and other people."

George Fox in his Journal notes the fact that in this meeting at the Cliffs, held after the establishment of the Yearly Meeting, "there were five or six Justices of the Peace, the Speaker of the Assembly, one of the Council, and divers others of note." The respectability of the Friends had been well established!

Following the meeting at the Cliffs, John Burnyeat made an extensive trip through New England and was one of three Quaker ministers who held a celebrated debate with Roger Williams. Roger Williams had been expelled by the Puritans from Massachusetts in 1636 for refusing to support their ideas of the control of the state by the church. He had been forced to flee in the middle of a bitterly cold winter and would have died but for the help of friendly Indians. In the spring he went to the Plantations of Rhode Island and there established a new colony which was to be a shelter for persons persecuted elsewhere for their religious beliefs. 

In spite of these tolerant ideas he was much opposed to the views of the Quakers and he challenged George 

Fox to a debate at Newport. Fox never received the challenge as he had already left the colony before it was delivered, but John Burnyeat, William Edmondson, and John Stubbs agreed to defend the Quakers in his place. Roger Williams, though a man of seventy, rowed thirty miles to Newport where the debate went on in front of a great gathering for three days, then it was adjourned to Providence for a fourth and final day. 

Needless to say, none who took part in the debate was at all convinced of the rightness of the other's position. 

Books and pamphlets were printed containing some of the speeches made at the debates which contained rather warm condemnation of the other side, but this method

of arguing was an improvement on the earlier methods used in New England when Quakers were beaten, had their ears cut off, or were hanged on Boston Common. 

John Burnyeat returned for a last visit to Maryland in the spring, spending some months in that colony, and then sailed for Ireland arriving in early summer, 1673.

For the next five years John Burnyeat traveled among Friends in Ireland, Wales, and England, having "many precious meetings." The laws forbidding the holding of religious gatherings other than those of the Church of England were still in force, and though he might travel for months without being molested, sooner or later he would find himself in difficulty. Once in Wales he and his companion had their horses taken away from them, and twice he was imprisoned for considerable periods in Dublin. On these occasions he often used to write letters to Friends whom he had visited in the colonies in America and in parts of Great Britain. 

In 1683, at the age of fifty-two, John Burnyeat was married and settled in Dublin, Ireland. English Friends still urged him to visit them and the following year he was back at Crabtreebeck, and then he traveled over the northern part of England. His many journeys must have tired him, however, for he wrote in his Journal, "I have had a very peaceful journey, and visited Friends' meetings very fully in Westmoreland, and all was

quiet. now my services seem to be fully over and 

I am preparing to go home."

John Burnyeat was only to enjoy a real home for five years, for in 1688 his wife died, leaving him with one son who long years afterwards was also to become a traveling minister. Of his wife, John Burnyeat wrote tenderly in his Journal, "We lived comfortably together; her nature was good, kind and courteous; she was merciful, very considerate, and of good understanding; she will be greatly missed in this place, for Friends had a good love and esteem for her." 

With the death of his wife John Burnyeat's thoughts turned back to the little stone house near Lows-water and he longed to return to Crabtreebeck. But just as he was planning to leave Ireland a period of great suffering and persecution again came upon Irish Friends. As the Irish Testimonies said, "many people, being possessed with great fears, fled for England our dear friend (John Burnyeat), though he had an opportunity, had no freedom to go for England; but gave himself up to stay with Friends here, and bear a part of the sufferings that might attend us." He never got back to 

Crabtreebeck. Throughout 1690 he traveled in Ireland strengthening Friends in their "great sufferings and calamities; for he was a cheerful encourager." He wrote in his Journal, "many in the country are under sufferings, as respects the loss of their goods, by reason of

the wars this land is greatly attended with"; but of other areas he could write, "Friends are generally well, and our meetings are full, and we enjoy them in quietness." In his last letter, written in the north of Ireland in 1690, "We (are) resting here, and waiting upon him; it quiets our spirits, and sweetens them . . and makes many bitter things sweet." He died on the 11th of June (old time). ...

In the early days of the Society of Friends, and for long afterwards, there was a constant stream of itinerant ministers who traveled from meeting to meeting in America, encouraging, stimulating, counseling, and holding up before assembled Friends the high ideals of Christianity. Many of these ministers, like John Burnyeat, came from England, and their influence held the Society together and made it a force in the religion and life of America. Of this number, John Burnyeat was one of the most influential and best beloved. As has been the custom of Friends, after John Burnyeat's death various bodies of Friends issued "Testimonies" concerning his life and influence. These summaries speak of his courage, faithfulness, and upright conduct. Some phrases taken from them show the high regard in which he was held: "He was faithful in the discharge of duty. . .and suffered imprisonment a long time. . . . . He was a pattern of righteousness. . . He was willing to spend

-- and to be spent... His whole delight was to do the will of God... He was a man in a thousand, clothed with innocency and beautiful with humility He would freely administer of his outward substance to such as stood in need . . . He was meek and gentle and of a healing spirit He was a messenger of glad tidings . . He was a dear friend, a true brother." 

Of John Burnyeat, George Fox wrote, "He was a man endued much with the wisdom of God." William

Penn wrote, "He was an able and powerful minister ... a great encourager of the good in young and old." "He was a peacemaker," said George Fox, "and he preached in his life and conversation, as well as in his words." 

Crab Tree Beck, Charmian, Pennsylvania.
