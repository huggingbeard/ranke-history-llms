RURAL GOVERNMENT IN NEW YORK M. P. CATHERWOOD 

Local units of government in New York include cities, villages, counties, towns, school districts, and special districts. Of these units, counties, towns, and school districts are of particular significance to persons in rural communities. No hard and fast distinction, however, can be drawn between rural local government and urban local government. 

The laws in accordance with which local units perform their functions are constantly changing. Special laws and optional provisions of law detract from the uniformity that is sometimes assumed to exist. Thus, difficulties are encountered in attempting accurately to describe local government in general terms.

The problems faced by governmental units in an age of factory production, large cities, and rapid transportation are necessarily quite different from those in an age of handicraft, sparse population, and difficult transportation. The activities and administration of government consequently reflect these new economic developments. Changing social standards are also of significance in influencing provision for such services as education and relief. Although thus influenced by changing conditions, government is a human institution which changes slowly; a lag occurs in adjustments necessary to meet changed economic and social conditions. Consequently, at any given time, government may appear to be determined more by the experience of the past than by the needs of the present. 

LOCAL GOVERNMENT-A PUBLIC BUSINESS 

Government represents one form of collective, or cooperative, activity. 

Governmental services have increased because of increased population, increased complexity of economic life, and the recognition that some functions can be best performed by joint, or collective, rather than by individual action. This increase in governmental services has necessarily resulted in increased taxation.

To meet adequately the needs of the citizens, government must perform the desired services in an efficient manner and must provide an equitable system of taxation to pay for the services. Although honest differences of opinion arise as to what services are desired, as to how they can be performed most efficiently, and as to what constitutes an equitable system of taxation, these objectives constitute a goal for our system of local government.

Although the accomplishments of a unit of government are measured by standards different from those of a private business, the organization 3

and the administration necessary for successful operation are essentially the same. The citizen, or voter, in the public business can be compared to the stockholder in the private business; the legislature, or policy-determining board, to the board of directors; and the elected or appointed officials to the administrative officers. The problems of policy determination, of effectively putting policies into operation, and of fixing responsibility arise in the operation of a public business as well as of a private business. If a governmental unit loses revenue because of faulty taxcollection procedure, the costs to those who pay their taxes are increased in the same way as the costs to those who pay their bills are increased by the unwise extension of credit in a private business. For the most part, units of government act and change less rapidly than do private-business enterprises. This is due in part to the slowness. with which most large organizations change, and in part to the organization and the administration of government in accordance with detailed provisions of constitutions and laws, both of which change slowly. addition, in local government the function of policy-determination is not so centralized as it is in most private businesses. A large number of relatively independent, elective, administrative officials often add to the difficulty of prompt and effective policy-determination and execution. 

Some town boards or boards of supervisors are popularly held responsible for the conduct of functions over which they have little control. Such situations make it difficult for the voter or citizen to fix responsibility and to act accordingly.

Government is of vital importance to citizens in rural areas of New York in terms of both services and taxes. With the present significance of government, it is of increasing importance that the governmental business be performed efficiently. In New York, the desire for local control of local government is keen, and there is naturally resistance to any measures designed to reduce such control. At the same time, however, the demand that local government be conducted efficiently is urgent. 

The problem of improvement in local government in New York, in the near future, hinges largely upon the development of a system which will retain local control but which, at the same time, will provide for the highest degree of efficiency. In order to obtain such efficiency, it is necessary not only that the best administrative procedures be practiced, but also that a considerable degree of flexibility be allowed to meet the divergent needs of different communities and to allow progressive communities to meet changing conditions. If such changes will increase efficiency, their introduction would be an intelligent method of insuring the retention of that which is best in our present system of local government.

SIGNIFICANCE OF LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

As a result of the needs created by modern developments, there has been a demand for more services of local government. The resulting increase in services has caused an increase in taxes. The difficulty of paying taxes in a period of greatly reduced incomes has focused attention on the whole problem of local government. This problem, however, is broader than that of tax reduction alone, and includes the development of a system which will provide the desired services in the most efficient. manner and which will also distribute the costs equitably. 

The services performed by local units of government in New York affect all individuals within the State throughout their entire lives. Local units provide an education, protect the health, and supply roads for all persons in the State. School districts provide educational facilities for more than 2,000,000 children. Cities and villages provide streets, police protection, and many other services. Among the services performed by counties and towns are the maintenance of approximately 70,000 miles of highways, the safeguarding of health, and the administration of protection and relief. It is important that no part of the State be without such services as an adequate system of roads and satisfactory educational facilities.

The services of local government are of particular importance to rural people of New York, where the population is predominantly urban. The marketing of perishable farm products adds to the need for good roads. 

The existence of large cities, together with improved transportation facilities, increases the need for proper police protection in rural communities. 

The presence of a large urban population gives rise to health problems in rural areas. With small units of local government, a large part of the services is used by persons from outside areas. Under such circumstances, the development of a modern and equitable system of taxation assumes additional importance.

With the exception of the relatively small state armory, and court and stenographer's taxes, all direct taxes on real property¹ in New York are for local units of government. The State, which has a wider taxing power than local units, exercises this power in taxing sources other than property. The funds for ordinary state expenses and for state aid and state-shared revenues for local units are raised by so-called indirect taxation. Among the important sources of such state revenues are the taxes on personal and corporation incomes, on inheritances, on stock transfers, on motor vehicles, and on gasoline.

HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT OF LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

It is sometimes said that local government in New York is the same as it was prior to the American Revolution. Although it is true that some elements of the system of local government have remained essentially unchanged, a brief examination of the history of the State will indicate that numerous important changes have been made. 

In a consideration of the historical development of local government, one must guard against attaching undue significance to similarities in names of offices or in forms of government. The changes in local government in New York, during the past three hundred years, have been much greater than might be expected from a study of the titles of local officials or of the names of local units during the development of the State.

RURAL GOVERNMENT IN NEW YORK 

The early Dutch claims to the area now within the State of New York were based largely upon the voyage and explorations of Henry Hudson. 

Hudson's voyage to the present site of Albany in 1609 was followed by the commercial activities of Dutch traders. Among the first important and permanent settlements were those at Albany and on Manhattan Island. These were settled largely by the Dutch, and consequently Dutch laws and customs prevailed. 

Long Island was settled under varied conditions. On the eastern part of the island, settlements were made by persons from the New England colonies. They were made under the jurisdiction of the colonies of Connecticut and New Haven. The laws and customs of New England were established in these towns. Farther west, several towns were settled by the English under Dutch jurisdiction, and, in these, different political institutions were established. These English settlements under Dutch jurisdiction had more political freedom than was granted the Dutch settlements, but had less than prevailed in the towns farther east. On the western end of the island were the Dutch settlements under Dutch jurisdiction.

Under Dutch rule, the Dutch towns had been granted no important powers of local self-government. A few local officials were selected by the governor from nominations made by the retiring officials. The English towns under Dutch jurisdiction had been granted somewhat broader rights of home rule, and in these the governor appointed local officials from the candidates nominated by the townspeople. These towns also had certain other privileges, such as the right to pass minor local ordinances and the right to limit the admission of new inhabitants. 

The English had settled the mainland to the north and the south of New York, basing their claims upon various voyages of discovery and upon colonization. In 1664, the English asserted their claim to the intervening territory by force, whereupon the Dutch colony of New Netherland became the English colony of New York. Under the early English rule, many Dutch customs and laws were continued. The colony was administered under the authority of the Duke of York, brother of the King of England, who had granted to the Duke a large area including New York and adjacent territory. Thus the early history of New York shows the New England influence on eastern Long Island, the influence of the Dutch in the remainder of the colony, and, following 1664, the influence of the English rule.

After the English acquired control of the colony, they followed the Dutch plan whereby the legislative and the executive powers were centered in the governor. The governor, appointed by the proprietor, the Duke of York, selected a council. The governor and the council regulated the

fiscal affairs of the colony, settled boundary disputes, and, subject to the ultimate control of the Duke of York, performed many of the functions of the executive and legislative branches of the government today. Certain legislative functions were sometimes delegated to the court of assizes, which was appointed by the governor. 

Colonel Richard Nicolls, the first governor of the colony under English rule, called a "general meeting" of deputies at Hempstead for March 1, 1665. These representatives were elected by the taxpayers of the several towns. The deputies, functioning in an advisory capacity, were to have little real power in the government of the colony. The Hempstead meeting, however, was the forerunner of later "assemblies" which were able gradually to take over certain legislative functions through the force of public opinion.

At the meeting of deputies at Hempstead in 1665, the thirty-four delegates acknowledged themselves to be within the limit of the patent of the Duke of York and acknowledged submission to the laws which should be made at the Duke's authority. At this meeting Governor Nicolls promulgated the Duke's laws. These laws contained a civil code, a criminal code, and detailed provisions relating to local government. They provided for the establishment of the boundaries of existing towns and conferred upon freeholders the right to vote at town meetings. Provisions were made for the election of eight overseers and a constable in each town; this group was similar to a town board. Four overseers were to be elected each year, and the constable was to be chosen from the retiring overseers. 

Later the total number of overseers was reduced to four. Provision was also made for the election of other local officials, such as fence viewers and viewers of pipe staves.

The early functions of the county were limited to those associated with the administration of justice. As population and commerce increased, and as additional functions were delegated to the county, it became necessary for the supervisors of the towns within a county to meet for the purpose of apportioning certain expenses among the towns. Such gatherings were the forerunners of present-day boards of supervisors.

Following the early years of settlement, the population of the colony increased rapidly. The number of inhabitants, which has been estimated as 10,000 in 1664, had increased to approximately 20,000 by 1700, to 200,000 at the time of the Revolution, and to more than 500,000 by 1800. 

During this period of increasing population and settlement, numerous changes in local government were introduced. As the functions of local government expanded, its powers were more clearly defined, and it assumed more definite form.

The first constitution of the State, adopted at Kingston in 1777, was a written instrument in accordance with which the affairs of the State were to be conducted. The people were recognized as the ultimate source of the powers of government. Provisions were made for the three branches of the government-the legislative, the executive, and the judicial. 

The right of the people to change the constitution was recognized. Since 

1777, the state constitution has been frequently amended and revised. In its revised form, it provides an outline for the conduct of governmental powers within which the legislature makes, the executive executes, and the judiciary construes the law. 

STRUCTURAL ORGANIZATION OF LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

Counties and towns in New York were recognized in rather definite form under the English rule, but important development of these units occurred after the Revolution. In the first constitution of New York, fourteen counties were recognized, and it was declared that future legislatures should have the power to divide the State into such other and further counties and districts as should appear necessary. The legislature from time to time changed the boundaries and created new counties and towns. New York City and Albany were the first cities recognized. 

Troy and Lansingburg were incorporated as the first villages in 1798. 

Although earlier laws had been passed relating to schools, one of the most significant acts dealing with the creation of school districts was passed in 1812. Later, laws were passed authorizing the creation of special taxing and improvement districts. Such districts supply limited services and improvements primarily outside of incorporated places. 

At present the entire area of New York State is divided into sixty-two counties. Five of these counties constitute the area of New York City and accordingly are not subject to the same type of county government as that prevailing in the remainder of the State. All of the area outside of New York City is subject to one general type of county government. 

The population in these counties ranges from less than 4000 to more than 700,000; twenty-nine have a population of less than 50,000. The forms of government of the counties vary slightly because of special provisions of the constitution applying to a few urban counties, because of special provisions of state law applicable to others, and because of optional provisions of law which have been adopted by some. 

The fifty-seven counties outside of New York City are in turn divided into cities and towns. The fifty-nine cities are situated in thirty-six counties; twenty-one counties have no cities within their boundaries. The area outside of the cities is divided into 932 towns (townships). The population of the towns ranges from less than 100 to more than 180,000

in one town; the population is less than 5000 in 823 towns. Any point in the State outside of New York City is in one of the fifty-seven counties and is also in either a city or a town.

Within the towns in New York, many special taxing or improvement districts have been created, of which lighting, water, and fire-protection districts are examples. Such districts numbered 2540 in 1933, according to the report of the New York State Tax Commission. Most of these districts are outside of incorporated villages. Many exist only as taxing districts for special services administered by the regular town officers, primarily for the benefit of the people within the limits of the special district. In some districts, special commissioners have the responsibility of administering the financial affairs, but, regardless of the system of administration, the financial affairs of the district are closely associated with those of the town. Certain abuses have arisen in connection with special-district development in some semi-urban areas, resulting in over-development and in excessive taxation.

Cities are recognized as relatively independent municipal corporations, ordinarily created only at the request or with the approval of a majority

of the voters or taxpayers therein. Although subject to the general provisions of the constitution and to numerous state laws, cities have considerable powers of home rule. Counties and towns, however, even though declared by law to be municipal corporations, have in large measure been treated as administrative subdivisions of the State The authority for the exercise of governmental functions by counties and towns thus rests with the State rather than with the local unit. 

Home-rule rights of cities are recognized specifically in the constitution, which limits the power of the legislature to pass special or local laws relating to the property, affairs, or government of cities. The constitution also provides for a number of local officials and specifies that those not mentioned in the constitution shall continue to be elected or appointed locally. This has been interpreted by the courts as providing for the continuation of local officials and local functions prevailing at the time of the last revision of the constitution in 1894, and as preventing the transfer of functions from one local unit to another or to the State. 

Thus, the power of the legislature over local government is limited by the constitution.

Cities and villages as municipal corporations are relatively independent of other local units. The election of local officials, the levy and collection of taxes, and the performance of other governmental functions by these units, are not closely associated with the functions of other units. On the other hand, property within the village is subject to town taxes, and property within cities is subject to county taxes. 

A close relationship exists between the town and the county as units of local government. The supervisor, the chief fiscal officer of the town, acts as the town representative upon the county board of supervisors. 

Cities are represented on this board by supervisors elected in accordance with the provision of the city charter. Certain county officials exercise supervisory powers over some town officials, and there is interdependence between towns and counties in the levy and collection of taxes. 

School districts are subject to considerable supervision from the State. 

The superintendent of the school supervisory district in rural areas is paid by the State and is selected by the school directors, two of whom are elected from each town. School affairs in each school district, however, are administered by locally elected officials. The school taxes are ordinarily levied on the basis of assessments made by the town or city; the collection of delinquent school taxes becomes a responsibility of the other local unit; and the state-aid money is received through the county treasurer or the town supervisor.

TOWN ADMINISTRATION 

In 1932, a law was passed embodying a recodification of the town law. 

The old town law had become confused because of constant amendment, of conflicting statutes, and of special legislation. The new town law became fully effective as of January 1, 1934. Although for the most part limited to recodification, the law contains several new provisions, and among these is the classification of the towns into two groups. 

This classification is based primarily upon population." Towns of the first class include all those in Westchester County and all others with a population of 10,000 or more, except those in Broome and Suffolk Counties. Towns of the second class include all those in Broome and Suffolk 

Counties and all others with a population of less than 10,000, except those in Westchester County. With the exception of towns in Broome and 

Suffolk Counties, a second-class town becomes one of the first class when the population increases to 10,000, as determined by the federal census. 

A town of the second class with a population of 5000 or more, or with an assessed value of $10,000,000 or more, or adjoining a city with a population of 300,000 or more, may become a town of the first class either through action of the town board, subject to permissive referendum, or upon the adoption of a proposition at an election.8 

The town law includes several new provisions applicable to towns of the first class, but for the most part has not significantly changed the organization of other towns. In this analysis attention is given more to the organization of towns of the second class, which are primarily rural, than to that of towns of the first class, which are primarily urban. 

In general, the governmental organization of towns of the second class is essentially similar, and, with few exceptions, is uniform throughout the State. Some variations, however, are due to special legislation, and to provision by law of some latitude in the governmental organization of these towns.

The New York town as a unit of government is dependent upon constitutional and statutory law for its powers. The functions of town officials are determined by such law, which also provides to a considerable degree for the manner in which the functions are to be exercised. 

Accordingly, local officials are sometimes unable to exercise their best judgment in the administration of town affairs. 

Policy determination for those town affairs subject to local control is, for the most part, exercised by elected representatives rather than by direct vote of the citizens. The town meeting, which once prevailed in

OFFICIALS IN A TYPICAL RURAL NEW YORK TOWN 

In addition to the town clerk and the five members of the town board, several other officials are elected in most rural New York towns. The town board appoints the health officer, the town welfare officer, and others such as constables and election officials responsible for the administration of most home relief within the town. 

The town tax collector, an elected official, receives county and town taxes which he remits to the town supervisor and to the county treasurer, in accordance with the provisions of law. The duties of the town tax collector may be transferred to the town clerk. Most towns elect three assessors, who are responsible for the assessment of most taxable property within the town. An optional provision of law allows the town board, subject to permissive referendum, to provide for a single appointed assessor.

The health officer, appointed by the town board, is responsible for quarantines and other methods for safe-guarding health. Although two school directors are elected in each town, these officials do not function as officers of the town government. The school directors in the towns within a supervisory district select the district superintendent of schools for a five-year term; the administration of schools is in the hands of officials elected in each school district. 

Some town officials are subject to supervision from county officials. 

The amount and nature of this supervision varies among counties and among officials. The town highway superintendent, the town welfare officer, and the town tax collector are subject to some supervision by the county highway superintendent, the county welfare commissioner, and the county treasurer respectively. Under most circumstances, this supervision is not such as to interfere unduly with the work of the town officials.

COUNTY ADMINISTRATION 

The functions of county government and the general system of administration are provided in the state constitution and by state law. Even with these limitations, there is a considerable field within which policies must be determined by some local agency. Such policies relating to the conduct of the county business are determined by the county board of supervisors, except as such duties are reserved to individual officials by the constitution or by state law. 

The county board is composed of supervisors, one from each town in the county, and representation from any cities, in accordance with the provisions of the city charters, City supervisors are usually elected by districts within the city. In most counties, each supervisor on the county board has one vote, irrespective of the population of the district from which he is elected. The state law provides that the supervisors in some counties shall be paid a specified salary. For most counties, however, the law provides a per diem rate, usually of $6. The per diem is paid for regular and special meetings. An allowance for mileage is usually made.

Supervisors are elected for a term of two years. In most counties, the fiscal year ends October 31. The annual meeting of the board is held during November and December. Since newly elected supervisors do not take office until January 1, the annual meeting is conducted by the supervisors who have been in office during the preceding year. 

In most counties, the annual meeting of the board of supervisors begins shortly after election and lasts for several days. Sometimes the meeting is adjourned until a later date to await the completion of necessary work. 

Business transacted at the annual meeting includes the auditing of a number of annual and other bills, the acceptance of annual reports from county officials, the development of the financial plans for the ensuing year, and the formal levying of county and town taxes. 

The law allows a variation among counties in the number of regular meetings of the board during the year; regular quarterly or monthly meetings are the most common. In addition, special meetings are held as occasion requires. At the meetings throughout the year, the board audits bills, considers special expenditures, and determines policy on numerous problems that arise.

The board of supervisors is usually organized into committees, the number and names of which vary among counties. Among the important committees are those dealing with highways, finance, and welfare. Usually each member of the board is on one or more committees, and frequently one member will be on several.

The board appoints the county highway superintendent, the clerk of the board, and usually appoints a county attorney, as well as other officials such as a county sealer and the election commissioners stenographic help. The clerk keeps the minutes of the board meetings, draws orders or warrants at the order of the board, and has the responsibility for various reports.

The board may also appoint a county attorney who is a legal advisor for the board and may represent the county in civil actions brought by or against the county. The services of the county attorney are frequently made available to the towns without additional expense. The county attorney should not be confused with the district attorney who functions as a public prosecutor.

In addition to its function as a policy-determining body, the board performs some administrative duties. It appoints some officials, advertises for and accepts bids on some types of work, selects newspapers in which to publish notices, and may supervise the purchasing of various materials and supplies. One of the chief administrative duties of the board is to examine and audit those claims against the county for which the law does not provide an audit by other county officials.

In accordance with an optional provision of law, the board in some counties has delegated to an appointed county auditor the responsibility for auditing bills. Such a policy relieves members of the board of routine and technical work which may well be placed in the hands of a single individual responsible to the board. In addition, the board may appoint a purchasing agent who is responsible for the purchase of all materials and supplies other than those acquired with county-road money. Occasionally, the work of auditor and purchasing agent is performed by the same individual.

Numerous county officials are provided by the constitution and by state law, and may be relatively independent of the board of supervisors. 

In most counties, the board can exercise some control over the expenditures of these officials, although in some instances the officials can spend money without consideration for the policies of the board. COUNTY OFFICIALS ELECTED AT LARGE, TOMPKINS COUNTY, NEW YORK 

A number of officials are elected in New York counties intendent, who administers the county road system, and the county sealer, who is responsible for checking weights and measures, are appointed by the board. In most counties, the board appoints two election commissioners who have charge of the preparation and distribution of ballots, the compiling of returns, and the general administration of election affairs. 

The county clerk acts as a court clerk and `as registrar. As the clerk of the court, he is responsible for such activities as jury drawing, the preparation of warrants for payment of jurors and witnesses, and the keeping of some court records. As registrar, he records various instruments such as deeds, mortgages, and judgments, and also issues various licenses.

The county treasurer is the chief fiscal officer of the county. He receives and disburses county funds; he receives from the State and distributes to local units the grants-in-aid and shared revenues. He receives taxes from corporations and from town collectors, collects delinquent taxes, borrows in the name of the county when so authorized, and pays bills after proper authorization. In some counties, the volume of business is such that the office of the county treasurer is open only part of the week or is operated in connection with a bank, while in other counties several employees are required.

The county welfare commissioner is responsible for the county home and farm and for other relief administered by the county, such as old-age security, hospitalization, care of dependent children, and out-of-settlement relief. In some counties, the welfare commissioner is keeper of the county home, in which case his wife is usually matron. In other counties, the commissioner appoints a keeper and spends his own time primarily in the administration of other relief. 

In most rural counties, the county judge acts also as surrogate and as judge of the children's court, although in the larger counties these functions are sometimes separated. The salary of the judge is specified by state law. In most rural counties, more criminal than civil actions are brought in county court. The work of this court includes some appeals from justices' courts or from city courts. As surrogate, the judge deals primarily with estates of deceased persons. As children's court judge, he handles cases of juvenile delinquency and some cases of responsibility for the care of homeless children.

Counties in New York are also grouped into nine judicial districts for administering the work of the supreme-court system. These courts exercise original jurisdiction for some types of cases, and also hear some appeals from lower courts. The judges are elected from within the judicial districts for a fourteen-year term. Each district has several supreme court justices. Sessions of the court are held at various times throughout the year in each county seat. Compensation and expenses for supreme-court jurors and witnesses are important county expenditures in many rural counties. Higher state courts not closely associated with county government are the appellate divisions of the supreme court and the state Court of Appeals.

In rural counties, the sheriff is elected for a three-year term and may not succeed himself in office. He appoints an under-sheriff and may also appoint deputies. In most rural counties, the jail and the sheriff's residence, furnished by the county, are in the same building. The sheriff's wife is usually appointed as matron of the jail. The sheriff acts as a court officer and also serves papers in civil cases. He is expected to investigate disturb-

ances of the peace. The sheriff usually receives a residence and food for his family in addition to his salary. In many counties, he also receives fees, particularly for work performed in civil cases. 

A district attorney is elected in each county in New York and prosecutes criminal cases in the name of the people of the State. He provides the grand jury with evidence concerning persons suspected of crime and he prosecutes indictments. In most rural counties, the district attorney pursues private practice in addition to his official duties. 

The coroner is responsible for the investigation of deaths that appear to have resulted from neglect or unlawful acts. He may call physicians for consultation, summon a coroner's jury, and hold inquests. The coroner may act in the place of the sheriff under some conditions. 

RURAL-SCHOOL ADMINISTRATION 

Schools in New York are administered under the supervision of the State Department of Education and are essentially independent of other local units. Approximately 8500 school districts are within the State; this number is constantly decreasing because of consolidation and centralization. Village school districts with a population of 4500 or more may have a village superintendent of schools. City and village superintendents of schools are appointed by the boards of education. Most school districts centering in villages include some area outside of the corporate limits.

Districts other than cities and village under superintendents are within the supervisory districts, of which there are approximately two hundred.10 

In general, a supervisory district includes from three to six towns, all within the same county. The superintendent of schools performs general supervisory duties for the schools in his district. Inasmuch as school districts cross town line, the ruling has been that, for purposes of administration, a district is in the town in which the school house is situated. 

The superintendents of the districts are appointed by the school directors, two of whom are elected from each town. The term of office of the supervisory district superintendent is five years. He is paid by the 

State but may receive additional remuneration if the town supervisors within the district so vote. Any such additional remuneration is levied as a tax in the towns within the supervisory district. 

Many different types of districts are included within the jurisdiction of the district superintendents. Such districts may be classified in several ways; for instance, as to whether a school is maintained or the district contracts to send the pupils to another district; as to the type of education 

10 Districts with a population of less than 5000 are also included in the supervisory districts even though districts with a population of 4500 or more may have their own superintendent.

offered, whether elementary only or elementary and high school; or as to the number of teachers.

In the one-teacher district, the school trustee (there may be, but rarely are, three) is the chief school official. He hires the teacher, is responsible for the preparation of the school tax roll, buys supplies, and draws orders on the school collector, the school treasurer, or the town supervisor for the payment of bills and of the teacher's salary. Taxes are paid to the school collector who usually receives the state-aid money from the supervisor and disburses the funds of the district on the order of the trustee, except in those districts with a treasurer. The school officials of contract districts function in much the same way as do those of the one-teacher district.

The financial policies of the one-teacher district are determined at the annual school meeting, held the first Tuesday in May. At this meeting, the budget for the ensuing year is adopted and decisions are made concerning such matters as supplying transportation for high-school pupils. 

The annual school election takes place at this time. The clerk of the school district is responsible for the preparation and preservation of the minutes of the annual meeting.

In districts with more than one teacher but in which no high school is maintained, three trustees are sometimes elected. The powers and duties of these trustees are essentially the same as those of the trustee in the one-teacher district.

In union free and in central rural districts, most of which maintain high schools, a school board is elected. Some questions of policy may be determined at the annual school meeting at which the board members are elected, but considerable authority is delegated to the board. The board prepares the budget, hires teachers and other employees, and administers the affairs of the district. In most of these districts, there is a tax collector and a treasurer. The school clerk is usually responsible for the preparation of the assessment roll for school purposes. 

Schools in New York receive a large proportion of their funds in the form of state aid. Complicated provisions of law determine the amount of this state aid, which is dependent upon numerous factors, such as the type of district, the amount of taxable property, the expenditures for school purposes, and the number of children in attendance. The following description of the determination of state aid for non-consolidated districts deals with the most significant features. Somewhat different provisions apply to consolidated districts. 

In order to receive the maximum amount of state aid, a school must be in session for 190 days, which may include a limited number of holidays, and each teacher must be paid at least $20 a week. State aid

based upon attendance, valuation, and expenditures of a given school year is not received until the following year. As a temporary measure, state aid based upon the school year 1931-32 was reduced approximately 5 per cent, and that based upon the school year 1932-33 was reduced approximately 10 per cent in comparison with the present requirements, a description of which follows. 

In contract districts, state aid is limited to the amount by which payments for transportation and tuition exceed the amount of a four-mill tax on the full value of the property within the district, but the state aid may not be more than the amount by which $1500 exceeds the yield of a fourmill tax on full value. In effect, this results in the State paying most of the school expenses in excess of a four-mill tax on full value, if the total of such expense does not exceed $1500. 

One-teacher districts receive as state aid a minimum of $425. With the exception of this minimum, the equalization apportionment is limited to the amount by which expenditures up to $1500 exceed the amount of a four-mill tax on full value. In effect, this means that a one-teacher district usually can maintain a $1500 school with a tax of $4 per thousand on full value. The actual calculation of state aid for one-teacher districts is further complicated by provisions relating to refunds, by the payment of indebtedness, by extra quotas for transportation of pupils to high school, by minor allowances for other purposes, by deduction for the teachers' retirement system, and by adjustments because of the percentage reduction previously mentioned.

In districts maintaining elementary schools with two or more teachers, state aid is calculated upon a different basis. With the exception of the minimum state aid of $425 per teacher, no such district may receive a greater equalization apportionment than the amount spent in excess of the yield of a five-mill tax on full value. Subject to these minimum and maximum limits, the amount of the so-called equalization quota is determined by multiplying the number of teacher-units by $1500 and subtracting six-tenths of a mill per dollar of full value. Teacher-units are determined on the basis of school attendance; for example, for most districts an average daily attendance of forty pupils constitutes two teacherunits, and beyond this figure an average daily attendance of thirty-two comprises an additional teacher-unit. Some rural districts with two or more teachers spend more than the amount of a five-mill tax on full value, plus their state aid, and therefore find it necessary to levy a tax of more than five mills on the full value of the property. The additional complications mentioned in the determination of state aid for one-teacher districts also apply to the determination of state aid for districts with more than one teacher.

Districts maintaining a high school receive a minimum state aid of $425 per teacher, but no such district may receive a greater equalization apportionment than the amount spent in excess of the yield of a tax of five mills on full value. The equalization quota is determined by calculating $1500 for each elementary teacher-unit and $1900 for each highschool teacher-unit and deducting six-tenths of a mill per dollar of full value of taxable property. Elementary teacher-units are determined as previously explained; academic or high-school teacher-units are calculated on the basis of a somewhat smaller average daily attendance per teacherunit. Other quotas for some expenditures, such as transportation, are also added. In most high-school districts in rural areas, it is necessary to raise a tax of more than $5 per thousand of full value, because the state-aid quota plus the amount of a tax of $5 is insufficient to maintain the desired school. Additional complications, including the building quota, enter into the calculation of state aid for central rural districts, which represent a type of centralization designed to improve educational facilities in rural areas. It appears that the quotas in most central rural districts are sufficiently liberal so that the school-tax rates will be reduced to approximately $5 per thousand of full value within a few years. 

High-school districts receive, from the State, tuition for non-resident high-school pupils from districts in which no high school is maintained. 

This tuition apportionment amounts to $50 per pupil in full attendance. 

Such pupils are also included in the average daily attendance figures of the high school in determining the equalization apportionment. On the basis of an average daily attendance of twenty-two per academic teacher-unit, the State pays the high-school district, as an equalization quota, $1900 divided by twenty-two, or $86.36 per non-resident highschool pupil in full attendance. This, in addition to the non-resident tuition quota of $50, totals $136.36 per pupil in full attendance. Sometimes the high-school district makes an additional tuition charge to the outside districts, but often high-school pupils are accepted from such districts without charge. The State also pays to an elementary district half the costs of transportation for pupils to an outside high school. 

HIGHWAY ADMINISTRATION 

Highways in New York outside of cities and incorporated villages are classified into three systems: state, county, and town. The administration of each of these systems is relatively independent of the others. The state system includes most of the heavily traveled "through" highways.

The county-road system is a secondary system and is composed, in large measure, of connecting links and feeders for state routes. 

The town system includes all those public highways outside of incorporated places not included in the state or county systems.

Confusion occasionally arises in interpreting legislation concerning the county-road system. The highways included in the county system are known as county roads. State and county highways, using the terms with their legal significance, are included in the state system. Roads outside of state and county highways include county roads and town highways. 

Most of the state highways are hard-surfaced and are graded and aligned for relatively heavy traffic at high speeds; the county roads represent a less expensive type of highway, most of which is hard-surfaced; the town system consists principally of dirt and gravel roads. The state highways are heavily traveled; most county roads are traveled less than state highways but more than the town highways, upon which traffic is relatively light.

The state system is administered by the Division of Highways in the State Department of Public Works. Most of the expenditures on this system are ultimately met by state taxation. Some federal funds are made available on certain routes, and some costs are paid by the counties. 

The State does not pay for construction or maintenance of state routes in cities, but in villages the State meets the cost of a highway of normal width.

Rights-of-way for state highways are purchased at the expense of the county in which the road is to be constructed. In most rural areas, snow is removed from state highways by the county; part of the expense of such removal is refunded by the State. Under most circumstances, the costs of grade-crossing eliminations are shared by the railroad, the State, and the county in the ratio of 50:49:1.

For purposes of administration of state-highway work, the State is divided into ten highway districts, each of which is in the charge of a district engineer. Most construction work on state highways is done by contract, but the State provides crews for maintenance work. 

Funds spent on the county road system are supplied by the State and the county. Five per cent of the yield from the original two-cent gasoline tax is returned to New York City; twenty per cent is returned to the counties outside of New York City for county-road work. These funds are allocated among counties in proportion to the county-road and townhighway mileage outside of cities and incorporated villages. Twenty-five per cent of the motor-vehicle fees paid by residents of a given county is returned to that county and is included in the county-road fund. Relatively small amounts are also received by the county from the State in accordance with Section 101 of the Highway Law. In addition, under the provisions of Section 320B of the Highway Law (Lowman Act), the State matches county contributions to the county-road fund up to a maximum of $30 per mile of highway outside of incorporated places and

outside of the state system. Some counties levy additional taxes for the county-road fund. Because of the financial stringency resulting from the depression, recent acts of the legislature have authorized counties to divert temporarily to other county purposes part of their county-road funds received from the State. 

Aside from these temporary diversions, the county-road fund is used almost exclusively for construction and maintenance of county roads. The county highway superintendent's salary and travel expenses are paid from other county funds. The county also pays for acquiring and maintaining highway machinery. A separate machinery fund is provided, out of which machinery costs are paid and to which are credited rentals of machinery paid from the county-road fund at rates approved by the State. 

In most rural counties, the construction and maintenance work is done by the county; in some counties most of the construction work is done by contract.

The county highway superintendent, appointed by the board of supervisors, administers the county-road system and may supervise the work of the town highway superintendents to a limited extent. The highway committee of the board of supervisors usually works in close cooperation with the highway superintendent on such matters as determining where available money is to be spent, the type of construction to be used, and the type of machinery to be purchased.

The county-road system is still being enlarged by the gradual addition of former town highways. Recent federal grants for the construction of a limited mileage of farm-to-market roads are increasing the mileage of this type of road. The roads to be taken into the county system are determined by the board of supervisors, subject to the approval of the State. The State also insists that a reasonable amount be spent on maintenance work.

Each town-highway system is administered by the town highway superintendent, usually an elected official, who works in cooperation with the town board in determining major questions of highway policy. Funds for town highways come from state aid and from local taxes. In a few counties, towns receive highway aid from the county. 

Towns raise property taxes for highway purposes under four headings known as Items I, II, III, and IV. Item I is known as the repair and improvement fund; Item II, the bridge fund; Item III, the machinery fund; and Item IV, the snow removal and miscellaneous fund. Not all towns follow the same policy in classifying their expenditures among these funds. With the exception of refunds, machinery-rental income, and other income for outside work, local property taxes form the only major source of funds for Items II, III, and IV. Tax levies for these

funds are spread over the property in the entire town, including property within any villages. Funds for Item I purposes come from taxes and state aid. Property taxes for this fund are levied only on property outside of incorporated villages. 

To obtain the maximum amount of state aid, as provided by Section 

101 of the Highway Law, a town must raise as an Item I tax either an amount equivalent to $37.50 per mile of highway outside of the state system and incorporated places, or a tax of $2.25 per thousand dollars. of full value of property outside of incorporated villages. To any town which raises $37.50 per mile of highway for Item I purposes, the State allocates at least an equivalent amount. To any town which raises for 

Item I purposes a tax of $2.25 per thousand, the State allocates at least the amount by which $75 per mile of highway exceeds the yield of such a tax. The State matches, dollar for dollar, any Item I tax which is less than $37.50 per mile and also less than $2.25 per thousand of full value. 

The state aid allocated to the towns in accordance with Section 101 of the Highway Law is based upon both town-highway and county-road mileage, and the law provides that the proportion applicable to the county roads shall become part of the county-road fund. 

Although highway expenditures in most rural towns far overshadow those for any other single purpose, the amounts available are not sufficient to provide for the construction of many miles of hard road. Since the type of macadam road built by most counties ranges in cost from $5000 to $15,000 per mile, most rural towns do not have sufficient funds to build a significant mileage of such roads. Most of the town-highway funds are spent on maintenance work and the construction of gravel, stone, or shale roads.

RELIEF ADMINISTRATION 

The complexity of the administration of relief in rural areas of New York has increased tremendously with the increase in relief expenditures and with federal and state participation. The costs of some types of relief are shared by the county or other local agencies and by the state and federal governments; the costs of others are shared equally by the county and the State; and a few types of relief are supported entirely at the expense of the county. The administration of relief is constantly changing, but up to the summer of 1935, most of the state and federal funds have been spent under the supervision of the Temporary Emergency Relief Administration, an agency set up by the State for this purpose, and ordinarily referred to as the TERA.

In most rural counties, the county maintains and administers a county home and farm for the care of some types of destitute persons. The

county board of child welfare grants allowances to needy widows with one or more children under the age of sixteen years. The county also cares for homeless children by paying for their board and care either in institutions or in private homes. Hospitalization for all relief cases is frequently administered by the county. Relief for out-of-settlement cases is also usually administered by the county. Old-age-security allowances for the support of some types of needy persons more than seventy year of age are administered by the county and city under the supervision of the State Department of Social Welfare. 

The legal settlement of a needy person determines the governmental unit responsible for providing relief. A person is said to have a legal settlement in the town within New York in which he has last lived for one year without public aid. It is customary for the towns to administer home relief for the persons within their borders who have a legal settlement therein. Home relief for such persons is principally in the form of food, rent, clothing, and fuel. This is the principal type of relief administered by towns.

The system of distributing the local share of relief costs between the county and towns or cities varies among counties. The share of the costs to be borne by a local unit is not necessarily borne by the unit administering the relief; for example, it is customary to charge part of the cost of the county home and farm to the towns or cities from which the inmates come, the remainder being a county charge. Widows' allowances are usually paid by the county. Homeless children may be county charges, but this cost is frequently charged back to the towns and cities on the basis of legal settlement. Hospitalization is usually charged back to the towns and cities if home relief is paid for by these units. Half of the cost of old-age-security payments is refunded to the county or city by the State; the county or city bears the remaining half. Home relief for out-of-settlement cases is usually charged back to the town or city in which the recipient has a settlement. The local share of the cost of home relief administered by the town is usually paid by the town. 

In addition to the refunds by the State of part of some welfare costs already mentioned, varying percentages of home-relief costs have been refunded by the TERA; during 1934 and the early part of 1935, such refunds amounted to 75 per cent of most home-relief expenditures. Hospital care is not classified as home relief and consequently was not subject to such a refund. The TERA usually refunded the 75 per cent to the unit administering the relief.

Some counties have adopted the county-unit plan for welfare or relief. 

The county welfare commissioner then has general charge of the administration of practically all forms of relief within the county-welfare dis-

27 trict, which may, or may not, include any cities in the county. In the county-welfare district, local costs of welfare are borne by the county as a taxing unit. Under either the town or county system the local costs are paid by property taxpayers within the county, but great differences in tax rates in different towns or cities arise when a large part of the welfare costs are met by towns or cities as taxing units. 

Numerous complications arise in handling the cost of relief administered in one city, town, or county, for an individual who has a legal settlement in some other city, town, or county. In some counties, the countywelfare commissioner has arranged for the town-welfare officers to administer this out-of-settlement relief. Appropriate arrangements are made to charge the relief for such persons to the unit of government responsible for bearing this cost. Delays and difficulties in the collection of such charges are frequently encountered. 

Work relief has developed considerably during recent years. Such relief apparently has been used to a greater extent in cities than in rural sections. Work relief has been administered under the rules and regulations of the TERA. When so administered, 75 per cent of the wages on work-relief projects, which for the most part are to be paid only to persons qualifying for relief, have been refunded to the city, town, or county. The TERA has also refunded a part of the cost of materials used on such projects. Although work relief is generally regarded as an expensive method of granting relief, some persons feel that it is more desirable than home relief. Present plans apparently contemplate increased expenditures of federal funds on work-relief projects and an increase in the proportion of home-relief costs to be borne by local units. 

ASSESSMENT OF PROPERTY AND COLLECTION OF TAXES 

11 In most towns, property is assessed by three elected assessors. The town board may appoint the three assessors or it may determine that there shall be a single appointed assessor.¹ Such changes, which have been adopted in relatively few rural towns, are subject to permissive referendum.

The assessors sometimes divide a town into three districts, with each assessor primarily responsible for a particular one. Many times, however, all assessors participate in valuing property throughout the town. The assessors are supposed to be paid on a per diem basis, although in some towns the practice has arisen of paying them a predetermined amount. 

The State Tax Commission prescribes rules and regulations for the assessment of property and the preparation of assessment rolls. It is

difficult, however, for the Tax Commission to change existing assessment practices in the towns, villages, and school districts throughout the State. 

The law provides for the assessment of property at its full value, which is usually understood to be the value at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller. Accordingly, actual sales of property are accepted as one indication of the level at which property should be assessed. During recent years, however, with relatively few unforced sales, it has been difficult to use this guide. Nevertheless, it has been definitely determined that in some towns the level of assessments is far below actual values. In other towns, assessments are admittedly higher than sales prices would warrant, but possibly such assessments may be justified until a sound standard for comparison can be obtained. If a town or city is given a fair equalization rate in the allocation of the county tax and for the determination of state aid, the assessment of property within the town determines the distribution of the tax load but does not influence its amount. The assessment roll, prepared by the town assessors, is the basis for prorating, among the properties within the town, the county and town levies and any state tax on the property therein.

Property within villages is assessed for town purposes by the town assessors. The village prepares a separate assessment roll, however, for the village taxes. In the preparation of the roll for village purposes, the town assessments may be used; but frequently different values are assigned. 

For the assessment of the school tax, the school trustee or the board is responsible for the preparation of a roll of the properties within the district. The valuations appearing on the last town roll are supposed to be used, unless there have been omissions or unless there has been a change in the value of the property. The law provides for an appropriate difference in tax rates for those parts of a school district situated in different towns in which property is not assessed at the same level. 

It is the duty of the town assessors to assess all taxable property within the town other than special franchises. Special franchise property includes pipe lines, telephone and electric lines and poles, and similar property in the public highways, together with the right to use the highways for such property. This property is assessed by the State Tax Commission, which provides the local assessors with the valuations to be placed on the roll. 

In determining such valuations, the level of assessments within the town is taken into consideration.

The assessment roll provides a space for designating the name of the owner of the property, the acreage, the abutting property owners, the school district, the assessed value of land and of land and buildings, and the amounts of various taxes levied against each parcel. Property is

supposed to be assessed as of its value on July 1. The assessment is ordinarily considered as being against the property described rather than against the individual designated on the roll as the owner of the property. 

Most of the actual work of the town assessors is usually done sometime during May, June, July, and August. The roll is supposed to be completed by August 1, and the assessors sit as a board of review on the third Tuesday in August. If the town has a single assessor rather than the customary three, the board of review consists of the assessor, the supervisor, and one justice of the peace selected by the town board. 

The property owner who is dissatisfied with his assessment should use this opportunity to object. If objections are not met by the assessors, the only remedy is by certiorari proceedings in the Supreme Court. Such proceedings are too expensive to furnish an adequate remedy for most property owners.

In rural areas, there is no county agency for the review of the assessments of aggrieved property owners. The only agency for the review of assessments, other than an appeal to the courts, is the town board of review, which in most cases is composed of the individuals responsible for the original assessments. One suggested improvement in assessment proceedure is that some county agency, such as the board of supervisors or a separate commission, should hear such appeals.

The amount of the tax levies for town purposes is determined by the town board, but these taxes are formally levied against the property within the town by the county board of supervisors. The county board also determines the amount of county taxes, and, unless there is a county equalization commission, fixes for the different towns or cities the equalization rates which are used in determining the proportion of the county-tax levy to be raised in each town. The amount of the town, county, and any state taxes to be levied against each property is placed upon the assessment roll, which becomes the tax roll when properly signed. The tax roll is then used as the basis for the collection of taxes within the town.

The need for correction or equalization of assessments among towns arises in allocating the county tax among towns in which the level of assessments varies. In equalizing the assessments, an attempt is made to prorate the county tax levy among the towns in proportion to the actual value of the taxable property therein. In determining the proper adjustments in assessed values, the equalization rates established by the State Tax Commission may be followed or a new set may be adopted. 

The equalization rates fixed by the State Tax Commission are designed to indicate the proportion of true value at which property within each town is assessed. These rates are frequently used as a guide in deter-

mining the proper equalization of assessments for county tax purposes. 

They also are important in the determination of the full value of property within towns and school districts, which is necessary in determining state aid for town highways and state aid for schools. For these purposes, the full value of taxable property is obtained by applying the equalization rate fixed by the State Tax Commission to the actual total assessed value of property within the local unit.

In most towns, the voters elect a town collector. The town board, however, may abolish the office of town collector and transfer the functions and duties to the town clerk.1 12

The town clerk is then paid on a salary basis and there is no tax-collection fee during the first thirty days of the collection period. Interest, at the rate of one per cent for each thirty days, is charged on taxes not paid during the first thirty-day period, but such interest goes into the town treasury. In towns of the first class, taxes are collected by an officer known as the receiver of taxes who also collects school taxes. In most rural towns, however, school taxes are levied separately and are collected by a different collector for each district. 

The county treasurer collects most corporation taxes for county and town purposes and most delinquent taxes. Taxes on other property are paid to the town collector. The town collector receives a fee of one per cent for taxes collected within thirty days after the signing of the warrant and may charge 5 per cent for taxes collected after the thirty-day period. 

He receives also the collection fee of one per cent for corporation taxes paid to the county treasurer. In practice, the penalty of 5 per cent for late payment is not always applied. The length of time during which the town collector receives taxes is not uniform in all counties, but, except when an extension is authorized by the legislature, usually terminates about May 1. warrant.

The town collector pays to the town supervisor the first taxes received, up to the amount levied for town purposes, as indicated in the collector's Additional amounts are paid by the collector to the county treasurer. If the town collector does not obtain sufficient taxes to equal the levy for town purposes, the difference is advanced to the town by the county, as provided by law. The county assumes responsibility for the collection of delinquent taxes.

At the end of the regular collection period, the town collector settles with the county treasurer and "returns" a list of the uncollected taxes. 

Such taxes are commonly called returned taxes. At the time of the return, a flat charge of 5 per cent is added and, if these taxes are paid to the county treasurer before tax sale, an additional charge at the rate of 10 per cent per year, beginning with February 1, is usually added.

Properties on which the taxes remain uncollected are advertised once a week for six consecutive weeks in two newspapers, following which the sale is held. Practically without exception, the consideration figuring in a tax sale is the amount of the original tax, plus the interest, penalties, and charges provided by law. These include the 5 per cent added at the time of the return, interest at the rate of 10 per cent from February 1, and an advertising charge which is, in general, between $3 and $5 per parcel.

Particularly in recent years, there have been relatively few private purchases at tax sales. In the absence of a private purchaser, the property automatically is "bid in" by the county. An actual transfer of the property cannot be said to occur, however, at the time of the tax sale. A redemption period of one year is allowed during which the owners may redeem the property by the payment of the amount advertised at tax sale, plus interest. At the end of the year, the county treasurer advertises for redemption any properties on which the taxes still remain unpaid. Following redemption advertising, a considerable period is still available during which the property may be redeemed and following which a tax deed can be issued. Thus, neither the county nor a private purchaser at a tax sale may acquire title to a property sold for taxes until an adequate period has been allowed for redemption. 

Many uncertainties have arisen concerning tax titles to property. Usually a tax deed is not considered as representing a secure title. An individual, however, who has acquired a tax-sale certificate may, after a specified time, foreclose this lien in the same manner as though it were secured by a mortgage.13 Such a procedure involves additional expense, but provides the holder of the tax-sale certificate the opportunity to recover his investment or to obtain a good title to the property.

PRESENT OPPORTUNITIES FOR CHANGE IN LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

In this country, the state and the federal governments have existed side by side. The activities of the federal government are supposed to be limited to those functions delegated to it by the Federal Constitution. 

In other fields of governmental activity, the States are supreme. 

The source of governmental power is with the people. Accordingly, they may modify the constitution when they see fit; may increase or

decrease the powers conferred upon the legislature or the policy-determining body of the government; or may reserve to themselves the right to pass on some questions of policy by a referendum vote. 

Among the governmental activities under the jurisdiction of the States are those performed by local governments. In large measure, local governments have been considered as administrative sub-divisions of the State, but some rights of local self-government have been recognized for centuries. In New York, as in some other States, definite home-rule rights of cities are recognized, particularly for those activities primarily of local rather than of state-wide concern. The city governments, however, remain subject to the general laws of the State, and must depend upon the constitution or statutory law for their rights of home rule. 

Counties and towns are dependent upon the detailed provisions of state law for authority to perform various functions. These functions have also been administered in accordance with detailed provisions of state law. Consequently, difficulties have been encountered because of the failure of the laws to meet the divergent needs of different communities. This situation has been met, in part, by the passage of special legislation applying to individual towns and counties. Such legislation has greatly complicated the problem of determining what laws are applicable in any given unit. Counties and towns also have encountered difficulties because the provisions of state law and of the state constitution do not keep pace with changing economic and social conditions. 

State laws, however, have provided some opportunity for progressive changes in the administration of local government in New York. Most of the following optional provisions of law are regarded by students of government as representing desirable modifications of the prevailing practices. For example, the law makes it possible for counties to have auditors, purchasing agents, and comptrollers. Although a few counties have taken advantage of such laws, most rural counties do not have these officials. 

The functions of public health and public welfare may be administered and paid for entirely upon a county-unit basis. Town boards may transfer the duties of the town tax collector to the town clerk, and, subject to permissive referendum, may provide for the appointment rather than the election of the assessors, or may provide for a single appointed assessor. 

Towns may also adopt the budget system which provides for a systematic estimate of receipts and expenditures and for a public hearing before the amount of the town tax levy is determined. 

An opportunity for rather comprehensive changes in county government, within the limits allowed by the present state constitution, is offered by recent legislation providing for the optional "County President" or

"County Manager" forms, and by legislation making possible the creation of a county charter commission to formulate and recommend a charter for a county.

Chapter 948 of the Laws of 1935 authorizes the adoption of the "County President" form or the "County Manager" form of government, subject to the approval of the voters in the county concerned. Under the first plan, a county president would be elected and would act as chairman of the board of supervisors. He would be the administrative head of the county government. Under the second plan, the board of supervisors would appoint a county manager to act as the administrative head of the county government. It is further provided that if either of the above plans is adopted by a county with a population of more than 100,000, a director of finance, a director of public works, and a director of public welfare shall be appointed. These same officials may also be appointed in smaller counties. The county president or county manager may serve as the director of one or more departments. The county manager form was approved by the voters of Monroe County at a referendum on November 5, 1935.

The county-president or county-manager forms of government make no change in the composition of the board of supervisors, which continues as the policy-determining body of the county. Neither of the plans appreciably changes the status of towns, villages, or school districts, nor their relationship to the county. Each of the plans does provide for a considerable degree of coordination of the administrative affairs of the county in the hands of an executive head; responsible under the county-president plan directly to the voters, and under the county-manager plan to the board of supervisors. Both plans are subject to the constitutional restrictions limiting change in local government, and under both plans, the constitutional officers would be continued. 

Chapter 846 of the Laws of 1934, as amended by Chapter 951 of the Laws of 1935, provides that the board of supervisors, may, and upon receipt of a proper petition signed by the required number of voters of the county, shall, provide for a commission to prepare and recommend a draft of a county charter. The law further provides that the board of supervisors shall consider the charter proposed by such a commission and may make any changes it deems necessary. After approval by the board of supervisors, such a charter is to be submitted to the voters. Το become effective, the charter must be approved by a majority of the voters. If such a charter provides for the transfer of any power or duty from a town or village to the county, such provision shall not become operative for any such unit, unless the charter is approved by a majority of the qualified electors voting thereon in such respective town or village.

No charter has yet been adopted in accordance with these provisions, although a charter commission has been appointed in at least one county. 

OTHER PROPOSED CHANGES IN LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

The present laws making possible some optional changes in local government offer some opportunity for the improvement and coordination of the administration of local government. Several, particularly those providing for a county president, a county manager, and a county-charter commission, are relatively new, and as yet there has not been an adequate opportunity to determine the success of these measures. 

It will be noted, however, that most of the above-mentioned opportunities for change in local government are limited to changed procedure in the administration of the present functions by the same unit of government. 

Thus, although a town board can make some changes such as the shifting of the function of tax collector from the collector to the town clerk, it has been impossible to shift this function to the couny treasurer. The town board, subject to permissive referendum, can appoint a single assessor in place of the customary three elected assessors, but there has been no provision whereby duplicate assessment of village property could be avoided nor by which the assessment of industrial properties could be performed by the county or the State. Although a county board can, with the approval of the voters, adopt a county-president or countymanager form for the coordination of the county government, little opportunity has existed for the reorganization of the system of local government under which services are now performed by a number of different units, often with duplicate or overlapping functions and jurisdictions.

In the past there has been no practicable method whereby the officials and citizens of a county could prepare and adopt a system designed to perform most efficiently the entire group of services of local government. 

Some additional changes and improvements could be made possible by changes in the law; most proposed changes in the unit performing à function, such as tax collection, have been prohibited by the court interpretations of the constitution. The constitutional amendment, adopted 

November 5, 1935, however, removes one of the obstacles to change in local government in counties desiring changes. 

A number of changes which have been suggested in various phases of local governmental administration have been prevented in the past by constitutional limitations or by the absence of legal authorization. Among those which have received attention are the following: the transfer of some of the functions of the sheriff to the state police; the collection of all property taxes by the county treasurer; the payment of taxes in instal-

ments; the compulsory mailing of a tax notice to each taxpayer; the creation of a county board to review assessments in the county in order to provide prompt, inexpensive relief for individuals whose property is over-assessed; the provision of modern property maps and other aids for the use of the assessors; the assessment of all utility and industrial properties by a county official or by the State Tax Commission; the elimination of duplicate assessment of property in villages; the adoption of a budget system by all units of local government; and the centralization, in the hands of the county treasurer, of the funds of local units, such funds to be paid out on orders drawn on the county treasurer by the local officials, thus facilitating the preparation of financial reports, obtaining increased bank interest, and reducing the cost of prompt and proper auditing.

Until the adoption of the local government amendment in November, 1935, the provisions of the State Constitution, in the light of court interpretations, prevented the legislature from making some of the above changes in local government; for example, by Chapter 510 of the Laws of 1914, the Legislature attempted to abolish the duplicate assessment of village property in Westchester County by requiring village taxes to be levied on the basis of the town assessment of village property. This law, which would have deprived villages of the function of assessment, was declared unconstitutional as violating Section 2 of Article 10 of the Constitution. 

Likewise, a law transferring to the county the function of collecting delinquent village taxes was declared unconstitutional. 

Section 2 of Article 10 of the New York State Constitution states that county, town, city, and village officers, whose election or appointment is not provided by the constitution, shall be elected or shall be appointed locally. A similar provision was included in the constitution prior to the last general revision in 1894.

This provision of the constitution has been interpreted by the courts to mean not only that local officials should continue to be elected or appointed locally, but also that the above units of local government could not be deprived of a function performed locally at the time of the adoption of the revised constitution in 1894. Although minor exceptions have been recognized by the courts, this section has resulted in an inflexible and relatively unchanging system, because each function of each local unit has been protected by the constitution, and consequently the system of local government has been essentially "frozen."

Although some changes in administration have been made by shifting certain functions from one town officer to another town officer, or from one village officer to another village officer, the interpretations of the above provision of the constitution have been such as to require that most

functions must continue to be performed by the same unit of government. 

Thus, the elected representatives of the people in the state legislature or on the county boards of supervisors have been without power to change the allocation of most functions of local government. The amendment to the state constitution, however, has removed the greatest obstacle to change in local government in those counties desiring change. In addition to providing for optional forms of government for counties outside of New York City, the amendment makes possible the modification of county government in the five counties within New York City. The following discussion relates to the influence of the amendment on counties outside of New York City.

This amendment continues the present powers of the legislature over local government, but directs the legislature to propose optional forms of local government. Such optional forms of government can include a wide range of changes, such as the transfer of functions from one unit to another and changes in the administration of functions. The amendment alters the present form of local government of a given county only if one of the optional forms to be provided by the legislature is approved by the voters of the county. The voters of a given county are granted the right to retain the present form of local government or to select, from optional forms to be proposed by the legislature, that form which they believe to be best suited to the needs of the county. Thus, no change in local government will be made on account of the adoption of the proposed amendment unless approved by the people in the county concerned. 

Boards of supervisors of two or more counties are granted the power to provide, by agreement, for the discharge of one or more governmental functions. Thus, two counties, if they see fit, might unite in maintaining a single county home and farm or a jail.

The amendment includes a home-rule provision similar to that applying to cities, by which the power of the legislature to pass local legislation is limited. For those counties which adopt an optional form of government the amendment provides that the legislature may not pass special or local laws relating to the property, affairs, or government of such counties, except on message from the Governor declaring that an emergency exists and on the concurrent action of two-thirds of the members of each house of the legislature. As additional insurance of local control of county government, the amendment provides that county officials shall continue to be elected, or appointed by the boards of supervisors or by other county authorities.

The amendment further provides that an optional form of government shall not become effective in any given county until approved both by the voters inside and by those outside of any city with a population of 25 per

cent or more of the entire county. This provision is apparently designed to protect both rural and urban minorities. 

Some persons feel that the amendment should have gone much further and allowed compulsory changes in local government; others feel that no provision should have been made for any changes. The voters have decided, however, that the constitution shall provide a method whereby optional forms of local government selected by the legislature shall be available for counties. The constitution, as amended, gives the voters a greater voice than they have had in the past in determining and controlling the system of local government.

The first step in any proposed change in local government as a result of the constitutional amendment must be taken by the legislature in the provision of optional forms of local government. It is possible, however, that the citizens of any county in which particular changes are desired can obtain legislative approval of such change. Although the legislature must of necessity insist on some uniform features of local government it appears desirable that the optional forms should cover a wide range of possible changes.

It is probable that optional forms of local government will be provided by the legislature in the near future. It is to be expected that such forms will include broad and comprehensive opportunities for changes in local government. Limited opportunities for change already exist under provisions such as the optional county-president and the county-manager plans authorized by Chapter 948 of the Laws of 1935. Owing to the adoption of the constitutional amendment, however, the legislature may now provide optional but more far-reaching county-president, county-manager, and other forms.

The amended constitution opens the way for increased flexibility in local government, both in meeting the diverse needs of different communities and in meeting changed conditions. It allows progressive communities to try proposed plans, and thus to point the way for improved local government throughout the State. At the same time, the interests of local government and of control by the people are protected. No change in local government becomes effective as a result of the adoption of the amendment, unless approved by the voters of the county concerned; the present form of local government is to be maintained unless the voters decide in favor of some optional form. After the legislature provides optional forms of local government, it is for the voters in each county to decide which, if any, of the optional forms is to be adopted.
