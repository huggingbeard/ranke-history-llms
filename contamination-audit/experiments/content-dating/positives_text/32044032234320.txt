CHARLES R. SWIFT, III, a senior at 

Yale University, was sentenced in December, 1940 to one year's imprisonment for refusal to register in the first draft call. His sentence was served at the Federal Correctional Institution at Danbury, Connecticut. He is at present continuing his studies at New York University. 

NOTE: The following is a general description of life in the Federal Prisons, designed to meet the questionings of conscientious objectors who expect prison sentences. Details of policy vary greatly from one prison to another, but the general rules, restrictions, and privileges are the same. This is a composite of the experiences of several men who have completed sentences in various Federal Prisons. People who go to prison for conscientious reasons should do so because their life is such that they have no other choice. The following material has no bearing on whether or not a man decides to refuse war service, nor does it touch upon the great evils in the prison system; rather, it intends to anticipate and answer those questions which might be asked by one about to go to jail.

The War Resisters League issues this pamphlet as a valuable contribution to current discussion. The views herein expressed, however, are those of the author and do not necessarily represent the official position of the League.

PRISON ROUTINE 

by 

CHARLES R. SWIFT, III ENTRANCE TO PRISON: When you enter a Federal Prison you are asked to hand over most personal belongings. Glasses usually may be kept and occasionally one's personal Bible, crucifix, or the like. On sentences under six months, personal belongings are kept by the authorities and returned upon discharge. In cases of longer sentence, personal belongings are returned to your home. You are given a superficial physical examination, prison clothes, and finger-printed.

QUARANTINE: Here you are taught pris on discipline, bed making, "count", and introduced to the various aspects of prison life. 

You stay in the Quarantine cell-block (or "housing unit") for about thirty days. During this time you are submitted to intelligence tests, a physical examination, and numerous interviews. These latter include 

Stalks with the prison disciplinary head, the social worker, chaplain, psychiatrist, and the classification committee, which determine your permanent job allotment and housing unit. During Quarantine there is occasional work to be done - mopping floors, shovelling dirt, washing windows, etc. While in Quarantine you will probably be completely segregated from all inmates outside Quarantine, although this is not done at the smaller institutions.

CLOTHING: Clothes are changed either once or twice weekly -socks and handker- 582168 3

chiefs more often. Normally inmates are dressed in blue denim shirts and blue dungarees (inmate hospital attendants are dressed in white). Heavy jackets, caps, gloves, overshoes are supplied to those who need them. Hair is cut normally; no beards or mustaches are allowed.

BOOKS AND PAPERS: Censorship policies vary widely and are dependent on the censor and general policy of the specific institution. 

Generalizing from a number of prisons, books can be acquired in one of two ways: either (1) books must be sent to an inmate direct from the publisher (when ordered by a friend outside), and if they pass the censor they must remain in the prison permanently; or (2) books must be bought by the inmate through the Commissary. These are alternative methods and do not exist in the same prison. No books on controversial subjects are allowed, "controversial" being variously interpreted but usually including political books, pacifist writings, and even some sociology. An inmate can subscribe to a magazine or newspaper or both, if approved by the authorities.

Every prison has a library - the older institutions having a large number of varied books. Most of these may be checked out and taken to your cell or dormitory for a specified number of days. 

LETTERS: There can be seven people on your correspondence list. These must be approved by the authorities and generally must be "harmless" from an institutional point of view. During Quarantine you are limited to correspondence with one (sometimes two) blood relatives (which includes wife), afterwards to any (or all) on your correspondence list. Incoming letters can total seven a week (three pages), and outgoing letters no more 4

than two a week (two pages). Strict censorship is imposed on letters. Outgoing letters can have no mention of, or reference to, any fellow inmate, any reference to the prison layout, routine, or dissatisfaction with treatment.

VISITS: Only blood relatives and wives are allowed regular visits. Usually special permission is granted for fiancees, although attempts may be made to use this privilege as a means of keeping the inmate in line with official policy. No visits may take place during the Quarantine period. Visiting time is either one or two hours a month (depending on the prison). This time may be divided into two shorter visits. All visits are closely supervised. On occasional holidays (Christmas, July 4th) an extra visit may be given. Special visits with prospective employers, ministers, etc., are sometimes allowed, although only if the inmate is in the good graces of the authorities at the time.

FOOD: The food is generally good in quantity and from fair to poor in quality. As one might expect, food is the greatest source of grievance for the average inmate. Manner of preparation often tends to make good food unappetizing. Special diets are available for diabetics, etc. No food can be sent in from the outside.

WORK: The principle of conscript labor is enforced in prison. If you refuse to work, it is considered one of the worst offenses to authority in prison and normally you are put in the "bing" (solitary) until you agree to work. Occasionally, if you persist, you may be transferred to another prison. Work assignments are themselves modified forms of punishment, and are sometimes made for that purpose without regard to ability or past record. Often there is not sufficient 5

work to be done and work is "made" to fill in time. The work period normally lasts about eight hours daily, except Saturday afternoon and Sunday. You may be assigned to a job in the kitchen, laundry, one of the "shops" (machine, carpentry, plumbing, paint, or electrical), or the garage, as a janitor, porter, hospital attendant, secretarial helper, or outside the walls on construction crews or on the farm. All jobs are closely supervised by guards.

RECREATION: Most Federal Prisons have no gymnasium. Normally during the winter there are weekly movies. During the summer you can play softball, volley ball, and horseshoes in the "yard". Indoor games available to men in their cell-block include ping-pong, chess, checkers, playing cards (in some prisons), and dominoes. Yard periods (when inmates can play games and talk freely) are less frequent in winter. Quite often there is an institution softball team which plays teams from the outside every week or so during the summer. There are occasional programs put on by the inmates, such as minstrel shows, plays, etc. Also, outside groups occasionally furnish entertainment for an evening.

MONEY AND COMMISSARY: There is no money in prison. If money is sent you (which must be done by an approved correspondent) it is exchanged for a commissary book from which purchases are made at the prison commissary (store). The commissary carries tobacco, cigarettes, candy, some fruit in season, soap, pencils, etc. For the average inmate, it is a matter of great importance whether or not he "makes commissary" (has money to spend).

This varies 

EDUCATIONAL PROGRAM: 

greatly with the institution. Always there are literacy classes, and usually classes in shorthand, typing, bookkeeping, and one or two languages. Usually, if an inmate wishes to teach a subject, and there are sufficient interested inmates, the class can be arranged. In most prisons there are possibilities of extension work from some University, although there is no encouragement along this line from the authorities.

RELIGIOUS SERVICES: Depending upon the institution, there may be a Catholic, Protestant, Jewish Chaplain, or there may be none at all. There are always Sunday services (to which anyone may go), and if the prison has chaplains, they are available for occasional consultation.

GUARDS AND 

ADMINISTRATIVE 

STAFF: These men are civil service employees and are well-educated men for the most part. As is true with all branches of the 

Civil Service program with its low scale salaries the men with the most ability and best training are attracted elsewhere, unless the men have an idea of real service, which is rare. Many of the personnel had altruistic motives when they took the job, but it is painful to see the deadening effect which the

if prison system has upon them. Rarely ever do you find a guard who would "go to bat" for an inmate in a case of injustice; such men just do not stay in the prison system. However, the guards are good, likeable men, whose attitudes toward the prisoner (and his rights) are considerably more advanced than those of the general public. 

WRITING TO BE TAKEN OUT: It is us ually possible to take out some original writing at time of release, although it is strictly censored and must be innocuous from an institutional viewpoint. This is one of the many privileges which can be retracted at the whim of those in authority, however.

MEANS OF ENFORCING DISCIPLINE: 

Generally speaking, there are two ways of enforcing prison discipline: (1) By giving rewards: many little privileges are given to the men if they are good, but taken from them if they buck the prison policy at any point. These special privileges include movie attendance, participation in inmate programs, etc. The most important reward is parole which is based upon several factors, an important one being the inmate's conduct in prison. (2) Punishment: this ranges in severity from a withdrawal of certain privileges to indefinite solitary confinement, with or without full rations, or even a transfer to a "tougher" prison. Punishment is meted out by an Adjustment Council (composed of the administrative heads in the prison) in the presence of the offender. Enforcement of discipline is quite mechanical, almost unfeeling, and is designed to break the will of the offender, despite the theoretical policy of "constructive guidance". It must be realized that at present the prisoner has no rights whatsoever, only privileges granted him conditionally by the authorities.

You may be surprised that anything in this country could be so totalitarian as the prison system. In an interview with a CO last year, James Bennett, Director of Federal Prisons, said, "The American penal system is the most authoritarian system in the world (consulted later, Bennett stated he had said, "... in any democracy"); if you buck up against it, you will feel the whole weight of the system upon you." This is why so many men emerge from jail with either spirits crushed or embittered and hateful souls.

HOSPITAL AND MEDICAL CARE; PRIS- ON CLEANLINESS: Hospital treatment is generally fair and the equipment is good. 

Personal treatment of the men is frightfully mechanical and unfeeling. When one makes "sick-call", it is pretty much assumed by the doctor that he is faking unless he can prove otherwise. Hospital and Medical Care are un der the United States Public Health Service, which is independent of the warden. It must be mentioned that Federal prisons are very clean and well kept, sanitary arrangements being of the best.

RACIAL SEGREGATION: The Federal 

Prison System is guided by a policy of racial segregation. There are exceptions to this: work crews have both whites and negroes on the same crew; negroes and whites may have adjoining cells in the cell-blocks, although there is no mixing in the dormitories.

OTHER DETAILS: You will be known by name and number (i.e., Smith, 6428). You and all other inmates will be counted six to ten times daily.

PAROLE: Two forms of parole are avail able to violators of the Selective Service and Training Act. The first is an executive pa-

role, one of four kinds: (1) and (2) to military service, (3) to C.P.S. Camp, or (4) to special projects. The other form is regular parole, for which every prisoner is eligible after one-third of his sentence, and which parole is obtainable only through the United 

States Board of Parole. 

At the present time, January 1943, those conscientious objectors who have not been classified IV-E by the Selective Service Sys tem, in the absence of new evidence are not granted parole to C.P.S. camp. This result is brought about by the punitive interpretation of Selective Service, who must recommend such paroles. Type 4 parole under the executive order has thus far been used very infrequently.

The net result is that very few conscientious objectors are actually being paroled. 

RELEASE: 

When one is released, he is given a prison-made suit and a bus or train ticket home, and may be given a few dollars. 

CONCLUSION: There are tremendous areas in which the individual can be of service in prison: (a) Except perhaps in war, there is no other human institution which precipitates more human suffering. A friendly and sensitive person can bring hope and comfort to many people. (b) Being with people so constantly, one has a real chance to bring some great purpose into their lives. If one is interested in personal evangelism, prison offers one of the best possible opportunities. (c) There is opportunity for service in crying out against the rottenness of the penal system at many points and in various ways. 

Specifically, one constantly encounters cases 10

of racial discrimination, unjust disciplinary action, and excessive callousness or authoritarianism on the part of the personnel. Many times conscientious objectors have felt so strongly about certain injustices that they have protested by striking, fasting, or other non-violent direct action. (d) One may encourage guards to assert their human sympathy and concern for the lives of other human beings. 

And never let it be said that in prison you are cut off from humanity and can do no good!
