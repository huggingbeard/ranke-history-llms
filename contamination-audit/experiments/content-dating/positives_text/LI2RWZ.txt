FARMERS' COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS IN FLORIDA II. Organization and Management By MARVIN A. BROOKER and H. G. HAMILTON* 

The study of farmers' cooperative associations in Florida was undertaken for the purpose of securing information relative to the development and present status of these associations, and for determining some of the factors which contribute to their success or failure. In analyzing these problems attention was given to both active and inactive associations. A classified list of these associations along with some supplementary information and a discussion of the important Federal and State laws affecting cooperative associations in Florida was published in Bulletin 245. 

The present bulletin, which is a continuation of the study presented in Bulletin 245, deals with the economic status and the organization and management policies of these associations. 

METHOD OF STUDY 

Personal visits were made to each of the associations listed in Bulletin 245 that had been organized early enough to operate during the 1929-30 marketing season. Often the investigator had no knowledge of whether the local association was active or inactive before arriving in the community served by the association. In the case of active associations he went direct to the executive office of the association and obtained there detailed information relative to its activities. The information obtained included details of the administration and management, membership and patronage, volume of business, sales policy, pooling practices, contracts, credit, advertising practices, and much general information, in addition to complete financial statements, when available, for the four years ending with 1929-30, and for Acknowledgments:-The Florida Citrus Exchange by means of its auditing system facilitated the study of the entire Citrus Exchange organization. 

Other associations cooperated as freely with the study of their own organizations. The office of the Secretary of State granted many courtesies in connection with the study of the charters of the incorporated associations.

This information was obtained directly from the books of the association and from the manager, director or other person intimately acquainted with the affairs of the association. Often there were no adequate records available, but in such cases as complete estimates as possible were obtained. 

In the case of inactive associations, inquiries were made for some of the charter applicants, or for someone else who would be familiar with the previous activities of the defunct association. 

In almost all cases, one or more persons were found who could give some information regarding the association, although frequently there were no actual records available. From these persons the investigator ascertained as accurately as possible the length of time the association was in existence, membership, volume of business, method of operation, difficulties, as well as all other general and detailed information available, including the principal and contributing reasons for ceasing to operate.

DEVELOPMENT OF COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS IN FLORIDA 

Attempts at cooperative organization among farmers in Florida are not new. However, the most consistent development has taken place during relatively recent years. The dates of organization of 374 associations are given in Table I. 

Interest in the cooperative selling of citrus fruits, as manifested by the organization of perhaps the first association for that purpose, began in 1889. In 1894 there was an enthusiastic movement for cooperative marketing, which culminated in the organization of the Florida Fruit and Vegetable Growers' Association, with headquarters in Jacksonville. This was a central association, composed of 84 local unions with a membership of over 3,000, and representing more than 3,000,000 packages of fruits and vegetables, the majority of which was citrus fruits. OTHER COOPERATIVE EFFORTS 

In addition to the organizations shown in Table I, Florida shared in most of the early cooperative movements common to other sections of the United States. Outstanding among these were the Grange, the Farmers' Alliance, and the Farmers' Union.¹

THE GRANGE OF FLORIDA 

The earliest attempt at cooperative effort by Florida farmers was through the Grange of Florida. The Florida State Grange was organized in 1875 when the popularity of the National Grange was at its zenith. It remained in existence for only eight years, but during that time there were organized 113 local Grange units, or Subordinate Granges, with a total of 1,500 members. Most of the local units had from 10 to 20 members, but a few were larger, one having 83 members. Perhaps a dozen of these local units organized and operated general stores, which were financed through the sale of capital stock to the members. Farm produce was handled for, and merchandise sold to, both members and non-members. It was the purpose of the stores to pay small dividends on capital stock, sell merchandise at less than prevailing prices, and handle farm products at cost. It is probable that merchandise and farm products were handled as proposed, but there is no record of dividends having been paid by any of the stores. 

None of the stores operated more than five years, and most of them operated less than two years.

The reasons given for the failure of the Grange stores are as follows: (1) Poor and inexperienced management, (2) antagonism of competitors due to price cutting by the Grange, and (3) too liberal extension of credit.

THE FARMERS' ALLIANCE 

The second state-wide attempt to organize the farmers of Florida was through the efforts of the Farmers' Alliance of Florida. This movement began in 1888 and died in 1892. 

The Farmers' Alliance of Florida developed more enthusiasm than had the Grange, and organized 186 local units with a total of 4,500 members. General stores were organized and operated by 45 of the local Alliance units, which were financed by the sale of capital stock to the members. The purposes of the Alliance stores paralleled those of the Grange, namely, they were to pay small dividends on capital stock, sell merchandise at less than prevailing prices, and handle farm produce at cost. Dividends were never paid by any of the stores and very little farm produce was handled, but some of the stores did transact considerable merchandise business. The failure of the Alliance stores has been attributed to the same reasons given for the failure of those operated by the Grange.

The Cooperative Alliance Mercantile Association, organized as a purchasing agent for the Alliance stores, and financed by the sale of capital stock to the local stores and members of the local units, failed at the same time.

True to the principles of the Farmers' Alliance, the State organization was active in politics. In 1890 the national convention of the Alliance was held in Ocala, Florida. The unsuccessful participation of the State organization in the gubernatorial campaign of 1892 contributed to its downfall. 

THE FARMERS' UNION 

Whereas the National Grange had its greatest development in the wheat growing sections of the country and the Farmers' Alliance was identified largely with political movements, the Farmers' Union has been associated closely with the cotton producing areas of the United States, although it has developed to some extent in other sections, notably in Nebraska. It was organized primarily to control prices, but, being unsuccessful in this, changed its field of activity and developed some large and successful business establishments. Warehouses, cotton gins, fertilizer plants, and local stores were the principal fields of

activity in the cotton belt, although the political field was not entirely neglected.

The Farmers' Union entered the State of Florida in 1900, reached its maximum development from 1908 to 1912, then gradually receded and finally ceased to function in 1920. It is estimated by a past president of the State organization that at one time the membership exceeded 12,000, acting through more than 2,000 local units. The Local Unions were federated into County 

Unions, which formed the connecting link with the State Union. 

There was an initiation fee of one dollar for each member, and annual dues of one dollar payable in quarterly installments. Sixty cents of the annual dues were retained by the Local Union, while 25 cents went to the State Union and 15 cents to the National Union.

The most extensive development of the Farmers' Union in Florida was in the western part of the State. Gadsden County, with 

18 or 20 Local Unions, handled $78,000 worth of farm produce and supplies in one year.

In addition to its economic activity the State Union promoted numerous educational and political enterprises. Its final failure is charged by its last president to the abnormally prosperous condition of the farm population during the period from 1915 to 1920, which caused the farmers, to a large degree, to lose sight of the benefits of cooperation. Data are not available to prove or disprove this contention.

LEGAL STATUS OF COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS IN FLORIDA 

The legal status of the 374 cooperative associations included in the study is shown in Table II. Associations organized prior to the Cooperative Act of 1909 were either unincorporated or were forced to incorporate under the general corporation laws. 

The law enacted in 1909 provided for the organization of cooperative associations without capital stock on a one-man-one-vote basis unless some other method of control was specified in the articles of incorporation. It will be noted that practically all of the citrus associations were organized under that law. The Cooperative Marketing Act of 1923 provided for either capital stock or non-stock financing, and has been used by most of the associations organized for handling truck crops, livestock and livestock products, and for miscellaneous purposes, since its passage. 

This law has now been repealed by the passage of the Agricultural

Cooperative Act of 1931, which is similar in most respects. Unincorporated associations are essentially partnerships, and have the disadvantage of unlimited liability for each member. A discussion of these laws and related Federal laws is included in 

Bulletin 245.

REASONS FOR CEASING TO OPERATE 

In the case of associations that had ceased to operate, the opinion of the informant was secured as to the cause of this situation. A tabulation of the reasons given appears in Table IV. 

In the opinion of the informants, lack of volume was the outstanding cause of failure, being given as the principal cause 28 times and a contributing cause in 15 other cases. Poor management, no need for cooperative, lack of cooperative spirit, insufficient capital, competition, and unsatisfactory prices followed in order as principal reasons for ceasing to operate. In addition, 42 other reasons were given from one to several times each for a total of 

82 times as principal causes of failure, and 26 similar reasons were given a total of 31 times as contributing causes. Among these miscellaneous reasons were organization difficulties, consolidations, membership dissatisfaction, lack of credit, crop failure, boom, dishonesty, too much credit, too strict grading, salaries too high, inadequate accounting, and others.

COOPERATIVE MARKETING OF CITRUS FRUITS 

The citrus industry is the most important agricultural enterprise in Florida. The preliminary report of the United States 

Department of Agriculture places the Florida farm value of citrus fruits for 1930 at $53,840,000. The average seasonal estimated farm value of all Florida citrus from 1924-25 to 1928-29, inclusive, was approximately $41,500,000. The volume has grown from a few thousand boxes 50 years ago to an indicated normal crop of more than 20,000,000 boxes.

This large-scale industry with its rapid expansion has confronted many difficulties, especially in the fields of marketing and distribution. In the early days almost every grower of any considerable volume owned his packing plant and directed the distribution of his product. As competition developed, however, it was found to be advisable to form combinations and eliminate numbers of these plants in the interest of more economical operation, and to place the problems of distributing the crop in fewer hands. 

This narrowed the field to a number of relatively large-scale independent operators and a group of cooperative associations.

During the past several seasons citrus fruits of a value of from $10,000,000 to $15,000,000, f. o. b. packing plants, have been handled by cooperative associations in Florida. This includes all fruit handled by locals affiliated with the Florida Citrus Exchange, the Gulf Coast Citrus Exchange, and independent local cooperative associations, but does not include that handled by cooperative associations for special shippers.

MEMBERSHIP AND VOLUME OF LOCAL ASSOCIATIONS 

The membership and volume of business of local citrus cooperative associations from 1926-27 to 1929-30, inclusive, are shown in Table VIII. The total membership increased from 4,791 in 

1926-27 to 6,692 in 1929-30, or approximately 40 percent. This increase was due in part to a 15 percent increase in the number of active associations, but a more important factor was an increase of 22 percent in the average membership per association. The latter method of increase is apparently more desirable than the former, since it tends to make more complete use of existing facilities and is presumably due less to the activity of outside promoters.

The result of this increase in the average membership per association is reflected in the last column of Table VIII, which shows the percentage of the total fruit originating at shipping points where cooperative associations are located that was handled by these associations. This percentage increased from 47.9 in 

1926-27 to 54.2 in 1929-30. The effect of the total increased membership is shown by the percentage of the total state volume originated by cooperative associations which increased from 29.4 in 1926-27 to 36.5 in 1929-30, or 24 percent. The wide variations in average volume per association from year to year were due to variations in the size of the total citrus crop.

FINANCIAL STATEMENTS OF LOCAL CITRUS COOPERATIVE ASSOCIATIONS BALANCE SHEETS 

A comparison of the average assets and liabilities of local citrus cooperative associations for a period of years is shown in Table XI. These averages are based on all the associations for which complete financial statements, including net sales, were available. 

The four years, 1927 to 1930, inclusive, were compared throughout with similar information for the earliest year obtainable on the same associations. In a large number of cases this early information was on the first year the association operated. 

The total assets of the associations increased quite regularly from 1927 to 1930. This increase was the result of similar increases in each of the groups of assets, so that the percentages of the total represented by current, deferred, fixed, and other assets remained almost constant, the largest percentage change being in the group of "Other assets." This group is composed of, (1) cash in litigation, which is for the most part cash in closed banks, (2) stocks, bonds, and equities, or investments, the greater part of which are in subsidiaries of the Florida Citrus Exchange, (3) contingent assets, or endorsements of growers' notes for production credit mostly from the Growers' Loan and Guaranty Company, on account of which the association assumes a contingent liability and holds a contingent asset, and (4) miscellaneous other assets.

Fixed assets, which represent the investment in land, buildings and equipment, showed an absolute increase every year from 1927 to 1930, but the percentage invested in these permanent assets remained fairly constant at slightly above one-half of the total. Most of the associations conformed to conservative business practices by charging off adequate allowances for depreciation. In fact the depreciation allowance in many cases was so large that a physical valuation of the property during the year 1929-30 by the American Appraisal Company on behalf of the Federal Farm Board, resulted in an adjustment of this allowance to the extent that the total depreciation allowance was less in 

1930 than in 1929.

Deferred assets are composed of prepaid expenses. Interest and insurance have been paid in advance, and supplies left over from the preceding season are on hand for future use.

Current assets represent approximately one-fifth of the total assets. This group is composed of cash and of items which normally will be converted into cash within one year. 

The liability side of the balance sheets shows considerable change during the period from 1927 to 1930, especially in the relationship between current and fixed liabilities. Long-term borrowings in the fixed liabilities group increased almost 300 percent from 1929 to 1930, and current liabilities were reduced in similar proportion. There was likewise an expansion of contingent liabilities in the "Other liabilities" group, already noted. Although net worth increased steadily from 1927 to 1930, the ratio of net worth to total liabilities remained practically constant. 

The changed status of the indebtedness from short-term to long-term is reflected in the increased current ratio from slightly below 2:1 from 1927 to 1929, to 3.6:1 in 1930'. Apparently, any excess of current assets over current liabilities which produces a ratio greater than 1:1 should be satisfactory, but this is not always true, due to the fact that the current assets may not be turned into cash at the same intervals as the current liabilities become due. Consequently, to have reasonable security a ratio somewhat greater than 1:1 usually is necessary. Bankers commonly consider a ratio of 2:1 a satisfactory margin, but this usually varies between types of businesses. The local associations make a definite charge per box for packing or otherwise processing the members' fruit, and in addition it has been a common practice among most of the associations to deduct a retain to be used for financing permanent improvements. 

The packing charge varies between different associations, but usually is approximately in line with charges for similar services in competing packing-houses. The retains vary also, but usually range from 5 cents to 15 cents per box. These retains represent borrowings from the members and hence are a liability of the association, but differ from other indebtedness in that they do not have a due date and do not bear interest. They are subordinate to all other liabilities of the association but must be paid before there is any division of the net worth. These retains have been carried in the balance sheets as "Certificates of indebtedness," although it is not a uniform practice among the associations to actually issue these evidences of indebtedness. Perhaps in most cases they are carried only on the books of the association. 

Many of the larger and older associations have discontinued deducting retains after their buildings and equipment have been largely paid for. In such cases packing charges are made high enough that there are sufficient profits to cover overhead expenses and pay back some of the older retains. The balance sheets show that the volume of certificates of indebtedness was less in 1930 than in 1929. More retains were paid back during 1929-30 than were deducted, due to the fact that the associations were able to finance their permanent improvements with the Federal Farm 

Board loans.

The percent members' equity indicates the percentage of the total assets that has been financed by the members¹. This percentage was almost constant at approximately two-thirds of the total from 1927 to 1930, but due to the increased borrowing in 1930 already noted the equity of the members fell to 61.5 percent. 

The total assets could shrink in value more than 60 percent before the creditors, other than the members themselves, would be in danger.

PROFIT AND LOSS STATEMENTS 

Local citrus cooperative associations derive their operating income from more or less fixed charges per box for all fruit handled for members of the association, packing for others than members, discounts, and other minor sources. The charge for members' fruit is the principal source of income, and is fixed at an amount somewhat in line with competing packinghouses, but sufficient to cover costs of packing or otherwise processing the fruit, and pay the sales retains of the central exchange and sub-exchanges. This charge is represented in the tables as the gross margin, or spread between the net selling price of the fruit loaded on the cars and the price paid to the grower. Since processing and marketing costs do not fluctuate widely between low-price and high-price years, the percentage gross margin is dependent mainly on the selling price of the fruit on the one hand and variations in processing costs between individual associations on the other. It will be noted in Table XIV that the gross margin in 1929-30, a year of relatively high prices, was 36.9 percent of the net selling price, while in 1928-29, a year of large volume and low prices, the gross margin was 52.3 percent. Years of large volume are the most prosperous years for the packers, while often the producers receive greater incomes with small volumes. As shown in Table 

XIV, the gross margin retained by the associations from 1926-27 to 1929-30 varied directly with the volume handled, while the payment to members varied inversely with the volume with the exception of one year, 1926-27.

In 192930 the 16 associations with net sales above $300,000 had net operating incomes of approximately 20 percent of the total operating incomes, while the eight associations with net sales of $50,000 and less had operating expenses 3.7 percent above operating incomes, so that there was a net loss. 

Similar results are shown for the year 1928-29. These differences in net incomes are noted in spite of the fact that the 16 largest associations in 1929-30 took gross margins of approximately 37 percent of the net sales, while the eight smallest associations took 46 percent. The smallest associations, although taking larger percent gross margins than the large associations, still did not take enough to cover operating costs, while the large associations with smaller percent gross margins were able to pay operating costs and make substantial profits. 

In general the percentages of total operating incomes spent for salaries and wages, building and equipment expense, office expense and general expense, increased as the size of the associations decreased.

The relation of volume of fruit handled to various factors is shown in Table XVII. These gross correlation coefficients are statements of the degree of relationship existing between volume and each of the factors shown.

The relationship between volume and percent gross margin is shown to be negative. This confirms the point already made that on the average the larger the volume the smaller the percentage of net sales retained for operating the association. The correlation coefficients in this case are not large enough to be of importance for any particular year, but when the four years are taken together they are regarded as significant. 

There is a positive relationship between volume and turnover of fixed assets. Closely related to this is the negative relationship between volume and investment per box. The larger associations have higher total investments in buildings and equipment than have the small associations, but the investment per box of fruit handled is less. Thus the larger associations are making more complete use of each dollar invested than are the smaller associations.

There is also shown to be a positive relationship between volume and sales of fruit per member. The larger associations are made up, on the average, of larger growers than are the small associations. As will be shown later, this factor has an important bearing on operating costs.

ASSOCIATIONS WHICH HAVE CEASED TO OPERATE 

Complete balance sheets were available on 25 associations that had ceased to operate, at the end of their last year of operation; on 17 of these associations, at the end of their next to last year of operation; and on 14, at the end of their third from last year of operation (Table XVIII). Details of the volume of net sales were not available, but the average volume in boxes of the 25 associations during their last year of operation was only about one-third as large as the average volume of 62 active associations in 1929-30. Also, the average volume handled by 57 active associations during the earliest years for which information was available was almost twice as large as that of these associations during their last year of operation.

Total assets seem to have been fairly well proportioned between the various groups of assets, but the associations were too small for efficiency. The distribution of the liabilities was more unfavorable than of the assets. Too large a part of the financing had been done by short-term borrowing, as shown by notes payable under current liabilities. This reduced the current ratio to less than 1:1 and subjected the associations to bankruptcy at the will of their creditors.

Additional weaknesses of these associations are brought out in the profit and loss statements shown in Table XIX. The percentage of total operating income spent for each of the groups of expenses was abnormally high for each of the years shown. There was an actual deficit during the next to last year of operation, and the very small surplus during the last year was not sufficient to keep them going. The financial condition of these associations was so weak that they quickly failed when faced with real difficulties. Apparently many failures could be avoided by combining the volumes of small packing plants into larger units so as to gain greater operating efficiency.

POOLING CITRUS 

When the growers of a cooperative association "pool", they lump together their produce to sell it as a single unit rather than as separate shipments on each grower's account; and they receive as a return the average price realized on the shipments during a given period of time.

Pooling has many advantages, both for the producer and for the association. For the producer, pooling serves as insurance, distributing risks over a greater number of people. For the association, pooling makes it easier to adopt orderly marketing policies and develop new markets, since experimental shipments are then made at the risk of the entire association rather than of certain individuals. However, to secure the advantages of pooling, it is necessary to establish an adequate system of grades; otherwise pooling would benefit the producer of the poorer grade and penalize the producer of the better grade, since both would receive the same returns per unit. 

Citrus fruits can be more accurately graded than most fruits, and for that reason there are fewer disadvantages to pooling than for many other commodities. 

Although some method of pooling was followed by more than four-fifths of the local citrus cooperative associations during the season 1929-30, the methods of pooling were not uniform (Table 

XXIV). There were 26 different methods of pooling reported, each method being used by from one to 12 associations, while 11 associations did not pool and the method used by one of the associations is not known.

The pool in most common use during the season 1929-30 was two weeks in length throughout the season by variety, grade, and size. The seasonal pool was used by 10 associations, and monthly

pools by nine. No other single method of pooling was used by more than two associations.

The two-weeks pool has as its chief advantage the fact that the producer gets the average price that fruit is bringing at the time of year that his fruit is marketed, without the risks of "off" days attending the daily pool. COOPERATIVE MARKETING OF TRUCK CROPS 

The farm value of all Florida truck crops ranks close to the farm value of Florida citrus crops. A discussion of the economic importance of Florida truck crops is included in another publication from this Station, in which it is brought out that the average seasonal estimated farm value of 12 of the leading truck crops of the state from 1924-25 to 1928-29, inclusive, was about $35,000,- 000.¹ It was also brought out in that study that most Florida truck crops are marketed during the winter and spring rather than during the spring, summer and fall as in most of the other states; thus truck farming in Florida does not conform to a calendar year basis.

Cooperative marketing of truck crops is relatively a more recent development than of citrus fruits. Insofar as records are available, only 14 associations were formed for handling truck crops from 1899, when the first one was organized, through 1917. 

Beginning with 1918, however, there were 83 organized before the 1929-30 season (Table I). Of the total of 97 associations organized during the entire period, there were 52 active during the season 1929-30. The location of these 52 active associations is shown in the accompanying map, Fig. 21.

42.9 percent of those active in 1927-28; and for only 38.7 percent of those that were active during the 1926-27 season. Due to this difference of functions, the proportions of total liabilities represented by current and by fixed liabilities are very different from the citrus associations. The percentage of current liabilities is much larger in the truck crops associations, and of fixed liabilities is much less.

Since the truck crops associations do a minimum of processing and thus have small proportions of long-term assets and liabilities, it is necessary for them to have a high ratio of net worth to total liabilities and net worth. There is little physical property to place under mortgage so the net worth must be large in order to command necessary short-term credit. Consequently, the percentage of net worth was much larger throughout the four-year period for the truck crops associations than in the case of the citrus associations.

As previously pointed out (Table XXIX) there was a wide range in size of the truck crops associations. This difference in size is reflected in the balance sheets when the associations are grouped according to volume of net sales, Tables XXXI and XXXII. Those associations having net sales above $100,000 in 

1929-30 had total assets more than four times as large as the average of all associations, while those with net sales of $100,000 and less had total assets about one-fourteenth as large as the average of all associations. Those with net sales not available had total assets about one-half as large as the average, but this was due mostly to one association whose net sales would undoubtedly have placed it in the group having net sales above $100,000 if this information could have been obtained. 

In general, the large associations were in a much stronger financial condition than the smaller associations. The current ratio indicates that at the end of the season 1929-30 the largest associations had, on the average, $3.10 of current assets available for each dollar of current liabilities, while the group with net sales of $100,000 and less had $1.50, and those with net sales not available had $1.40. The average for all associations was $2.30. 

At the same time, the net worth of the largest associations averaged 80.6 percent of total liabilities and net worth; those with net sales of $100,000 and less averaged 53.6 percent; those with net sales not available averaged 39.3 percent; and the average for all associations was 71.1 percent.

For the year 1928-29 and 1929-30 these associations were divided into two groups, namely, those with total net sales above $100,000, and those with net sales of $100,000 and less. The group having above $100,000 in 1929-30 was composed of nine associations which averaged $719,724.21 total net sales, while the group having $100,000 and less was composed of 22 associations which averaged $20,834.84. 

The percent of net sales retained as gross margins by the associations was almost twice as great in the latter group as in the former.

The large associations handled a great deal more supplies for their members than did the small associations. These supplies were bought in quantity and at advantageous prices, since most of the large associations were able to pay cash and receive discounts. These supplies were then sold to the members for a small margin over the purchase price in order to pay the cost of handling. The small associations handled very small quantities of these supplies and many of them attempted to sell them to the members at cost. The average gross margin charged by all the small associations probably was not large enough to pay the cost of handling, so the produce sold through these associations had to bear this expense.

The large associations also had larger incomes from other sources than did the small associations. An adequate retain was deducted to build up the building and reserve funds, and a considerable income was derived from contract processing. The total operating incomes for the average association in each of these groups, for the group with net sales not available, and for the three groups combined, for the two years, 1928-29 and 1929-30 are shown in Tables XXXIV and XXXV.

CREDIT OPERATIONS 

To compete with private dealers and with other cooperatives, many cooperative associations find themselves forced to provide production credit for their members. This is sometimes done by making advances to members out of surplus, or by the associations borrowing money on behalf of their members, but perhaps the most practicable means of rendering this service has been found to be through the medium of subsidiary corporations organized expressly for carrying on credit operations. 

These credit corporations are organized on a capital-stock basis, with the parent organization, or the members, ordinarily owning most of the common stock. However, stock may be, and often is, sold to others who may be interested in the organization. The money obtained by the sale of stock is then invested in strong securities (usually government bonds or Federal land bank bonds) which are in turn used as supplementary collateral when the credit corporation rediscounts the notes of producer members. 

The grower commonly gives a crop lien and real estate mortgage as security for his loan. The credit corporations may keep these receivables in their portfolios and supply the borrowers with funds obtained from commercial banks by using the bonds as security, but the most usual procedure is for the credit corporation to endorse the crop liens and real estate mortgages and attach the amount of bonds required as additional collateral and forward all to the Federal intermediate credit bank. If this collateral is found to be satisfactory, funds are then forwarded to the credit corporation which distributes them to the borrower members, or to the parent organization for supplies furnished the growers. 

Information was secured on six such credit corporations oper-

ating as subsidiaries of truck crops cooperative associations during the season 1929-30, and on four for the prior two seasons.

All but one of these credit corporations obtained practically all of their production credit from the Federal intermediate credit bank, while one dealt mainly with commercial banks. 

The condition of these subsidiary corporations at the end of the three seasons indicated is shown in Table XXXVI. At the end of the 1929-30 season, 94.4 percent of the assets consisted of notes, crop liens, mortgages, stocks and bonds. The largest other item was cash. Very little overhead investment was required by these subsidiaries, since the headquarters were in the offices of the parent organizations. The fixed assets consisted almost entirely of a desk and filing cabinet. 

Practically all of the liabilities consisted of notes payable. In the main these were notes that had been rediscounted, and were offset by notes receivable listed under current assets. Net worth was composed of capital stock, a limited amount of reserves, and surplus.

Interest and salaries were the principal items of expense, Table XXXVII. The income was derived from the difference between interest paid for funds and interest charged borrowers. ADMINISTRATION AND MANAGEMENT 

The administration and management set-up of 52 truck crops cooperative associations operating in Florida during the season 1929-30 is shown in Table XXXVIII. Each of the associations had a president, 32 of whom were chosen by the directors and 20 by the members. Fifty of the associations had first vicepresidents, while a limited number had second and third vicepresidents. There were many combinations of the positions of secretary, treasurer, and manager, but the most usual practice was to have one person for manager and another for the combined positions of secretary and treasurer. One of the associations had an assistant manager, and two employed special sales managers. 

All but two of the associations were governed by boards of directors, which varied in number from three to 15. Of the 50 associations which had boards of directors, 43 had a total of 265 directors, or an average of 6.2 per association, who were elected

to serve for a period of one year, while seven had a total of 47 directors, or an average of 6.7 per association, elected to serve for a term of three years. The practice in most common use is for the directors to select the officers from their own number, but the manager is ordinarily employed from the outside.

POOLING PRACTICES 

A large part of the truck crops of this state are graded and packed in the field, so there are likely to be greater variations in the grades and in the quality of the pack than is the case for citrus fruits which are graded and packed almost entirely in packinghouses. For most truck crops the grades are not so well defined, and they are less subject to processing by machinery, than are citrus fruits. As a consequence, pooling is less prevalent than was observed in the case of the citrus associations. A large proportion of the sales, especially in car-lot volume, are made for individual account.

When pooling is done, the daily pool is the prevailing type. 

This is because most truck crops are subject to violent fluctuations in price within short periods of time. This is especially true of regions, such as Florida, that are far from the markets and so have relatively high distribution costs. Consequently, a few days' difference in the time of marketing often accounts for the difference between a profit and a loss for the individual producers. 

Those who are successful in being among the first to place produce on the market are therefore unwilling to pool returns with those. who market even a few days later.

The car-pool is widely used for shipments in small volume. 

There is a considerable saving in freight cost when shipments are made in carloads rather than in smaller quantities. Producers with less than a carload ready for shipment often pool their produce in order to fill a car and secure the advantage of the car-lot

freight rate, and divide the proceeds on the basis of an average price per unit.

Among the reasons given by the managers for pooling are the 

1. All producers get the same price for produce at the time it is sold, regardless of the market to which it is sent. 

2. Manager is not subject to criticism that best markets are given to certain favored producers. 

Reasons against pooling are as follows: 

1. More accounting and clerical work is required. 

2. Grades are not clearly enough defined. 

3. Producers of best quality are penalized, thus destroying incentive for good quality.

Approximately 80 percent of the total assets of the 15 associations at the end of the 1929-30 season was represented by one association. When this association is eliminated the total assets average only slightly more than $2,000 per association. 

However, even with the conservative investment per association the financial condition of the average of these associations was weak. Net worth, or the equity of the associations, was 4.7 percent and 3.8 percent of the assets at the end of the seasons 

1928-29 and 1929-30, respectively. Thus with only a slight shrinkage of the assets the ultimate solvency of the associations would be endangered. The current ratio at the end of each season indicated that the associations were not then in a currently solvent condition, since there was less than one dollar of current assets available to meet each dollar of current liabilities. Obviously, the average association handling livestock and livestock products was liable at any time to be forced into bankruptcy by its creditors.

Some sound basis of financing, greater volume of business, operating margins that will enable them to render greater service to their members and compete for business more efficiently, and improved accounting practices are among the outstanding needs of this group of associations.

MISCELLANEOUS TYPES OF COOPERATIVE ASSOCIA- TIONS IN FLORIDA 

The cooperative associations in the preceding groups were classified on the basis of the principal products handled. Data were secured on 17 additional associations which were active during the 1929-30 season but could not be classified in one of the major groups that have been discussed. The commodities handled and services performed by these miscellaneous associations are shown in Table XLV. 

A number of associations in the preceding groups handled fertilizers and supplies, but since this was not their primary function they were placed in the other groups. Data were secured on three associations, however, which were organized primarily for purchasing fertilizers and supplies for their members and so were brought into the miscellaneous group. Two associations, which handled considerable quantities of truck crops, especially straw-

berries, were put into the miscellaneous group because their primary function, as indicated by their principal source of operating income, was to operate retail stores which handled general merchandise and farm supplies. One retail curb market was placed in this group because of its dissimilarity to the other groups in method of operation, and the wide variety of products handled.

Data for this study were secured on 341 incorporated and 33 unincorporated cooperative associations that had been organized during the 40-year period, 1889 to 1929, inclusive. Of this total of 374 associations, 195 had been organized to handle citrus fruits; 

97 for truck crops; 29 for livestock and livestock products; and

53 for miscellaneous products and services, including subsidiary corporations for credit and other purposes, Table II. 

One hundred ninety, or 50.8 percent, of these associations were active during the season 1929-30; 129, or 34.5 percent, had once operated but were then inactive, while 55, or 14.7 percent, had never been commercially active, Table III. 

The outstanding cause of failure as given by informants was lack of volume, followed in order by poor management, no need for cooperative, lack of cooperative spirit, insufficient capital, competition, and unsatisfactory prices, Table IV. 

More than one-fourth of all failures took place during the first year of operation, and two-thirds occurred within the first five years. Obviously, with increasing age and experience the chances of failure became materially less, Tables V and VI. 

Data were available showing the total net sales of 115 of the associations that were active during the season 1929-30, excluding associations that handled the same product a second or third time due to vertical integration. The total net sales of these associations was $23,075,931, of which the citrus associations accounted for $14,654,011, the truck crops associations $6,935,884, the associations handling livestock and livestock products $615,- 883, and the miscellaneous associations $870,153, Table VII. 

During the period 1926-27 to 1929-30, inclusive, the number of active local citrus cooperative associations increased from 61 to 

70, the membership from 4,791 to 6,692, the percentage of total state volume handled by local cooperative associations from 29.4 to 36.5, and the percentage of the total volume at stations where associations were located that was handled by these associations, from 47.9 to 54.2, Table VIII. 

The farm value of Florida truck crops is second only to that of citrus fruits. During the 1929-30 season the total net sales by truck crops cooperative associations was approximately onehalf that of the citrus associations. Most of the truck crops associations were small, but a few had total net sales larger than any of the local citrus associations, so the average net sales per association for the two groups was approximately equal. Celery, tomatoes, and potatoes, made up about three-fourths of the total carloads of truck crops sold by cooperatives during the period 

1926-27 to 1929-30, inclusive.

The truck crops cooperative associations handled larger quantities of fertilizers and supplies for their members than did the citrus associations, but rendered fewer services in connection with

processing and marketing the products. A large proportion of the truck crops are graded and packed in the field. 

The cooperative marketing of livestock and livestock products in Florida is relatively unimportant, but has recently shown some tendency to increase. Sound methods of financing, larger volume and more adequate accounting practices are essential to the development of this group of associations. 

The total assets of the local citrus cooperative associations increased quite regularly during the period 1927 to 1930, but the relationships between the various groups of assets did not change significantly. However, the relationships between the various groups of liabilities showed considerable change during this period. Due to loans from the Federal Farm Board, long-term borrowings in the fixed liabilities group increased almost 300 percent during the season 1929-30, and current liabilities were decreased similarly. The ratio of net worth to total liabilities showed no significant change from 1927 to 1930, but the current ratio increased from 1.7:1 to 3.6:1, Table XI. 

In general, the percentages of total assets represented by fixed and deferred assets increased with decreasing size of business, while the reverse was true of current assets and other assets. 

Also, in general, the percentages of total liabilities and net worth represented by current and fixed liabilities combined showed increases with decreasing size of business, while the percentages in other liabilities and net worth increased with increasing size of business. The current ratio, the percent members' equity, and the turnover of fixed assets reflect clearly the advantage of the large associations, Tables XII and XIII. 

For the years 1928-29 and 1929-30 the large associations had on the average larger percentage net incomes than the small associations. The smallest associations, although taking larger percent gross margins than the large associations, still did not take enough to cover operating costs, while the large associations with smaller percent gross margins were able to pay operating costs and make substantial profits, Tables XV and XVI. A similar situation was found to exist in the truck crops group, Tables 

XXXIV and XXXV. 

Costs of processing citrus fruit per box were influenced favorably by large volume of fruit, turnover of fixed assets larger than the average, investment in fixed assets per box less than the average, and by large volume of fruit per grower, Tables XX to 

XXIII, and Figs. 5 to 20, inclusive.

Pooling was practiced rather extensively by the citrus associations (Table XXIV). Little pooling was done by associations in the other groups, however, due largely to the difficulties of establishing and enforcing accurate grades and standards. 

Many of the citrus and truck crops cooperative associations were found to be rendering an important service for their members by aiding them in securing production credit from the Federal intermediate credit bank and commercial banks. Most of the citrus associations secured this credit through the medium of the Growers' Loan and Guaranty Company, a credit corporation affiliated with the Florida Citrus Exchange, while a number of the truck crops associations had organized subsidiary credit corporations for this purpose.
