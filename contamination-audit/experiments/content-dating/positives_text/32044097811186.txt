STUDY OF UNIFORM COMMERCIAL CODE LEGISLATIVE TECHNIQUES AND PROBLEMS IN THE CODE I. Introduction 

In its Report to the Legislature for 1955 the Commission discussed at length the "Problems Presented by the Code. As was there pointed out, the Code would not only revise or replace numerous older uniform commercial acts heretofore adopted in New York, which would be repealed if the Code were to be enacted, but would also "regulate many questions now governed only by decisional law." Within the sphere of commercial transactions the 

Code "would also modify the operation of many statutes of general application, such as the statute of frauds, statutes of limitation, procedural statutes or statutes limiting common law doctrines like the requirement of consideration for contracts, or the privilege of infants to disaffirm their contracts." Indeed, the Code is superimposed upon the general law, common and statutory, and this fact is recognized in Section 1-103 which states that, "unless displaced" by the Code, the principles of law and equity on various enumerated topics supplement the Code's provisions. The list of those specific topics in Section 1-103 might well have been much longer. 

Under these circumstances, a discussion of legislative techniques and problems would seem to call for an inquiry as to how much of existing law has been "displaced" by the Code. In other words, it is proposed to explore the relationship between the Code and rules of law (both common and statutory) outside the Code with a view to determine the possibilities and problems of their co-existence. 

A second major item of the Code's legislative technique is its combination in one comprehensive and bulky piece of legislation of subjects heretofore covered by numerous separate statutes and by decisional law. It is true that many of the separate statutes survive in modified form as separate Articles of the Code; yet, these articles, preceded and followed by the general provisions of Articles 1 and 10, were designed as parts of a single enactment. (See Comment to the Title: "The concept of the present Act is that 'commercial transactions' is a single subject of the law, notwithstanding its many facets. A single transaction may very well involve a contract for sale, followed by a sale, the giving of a check or draft for a part of the purchase price, and the acceptance of some form of security for the balance. The check or draft may be negotiated and will ultimately pass through one or more banks for collection. If the goods are shipped or stored, the subject matter of the sale may be covered by a bill of lading or warehouse receipt or both. Or it may be that the entire transaction was made pur- ? 

(2115)

[8] suant to a letter of credit, either domestic or foreign. . . .") Hence, it will be necessary to inquire as to whether a Code consisting of separate articles for separate topics has been integrated into a coherent entity, or whether there are inconsistencies between the various articles which may or may not be reconcilable with the idea of one code.

Finally, some observations as to style, terminology and drafting techniques used in the Code may be appropriate. 

II. Fitting the Code into Existing Law 

As mentioned above, the Code necessarily refers in many places to general rules of law which exist outside of and are independent from the Code. Sometimes, these rules are taken for granted without any specific reference, the general reference of Section 1-103 being deemed sufficient to provide for the application of such rules as subsidiary and supplementary to the Code provisions. 

In other instances, the rules outside the Code may be specifically invoked as the test of operation of a Code section. The Code may also contain a specific statement of a particular application of a general rule, which may or may not be consistent with the present law of a particular jurisdiction. In some instances the Code appears to assume as background a rule which prevails in some states but not in others; thus, reference to the general law outside the Code may limit attainment of the goal of uniformity, because the law to which reference is being made may not be uniform in the first place.

These comments, in order to be meaningful, require concrete illustrations: 

(1) References to procedural rules 

(a) Section 3-307 (1) provides that

Unless specifically denied in the pleadings each signature on an instrument is admitted. 

In this instance the Code states a rule of pleading, a subject not mentioned in Section 1-103. In fact, we have here a provision requiring specific pleadings in order to raise the issue of forgery on an instrument. The Comment states that a specific denial is required in order "to give the plaintiff notice that he must meet a claim of forgery or lack of authority as to the particular signature, and to afford him an opportunity to investigate and obtain evidence." It is noted that "similar provisions are found in a number of states." 

As far as New York is concerned, the rule of pleading is to the contrary, both generally and with respect to the specific case of a plea of forgery of an instrument. According to the leading treatise, 'the general rule is that under a general denial the defendant may controvert by evidence anything which the plaintiff is bound to prove in the first instance to make out his cause of action;' and 991

Presumably, the authors of the Code felt that uniformity on this point was desirable in conjunction with uniformity of substantive law rules; otherwise this rule of pleading might well have been omitted. (See Comment to Section 6-104.) (c) Section 3-122 may also be mentioned as an illustration of the problem of coordinating statute of limitations provisions in the Code with existing statutes. That section specifies when a cause of action accrues against a maker, acceptor, obligor, drawer or indorser of a negotiable instrument. The concept of "accrual" of a cause of action is, of course, essential for the administration of statutes of limitation as well as for other purposes, such as prematurity of action and computation of interest. Indeed, Section 11 of the Civil Practice Act explicitly states that the periods of limitation prescribed by this article, except as otherwise specially prescribed therein, must be computed from the time of the accruing of the right to relief by action, special proceeding, defense or otherwise, as the case requires, to the time when the claim to that relief is actually interposed by the party as a plaintiff or a defendant in the particular action or special proceeding.

Since Section 3-122 does not state a period of limitations, the Civil Practice Act provisions as to period will apply, and Section 11 will require computation of the period from the "accrual" time specified in Section 3-122 except when another provision of the Civil Practice Act applies to postpone or accelerate the commencement of the period. Such other provision does now exist in the Civil Practice Act. Thus, while Section 3-122 postpones accrual of the cause of action until demand, in certain cases, Civil Practice Act, § 15 operates in some of these cases to require that the period of limitation be computed from the earlier time "when the right to make the demand is complete." Complete uniformity in computation of the statute of limitations on commercial paper will exist only among those states whose rules are similar on the question governed in New York by Civil Practice Act, 15. In summary, the legislative technique employed by the Code in this instance is as follows:

As to Article 2 (replacing the Uniform Sales Act), the Code replaces present language by the "severability without material harm" test, for the avowed purpose of establishing a uniform criterion as to what chattels affixed to realty are subject to the Sales Article. The Code thus declares its independence from local property law.

As to Article 9 (replacing the Uniform Trusts Receipts and 

Conditional Sales Acts) the Code discards the "material injury" test and the definition of "goods" now to be found in the Uniform Conditional Sales Act and replaces these with language leaving it to local property law to determine under what circumstances a chattel loses its identity by becoming a part of the realty.48 

These two examples epitomize a basic problem of legislative technique. Where the Code requires references to the law outside of its scope of operations,--such as, in this instance, the law of fixtures,--the authors of the Code had to decide how these outside rules and concepts were to be treated in the Code. On the one hand, there was the possibility of invading the "outside" field and declaring in the Code what the guiding principle should be; this would be most appropriate whenever it was felt that the need for uniform treatment was imperative. On the other hand, the codifiers may decide to leave the particular matter entirely to the "outside" law, thus abandoning the goal of uniformity with respect to the particular point or points; if that method is to be chosen, the further question arises as to whether the reference to the local law should be implied, or spelled out expressly. The treatment of "fixtures", discussed above, shows the application of both techniques in different articles dealing with closely related problems. STUDY OF UNIFORM COMMERCIAL CODE THE DEVICE OF AN INTERSTATE COMPACT IN RELATION TO THE UNIFORM COMMERCIAL CODE 

A suggestion has been advanced that the Uniform Commercial Code might be incorporated in an interstate compact rather than in a uniform state law. The suggestion raises a number of possibilities and problems, from a discussion of which the advantages and disadvantages of the compact device as applied to the Code may be inferred.

The interstate compact is derived from Article 1, Section 10 

of the Constitution: "No state shall, without the consent of Congress enter into any agreement or compact with another state, or with a foreign power "The significance of this authority, negatively stated, is clarified by contrast with a preceding clause of the same section : "No state shall enter into any treaty, alliance, or confederation.

Obviously a compact or agreement is to be differentiated from a treaty, alliance, or confederation. The distinction generally taken is that in this context a treaty deals with matters of basic political concern, such as a treaty of war or peace, or for the raising of troops, whereas a compact or agreement deals with matters of less fundamental political import. It is clear that the Code in the form of a compact would not run afoul of the prohibition on treaties by any state. 

The Code would mark an unusual employment of the compact device.2 Traditionally the compact has been utilized on a regional or sectional basis. Moreover, compacts have generally been addressed to matters of continuing administrative concern, for which some central administrative machinery is established. The subject matter has ordinarily been in such fields as natural resources, or safety measures, or transportation, all of which involve active governmental regulation or operation. Intrinsically, however, there seems to be no reason why the compact would not be legitimate in the realm of private law. This would be all the more likely if the compact provided for a joint body with continuing

1. The coming into force of the compact. There would presumably be a provision in the compact itself determining when it would become operative. Such a provision might refer to approval by a specific number of states, or it might refer to approval by certain designated states, as a prerequisite to the effectiveness of the compact in a state which adopts it. 

2. The consent of Congress. The constitutional provision requires Congressional consent. Certain dicta in Supreme Court opinions have indicated that for some kinds of compacts or agreements the consent of Congress would not be necessary. For example, an emergency measure for mutual inspection of cattle at a border would hardly require Congressional consent. Nor would an agreement whereby goods owned by one state were to be stored in a warehouse owned by another state. These emergency measures or normal commercial contracts, though in a sense agreements between states, would scarcely be covered by the constitutional provision. On the other hand, certain subjects would probably require the consent of Congress to legitimize any state action in the field. Thus legislation regulating interstate or foreign commerce might be invalid on the part of the several states except for the permission contained in the Congressional consent to a compact. 

The case of the Commercial Code falls somewhere in between. 

It is not a temporary or normal proprietary measure, and on the other hand, it is not the regulation of a field reserved to Congress under the Constitution. It might be argued that since the subject matter would be embodied in reciprocal legislation, it may equally be embodied in a compact or agreement without the consent of Congress. The issue is in part one of terminology. It would doubtless be possible to achieve certain of the same results by means of reciprocal legislation, which might be dependent on coming into force upon adoption by certain other states. But it is assumed that the suggestion of a compact implies certain special features flowing from Congressional consent, particularly in making the interpretation of the compact a Federal question. Hence it will be assumed that Congressional consent is involved in the proposal.

The effect of Congressional consent in the case of a compact is not necessarily to make the compact a law of the United States. The logic of the situation would require the state courts to follow as authoritative the decisions of the United States Supreme Court. Thus the philosophy of Erie Ry. v. Tompkins would be reversed, and the state courts would be obliged to follow the decisions of the district courts rendered somewhat fortuitously in the diversity jurisdiction.

This somewhat anomalous result might possibly be avoided by regarding the interpretation of the compact as a Federal question only in the sense that the Supreme Court would review and reverse a patently erroneous interpretation, as the Court will review decisions of state law to a limited extent where these underlie Federal rights. This possible outcome serves to point up the dilemma. Either the virtue of uniformity is achieved through the binding decisions of the Federal courts, leaving little scope for state judicial interpretation in a field which has been peculiarly the concern of state courts, or a measure of authority is retained by the state courts at the cost of uniformity of judicial interpretation. 

What has been said regarding the jurisdiction of the Federal courts is based on the existing law of jurisdiction. It would be open to Congress, in consenting to a compact or by independent legislation, to alter the jurisdictional pattern. This might be done by opening up the Federal district courts to cases raising a question under the Code as embodied in the compact, or on the other hand by withdrawing all such cases even from the diversity jurisdiction of the district courts.
