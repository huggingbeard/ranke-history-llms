PREFACE 

Another book on dog training? That possibly may be the first question of the superficial observer, considering that so many works of this class have been placed before the public in recent years. 

And yet, here is a book that for thoroughness of method, lucidity cf diction and a general background of abundant experience on the part of the author is distinctly different. Its chapters convey the impression that old country methods and modern American ideas have been combined in making this a training book that should be valuable not only to the amateur novice, but for the most sophisticated professional as well.

Its author, Señor Sanchez Antuñano, is a resident of Yucatan, Mexico, where he has had a wide experience with bird dogs and the general hunting conditions of that country, but his knowledge of the setter and the pointer is cosmopolitan, for it has been acquired in all parts of Europe and America, where he has not only shot over dogs, but has also attended numerous field trials. His observations as set down in these chapters are accordingly the concentrated convictions of first hand knowledge gleaned through his years of activity devoted to the bird dog and his training in an amateur way.

The various phases of dog education are set forth in a clear and thorough manner, not in the abstract, generalized forms so common in many books on training. I have only to call attention to his observations on "scent" to make this point clear, but there are many other chapters which indicate that a new note has been struck in elucidating the various methods of bird dog training. 3

INTRODUCTION 

Unfamiliar with the art of writing, I submit these lines in all modesty, inspired only by the attachment which I have always had for good dogs, especially for field dogs, as well as for the fact that in our country (Mexico), where such a variety of game abounds, no writer has as yet concerned himself with the matter of dogs, whereas, outside of Mexico many works have been written on the subject. Some of these are good, others indifferent, but with keenness on the part of the enthusiast for canine zootechnics, or the noble sport of hunting (shooting).

It is true that for the sum of between one hundred to five hundred dollars, a good dog can be bought abroad, but this is not within the means of all my fellow-sportsmen. For this reason I believe that a book written to assist such sportsmen in the interesting work of training their dogs will be the role of a friend in need and prove very useful, and I trust will be hospitably accepted, even if only because it is the first of its kind appearing in our country (Mexico). 

The opinions of dog trainers vary considerably, recommending kindness and rewards exclusively; some are open partisans of the whip; others favor the spike collar; others, again, recommend both. I do not deny that all these methods give results when the trainer is efficient but, without any desire to appear presumptuous, I believe that my methods, the fruition of thirty years' experience, of constant study of the works of authorities on the subject, of assisting at field trials in the United States and in Europe, of attendance at dog shows at different places, and observing prominent dog kennels on both some

Nevertheless, after closely following their methods, I venture to state that my system is practical and perhaps more rapid and sounder than some others. 

Not only is it useful for the sportsman who trains but one or two dogs, but also for the professional who has many dogs in hand and whom it, of course, suits to complete the training of each as soon as possible. 

Furthermore, a dog trained under my system can be handled by any one familiar with my methods, even though he neither owns him nor has known him before. The essence of my method is to encourage the dog's natural aptness and avail myself of this during training, for which I employ punishment, associated with the command, in order to obtain immediate obedience. 

I at first employ force and persistence to obtain my ends, gradually easing up on the former as the dog inclines to obey. I am in the habit of employing the spike collar during the course of training, and occasionally I use, to lash lightly, the loose end of the check cord, at other times a whip.

INTRODUCTION 

In my estimation the spike collar is far superior to a whip in some cases, because its effect is felt at the precise moment that the dog attempts disobedience, whereas the whip, as a rule, comes into play after the fault has occurred, i. e., to teach the dog to come to you, the check cord and spike collar being attached, they can be brought into play the very moment that the dog attempts disobedience, and the strength with which they are used will be in proportion to the amount of stubbornness and force employed by the dog in disobeying. The vigor of using these implements ceases just as quickly as the dog complies with the trainer's wishes, while the use of the whip is sudden and employed after the dog has committed a fault and surrendered to the trainer's will, and for this very reason may be easily misinterpreted. 

The training of a gun dog during "Yard Training," under my system, does not exceed three weeks in duration and I have even had pupils which I have broken in in a week.

Timid dogs will even become bold and useful, though some authorities do not appear to think so and condemn the spike collar as an instrument of torture. I refute this and maintain that the most nervous of dogs will become bold under its proper use. 

The cruelty is not in the instrument itself, but precisely in the way it is used. 

Another factor in its favor is that the dog never forgets what he learns, so that should the training be intermittent or interrupted, he will not, for that reason, fail to remember what he has already learned and his training can be resumed at any time later. Of course, should the dog lack practice he will naturally be more or less dull, but nevertheless anxious to obey. 

I may also say that the dog trained to retrieve under this system will do so whenever so ordered. 

My method is divided as follows: Preliminaries; 

yard training, which is suitable for feld trial work or for shooting dogs, whether spaniels or retrievers: retrieving, which is only for use when out shooting and in some cases for field trials, including spaniels, retrievers and all dogs used in retrieving; lessons in the field, which are used during field trials and when shooting: lessons applicable to the shooting dog (gun dog), including all pointing dogs, and some of them also useful for retrievers and spaniels; shooting with spaniels and retrievers, their use being indicated by their names; bad habits and how to check them.

As pointing dogs I believe the pointer and setter are unparalleled. They are delicate of scent, ardent searchers, steady in their work, of good stamina and stanch on point and in the majority of trials superior to the first mentioned breeds. There are, of course, temperamental differences between pointers and setters. According to some trainers, the setter is harder to train for the man who is not an expert trainer, as they credit this breed of dog with being more fearless and independent, but they maintain that in expert hands he may be made more of than the pointer. In the setter's favor they state that he is more energetic, a better backer by instinct, more suitable for cold climates and for thorny or swampy lands. On the other hand, the pointer has a more solid pointing stance, and this is developed in the latter sooner than in the setter. He is good also in hot and dry climates, where he seems to have a better sense of scent. I personally think this is more a question of individual and family than of breed. What is certainly . true is that both breeds require water when working on a hot day. Some sportsmen clip their setters' coats short to avoid having thorns, etc., sticking to them.

It may be noted that the setters, as well as all longhaired dogs, lose much of their hair in hot climates and recover it again when taken to a cold climate. 

There are three principal breeds of setters-English (Laverack, Llewellin, etc.), Irish and Gordon, the first named being the most popular, having better qualities in general and particularly for field trials. Next in favor comes the Irish and lastly the Gordon setter.

Among pointers and setters you get dogs capable of serving any purpose. I have had setters which resisted the hot Yucatan climate, as also pointers which worked splendidly in swampy grounds. They are the more suitable breed of dogs for our fields, especially for those not too plentiful of game, for their wide working range gives them better opportunities of meeting with success.

The other breeds of pointing dogs are slower, heavier and more sensitive to fatigue, for which reasons I find them not so suitable for our conditions, especially as on the coast they feel the heat, and higher inland they suffer from the dry altitude. In damp climates they are at their best. 

You will find among both pointers and setters, even of the same family and litter, dogs of short and wide

working range. This should be remembered, so that the shorter range dog can be selected for bushy country and the wider dog for open fields. The middle-sized dog is preferable. The large dog is generally clumsy and lacking in nimbleness, while the small dog is weak and cannot retrieve heavy game.

The pointer in France has been admirably well bred, combining good field qualities with good frame. Some of the black coloring, although not having as rapid or as wide a working range as the others, their pointing, scenting and docility qualities are worthy of mention. 

Among these I have in mind are such kennels as those of R. Dommanget, president of the Pointer Club of 

France, Dr. Menciere, Herbelin and others. 

The Belgians have magnificent Irish setters, which is not remarkable, as they are careful breeders of the 

English breeds, as well as of their own. At the Crystal 

Palace Exhibition, near London, I saw magnificent dogs of all breeds. I especially noticed in the gun-dog class spaniels and retrievers were in the majority and this was probably due to the growth in favor of large drive shoots where these breeds of dogs take important places. Of the pointers and setters which I saw, it seemed to me that the latter were better developed than the former, but different from the old type. 

In Germany I saw the German pointing dog, the favorite in that country for all conditions and situations. He points and retrieves small game and he also serves for following the wounded stag or deer, or retrieving from water. There is no doubt that he is an ideal dog for Germany, but probably not suitable for every one desiring a short-range dog or an allaround dog for dry and arid countries where game is scarce.

In Spain the famous Spanish pointer hardly exists. 

I shot over different parts of the country and never had the fortune of seeing a pure-bred one. I also shot with Señor Miguel Seco, whose pointers were taken over from Puebla, Mexico, descendants of Rowdy and

Felck, and trained by me. They gave good results. 

For many years, in the United States of America, the setter was more popular than the pointer. The braque generally is not much in vogue and it is only within the last few years that sportsmen in that country use the griffon. French braques, Pleus d'Aubergne, braques St. Germain, braques du Bourbonnais, German braques, Italian braques, Boulet griffons, French and 

Italian spaniels, etc., are popular among Frenchmen, Italians, Belgians, Swiss and Germans, but American

sportsmen are decidedly little in favor of these breeds of dogs, for I understand they consider them of not much use, due to their manner of working the kind of game, and to the topography of the shooting grounds. Nevertheless, for sometime some American breeders are now raising this kind of dogs. Griffons and braques, as is known, are short-range working dogs, therefore useful for the sportsman who shoots over small or rough ground, but not suitable for the great western prairies of the United States or where quail is scarce, which, of course, require dogs which have an extensive ranging faculty, resistance and much "bird sense." They would, of course, be very useful for ruffed grouse, woodcock and snipe, which require detailed close ranging. Furthermore, many of these dogs retrieve excellently.

American field trial pointers and setters have unquestionably more endurance and wider ranging faculties than their European kin; on the other hand, the latter are handsomer and larger.

For many years, in the United States, the English setter and Llewellin were preferred for field trial work and generally defeated the pointer, but recently the pointers have commenced coming in to their own more and more each day. It seems that, by skillful selection, pointers have acquired all the qualifications required for field trial work in the United States. 

Taking that into consideration I am of the opinion that the trained dogs which most readily suit our conditions in Mexico, due to the similarity of quail and shooting conditions, are American pointers and setters.

My opinion, born of careful observation, with respect to the tendencies or keenness of the pointer and setter in relation to the shooting of furred or feathered game, is as follows: The setter shows greater enthusiasm when after feathered rather than furred game, whereas, the pointer, though a keen hunter of feathered game, seems to show a greater inclination for furred game than does the setter. This can be explained, it seems to me, by the fact that the setter is descended from the spaniel and the latter was always specially intended for feathered game, whereas, the pointer, at a certain epoch, was crossed with the hound, which would of course answer for his inclinations, though some of the pointer family, espe Icially the black pointer, have not been crossed this way. Some are of opinion that the short-haired pointing dog is descended from the hound, and that by skillful selection among these with the pointing in-

stincts there resulted a short-haired pointing dog from which all these breeds of dogs in favor today have originated.

For the trainer who is new to the work I recommend the pointer, for should he get one of good pedigree and have him frequently on good shooting grounds, he will quickly become "classy."

The Irish setter is a fine dog, energetic and intelligent and very useful in the shooting field when well managed, but he cannot compete with the pointer and English setter in the field trial work. The Gordon setter less still, but like the Irish setter, he is a good gun dog. Both Gordon and Irish, though slower than the others just mentioned, are superior to braques and spaniels. 

The cross between the pointer and setter is what the English call the "dropper" and bears a resemblance to both types. As workers they are sometimes quite good, but they are considered difficult to train and worthless for breeding purposes. Of all the mixed breeds this is probably the best, but, of course, homogeneity in the breed is preferred.

W. Arkwright, in his book on the crossing of the pointer breed, page 185, says as follows (The Sportsman, 1836, volume 3, number 4, page 185): "Dogs known among sportsmen as cross breeds (mixing different breeds of dogs) are despised by us. Nevertheless, an extraordinary good dog may result from this (though even one in ten thousand), as has been proved by Dash, the property of the late Colonel Thornton, and is the most celebrated specimen resulting from such cross breeding. Dash is the offspring of a cross between a foxhound and a pointer bitch of good pedigree. He has become celebrated owing to the manner of working in the moors and for his superior ability in finding game. He is equally excellent at working for partridge as at backing point stanchly when working with other dogs. This dog was sold to the late Sir Richard Symons for the equivalent of one hundred and sixty pounds sterling in the following form: Champagne and burgundy (the outcome of a sale by the French Ambassador), one hogshead, 238 liters of claret, a beautiful gun and a pointer. It was also stipulated, in the transaction, that if Dash, through any accident, became useless as a gun dog, he should be returned to the Colonel, who would pay fifty guineas for him. Dash injured a paw and was returned to Colonel Thornton, who paid the fifty guineas and retained the dog for breeding pur-

Owing to the fame Dash acquired, English breeders of dogs crossed their pointers with foxhounds, for which reason Mr. Arkwright expresses so much regret. 

I believe that with the foregoing remarks the enthusiast will have enough information to guide him in making a selection. I would merely add, however, that he should buy a puppy of over six months of age, to avoid much trouble and waiting less time for his training.

If he merely wishes a dog for the purpose of a "springer," I recommend cockers and such like, and for retrieving merely he should get the retriever. 

For water I recommend the Chesapeake Bay Dog or the water spaniel. 

The cocker is a very companionable dog and very useful when shooting in brushy grounds. He is very daring and keen. He does not point, but at times will bark when near game, which helps nicely to start the game to wing. His training is comparatively simple.

Retrievers, as explained by that name, are specially intended for retrieving dead or wounded game, which they do wonderfully well, especially those from Labrador. They are particularly serviceable for drive shoots or for places where, as in England, the pointing dog is merely intended for pointing; or for anybody merely requiring a dog to retrieve only. 

The water spaniel is the dog which excels for duck shooting, as does the Chesapeake Bay Dog.

When selecting the breed of dog required it must be remembered that there are two classes-dogs for exhibition purposes and shooting or game dogs. In the former, appearance and shape are required first and foremost. In the latter, abilities in the special line of work in which the dog will be employed, either for field trials or for shooting. For this, the dog's pedigree must be taken into account. He must be pure bred, so as to get his predecessor's abilities, which will be inherited in the pure-bred dog, that is, hunting instincts, energy and above all a fine sense of smell and stanchness on point, indispensable in the pointing dog.

It is true that there are good dogs among crossbreds, but this is not the general rule and you must take into consideration the question of likelihoods, for

the puppy does not show his possibilities until fully grown.

It is advisable to take experts' advice, because buying from the exhibition bench is not sufficient guarantee. Of course, when buying a trained dog, which is not intended to use for breeding purposes, one may then leave aside the question of pedigree. 

Remember that there are many good dogs, which will not necessarily transmit their good qualities to their progeny, whereas, others, not so good, will prove good transmitters of ancestral and other qualities. It is superfluous to say that among the last named, the following qualities of transmission are of paramount importance: Nervous energy, scent, pointing, and inclination, for it is harder to train puppies of whimsical temperament. And yet, this is not all, for the pedigree (which may be a false one) is not necessarily sufficient guarantee. The vendor's integrity must be considered. 

If the enthusiast owns a dog of good pedigree and wishes to use him for breeding purposes, he should seek the advice of a competent person with regard to the bitch or dog most suitable for the purpose, for zootechnics is a science, which, has hard and fast lines of logic that cannot be scoffed at with impunity. 

Much has been written on the matter of the influence of a previous sire, or the influence on the progeny of subsequent sires derived from the first impregnation of the female. I knew of a setter bitch which was served by a tailless dog, born that way, and among the litter from this cross there were puppies born without tails. Later the same bitch was served by a pure-bred setter and in the litter there again appeared tailless puppies resembling the first dog. In spite of this, I refrain from giving an emphatic opinion. 

A bitch should only be entrusted to reliable hands.

For should she not be served in the owner's presence, she may be put to the dog once only to avoid fatiguing the latter, or else, in order to safeguard the dog's reputation as a breeder, when he is perhaps already beyond value, the bitch is given to a second dog after having been served by the one selected, with the consequent result that you are given a false pedigree.

The right time for having the bitch served is the eighth day after she has come in heat, and the precise moment can be judged by the inflammation, and because, in the dog's presence, she will move her tail to

Fifteen days after she is served, she can be given mild exercise, up to two weeks before the time the litter is expected, reckoning sixty to sixtyfour days from the time she was served and during the last few days the exercise should be very moderate to avoid causing her trouble with her litter. 

The good-tailed puppy is more easily judged when just born than after having developed somewhat. The males look more like the mother and the bitches resemble the father, which, when picking one, you should bear in mind.

PRACTICAL EDUCATION DOGS FOR FIELD TRIALS 

Field trials mean, as the name implies, tests in the fields, and this name has been adopted in all languages, which is the reason I have so often used it in this treatise. They took place originally in England about the year 1865, for pointers and setters, and dog shows as well were originated in the same country a few years previously. These shows were very popular and at that time nobody showed a dog which could not work well in the field and, much less still, if untrained. It was a sight worth seeing at these shows, splendid specimens of the best breeds, not only beautiful to watch, but perfectly trained. These dog shows had, as an object, the bringing together of the best dogs, in order to study their respective qualities with a view of improving the breeds. 

Later on, certain unscrupulous persons, seeing the possibility of making money out of these so-called sporting dog shows, created the bench show dog, without consideration of his working ability. 

The pointer was crossed with other breeds of dogs and this alliance with other dogs (among them the foxhound), perhaps intended to give a more robust constitution with which to stand the strains of the show bench. As a result today we have everywhere all sorts of breeds intended for field trials and for the show bench, some even combining both purposes, which, in my opinion, should be the aim of all breeders, for I cannot conceive such a thing as a pointer or a setter which has not the necessary qualifications to make a shooting dog, even though the dog is a perfect specimen of his breed in everything else. This also took place with the other breeds of dogs. 

We have seen, therefore, that today there are pointers and setters intended for the show bench which have not the qualifications for field dogs as well, but are merely handsome specimens. On the other hand, we have dogs intended for field trials whose qualifications are efficiency in the field and not beauty, and some again, though not many, which combine both features.

In Europe, especially in France and Belgium, this division is made: Field trial work on the English lines (ranging widely) and on the French lines (ranging for the gun). The English style is carried out by having a brace of dogs working at once. Each trainer takes his dog on a leash and both judges and trainers go on foot. The "course," to be selected by the judges,

once decided upon, both dogs are loosened at the same time, face to the wind, and it is managed in such a way that one swings toward the left and the other toward the right, so that theoretically they should cross each other in their beat.

It is indispensable, during field trials on English lines that the dogs work quartering, but always well in hand and never so extensively as in field trials in the United States.

The regulations and the judges' powers are very strict. They lay great stress on the training and seriously penalize the dog which is not perfectly obedient and has not a stanch point. The dog must also be steady at the flight of the game, be it feathered or furred, and the dog should also back his companion's point stanchly, though these are generally taught to lie down to the point, as well as to the detonation and at the flight of the game. Great importance is also given to ranging by quartering. A dog which chases feathered or furred game is liable to be disqualified there and then.

Field trials on the French system, that is, ranging for the guns, is done by one dog, which is made to quarter ahead of his trainer, but keeping near him, and in the manner shooting is generally carried out in France in flat country, where, as a rule, several guns are spread out in line abreast, each with his dog ahead, for which reason the dog must search straight ahead, quartering the ground immediately in front of his trainer, without giving any attention to the other guns or dogs.

The rules on these occasions are not so stringent with regard to chasing the game and breaking at shot, for it does not constitute an error, provided always that he stops at the command of his trainer, or returns if called to do so, when chasing game. The point must be stanch and obedience the bye word. These dogs are made to retrieve, though under English field trial conditions such is not necessary. After a point, in both these systems of field trials, a revolver or a gun is fired in the air.

The object of the English system of field trials is to facilitate the selection by test of qualification, so that breeders may use their judgment in finding the dog most suitable to reproduce future field trial winners and select many dogs which will be first class for shooting conditions.

These field trials are also intended to foster the hunting spirit or shooting with dogs of first-class

PRACTICAL EDUCATION 

hunting qualities, as well as to further develop and provide useful information to amateurs and professional trainers.

Field trials in the French way have as an object the same purposes, but taking their condition into account they also serve to give any breed of pointing dogs the opportunity of showing their field abilities, even though they may not be wide ranging, and also to give prize show dogs the chance of showing their usefulness in the field.

I may also say that field trials are held for braques, spaniels and retrievers, in which each class is given the opportunity of showing his particular qualities. 

In Germany the pointing dog is given a more extensive and difficult training than in any other country, for not only is he expected to seek and point feathered and furred game and retrieve from land or water, but to follow and kill harmful animals, such as the fox, etc., follow the trail of the wounded stag or deer, and once he comes upon him to give signs to his master by barking. Some are taught not to abandon the dead animals, although called by shouts or shots, while another must abandon the animal, go in search of his master and lead him back to it. He is taught to defend and take care of the dead game or any other object left in his charge, as well as to perform sundry duties which in other countries are not considered necessary for the pointing dog. This is the reason why the different kinds of German braques, either short or long-haired or Griffons, are so highly prized in Germany. These dogs are taught to bark to order, during training.

In the English field trials the dog is required, above all, to have a special constitution, which will allow him to run as hard as possible during the whole process of the trial of each pair, which generally lasts twenty to forty minutes; to have a very fine sense of smell, so that, notwithstanding the speed at which he is working, he can scent easily with a high head, for a dog working with head down cannot scent readily if going fast. The trainer must teach him to draw on the swiftly running game, for partridge will run along the ground. He must instantly obey any order from the trainer, be it either to go anywhere or to lie down by signal or whistle, as well as to come up, and on no account must he show jealousy of a companion's point.

It is not necessary, as already said, that the dog should retrieve, so it is not essential to teach them

that. Many experts declare that a dog which retrieves also is at a disadvantage at English field trials and the dog which looks for highest honors there must be a specialist. As the European field trials are generally held in the Spring, by which time the partridges are mating, it will not be necessary for the dogs to range for singles, but principally to hunt rapidly for couples of birds and for hares.

For this kind of work the trainer will have to get the dog into good shape. A dog which is not fit never works efficiently. The dog should be trained in such a way as to develop, as far as possible, his natural ability without killing his initiative. In these trials all competitors should, above all else, show snappiness, energy and hunting enthusiasm. The dog should, after "yard training," be given "field training," and have his potential hunting instincts aroused, after which he should be taught ranging by quartering. It is a pity that in Europe the trainer does not teach his dog from horseback, for in this way it is easier to get him to extend his hunting range. Once he knows well how to quarter, he should do this in company with another good quartering dog and have his companions frequently changed, so as to accustom him to work together with any dog. Afterwards take him alone to a field where you will find game, and there make him quarter and point, and with the specific lessons relating to pointing, he will learn to point stanchly, and also to stay steady at the flight of game and at the shot. Only when he has learned all of the foregoing should he have a companion which is not his superior, in order that he may not lose his initiative and stanch point, and so he may learn to work in company with other dogs.

Remember that the trained dog should be in charge of another trainer, so that you may give your full attention to your pupil. The lessons should not be long, for it is a well-known fact that a tired dog shortens his searching range and loses interest and style. This last should be taken into consideration for exhibition purposes, for which reason this should be well regulated.

The aforesaid is what every dog to work in the English-style of field trials in France has to know. 

A dog intended for the French form of field trials should also be in sound health and good condition.

His search will not be so wide as in the English style, as he will be required to work nearer to his master and alone. He will not be required to back, but most of

As these trials take place in the Spring and in the month of August, during the closed season, the French are in the habit of firing shots in the air, as in other field trials, in order to test the steadiness of competitors. Retrieving is done in another field with a recently killed rabbit or some such animal, and the dogs are expected to retrieve live but tied game.

During these trials it is a frequent sight to see dogs refuse to retrieve and nothing will persuade them to do so, because they have not been taught to retrieve game in that form. Such a thing will not occur with any dog which has been taught to retrieve under the force system. I will never forget how we were amused by seeing a famous pointer, which worked beautifully in the field, but which, upon being ordered to retrieve a live and tied bird, went up to it, sniffed at it and showed further contempt by adding even more ungentlemanly insults to the poor game, after which, Trak, as the dog was called, returned to his horrified master feeling full of dignity and satisfaction. I repeat that a dog trained to retrieve under the force system would not disobey as just mentioned, if he is in the habit of obeying and retrieving anything he is ordered to.

These dogs should be given a thorough lesson on "yard training" and it is advisable to give them "lessons applicable to the shooting-dog" as far as surrounding circumstances permit. There are also, in Europe, trials at which the same trainer works a pair of dogs simultaneously. I may say that the trainers who can do this are only a few, for in order to do so several conditions are necessary. Both dogs must be of the same temperament, must have the same way of searching, the same speed, must know how to back, must not be jealous and must be steady and stanch. If it is difficult to get a pair of horses which are well matched, it is even more difficult to get a pair of dogs which are alike, especially as the former work by the combination of training and the reins, whereas the latter only from the reins of training. Of course, there are many persons who work a couple of dogs simultaneously, but very few who will exhibit a pair of dogs at a field trial, which can be called presentable and worthy of such an event.

On the supposition that you have a brace of dogs

good enough to enter for field trial work, the following is the form in which they should be handled: Once on the "course" where the trial will take place, order them "down," and when ordered "go on" they should go one towards the left and the other toward the right, so that in their search by quartering, they will cross each other in front of the trainer, who will walk as straight ahead as possible in the direction required.

Thus his course will be the center of the ranging, in order that the search be well executed, and in this way, by making them work continually together on the quartering system, they will do the same thing in the field. They should also be taken out hunting together, so that they will get to know each other's methods and have mutual confidence. It is indispensable that they have a stanch point, because any dog that is not sure cannot be backed by his companion on a point in the proper way. Whenever one of the dogs hesitates, so as to get the wind or to ascertain the position of the game, the other should stop also, and they should work together until finding the game. 

If one of them should point only momentarily and immediately renew the search, the other should do the same, stopping and renewing simultaneously. Should one of them assume a firm point the other should back stanchly, and at the flush of the game, or at the shot, both dogs should drop to a lying position.

In order that the trainer may get the dogs to work in the aforesaid manner satisfactorily (by no means easy, if not impossible) he must exercise much patience and energy. He should have a good shooting field, where there is ample game, so that by reason of progressive experience the dogs will gain useful knowledge and mutual confidence. Jealousy will have disappeared entirely and blind obedience will prevail. These points once mastered, the trainer will have a feeling of great satisfaction and content, because he will have reached the sportsman's highest ambition, that of being able to present a couple of dogs working in perfect harmony.

The first field trial in the United States was run in 1874. The American field trial dog is the real type of dog which is taught to work by his own proper initiative and to use his full faculties to the limit, helped and developed by skilful training for that purpose. It is superfluous to add that his faculties must be perfect, especially as his powers are fully exercised, owing to the nature of the country and heavier work, especially so in the trials lasting over an hour.

The qualities most necessary are "bird sense" and rapidity in finding and pointing bevies and coveys. 

It does not matter so much what form of ranging the dog uses, provided he runs hard and finds the bevies of quail or coveys of prairie fowl.

As the trainer and judges generally ride on horseback (as do the spectators), it will be appreciated that however rapidly the dog works judges and trainers will be near him all the time. The dog realizes that it is his duty to find the game and he starts off at full speed with this object in view, judging his direction merely by a quick survey of the surrounding country in order to go to the most likely places, guided also by the hour and class of game he is after. Once he finds game, he points stanchly, waiting for his handler to flush the game. Steadiness at the flight of the game and at the shot is essential, as well as while backing, though not the case in retrieving, which is taught the dog and is not a natural instinct. 

Looking for scattered birds is necessary and the dog which can quarter well will show the best results.

During field trials a dog should keep to his work, without returning to his handler until ordered. He should use good judgment in going over the ground, that is to say, going from place to place, in the most likely spots where game is apt to shelter or be found, locating it with determination and precision once he gets the scent, pointing intelligently and stanchly.

The most suitable lessons for ranging are instructions in "bird sense" and some lessons related to "yard training," without necessarily teaching him to retrieve, and that part of “field training" which is suitable for all pointing dogs.

When trying to get range development and rapidity the trainer should select the time and day in which the scent is good, for the scent will make a dog range wider and faster than when there is no scent to lend him enthusiasm and snap. The early hours of the morning or late in the afternoon are the best, as the game is then likely to be roaming in search of food. 

Should the dog hesitate he should be encouraged to go on until he gets the scent of game.

In the United States, during trials of puppies and Derbies, training is not taken much into account. 

Stamina in ranging and, above all, nervous energy, combined with scenting powers and "bird sense," are principally considered. Proper training will make a classy dog later.

Although Derbies are considered as puppies, some

of these should not be so classed, for they are nearly two years old, by which time a dog should have developed properly and have a complete training, which should warrant his competing under all the conditions of an All-Age dog, as the full grown dog is called in field trial language.

To be a field trial judge in the United States is a difficult proposition, for it is not enough to have owned and shot over many dogs or even to have trained them. It is necessary to know what is wanted at these field trials and to know the field trial dog; know the rules enforced at the field trials at the different places where they are held; be able to judge the dog, not as an aid towards killing game, but to consider his qualities as compared with those of the other competing dogs; know the habits of the game, the topography of the land, in order to give each dog, so far as possible, even chances with the others, to the end that he can have equal opportunities of showing his faculties; be able to discriminate natural instinctive qualities from work done at the trainer's orders or from training. It must not be forgotten that a dog which it is intended to enter for field trial competition should be well trained and have his muscles in good hard condition, his paws hardened so as to prevent foot soreness, all of which can be secured by proper exercise, good feeding and general care. The dog should be given plenty of practice with the class of game which he will probably have at the field trial, for which purpose it is advisable to take him to grounds which are similar or near the ones where the tests will be held, so that he may become as acclimated as possible and so that there will be no fear of set-backs owing to change of location, food, etc., which so frequently affect the dog's condition adversely, and the quality of his work, during several days, or even weeks.

In the United States, where heats last over an hour, it is necessary to consider the question of stamina more seriously and the dog's training should be done with a view to develop more endurance than rapidity, for which reason his preparatory work should be more prolonged, to accustom him to great fatigue for tests in which stamina is the main factor.

Dogs differ considerably in their aptitude for work. 

While some are consistently good daily workers, others are erratic and have their good and off days, for, as it can be realized, each one is governed by his respective idiosyncrasies. The trainer should have

this always in mind, so as to work the dog at the proper time, that is to say, at times when the dog shows keenness.

Those of my readers who never saw a field trial may be amused at the amount of preparatory work I advocate for these trials, but anyone with experience will appreciate the fact that very few dogs go steadily through one of these ordeals without lagging or can finish up as snappily as at starting.

As the reader will, by now, understand, there is considerable difference in the manner in which field trials are carried out and the dogs are trained for same in the United States and Europe. Anyone who is familiar with European field trials will conclude, at first sight, that the American field trial dog lacks training and seems to run around wildly. If, for example, he sees a French field trial and he is accustomed to attend American field trials, he will conclude that in the former case the dog's ranging powers are very restricted, even in the case of a good searcher, with much training or over training. But when one considers both cases carefully one realizes that each has its own peculiar advantages. 

In the case of the North American trials you will finds dogs of great stamina and priceless searching powers, combined with bird sense. They produce dogs with fitness for work over extensive, rough country and are invaluable to the sportsman in helping him to find game which is scarce in such grounds. 

In Europe, on the other hand, you will find that the dogs are suitable for the conditions of ground and game peculiar to the continent, where systematic ranging, and docility for being easily managed in the way they are trained for work are required. 

Dogs intended for shooting are required to help their handler to secure as big a bag as possible. If it is true that these dogs need to have their natural instincts developed, as well as keenness and "bird sense," it is equally true that they require to have considerable more discipline, that is to say, be obedient and instantly ready to carry out any order given them so that, by close cooperation between dogs and men, as it were, the best plan can be executed to obtain the desired results.

In the case of the field trial dog training is aimed toward getting his aptitude and faculties well fitted for these, so that he can be judged from the standpoint of class and as a suitable progenitor. In the shooting dog these qualities must also be developed

by right training, so that his master can get the best results from his assistance for his own benefit and do valuable work which man is not able to do. 

It must not be thought, as many people do, that the dog hunts enthusiastically for the mere benefit of his master. The dog hunts from sheer self-inspired keenness and his object is to capture the game with the object of eating it. Man seeing this, and noticing the dog's abilities, trained such dogs to assist him in the hunt, so that they might, by each contributing their own peculiar abilities, cooperate on the shooting grounds. This is the reason that in the fields the slightest motion or expression of one or the other (man or dog) is quickly understood and plans are jointly carried out, under the superior guidance of the man, for the benefit of the latter only, which in fair justice should be for the benefit of both. But such is the dog's hunting keenness that he is gratified by merely seeing the game caught or a least at having pointed it. Having in consideration that the shooting dog is intended as a help to the sportsman, he should be given adequate training, to prepare him for this object, so that he learns only what is essentially of value, in order that he can be as useful as possible. 

The most useful form of ranging is that combining bird sense, but in order that he be an ideal dog he should be also taught systematic ranging, either extensively or short ranged, according to local requirements, not necessarily very rapid, but with powers of endurance, so that the dog may be worked an entire day with the guns, if necessary.

Once a dog has worked some time in the field he loses a considerable amount of his snappiness, but that does not make him less enthusiastic. To summarize it may be said that a dog intended for shooting should above all range with bird sense, be a stanch pointer; be firm at the flight or wing of game, either furred or feathered, and at the shot; be a good backer in case he is taken out with other dogs; be a good retriever, either of dead or wounded game, on land or in the water. For all this, he must be given yard training and the other necessary lessons. 

If a field trial dog which has wide-ranging capacity, or say an American field trial dog is wanted as a shooting dog, he can easily be broken to such use by giving him full yard training, teaching him to retrieve and reducing his excessive keenness by means of continual and prolonged work, so that he ranges and works calmly.

THE BEST LESSON FOR A PUPPY 

The best lesson that can be given a puppy pupil when he has reached six months of age is to take him out to the fields frequently, always avoiding getting him tired. In this way the hunting spirit will grow in him steadily.

Ranging and pointing are natural instincts in the pointing dog, in consequence of which the trainer should avail himself of every opportunity offered to develop these instincts gradually and naturally. 

Trainers usually pursue two different methods. The 

European prefers yard training before taking the dog out to the fields. Modern American methods, on the other hand, do the opposite. I agree with the latter, because natural tendencies of scent peculiar to the breed, and also ranging and pointing, will instinctively arouse the hunting spirit of each dog, which no trainer can accomplish, especially concerning the following points: Physical development, familiarity with the fields and shooting grounds, the nature of these fields, the means and guile necessary to capture game all these are taken advantage of for the hunter's benefit.

When I first began training dogs for shooting, I followed the advice of some authorities loyal to the old system, who recommended, above all other things, blind obedience and a full course of yard training before taking dogs out to the fields, and, in the course of which I used to train them to point a rabbit or quail placed in a certain spot, in order to teach them to be stanch during the point. Dogs taught in this way were certainly models of obedience, but quite useless. When taken out into the fields it became necessary to teach them to follow, because everything was strange to them, and their previous work only served to delay their acquiring valuable knowledge, only attained by actual contact with nature. 

When a dog is trained to range and point tame birds or game before being made familiar with real hunting and wild game, his natural guile for approach without flushing the game is lessened. There is a tendency to lose his natural instinct. When the occasion arises to test this it will be necessary to instruct him in using craft, but this means the loss of valuable time, which could have been avoided by following the instructions recommended by me. 

Taking the dog out should be done in the morning or afternoon, on fine days, but never during the heat

of the day, and the length of these outings must be left to the judgment of the trainer, who will bear in mind, age, condition of health and stamina of the puppy. At the start two days a week, then every other day, and finally daily. During the walk out he should be kept on leash, to avoid accidents and undue fatigue, which would inevitably deprive him of the natural enthusiasm so necessary once at the training fields. Incidentally, he could also then be taught to come "to heel," but without the spike collar and following the instructions laid down in the corresponding chapter. 

He will, at first, be at a loss, running aimlessly around his master until, little by little, he becomes used to the roughness and topography of the ground, to all his surroundings, and gradually overcomes his fear of being away from his master. Very soon you will notice him chasing butterflies and small birds, and later on trying to use his scent in approaching game until, when least expected, he suddenly runs into game, when the hunter will be confronted with one of these three consequences: If the puppy be timid, he will become shy; if courageous, he will go for it; if classy, he will point. In the first instance it is up to the trainer to encourage him with the greatest caution, for the slightest imprudence will cause him to become game shy, and this is very difficult to overcome. In the second case he should not be prevented from chasing some, for he will realize very soon the necessity of being astute. In the last mentioned event, the least frequent, the trainer's approach should be careful. Pat him on the back, speak soft words of appreciation and allow him to stay stanch as long as the puppy cares to or the game allows it, without scolding him later, if he breaks, because this will only serve to show him the means used by the game to escape.

Later on, when he becomes keen to the sport of hunting, it will be convenient and ample time to incline him to the game he should show interest in, to wit, allow him to hunt what one wishes and prohibit him hunting uninteresting things. But in doing this the greatest care should be exercised in order not to discourage him through fear or intimidation.

In Europe, as here in Mexico, they prefer that the pointing dog hunt furred and feathered game. In the United States the dog is taught to specialize on feathered game, and it is precisely at this period when this differentiation should begin.

Always start work against the wind, so to accustom the dog to scenting with a high head and not with his nose close to the ground. Do not allow him to hunt lizards or rats, or to dig holes. To work him in company of a veteran dog, well trained, will be very useful at first, especially so if the field is scarce in game, for by the aid of such companion's experience he will learn sooner what kind of game is around.

Later on, when his own senses are aroused, it will be more advisable to take him out alone, so that his own ardor will be developed.

If the puppy is timid, or you have several of the same age or stage of training, they may all be let loose at the same time. They will mutually encourage one another and will lose fear of the rough ground or of being far away from their master. During this period of the dog's life and training everything should be done toward making him courageous, bold and to acquire the knowledge of how to proceed in the sphere of his calling, the field. As his development and keenness for hunting increase he should have his companions gradually taken from him, to accustom him to hunting alone. 

Modern trainers, during these outings, accustom the dog to the report of firearms. (See chapter introducing firearms.)

It is true that this period is the slowest and the least interesting of the dog's training, but it must be borne in mind that each outing in the fields is of the greatest practical value to the dog. One must always have patience, for it is of more practical value to the puppy to learn to jump a hedge, move and run about over rough ground, become accustomed to the hazards of the field and the inclemency of the weather, than to acquire yard training as yet. The shooting dog is intended for service in the field and it must be his first aim to get familiar with it. 

With a courageous dog, having enthusiasm for hunting, much can be accomplished, while from a timid animal, without initiative or stamina, very little is to be expected.

From what I have written the reader must not conclude that I speak in defense of the disobedient or savage dog; in fact, quite the contrary. I am so ardent a supporter of true, real training that I am sanguine in this respect the reader will observe, as he follows the methods I recommend in the course of this treatise.

HOW LESSONS AND COMMANDS SHOULD BE GIVEN 

It is far easier for a dog to learn under an efficient instructor or handler than for an inexpert trainer to learn to train in the same way as a veteran. For this reason, one who is new to training should endeavor constantly to ascertain if faults and failure should be blamed on the dog or attributed to his own methods of teaching, so as to avoid inflicting unjust punishment to the animal for errors due to human incompetence.

When a dog has been trained by someone else, the new owner should first of all ascertain the lines on which he has been trained. It must be remembered that there are fewer men who understand the handling of a dog than there are dogs trained to obey. 

The trained dog will invariably obey, when he has understood instructions, but the individual, himself, does not always know what he is ordering, and to secure obedience it is as necessary to know how to order as, when ordering, to know what one is ordering. 

Orders should be given with precision, that is, smartly, but quietly, for a great deal of shouting and whistling is conducive to nothing but confusion to the dog, frightening the game and annoying your hunting companions. Once given, an order should be obeyed instantly; otherwise, the dog should be compelled to obey by means of the check-cord attached to his collar and, if necessary, the whip, taking care that the dog does not run away from the trainer, for this would tend to make him endeavor to escape whenever he realizes he has made a fault, for which reason it is preferable to punish with the aid of the check-cord and collar than with the whip; the former does not scare the dog nor can he escape, while the latter may cause both. 

A dog should only be struck when the reason can be easily associated with the cause. For example, if, when a bird rises, the dog is struck and ordered to drop, he will know the cause and learn, without any order, to drop as soon as the bird rises, in order to avoid the whip; but it is not the same thing if he is struck after disobeying, or after he breaks, for

he might then be led to understand that he has been punished for failing to catch the game. 

If, through breaking point or shot, chasing, or on starting away, he is pulled up by means of the checkcord attached to the spike collar, or is struck with the whip simultaneously with the action, he will avoid repetition of this fault to escape similar chastisement in the future. Therefore, it may be seen that it is necessary to associate the punishment with the faults. 

The greater the stubbornness, the harder should be the punishment, and in proportion when the fault is slighter, but always simultaneously, never afterward. 

A dog understands expression and intonation, not the significance of words. For example, if he be commanded to "down" in a different tone from that which he is used to, he will hesitate, but if another word is used in the same intonation as used for the usual one (take the word "pants," for instance), he will at once obey, just as though he had been ordered "down!" I have proved this to many individuals with different dogs and in different languages. 

In the same way the dog understands signs and the same signs must always be used with the same object. This is the reason a dog more readily understands his trainer than if he is handled by someone else. It is only obvious that his new handler must learn his trainer's voice of command and his signs.

A dog's lessons should be from ten to fifteen minutes' duration daily, but may be extended as he gradually progresses in his training. When the lesson on any certain day may have proven overtaxing for the dog, the next day must be one of complete rest; it is preferable that the dog should rest after a diffi. cult lesson; repetition, on the same day, is inadvis. able. But no lesson should be suspended until the point aimed at has been accomplished; once this is achieved, work may be suspended.

Success of a training course consists in not repeating a lesson the dog has understood. Once this is accomplished, the next step should be taken immediately.

The course of lessons recommended in this book should be adhered to closely, for, as the reader will see, they are laid out in such a way that they range from the easier ones, at first, to the more difficult, in due course. I warrant that, if the lessons are properly followed, the trainer will be astonished at the pupil's progress.

PRACTICAL EDUCATION SUITABLE PLACES FOR YARD TRAINING AND THE RIGHT TIME TO COMMENCE 

Among other suitable places for yard training may be a room, a flat roof provided with low walls, a yard, a garden or any location where there is quiet during the lesson, so that there will be nothing to distract the attention of the dog. This is especially advisable when the trainer is inexperienced. An experienced trainer does not hesitate to go on with this work in the presence of others. I often train while there are observers, especially so when giving a demonstration in training work. 

The right time to start yard training will vary and depends principally on the physique and attitude of the dog; generally, when about ten to twelve months old, if he has grown up normally, and after he has been given field outings to accustom him to the sight of game, and his instincts have been aroused. At that age, or at any later time, he can be taken in hand for training, but never before his pointing instinct has been aroused, as it would be useless and detrimental.

While I, personally, prefer training a full-grown dog rather than a puppy, because better results can be obtained from the former, yet I do not recommend this selection for the inexperienced trainer, because much greater energy is necessary. 

It must be remembered that yard training is intended primarily to teach the dog obedience, with the aid of which he will learn to understand commands and which will also later aid him in the process of field training. Under no circumstances should this method of training be such that he becomes a model of discipline in the yard, only to lose this in the fields, or, in other words, that his submission has reached such a stage that he loses all initiative and is nothing more or less than an automaton, or a disciplined dog, for should he develop that sole quality he will be absolutely worthless. More can be obtained from a dog which is not thoroughly trained than from an overtrained dog.

The trainer should also bear in mind that the weaker and less resourceful, or lacking in energy, a dog may be, he should be allowed less yard training, but more attention should be given to developing his courage, for a dog lacking in this quality and also in natural initiative is not worth his keep.

TEACHING THE DOG TO LEAVE HIS KENNEL WHEN ORDERED 

All dogs, by natural instinct, when in their kennels believe they are free from their trainer's influence, especially when they have been punished. A thorough training is ncessary, so that, to order, the dog will leave his kennel, or that, when passing, he won't enter it without the trainer's permission.

To get the desired results in doing this, place the spike collar and check cord on him and allow him to go into his kennel, taking care that the cord extends outside. Let him stay there quite a while, then order "up," "here," immediately pulling, fairly violently, on the rope, as illustrated in the lesson teaching him to come to you. It does not matter if, in doing this, he should strike the door of the kennel; quite the contrary, this will help to impress the lesson. Repeat this lesson whenever necessary, taking care to vary it, in the manner of giving the order, either by voice, signal, or the two blasts on the whistle.

Afterwards, take the cord end and lead the dog past his kennel. Should the dog go in the trainer will pretend not to notice and keep on walking. The length of the rope will allow the dog to go into the kennel, but when getting to the full limit of the cord, you will give a sharp jerk, which will compel

If the dog is not taught this lesson thoroughly it will be the cause of a thousand and one inconveniences, for the more you call him the further inside he will attempt to get, going down on his back, not caring a snap for the trainer's troubles to get him out. 

This same lesson will prove useful on any occasion when it may be necessary to break the dog from hiding or getting into any places contrary to his owner's will.

There are some dogs which are always pulling their chain when fastened to their kennels, thus dragging same from its original place. This fault can be checked by placing on the dog the spike collar, instead of the plain one, so he will inflict on himself a punishment whenever he attempts to pull at his chain.

INTRODUCING THE FIREARM 

On this lesson depends greatly the success or failure of the gundog. The culture of the breed frequently produces dogs of very nervous temperament and with such getting scared will sometimes make them worthless. The reader must bear in mind that this chapter refers to dogs which have not been thus affected. 

If they have this fault, then consult the chapter deal. ing with this subject. 

The trainer should make use of a percussion cap pistol, as it is very useful for firing off caps only at first, and adding powder to the charges so as to get louder reports gradually. If one of these pistols cannot be secured, being out of date, get an ordinary pistol and use blank cartridges. Later on you may use the gun without fear of scaring the dog.

The use of the pistol is very convenient at first, because by holding it in the uplifted right hand, as in the signal for "down," the dog will naturally very soon associate the report of the pistol with the signal, and in this way will drop correctly when he hears the detonation. Another advantage derived from the use of the pistol is that it is not so cumbersome when you have to correct any of the dog's faults.

The lesson is given as follows: The trainer having secured a pistol, caps, powder, wads and a packet of food, will start off for the kennel. Show the food to the dog (the dog should be fasting and tied or inclosed in his run), without letting him taste it.

Put the food on the floor within sight, but out of

the dog's range; give the dog a morsel of food, at the same time loading the pistol. Move some twenty paces from him and fire, then go up to him again and give him another bite of food. If the dog shows signs of nervousness continue firing caps at the same distance, and after a while go slowly, step by step, towards him, firing caps until you are firing near him without any signs of fear on his part. After each report of the percussion cap or blank cartridge give him another mouthful of food. 

Leave the dog without food until the next day. Then repeat the lesson in the same form, noticing closely whether the dog shows any signs of fear or pleasure. 

If the former, then do not give him his afternoon meal, but keep him fasting until the next day at the same hour. If the latter, then commence charging the pistol with powder, repeating the lesson in the same form as was done when using the percussion caps, that is, firing and giving him mouthfuls of food at short intervals until the dog can tolerate reports near him as loud as that of a gun. As it is not always possible to give this lesson in the yard, it may be given out in the open, taking your shooting bag with food (cooked meat and bread are very suitable) and the necessary implements. 

Never attempt to give this lesson with the dog loose. He should be taken out, worked and brought back on the lead. The dog should be securely tied to a stake or tree during the lesson, to prevent him running away, as this would be disastrous. 

Some dogs of nervous temperaments will gladly tolerate the report of powder being fired off, but not a percussion cap. Take this into account, so as to insert a little powder into the charge if the former be the case.

The foregoing lesson is suitable for any breed or class of dogs which it is required to accustom to the report of firearms. It is also very useful for police dogs, etc., but when the dog in question is intended for shooting in the fields it is better to give him the lesson in the fields, when his hunting instincts have been fully aroused and when his thoughts are concentrated on this.

During the time when the puppy is being taken out for runs in the field so that he may hunt of his own free will, as explained in the chapter on "The Best 

Lesson for a Puppy," he may then be made familiar with the report of firearms, without any fear of adverse results if one proceeds with care.

DROPPING TO SHOT AND AT SEEING THE GUN POINTED 

When the dog has become well accustomed to the report of firearms, he can then be taught to lie down at the report of the gun and upon seeing the gun brought up, but be quite sure before commencing this lesson that no detonation, however loud, will scare him in the slightest degree. If this warning is not heeded he will grow more timid on each occasion, until it becomes impossible to cure him of this fear. 

It can be taken for granted that once the dog lies down at a signal, or order, it will be an easy matter to teach him to lie down at the report of the firearm, if he has entirely overcome all fear. This lesson may be given in the yard, by firing off percussion caps or small charges of blank cartridges.

Having followed the advice given above, the lesson can be commenced as follows: Place the necessary implements in your bag and start for the place selected for training. Having ordered the dog to "sit," take the check cord in your left hand and with the other raise the charged pistol until your arm is in position for the signal "down" (see Fig. 12), fire, and give the cord a pull at the same moment, ordering him "down," so that he will associate the pull of the cord, the report and the order as the same command. Repeat this operation until you succeed in making him obey without using the word of command. After a while get gradually further away from him, leaving the dog sitting down, until at a good distance he learns to drop merely at the report of the shot. 

Check any attempt at disobedience, making the dog lie down at the exact spot where he was when the shot was fired. Keep him lying down a while after each shot, then order him "up" alternately by word, signal and whistle and make him come to where you are, again ordering him "sit." Once you have succeeded in this the dog should be taught to drop to shot when coming towards you, as in the lesson "Drop." The same thing should be done when he is on the run at any distance away, always employing the progressive method. Once he learns to drop to shot you may change the pistol to a gun and a light charge. Make him sit facing you and hold the gun near his head; pull the check cord and order him "down," all simultaneously, so that the dog may realize that the object intended is to make him drop when the trainer makes that movement, for he will take it to mean "down." Afterwards do this, combining the movement with a shot, so as to complete the instructions, though this latter, not being absolutely necessary may be omitted if thought advisable, especially if the dog is of a nervous temperament.

As I have just said, if the dog is of a nervous temperament, I am of the opinion that he should not be taught to drop to shot in the way I have shown. 

Then it will be more advisable to teach him out on the field, when he is pointing and his enthusiasm has been fully aroused, so that his keenness and the proximity of game will get his full attention, and when firing at your bird or game you can order him "down."

The routine of this, more than the use of force, will be valuable instruction in teaching him.

Dropping to shot is a precautionary step, to prevent the dog from chasing off at the shot without his master's orders; also prevent him from going after dead game, or from chasing game which may have been missed. Further to prevent him from doing so at the report of a neighboring gun and so scaring any other game which may be nearby. He should wait until his handler reloads and is again ready to fire, before he is ordered to retrieve or to resume hunting. 

In Europe all dogs are taught to drop to shot, especially during field trials. Spain is the only country on the continent where this is not the general rule. 

This should be done also to teach an excessively keen puppy to control his ardor, for it is obvious that a dog lying down will be more readily checked than when standing.

Dogs which have developed the fault of chasing off at the shot are nothing short of a calamity. At every shot they rush off and search furiously in all directions, even though the bird may or may not have fallen. They scare everything away. They start off at every report of a gun, even though a neighboring one, and when one is shooting hare, which in Mexico generally break away in couples, they will nearly always chase after the one which has not been fired at, making it difficult for another shot, and after a long, stern and useless chase they return dead tired, having scared away all the game, and not fit to resume work satisfactorily. 

Racing off at the shot or chasing game are similar faults and almost go together. Many hunters, upon seeing their dogs chase off after a wounded hare and catch it, seem well satisfied and for that reason approve of the dogs chasing off at the shot and going after the game on the plea that but for this they might lose the wounded game. Perhaps this fault may have proved useful at times, but it must be remembered by any one contenancing such a procedure, that in order not to wound the dog you must forego shooting at the game he is chasing or has startled in his chase. If such a hunter takes all this into account he will find that the latter situation is of much more frequent occurrence than the times when a wounded hare is chased and caught. For these reasons it is indispensable that the dog does not move at the shot or at the flight of the game. With regard

to dropping to shot or remaining standing, both have advantages and disadvantages. For a dog which is being trained, it is advisable that he should drop to the shot, for, as already said, it is easier to keep him quiet lying than standing. When shooting with two dogs it is necessary that one stays lying down while the other retrieves. If both went in search it might cause jealousy and perhaps lead to each retrieving a piece of the same dead game. Finally, the fact that the dropping to shot is insisted upon during field trials in Europe again emphasizes the necessity of lying down. The disadvantages are that while lying down it is harder for a dog to see where the game has fallen or where it has again landed, if the country around is bushy; that being at any distance away from his master at the moment of firing, if there is much brush he cannot be seen and may be hit by the next shot; that in stony, snow-covered or swampy ground the dog will naturally suffer quite considerably from having to drop every minute. Finally, it is not absolutely necessary for the dog to lie nowadays with the modern gun and the ease with which it can quickly be reloaded, while with the muzzle-loading gun this was perhaps necessary owing to the delay in reloading, which originated the term "down charge," "down," for the dog to lie down and charge for the assistant to reload.

In the United States very few trainers teach their dogs to lie down to the shot or flight of the gun, compelling them merely to stand still. 

In conclusion, the dog should, without doubt, stay motionless at the shot or flight of the game, either lying down or standing up, but as it is difficult to teach a young dog to remain still while standing, it is preferable to make him lie down, and if the trainer thinks it a good plan to continue making him drop to shot; there is no reason why he should not, even punishing him when he fails to do so. If he prefers that, later on, the dog should stand to the shot it is an easy matter to obtain this by merely not correcting him when he fails to drop, so long as he stays motionless. After a few times of letting him stay standing without obliging him to drop, the dog will get into the habit of standing to the shot, for he will prefer to do so in order to have a better opportunity of watching where the game falls or has gone to.

Should he, through being allowed to remain standing, become inclined to move or chase, this can be corrected by again making him continue dropping to shot.

PRACTICAL EDUCATION ORDERING THE DOG TO STOP ("TO-HO") 

Many trainers in the United States, as well as in Europe, teach their dogs to stand firm, without stirring, by word of command or signal. The French use the words, "tout beau," and the English equivalent generally used is "to-ho." The object is to make dogs stop and keep them standing stationary, either so that they do not flush or for any other similar reason (pointing or backing), when near or at a distance from the trainer.

I do not use it in my system, as I consider it unnecessary to devote a special lesson to it, for the reasons I have mentioned in the preceding chapter, and because it is easier to keep a dog still lying down than standing up, and if you require him to stand, this is easily done by using the dog's natural inclination to remain standing in preference to lying down, as I have demonstrated. Furthermore, when teaching a dog to "to-ho" separately you lose time, as the dog will frequently confuse the order to halt with the order to lie down, or vice versa, especially so when at any distance from you, and you will have to work considerably to get him clear on both points.

But, as my treatise would not be complete if I did not devote some space to this part of the dog's training, I intend to give the reader an idea of how it is carried out. 

Place the spike collar and check cord on the dog.

Put his food near him and as soon as he rushes for it, order him to stay, by means of a word or sound which you intend using for the purpose. For example, "stop," "to-ho," or, even the word used to stop a horse, as "whey" or "whoa," or any such word or signal that the trainer may select, but which is entirely different from the one used to make him lie down. 

At the same time gently pull him up with the cord at a certain distance from his food. At first make him stand still for just a few moments, increasing the length of time and distance gradually, then order him to go on, and before getting to it again order him "to-ho," and after several repetitions of this lesson allow him to eat. This will be repeated daily with great patience and varying the distances until he stops by order without using the check cord. At the same time he can be taught to walk slowly at the trainer's side or at heel towards his food, as though he were drawing.

Later, previous to feeding, or to eating something thrown for him, and when he can be so ordered successfully to "to-ho" before reaching same, he should also be taught to halt when approaching the trainer, for which purpose he will have to be taken to the garden or a field. Take with you two cords of about twenty yards each. Put on a plain collar with a cord tied to it, also put a spike collar on him. Once out on the field, tie the end of one cord to the spike collar, and the end of the other cord attach to the ordinary collar. Tie the cord which is on his spike collar to some tree or stake, allowing about four yards length and leave him lying near the tree and withdraw to about six yards from him, taking the end of the rope which is tied to the ordinary collar. 

Call him, taking in rope at the same time, and just before he gets to the limit of the rope tied to the tree or stake, signal him to stop, giving him the order, "to-ho," or the word selected for this purpose. 

Hold him with both ropes taut, so that he cannot move, but do not allow him to lie or sit down. Should he do so make him stand up at once by lifting him by his hide near the hindquarters. The trainer will then go back to where he stood when the lesson was interrupted. After keeping him standing for a while, and if he has stood quite still, then go up to him and show your satisfaction by patting him. Before repeating the lesson change the locality and at each repetition of the lesson lengthen the distance of cord until he can go through with the lesson at the cord's full length. When the dog halts at the signal, word or whistle (the whistle should be very different again from the one used in making him lie down or else he will become confused), he should then be made to go through the lesson loose, merely letting him drag the rope attached to the spike collar along the ground. At first make him do it over a short distance, increasing the distance. Then let him perform again at full speed, never failing to take him to the exact spot where he was ordered to "to-ho," should he have refused, leaving him there long enough and the trainer withdrawing to his previous place, as was the case when teaching him to lie down. 

This lesson should never be given with the lie down lesson or until he has learned the latter, as it might easily confuse him. This trick of the two cords may be used for a headstrong dog in the third lesson, when he is ordered "down" while coming towards the trainer.

PRACTICAL EDUCATION RETRIEVING 

Retrieving is the act of looking for and bringing in the dead or wounded bird, or some object desired by the owner; that is to say, a process executed by the dog as the outcome of individual training. In some cases, a tendency to retrieve is inherited from trained ancestry, but in no case is it a natural instinct, such as hunting and pointing.

There are two methods for teaching to retrieve, the one misnamed "natural" and the other known as "forced," which latter term is also a misnomer. An action cannot be considered as natural if it is not done voluntarily and since retrieving must be taught the dog it is not a natural instinct. It is commonly known as "natural," because in the process of training neither force nor punishment is used, but the puppy's natural tendency to pick up things and play with them is availed of for the purpose, for which reason it would be better called "a method of teaching a dog to retrieve by means of his playful instincts." 

Under this system of training some dogs become great retrievers, but it is not necessarily the best system in all cases, as it depends entirely on the dog's temperament.

To make a good retriever with this system, certain peculiarities are necessary. First, natural tendency of the dog; second, the dog must be a youngster and, finally, much patience exercised and ample time allowed. Notwithstanding this, a dog trained in this way can never be relied upon with certainty to retrieve always and whenever called upon to do so, and the time may come when, although apparently trained, he refuses to obey and will not retrieve at all, especially if he has been whipped for disobeying. 

To call the other method "forced" or "by force" likewise is not proper, as it leads one to believe that the dog acts always through compulsion or pressure, which is not the case, because though, at first, methods which inflict some pain are employed to force the dog to open his mouth and hold an article, this process ceases just as soon as the dog understands that he must and does obey. Indeed, the dog becomes enthusiastic and enjoys the lesson. This method has the advantage that at any later time, when the dog refuses to retrieve, he can be forced to obey, since discipline has been the system involved and not play, which are quite contrary processes.

In Europe, as in the United States, the so-called "force" methods are used, which only differ in the manner of associating the pain with the order. They are all based on the same principle that any pain inflicted ceases the moment the order is carried out, and vary according to the ideas of the trainer. While some pinch the dog's ear, nose or tail, others use the spike collar and whip; still others press the upper lip against the animal's fangs, or teeth, and again others use only the spike collar. Even with the latter the method varies. All the methods lead to the same object except that some, by the improper use of the whip, make the dog really timid. 

In my opinion, the spike collar methods are the best.

Which was the original method, and who was the inventor, is not known. C. B. Whitford, an Amercian authority, says in his book, "Training the Bird Dog," that about the year 1870 there was a trainer in New York who specialized in training dogs to retrieve by means of the "force" system and a spike collar, but who kept his system in the dark, only showing the collar he used. The nature of his method was not known. Whitford further states, that, having secured one of these collars, he after a while, put it into practical use under a system which he published in 1874, in Arnold Burges' book and, magazines of that period. 

He also says that in the year 1870, in New England, there were trainers who used the "force" system without the spike collar and employed the method of nose and ear pinching. In Europe the spike collar was known almost before the shotgun came in.

Today, there are many who use these systems and have written works on them. They are popular in the 

United States. Personally, I have used two or three different "force" systems with good results, but the one I advocate in this treatise has certain modifications of my own, born of personal experience. 

Many trainers are inclined to think that the mere fact of using the spike collar is all that is required, or that by dint of shaking the poor anmial with it they will accomplish their aim. This is preposterous. 

The collar of itself is merely intended to inflict punishment in the same way as a whip or something similar, and if not used with good judgment and systematically it becomes nothing more or less than an instrument of torture and an escape valve for the bad temper of the user. It is of no avail under such circumstances and likely to cause adverse results and even the dog's death, but, if a person uses it

judiciously and sparingly, it will be of great value and in no way cruel, only causing momentary pain in the form of mild punishment, and by no means more cruel than any other analogous treatment. For this reason, be careful of its use. 

Do not go from one lesson to the next without being through. When you find difficulty in getting the dog to progress it will almost certainly be caused by too much hurry, instead of working gradually and surely, and you will have to go back and renew until he works well and spontaneously. It must be remembered also that no lesson should be "slept over," once learned. The first steps are wearisome and difficult, and the beginner should not easily lose heart, but be persevering and consistent and never lose his temper, as unfortunately is so often the case with whimsical natures. Difficulty may be found in getting the dog to open his mouth when ordered, so that he will hold the object, but once he opens it a little on being ordered and takes hold of the object with his front teeth he will progress very quickly. Go slowly, always patting and praising him when he has accomplished the point, so that he may gain confidence and realize that any pain inflicted ceases immediately upon his obeying and he has earned your approval instead. The trainer should use judgment in order to get the dog to understand intelligently what is wanted in the course of training and not to make a mechanical unit of him. Aim at developing the dog's intellect, and as this gradually develops it should be used to advantage. 

Any dog, regardless of breed or age, after ten months old, can be taught to retrieve by means of this system. Of course, much depends on his individual capacity, but the results, as a rule, will be satisfactory, and all dogs, without exception, will learn. 

Under this system I have taught a number of different breed of dogs to retrieve, police dogs included. 

The use of a thick corn-cob is very useful for the first lesson in instructing a large dog, and a thinner one for a small dog. The corn-cob has the advantage that it can be changed as soon as it gets dirty or old; also because it is light, soft and easily held. If this cannot be procured, make one similar out of cloth.

These lessons are suitable for all dogs intended for retrieving, which have successfully gone through the yard training described further back.

The pupil has been retrieving to order, but within range of sight. Now it will be necessary to retrieve with the aid of his sense of smell on hearing the order, "dead," "fetch." These words of command are used so that the dog will become accustomed to search in different styles for dead or live game. 

The dog, when looking for live game, should do so with his head raised. When looking for dead or wounded game, on the contrary, he should have his nose close to the ground, searching minutely. For this reason the dog that searches for game with his

head up and does not examine bushes, etc., minutely, is seldom an excellent dog at finding dead or wounded game. A dog can be a good retriever, that is, obedient at retrieving when ordered, and nevertheless be bad at finding wounded or dead game. On the other hand, he may be indifferent at retrieving what he is ordered to do and yet be good at finding wounded or dead game.

In the former instance any dog trained under the "force" system may be good. For the latter natural qualifications are necessary, which are developed by the right sort of training. 

To teach a dog to retrieve with the nose down, searching minutely, the following method should be employed: Place in a bag a good quantity of bread and meat. The dog should wear his collar and rope, so that he may learn to seek with them on. Having graduated his supply of food, so that he will be hungry, because during this lesson you will only use his greediness. Go out into the garden or a field where you have grass and bushes, give him a piece of bread or meat to stimulate his appetite and order him "down." Keep him in that position for a while and then order him "up," "dead bird," "fetch," at the same time throwing away a piece of bread without him seeing it. Commence to search with him, showing great interest and encouraging him as much as possible. After purposely looking in the wrong direction, go to where the morsel was thrown, showing him it and saying, "fetch." Repeat this over and over until the dog has understood and shows keenness in searching. Afterwards you will not show him. 

On the contrary, the trainer will search together with the dog, endeavoring to reach the spot where the piece of bread may be both together. The trainer will recover it, if the dog has not seen it, but let the dog be aware that he has retrieved it and then not let him have it. This obviously is done to awaken keener interest on the part of the dog, for in this way he will know that if he does not find it he will not be allowed to eat it. A piece of bread or meat should also be placed on a bush or some high place, so that when the dog is out with the gun he will learn to search up above if he fails to find the dead game on the ground. It often happens that the wounded or dead bird remains lodged on a branch. When the dog realizes the bird has not fallen to the ground he will search above. In Germany, where the braque (German pointer) serves either for following the track of the stag or of a wounded deer, dogs are taught with the aid of a small apparatus shaped like a reel, with two little compartments, one where they place bits of entrails and the other blood. This apparatus is dragged by the assistant along the ground and once the smell of the track has been set the dog is shown the place, taking him by the leash, and made to scent and follow it until he comes up to the game, which had been previously killed and from which blood had been taken for the apparatus mentioned.

The dog, not having direct emanations from the piece, is compelled to follow the emanations from the ground. With method and patience you will succeed in getting him to search for and retrieve, always varying the objects used with different game. Once he can search and retrieve keenly he should be made to follow the trail of some game, which will have been dragged along the ground in the way shown with the piece of meat. This stage of his training can be combined with the report of the firearm, so that he will lie down and then be ordered to "fetch."

There are many opinions against the use of a pointing dog for retrieving, because he loses some of his abilities for ranging and pointing live game. Certainly, dogs which are made to specialize are best, and it is owing to this that some English trainers use their pointing dogs solely for that purpose and they are made to remain lying from the moment the game takes wing until the bird has been retrieved by the other dog specially intended for this purpose, which is the retriever. As the majority of sportsmen cannot indulge in the luxury of two special dogs, it is an advantage if a hunting "companion" is versatile and can be used in any emergency. Many dogs can be trained both to point and retrieve satisfactorily. 

Under the system I have described I have obtained many splendid results, the best having been an Irish setter called Gay, and which was my special pride until her death.

Some trainers teach their dogs to point the dead game or object they are retrieving before bringing it in. I consider this useless, because few dogs do this well and time is lost, and also because the dog may lose keenness for retrieving and many wounded birds will escape.

RETRIEVING FROM WATER 

The first thing to teach a dog for this is to lose the fear of water and to learn to swim. To break him of water shyness select shallow water, or, better still, a spot where the water has a level edge or beach, so that he can go in gradually. The hottest hours of the day should be chosen. Take the pupil, if possible, in the company of another dog, stronger than he, obedient and used to retrieve from water. 

Place the spike collar on the pupil and a plain collar on the trained one. Tie them together, but not too far apart, because they may get entangled and drown. 

Once properly coupled up with a rope or leash, throw into the water something which floats, such as a sack full of cork or a piece of soft wood, and order the trained dog to fetch. Being the stronger of the two he will pull the pupil in with him and force him thus to swim. This will be repeated until it is seen that the beginner swims well and offers no resistance to going in. If you cannot secure the assistance of a trained dog, take the pupil to some spot, as explained, where the water is shallow and free of floating objects, so that he can readily see the pieces of hard bread you will throw in nearby, and then farther in so that he may swim a little to reach them. It is unnecessary to say that the dog should have been fed with moderation previous to the lesson, so that the keenness of his appetite will make him lose all fear. Once he is free of water shyness start teaching him to retrieve. 

For this put the spike collar and check cord on him. 

The trainer, of course, will not attempt to teach him to retrieve from water without having previously taught him to retrieve thoroughly and enthusiastically on land.

Use the small bag with corks, or a small piece of wood similar to the saw-buck used on land, but without the short spikes at the ends. Having hold of the check cord, throw the bag a little distance and make him go after it. So that the object may not be unfamiliar to him first make him retrieve it on land, obliging him to do so should he refuse. Gradually throw it farther and farther in the water until he has to swim to reach it. Once he can do this take the check cord off, leaving the spike collar only on him.

After this, substitute a dead duck for the bag, using a widgeon or blue-winged teal, or some small species of duck preferably, first making him retrieve it on shore. On throwing it in the water, after he has

successfully retrieved it from some distance, fire a shot. If you cannot readily get a duck, use a dead tame pigeon, but the former is more practicable for many reasons, if obtainable. I have taught dogs to become good swimmers by placing a live duck on a small pond and urging the dog in after it till he tires it and catches it. This also proves useful in securing wounded ducks afterwards. When a dead or wounded duck falls into a lake a long distance from the shore and the dog can neither scent nor see it, since it may have fallen one hundred or two hundred yards from the bank (a dog's range of vision when swimming is naturally short), I have managed to regulate the direction when the dog is swimming by means of shots fired from a 22-calibre target rifle ahead of the dog, hitting the water gradually nearer the bird until the dog gets up to it. Dogs learn this easily. 

A dog should likewise be taught to search for dead bird's midst water reeds and plants. For this it will be necessary to take off his collar, so that he will not get entangled among the reeds.

TEACHING A DOG TO FOLLOW A SADDLE HORSE OR TO REMAIN QUIET ON AN AUTOMOBILE 

To teach a dog to follow a saddle horse will be easy. 

Hold him by the check cord from the horse and order him to "heel." As he will recognize the order, since it has been part of his "yard training," a mere pull of the rope will be sufficient to make him go to his place. Take him along, this way for a while, then allow him to run with the rope trailing behind, and once he has become accustomed to this take the rope off, leaving him with his collar on only. 

At any attempt to disobey place the check cord on him once more.

If the dog is to be taken on a motor car he should get accustomed to staying quiet inside or on the footboard, the latter being perilous, because on a curve the dog might fall, though he be well used to it. To train the dog to stay on the foot-board put the spike collar and cord on him, ordering him "down" beside the car, so he won't move while the trainer gets aboard. Once inside, order him "up" and jerk the cord towards you until the dog stays lying or standing on the foot-board, punishing by means of jerks or by the whip if he tries to get down. This should be repeated several times with the car parked and once accomplished you can start slowly till he gets used to

the movements, stopping once in a while without allowing the dog to get down when not ordered. If taken inside, which is preferable to avoid accidents, insist on his quietness, punishing him with the whip if he does not or attempts to get down without permission.

Many dogs are so fond of the car or coach that, on seeing one, they jump promptly inside, without the order, refusing to get down even if coaxed to do So. This fault can be erased quickly with the spike collar and check cord, which should be used in letting the dog enter and also in ordering him to come down, jerking the cord at the same time, repeating till object is accomplished.

PRACTICAL EDUCATION RANGING WITH BIRD SENSE 

Α dog can be said to have "bird sense" when, without hesitation, he goes straight for the likely places where there is more certainty of finding game and which he recognizes by the topography of the ground, the season and the hour.

This is a great qualification, very much appreciated in our shooting fields, especially when after quail.

This can only be learned by experience and continual practice on certain game and on certain fields. In our fields, vast and rugged as they are, cultivated and also uncultivated, game will look for those places where they are most likely to find food, quietness and safety. These places will also be selected by the dog having "bird sense," and he will recognize them and proceed there at once, thus avoiding unnecessary exhaustion in working unsuitable places. 

In field trial terms this is known as "bird sense," but I am of the opinion that it should be called "game sense," instead of "bird sense," as this peculiarity is equally advantageous for use with any game you may be after.

In Europe, and especially in England, they prefer that the dog should range systematically, as the fields are generally fenced off and the abundant game wanders as it likes.

In the United States, where the conditions and the requirements of the sportsmen more closely resemble ours, trainers give more attention to the importance of developing bird sense in the dog. My bitch, Lady, had this bird sense developed more pronouncedly than any of the others I have owned.

At the time I hunted very much at a farm called Manzanilla, near the capital of the state (Puebla,Mexico), and where, at a hill called "Resurrection," and on the banks of the surrounding streams, the quail generally could be found. As soon as I loosened my dog she would head straight for that locality without any need of signals, and you could bet with certainty that any places she left unranged were devoid of game, and wherever she ranged keenly the result would be entirely satisfactory. Once reaching the hill in question, I had merely to climb to the summit and watch the bitch ranging at a long distance, until she would come upon a bevy and, pointing stanchly, would wait for me to get into action. Dogs which practice this manner of ranging are generally those which have been allowed ample scope to run in

search of game before educating them and are left to develop their own natural instincts. This allows the dog to develop intellect and experience better than under the trainer's guidance.

It must not be concluded by this that the dog should range independently and at will; that is to say, without discipline or without heed to the trainer's orders. 

On the contrary, any attempt at disobedience should be checked and punished at once; otherwise it might lead to the disastrous end of the dog becoming entirely independent, hunting for his own enjoyment only, which is extremely unpleasant. The word of command to make him range should be "go on" and to recall him, a whistle or signal.

SYSTEMATIC RANGING [QUARTERING] 

our Systematic ranging is not indispensable in country or in the United States, but it is necessary to complete the dog's training. In other words, a dog which has the bird sense developed and ranges systematically is the ideal one. Systematic ranging is required where game has not a fixed place, such as at field trial work in Europe; at shoots on the continent; at the hemp (henequen) plantations of Yucatan; the the maguey fields; on the plains, in the bush and when the game is scattered. 

It is the most difficult part of the dog's training.

The lessons should be given the dog when he is full of enthusiasm at the commencement of work and suspended just as soon as he shows the least disinclination or fatigue. As a general rule, it is best to give intervals of ten minutes work and ten minutes rest during the first lessons, stopping work as soon as the dog shows the least fatigue or disinclination to work, especially so as this lesson cannot be taught him by force or compulsion, but by means of encouragement and incitement, so that the trainer will use judgment in harmony with conditions as to when it will be advisable to break off the lesson for the day.

AMERICAN POINTER WHOSE POSTURE INDICATES SCENT OF DISTANT GAME

POINTING 

This is the most important part of the work to be done by a pointing dog, so far as the hunter is concerned, when the pointing is sure and stanch as the complement of good ranging.

Pointing by dogs has been taken advantage of for many years, even from the time when nets and crossbows were in use for hunting, although circumstances compelled them to modify the training of the dogs in that regard according to the manner in which hunting was effected. When nets were in use, the dog was required to lie down as soon as he scented the game and he had to remain pointing in that position so that he (the dog) and the game might be covered by the net. In this kind of work the Spanish poodles were noted and these dogs were called in French, "chiens couchant," and in English, setting dogs or setting spaniel, which were afterwards called "setters" when this work began to be carried on by hunters using guns. Afterwards when the cross-bow and the flint gun were in use, and before wing shooting was introduced, there used to be three kinds of pointing dogs called: "pointing dogs," "circling dogs" and "pointing and circling dogs."

The pointing dog is the one that stops right opposite to the game as soon as he scents it, and he is the one that is in use nowadays, because, owing to our modern guns, what the hunter requires is to know where the game is so that he may prepare himself to fire at it.

Circling dogs were those which pointed the game by giving turns of greater or less extent around it, and at that time this was considered very important as it did not frighten the game. As is well known, game is still when the hunter is in motion at a certain distance, rather than when he is quiet before it. 

The hunter kept going around with the dog until the former succeeded in seeing the game on the ground, then stopped for the purpose of shooting.

And finally, the pointing and circling dogs, which were also in use in those times, were highly esteemed because this kind of dog pointed and then began to circle around and would stop for a short time turning his head around when passing opposite to the game, if the wind was blowing to his face. This allowed the hunter to determine with greater rapidity the place where the game was to be found. Nowadays there are to be found some dogs of this kind, and

they are those that hunt following the game that walks swiftly. I have seen a pointer do this instinctively when our quails had run swiftly and he succeeded in holding them back in this manner. 

There are two methods for developing in the dog the "pointing instinct": one that is carried on generally by artificial means; that is to say, with tame animals which are placed at a point where the dog to be trained is taken so that by seeing or scenting the game he might spontaneously point it, or by means of a command given to the dog to remain quiet standing, or lying down, as the instructor may desire. The other method, which I advocate, consists in giving plenty of opportunity to the dog to look for the game at a place where there may be a great deal of it, hunting by himself so that he may attain this quality which is instinctive in him and which is nothing more than the pause made by all animals that hunt before trying to spring on the game to catch it. 

This pause, when the dog stops, concentrating all his energy and forming his plan to spring on the game, is styled in terms used by hunters "pointing." It is something that is done by all dogs more or less clearly every time they want to catch something, an attitude from which man has profited, by training and selecting pointing dogs so as to prolong such pointing pause to serve him as a sign in discovering the place where the game is to be found.

The dog, when hunting for his own subsistence, shows the same manner and method of doing it as does the lion, the fox, the cat and any other animal that hunts. We may notice that even some spiders, when they go hunting for prey, stop and remain quiet a moment before throwing themselves on it. There have been other animals that through training and by making use of their ranging instinct and scenting have been made to do this. All animals that hunt are seen to get near the game in a very careful manner and to stop at a proper distance so as not to frighten it, and in order to be able to catch it by making one or several jumps at it. 

The same thing happens with dogs and it is noticeable that those that break the pointing or those that hunt on their own account, when they think they are near the game they "point" and jump towards the place where they think the game is to be found.

Sometimes they get it either before it can escape them or when about to effect escape. But many times they fail in this on account of their lack of skill or because they cannot really find the place where it is, owing to the manner in which they receive the scent or on account of their deficient scenting qualities. 

That is why, relying on my own experience, I prefer that the dogs should know by themselves the manner in which they must capture the game and this can only be learned by coming in contact with the game and knowing its habits, and the errors they commit in their practice gives them experience and renders them cautious.

Due to the natural instinct of the canine race to catch these animals, which prompts dogs to run after them if they were not able to catch them when in a form, it is very difficult for the dog to forego his own instinct and to limit himself to pointing and remain steady while he sees quarry running away from him.

While practicing hunting birds in the field, the dog learns how careful he must be and what skill he must display before the game actually takes wing. But with regard to rabbits and hares, it is not necessary that he should be so careful, because if it is true that he might get them when they are lying down, he also knows that by running after them he may also catch them and for this reason when a dog scents a fresh trail of a rabbit or hare or sees him running, he tries to run as much as possible, sometimes giving tongue, as this is a trick which he uses precisely to deceive his victim. Therefore it is much easier to make a dog point steadier at birds than at rabbits,

and it is required that the lessons given him be repeated often, until the dog may at last counteract his instinct in order to obey his master's orders. 

The spike collar and check cord are of great advantage while training the dog in this manner, more so than in any other case. Some jerking of the check cord, and making him turn over on the ground when he breaks pointing, and also ordering "down" whenever he is about to chase the game, all will teach the dog what he has to do, or else one must whip him on the shoulder when he is about to break pointing. 

When through the scarcity of rabbits or hares it is difficult to train the dog in this manner, he can be trained by using a rabbit or hare in some place fenced in at first, then in the field, to which the rabbit or hare must be taken with its neck tied by means of a small chain (about four inches long) in order that it might not cut the cord, and to this, a light cord several yards in length should be added so as to prevent the rabbit from escaping. 

The dog must be held by the cord letting the wind strike him full in the face so that he may feel it. 

If he should point he must be left there as long as it is possible, but should be ordered "down" at his least sign of impatience or desire to catch the game.

When the rabbit is taken away and changed to another spot, the dog must remain lying down at the place where he was pointing. Anyone who may be aiding the trainer must take away the rabbit, hide it, and the lesson must be repeated. Sometimes the rabbit may be left uncovered so that the dog will see it, in order that he may get in the habit of not following an animal that has not been pointed by scent. The object of these lessons is mainly to let the dog acquire the habit of remaining perfectly still.

If the dog is intended to do special work with feathered game and to forego hunting rabbits and hares, he may be placed in a yard wherein some rabbits are allowed to be about, provided there should be some holes where these little animals would escape in case the dog does chase them, and the dog must be punished should he do so. This must be repeated until the dog shows no interest in chasing rabbits. 

If one should see that in the field the dog follows the scent of a rabbit or hare, or that he points either, the dog must immediately be ordered "down" and taken away from the place, even whipped if he persists, but taking good care that he may not lose his inclination for hunting.

For this reason, when one wishes to judge a dog's ability and training it is preferable to watch him during a flush than when pointing.

RANGING FOR ISOLATED QUAIL 

One of the most useful parts of a dog's work in the shooting field is that of ranging for isolated quail. 

By isolated quail it is intended to mean those which have strayed from the bevy after this has been forced to wing and has landed once more. For this work the dog must seek with greater care and attention to detail, as the scent from one in naturally less noticeable than that from a bevy. Besides, when isolated, a quail crouches as much as possible and it becomes necessary to almost tread on it before the bird takes wing, for which reason the dog will have to exercise greater care and judgment, seeking in a zigzag manner and going wherever the master should wish, even examining the small precipices or ravines, favorite spots generally chosen by our "Macena" partridges, living near these ravines. The dog which has learned to "quarter" and is easily handled and thorough in his work will be the best.

Some sportsmen will utilize those dogs which range actively over a large area for bevies and those which are less active and more tractable for isolated birds. 

But as both types of dogs are not always within one's means, it is well to train a dog so that, as far as possible, he can be adapted to both ways of working, though it is a fact that a dog which prefers extensive working range will seldom reduce the area except with little keenness and diligence, preferring instead to range for a fresh bevy. As regards the dog that quarters more minutely and searches for isolated game, he will probably find less bevies but will be unquestionably more apt for close work. 

When teaching a dog this form of ranging, the trainer should be very careful to notice the place where the bevy has gone for shelter; order the dog to "heel," taking him thus to the place you expect to find the birds, then ordering him to "go," "seek," sending him against the wind if possible. If the place should happen to be a hill you should commence working from the summit down so that master and dog may both govern conditions better.

On commencing ranging the trainer should take a zigzag course as explained in the chapter on "Systematic Ranging," calling the dog by means of whistles or signs and soothing him so that he will work at close range and thoroughly. If the trainer happens to be on horseback, he should dismount and continue on foot. Should the dog refuse to seek in certain

As is only natural, at first the dog will flush more game than he points until he has gained experience in this work. The trainer must not omit to make the dog "to-ho" or "down" on each occasion of a flush. All these lessons should be given patiently and at length so as to get the best results. 

BACKING 

In sporting terms the word "backing" means: a dog is said to back when he points without having seen or scented the game, but because he sees another dog pointing nearby. This is the consequence of a rational process of cause and effect acquired by experience in hunting with two or more dogs, either of their own free will or with the guns. Experience will have taught them that when a companion is pointing, the game will be nearby and care must be taken so that it will not escape and to assist in its capture. Positive proof that this is the result of knowing that the game is near the dog pointing, and that as a result of this knowledge they also stop and act cautiously to avoid flushing, is the fact that, however good a backer a dog may be, he loses interest in backing and will almost certainly not do so in the future when he works together with a companion which continually false points.

Proper backing is caused by the same instinct as pointing and is a dog's natural action for his own ends, which training turns to account for man's use. 

For field trial work many dogs are taught to stop or lie down upon seeing another dog in that same position, but that is not the same as backing; it is

simply one of many other results of training such as lying down or dropping to order or sign, etc.

Good backing is the result of a well developed intellect, because the greater the dog's intelligence the easier it is for him to acquire hunting sense when working in company with another dog or with his master only. An intelligent dog, without initiative, will back equally well because he realizes that it is less work to watch and back a companion than to seek on his own account. For this reason a puppy should not be worked constantly in company with another more experienced dog. He should be taught together with a dog which is stanch when pointing and well trained, but which is his inferior in natural powers.

In Europe many handlers train their dogs to back artificially; that is to say, to drop when seeing the other dog down so that during trials they are not likely to be eliminated through forcing their companion's point. This training is good but not so pretty as to see a dog back standing up. The act of backing being entirely a spontaneous one, as before said, the trainer should endeavor to develop and take this gift to good account, giving the pupil every opportunity to let this instinct grow in him. This can be done by letting the pupil work with an experienced dog of good pointing style so that he may be more readily noticed. He should have good action and expression and, above all, be reliable, for as I have so frequently said a dog which persists in false points is useless to the pupil, for the latter will lose confidence and the lessons will have been thrown away.

Both dogs, during this work of instinct development, should be taken to a locality where game abounds and there is little brush so that they may see each other and the trainer easily at a long distance. When the trained dog points, the trainer should do all he can to get the puppy to notice this, whistling or calling him but just enough to catch his attention. The trainer should endeavor to stand near the pointing dog and do everything to pretend he is intensely interested by expression and motionless keenness, for dogs are quick at understanding and imitating attitude. When, therefore, the trainer sees that the puppy has noticed the proceedings and is coming to him he should not speak or notice him again, but remain in his position of intense and even exaggerated interest. PRACTICAL EDUCATION LESSONS FOR THE SHOOTING DOG 

After the dog has learned all the foregoing lessons, it only remains for the trainer to drive home their proper performance at all times. This is done by giving the dog's work all your attention during his first real experiences and giving secondary attention to the filling of your game bag. The time will come when the hunting trainer will find this sacrifice richly rewarded, when his dog, full of experience, will be a great help to him in the hunt, for by then the dog will not require constant, careful watching, but will be anxious to place all his abilities, combined with his training, at his master's service.

This part of his education varies considerably and depends, to a large extent, on his intelligence and to the greater or less abundance of game. It is obvious that the more game obtained the better the practice and the sooner the dog will learn by experience.

The hunter and trainer of little experience can consider his dog's training as finished when he obeys orders, ranges points and retrieves without committing a fault. If his owner has trained the dog himself, he will naturally feel more proud of him, but still greater will be his desire to show him working in front of his gun, before his friends.

The experienced and conscientious trainer, however, will realize that the time has not yet arrived when he should take him out shooting with other guns, especially so if your friends' dogs are lacking in training, because a puppy which has just been brokenin will be liable to get demoralized under such circumstances however well he may have been trained, and more so if of a nervous temperament. 

It is very trying to shoot with people who cannot control their enthusiasm and shoot at any game they see regardless of the dog. In such an event, one of these two things may occur: your companions will have to submit to your wishes, which may not suit them, or else your dog will become demoralized on hearing firing all around him and seeing shooting when no pointing has happened and will, to put it in a nut shell, get confused and not know how to proceed further.

Should the bird be only wing damaged and still moving around the ground, and the dog has seen it, order your dog "down." It is preferable to give the wounded and running bird another shot to letting the dog chase him around.

Once the dog is well broken, there will be no inconvenience to his securing the wounded bird. I am of opinion that you should never let your dog

chase a wounded rabbit or hare, even if the dog is a veteran, because this would only encourage him to go after sound ones and it is preferable to lose an occasional wounded hare, than to let him get into the habit of chasing all he sees. 

At first the dog should not be made to retrieve all the dead game, but merely allowed to retrieve one out of every four or five-and even less-if he is much disposed to rushing in or chasing. There are many trainers in Europe and in the United States who will not allow their shooting dogs to retrieve when out shooting until their second season's experience, so that the dog will not lose his steadiness at the detonation.

For my part, I see no harm in allowing him to retrieve from the very beginning as long as method and care are exercised. With our quails, which are abundant in Yucatan, I have taught dogs not to retrieve the dead birds whilst there may be other live ones scattered around. By constant practice and teaching them not to retrieve directly after the shot, but ordering them to "go on" instead, so that they will quarter the other scattered birds around and ignore the dead ones for, as yet, they have not been ordered "go," "fetch," they can finally be made to understand what is wanted and the reason of it. 

This teaching is very useful after the bevy has taken to wing the second time and are therefore isolated within a small range. They will take wing singly, or, perhaps, two or three at a time, and if the dog is well trained he will secure very fine chances for good shooting. The hunter should, of course, mark the places where the birds have fallen so that the dog will not have so much difficulty in finding them later and there will be less chance of losing them entirely.

It must not be surprising if in working with the wind, and not against it, he will make less points and more flushes. At all the latter moments, the dog should lie down and not get up until ordered.

There is an old Spanish slogan which says: El buen cazador hace al buen perro. (A good hunter makes a good dog.) But in my opinion there is nothing more untrue than this saying, for there are magnificent hunters who cannot train a dog, in the same way as there are many fine jockeys who could not break-in a young colt. I think that a good trainer makes a good dog in the same way as the good horse-breaker makes a good saddle horse. For this reason, I think the good trainer and huntsman should, at first, leave their trigger-finger unengaged (a difficult sacrifice to many), and if they find this hard, they should put the strap on their gun and carry it over the shoulder, and so only shoot when the dog has pointed. In this way the trainer has enough hold on himself to keep his nerves in check so as only to shoot when a suitable opportunity, as explained, offers, and also to avoid missing aim at the first birds or game pointed by his dog, which too often happens with beginners.

If he still finds it difficult to control himself, it would be more advisable to have someone accompany him who is a good shot, discreet and cool-headed, so that he will do the shooting when convenient to the trainer and the latter, now without his gun, can concentrate his attention on the dog.

One thing the trainer and hunter must not do is to run personally in chase of a wounded bird after the shot or to see where the wounded game may have hidden, for nothing will more incite a dog to give chase than seeing someone else do so, especially when this person is his master. It is preferable to lose the wounded game than to jeopardize all your previous efforts in training your dog.

When the hunter shoots from horseback he should take the pupil at heel till he reaches the hunting ground. On arriving there, make him "go on" and when he is pointing, ride up. If you intend firing from the horse's back, take a good position so that you may get a good shot and watch your dog at the same time. Do not forget that when the pupil sees his master on horseback he may show a greater tendency to disobey, for he realizes that it is more difficult to check him this way than when his master is on foot. It is preferable that the trainer, if wishing to ride, should do so while the dog is seeking and dismount when he points and fire while afoot.

The next thing to be done, if the game does not take wing on the hunter's approach, is for master and dog to proceed a little further ahead and, after seeking a little in that same direction, turn back.

HUNTING IN COMPANY WITH OTHER SPORTSMEN 

Hunting in company may be with the aid of other dogs or without. The best formula for a puppy at first is to have one or two companions with you but no other dogs, keeping in line and not too far from the trainer so they will be ready to assist at the point. 

Whilst hunting they should not talk loudly nor order the pupil. The dog should only be ordered by his master, who will be between the other two hunters so that the dog, during his search, if quartering, may range in front of all three. When the dog points all hunters should approach, getting, if possible, behind the pupil, the trainer standing close so as to make the dog draw up should the game run swiftly, or to know the direction it has taken.

When the trainer goes up to him he should make the dog go on ahead and locate the game if the point has indicated that the game is some distance away.

The hunters, following in the same direction and not too far from each other, will be ready to fire as soon as the bevy takes wing. One man on the right and the other on the left, each respectively accounting for those birds which may take wing ahead of them, whilst the trainer, who will be in the center, will give his principal attention to the dog, to make the latter stay still and observe the result on him of several reports of the guns at a time. Should he show signs of nervousness, make him lie down; but if unaffected, the trainer may also fire on the next occasion, selecting those quail in the middle of the bevy for his aim, or the ones which are known as the "hunter's birds," meaning those which generally stay after the bevy has taken wing, especially if the latter has been numerous.

HUNTING WITH TWO OR MORE DOGS AT A TIME 

Shooting with two or more dogs at a time is magnificent sport and very effective in places where the quail bevies are not plentiful and where the country is large and appropriate for the purpose of allowing you to watch all your dogs at one time. The dogs will, of course, have to be expert and perfectly trained. 

I have many times shot with four or six dogs at a time in the young henequen (hemp) plantations of Yucatan. During such occasions there have been moments when each dog would be pointing different bevies, and what a pleasure it gave me to attend to each point and notice how stanch the rest would be until ordered to carry on.

Several dogs are generally loosed at a time so that the bevies may be discovered sooner. Once birds have been found, one or two dogs are still left loose so as to range for the single birds. Afterwards all are again released so as to again find another bevy. 

When taking a puppy to these shoots, he should be chaperoned by other skillful dogs, which have hunted together previously. 

First attention should be given to the puppy's point, or to the dog which is least stanch, leaving those which are steady and most stanch till later. 

After attending to the dog's point he should lie down at the same place or he should be made to follow at heel so as to give one more confidence and scope for shooting. 

English sportsmen are in the habit of shooting with a couple of dogs and many are also accompanied by retrievers, which follow quietly at heel and without leaving their master's side until the opportune time for retrieving. After the pointers and setters have pointed, they remain impassively lying down waiting until the game has been retrieved by the retriever and the latter has once more resumed his station at his master's heel, where he follows calmly again, when his companions renew their energetic search.

In other parts of Europe and in North America, very few sportsmen employ the foregoing method of hunting, but use the same dogs for pointing as for retrieving. 

When shooting with several dogs at once and after having found a bevy it is advisable to allow your slowest and steadiest dog to look for the single birds and retrieve the dead ones. Meanwhile the other dogs will be resting, to return later to the search for

On these occasions, be careful not to take a dog which is liable to rush off at the shot and chase the game, for the bad example may easily affect the rest of the dogs.

SHOOTING OVER WATER OR SWAMPS 

When shooting waterfowl the dog generally acts merely as a means of retrieving dead or wounded birds. 

Duck shooting is generally done from behind screens or blinds, so as to take them when flying past or making for water. The dog should be lying behind the blind with the trainer or in a special screen of his own.

Put the check cord and spike collar on him at first and oblige him to remain in the place you select for him without allowing him to move until ordered. Do not tie him up as he will not learn, when he is loose, to remain quiet that way. He must be taught, by orders, not to move, not by tying him up. If he commits any faults, take him by the cord and shake him with it, forcing him to stay still; and if he still insists on being restless, give him a taste of the whip, but letting him associate the cause with the order to lie quiet. 

After you have shot and when you judge it suitable, call the dog, take the collar off him if there is much brush or undergrowth where he might be entangled, and then order him to "fetch" the dead or wounded ducks. Some dogs become out of hand in the water when retrieving and will stay there, refusing to come out and, of course, upsetting further proceedings this way. Some are a model of obedience on land, but as soon as they get into water become disobedient and deaf to any orders, however much you may shout to them, for they realize that they are out of their master's control and act as they please. Such dogs should be sent into the water with check cord and spike collar on, so that they can be pulled forcibly when called. 

Shooting from a boat reduces still more the dog's sphere of cooperation, which is merely limited to retrieving such birds as the boat cannot get near enough to. The dog should lie at the bottom of the

boat so as not to be in the way. As most boats are subject to leaking, it is advisable to put foliage or weeds on the bottom to keep the moisture off, especially in cold climates. This should also be done inside the blinds or screens. 

Shooting over swamps where, as you go along, the ducks continually take to wing, reduces the dog's scope of action to "forcing to wing" and "retrieving."

Always bear in mind that it is inadvisable to make a dog retrieve wounded birds of the heron family, because it might very easily cause him the loss of an eye or make a nervous puppy timid. For this reason 

I recommend killing off wounded mud hens, for they are given to pecking the dog in the water and, should the dog be inexperienced, it will make him timid. 

These should only be retrieved by a reliable dog.

Shooting snipe is very interesting when done with the aid of a pointing dog, but in places where they are very abundant, the dog's services are restricted principally to retrieving and for this reason he should keep near the trainer. When these birds are not abundant and are found in newly tilled ground, corn fields, inundated land and shallow swamps, then the dog is able to point them as well as quail if he has had any previous opportunity of knowing the former, for some dogs will disregard them out of contempt at first. Those dogs which have not been trained to retrieve by the force system may very likely refuse to retrieve snipe.

German pointers (braques) and pointing griffons are very useful for this class of work, but I do not, for a single instant, mean to imply that pointers and setters are not equally adaptable, especially the latter. 

To teach a dog to search for snipe he should be taken to a place where these birds are known to be present and allow him to range for them at will without mattering if, at first, he only flushes them. Very soon the dog will learn to seek and point them and he will instinctively make for those places where there is less water in order to facilitate things. If after having had opportunities to point he still refrains from doing so, proceed as instructed in the chapter on "Pointing."

PRACTICAL EDUCATION SHOOTING WITH SPANIELS AND RETRIEVERS AND THEIR LINE OF TRAINING 

There are two ways of shooting with the spaniel: one, using them to move the game as beaters so that the game will make in the direction of the hunters, or else working ahead of the gun and retrieving the dead or wounded birds after they have flushed them within, gun shot. For the former case there is no necessity of a thorough training but merely the training given any dog which works in packs and the idea is to encourage his enthusiasm for hunting so that he shows keenness and comparative obedience. The other case, the one which will take more of my attention, is the one which requires most care in order to prove of utmost use to the hunter and is the subject of this chapter. 

The use of these intrepid dogs when shooting over thickly covered ground, as well as in swamps, is very important. In more open country they are not so useful as, owing to their short working range and to the fact that they do not, as a rule, point, they are handicapped.

The well-trained spaniel should be limited to use in seeking and making the game take wing so that his master may get opportunities to shoot. He should not go after the game, once it has been forced to wing and, still less, after the shot. He should be able to retrieve, on land or in water, anything which his little body will permit him to, for, though intrepid and willing to attempt retrieving any big sized game, such as a hare or large duck, they are not always able to do so and they then endeavor to drag it, being unable to carry it properly. They retrieve smaller sized game admirably and enthusiastically. They are priceless at working over ravines and rabbits find them in such places a very formidable enemy. 

For a huntsman who is not well off, the spaniel is a magnificent companion, for his keep is not costly.

His searching range is keen and short, going over and through everything he sees and generally giving tongue when coming on a fresh scent. His education will consist in giving him a little opportunity in the field where game abounds, so that his keenness for the work will be awakened. This should not be overdone for, instead of getting keen, he may learn bad habits-which might easily happen, when one considers his natural ardor-and would make his training harder.

Afterwards give him "Yard Training" in the same way as previously described and following the identical lessons. Also the retrieving lessons, but you will, of course, require a special spike collar, for, considering his size, it is only reasonable to suppose that a collar intended for a large dog will not suit his size. If you cannot get the right size have one specially made on the lines of the ordinary big ones. 

Once he has had his "yard" and "retrieving" lessons, take him out to the field, having his spike collar and check cord on him. Make him search within a radius of fifteen yards either side of you. As soon as you notice by his movements or barking that the game is about to flush or go off, take up the cord so that you can force him to lie down at the moment that the furred or winged game starts away. At that very moment order him "down," either by word, whistle or sign. Keep him lying down a while and then commence to search once more. Repeat the lesson until you have succeeded in making him steady after the game has gone. Should he be too eager, allow him to chase after the game; but you have him on the cord and, at the moment he is at its length, give a hard jerk. This will make him more careful and he will soon learn to stay firm. You may strike him on the shoulder with the whip as previously explained, associating it, for his guidance, with the chasing after the game.

He will previously have been shown, in the manner explained, to become accustomed to the shot and to drop on hearing the report. Once he has learned to stand firm at the flight of the game, you may commence shooting at it, but he should drop at every shot. 

Never fire should he give chase, for should you do so and kill your game it will encourage him to chase always and would make it very hard to break him of this fault, which is a serious one for it handicaps your sport and might even be the cause of the dog receiv ing the charge intended for the game.

In going over very densely covered ground, it is advisable to put a bell or rattle on him so as to know his exact whereabouts. After shooting over such ground always clean his eyes and wash them with a solution of boric acid, otherwise they may become sore.

The retriever, as implied by the name, is the dog best adapted for recovering dead or wounded game, being really wonderful at that kind of work. His use

They are also used next day for going over grounds which were shot over the day previous and thus recover any amount of birds which died later or are wounded still. FEAR OF THE DETONATION AND FIREARM 

Fear of the detonation reveals itself, in dogs, in different ways and originates from numerous causes which must be considered carefully so as to remedy the defect, for without doubt it is a most serious handicap and very detrimental towards making the canine a useful factor as a gun-dog. Many authorities claim that this defect is hereditary, whereas others maintain that the dog does not inherit the fear of the firearm but that his nervous temperament, which is certainly inherited, predisposes him to contract gunshyness and the latter is mainly attributable to mismanagement on the trainer's part. I certainly am of the latter opinion. Fear in the dog is, in most forms, caused by the nervous system and most frequently among those of very refined breeding, where there is more likelihood of consanguinity, this same having probably occurred between nervous individuals, sick animals or cases suffering from worms. The nervous condition becomes aggravated on occasions such as sharp change of temperature, especially on windy,

foggy or cold days; that is to say, on such days as even will affect the human nervous system as I have been able to observe from personal experience. 

The principal signs and causes are as follows: 

(1) Fear of the report of any firearm, either in the house, in the open or elsewhere, even though the firearm cannot be seen. Cause:-Predisposition. The firing of one or various shots near him without having familiarized him with the noise. Having fired at him either before or after training. If this was not of much consequence, through not having made him continue work or retrieve directly afterwards, so as to show him it was not intentional or a punishment.

Too much punishment when teaching him to drop to shot without familiarizing him with the detonation first. Taking him, when a puppy, out shooting or to some event where considerable shooting was done at the same time. (2) Firing only in the yard and not when actually shooting in the field. Cause:-The dog's nervous system, which makes firing in an enclosed space terrify. ing to him without the combined incentive of shooting whilst hunting, or else having given him the drop to shot lessons too rigorously during yard training. This is principally evident in fully grown dogs which have been taken out shooting previously and allowed to break off at the shot, then when correcting this fault without good judgment during yard training they have been scared. (3) Upon seeing that you are about to fire or at merely seeing the firearm. Cause:-As a consequence of the knowledge the dog has of the noise producing instrument which also caused him an injury when he was shot at. The movement which precedes the shot when teaching him to lie down at the detonation or on seeing the gun brought up to the point. Also through being tied up to his kennel or elsewhere and watching the gun being pointed at him and even fired at times, such a nervous tension finally affecting his system. (4) Of the pistol and not of the gun, or vice-versa. 

Cause:-When he only knows one of these two firearms as the instrument which has been the cause of scaring him. (5) of the report of a percussion cap and not of a loaded charge. Cause:-Merely a question of nerves which the penetrating sound of a percussion cap creates in him or when the lessons of dropping to shot have been given with caps in an irrational manner.

If the dog is really worth the trainer's trouble and patience, the following treatment may be given him: (1) Get him into good health, giving him worm treatment and purge if his nervousness is caused by worms. (2) Encourage the hunting spirit in him by every available method, either with game in the open country or by allowing him to chase and even kill tame rabbits. (3) Let him be accompanied by a bold dog which will chase any game he may see, especially furred game.

Whilst this period of curing gun-shyness is proceed. ing, no firearm of any kind should be handled, the only object being to arouse in the dog the utmost keenness for the hunt. Once you have done this the real serious treatment may commence. 

Keep the dog in a kennel away from the others, if you have other dogs with you. Tie him up with an ordinary smooth collar and chain, always letting him have fresh drinking water close to him. Leave him without food for twenty-four hours. After that lapse of time go to him with a plate full of food in one hand and a gun in the other. Should the dog on seeing his master approach him with food and gun come out and commence eating regardless of the sight of the gun, the defect will not be of such a serious nature and can be easily remedied. But if he merely smells his food, takes a bite and retires back to his kennel, then the defect is of a more serious nature. In whatever way he has behaved upon seeing his master approach him with his dinner, this should not be given him immediately, but he should be allowed just a taste of it to excite his appetite and the food should be placed just beyond his reach where he can see it. 

Leave it there, withdraw about ten or fifteen steps and commence handling the gun, holding it across the arms or pointing it but without saying a word or noticing the dog. Should the dog, instead of watching

his food, retire into his kennel through fear of the gun, go toward him and, without a word or taking notice of him, take his food and allow him to stay another twenty-four hours without eating. Next day go through the same motions. During each period of twenty-four hours the dog should be left alone and not loosened for exercise, but should only be visited by the trainer at the termination of the twenty-four hours during the lessons of handling the gun whilst placing his food near him. If after three days he still persists in showing gun-shyness, leave him one day more. He will then almost certainly, on seeing his master and food appearing, come out eagerly, without regard to anything else. If, at this stage, he pays no attention to the gun motions, even though you point and play about with the gun, you have arrived at the time when you may commence firing. 

But should he still show fear, he should be left one day more, for on the fifth day of this process it may be taken for certain that no dog will allow anything to interfere with his hunger and voracious appetite. 

The dog will heed nothing now, for his hunger will be so intense. Give him a bite of his food and leave the dish just beyond his reach. The trainer will now retire to about fifty or sixty paces from the dog and, without apparently noticing him nor pointing in his direction, fire off a cartridge with only a little powder, without looking towards the dog so that he may not imagine the shot was intended for him. In this way fire a few more shots and with all calmness and unconcern, holding the gun in your arms, speak quietly and encouragingly to the dog, giving him another mouthful of food, moving the gun about at the same time without seeming, when doing this, to notice the dog. Go the same distance away but in another direction and fire a few more shots with a slightly stronger charge, gradually increasing the strength of these. 

Again go towards him, as before, with the gun in your arms. Take the food and, standing in front of him, act as though you intended giving him it all, but merely give him one good mouthful and move away again, leaving the food once more beyond his reach. Go a shorter distance off this time and fire a few more shots. During all this time the dog will be straining forwards on his chain stretching out his front paws to reach the food so as to finish it regardless of the firing. Again walk up to him and give him a few more mouthfuls; then walk a little distance away

with the food, say towards a door. Place the dish on the floor and commence once more handling and pointing the gun in different directions. The dog will be intently watching his food and will involuntarily be watching the gun movements as well. Go back to the kennel, lean the gun against it and give the dog a little more food at the same time caressing him. 

Again go away with the gun and food and disappear through the door where you had just previously stood. 

The dog will be intently watching the door with his appetite more keenly roused than ever. Half-an-hour later return with the gun and with the dish fuller than ever of appetizing food. The dog will show pleasure, expecting now to appease his appetite, but place the dish at about two or three yards from him and withdraw about thirty paces. Commence firing in different directions, but not in the direction of the dog. After a few shots go up to the dog and caress him a little, giving him another mouthful. If he showed no signs of fear when last firing, diminish the distance to twenty paces and, after a few more shots, give him one or two mouthfuls. If you notice that he shakes at each shot, this will be caused by firing too near and the distance should be increased until the dog shows no signs of minding the shooting.

Accompany his daily feeding with shooting, diminishing the distance little by little, until you are able to fire within a few paces from him, not forgetting to reward him in the manner explained after each shot. 

By means of this form of training the dog cannot fail to see that the gun shot is harmless to him. Once the dog, upon seeing his master coming towards him with gun and food shows keen delight, it will be time to take him out to the field for further training. 

It will no longer be necessary to keep him fasting so strictly, but a slight diet is still needed. Take a piece of bread and meat with you in your pocket. Go toward the dog, taking your gun in hand, and put a spike collar on him with a length of six or eight yards of cord, having a knot at one end so as to be able to attach it to your belt especially intended for this purpose in the field. Take the easy collar off him. Then take the piece of bread and meat out of your pocket and put it back again, letting the dog see you do this. Do not give him anything to eat as yet, but order him "come on" and start for the field, resting a second now and again so as to feed him small morsals of bread, keeping the rest and continuing walking.

Upon arriving at the right place take a small bit of bread in your hand and let him smell it without eating it, at the same time handle the gun freely and indifferently. Again let him smell and lick the bread and then throw it on one side about two or three yards off. The dog will immediately lunge for it and at the precise moment when he is about to eat it, fire the gun in the opposite direction. Even if the dog should get frightened and attempt to escape he will not be able to, owing to being fastened by the cord to your belt and he will be halted when getting to the cord's end, when the spike collar will come into play. Take no notice of all this, but continue firing until he returns of his own free will to pick up the pieces of meat you will also throw down for him.

When he has come several times and he has been given several pieces more to eat, change the place for another and again throw down pieces of meat and fire shots at the same time. When he is no longer gunshy, he should be taken to a place where there is game, preferably quail, if he is more enthusiastic about these. If not, take him to the game he prefers or even give him tame rabbits if you used these in awakening his keenness. Whatever game is being hunted be careful that it is in open ground so that without loosening him from the cord he can seek and point, for in this way you will be able to prevent him running away, which would be disastrous. 

Once he has pointed and at the moment the game attempts taking to wing, fire and be careful not to miss your shot, killing with one barrel and as nearby as possible so that the dog can realize exactly what has occurred without preventing him breaking point or chasing off at the shot and picking the dead bird up, even letting him chew it should he wish and encouraging him in this. If he wants to retrieve, let him do so. 

Do not let it matter if he has mutilated the bird and, should he wish to eat it, do not prevent him from doing so, but always taking it from him with all signs of keenness and interest. Now give him a good-sized piece of bread and meat, but bit by bit, speaking to him and showing satisfaction with each bite given him. Continue hunting, rewarding him with more bits of bread and meat until you have finished the supply. Return home but do not fire any more on your way back. Keep the dog on a certain diet and take him out shooting each day until all

It is also advisable during this part of the training to have the dog accompanied by another dog which is very keen and rushes off at the shot to recover the dead game. The sight of another dog giving chase after a rabbit will arouse his ardor and help to make him bold. The rabbit should be fired at and killed so that the dog may realize that the shot is the cause of the killing and allows him to get the game more quickly. If it is also intended to cure the dog of pistol-shyness, this can be done on the same lines by substituting the pistol for the gun during the first lessons. Fear of crackers and any other noises can be cured by the hunger method and by the firing of crackers further away from him at first and decreasing the distance, little by little, until he is no longer scared.

In places where trap-shooting is indulged in, good results can be obtained in curing shyness of the noise by taking him there constanly during tournaments.

The large number of shots fired continuously will make him accustomed to detonation. Take the dog, tied up so that he will not escape, and leave him tied up some distance from the firing line and gradually diminish the distance. On no account speak to or caress him at these times, however worried he may be. Indifference is the surest way to cure him. At first the dog will try to escape, but by constantly taking him there he will become accustomed to the noise and surroundings. You may tie him up in the shooting gallery during firing and feed him there. In time he will become insensible to the shooting and the sight of the guns.

By these means I cured a German police dog which had a great dread of shooting and of the sight of firearms. To such an extent was he scared of the shots that on the first day he literally shook as though with convulsions or in an attack of epilepsy.

FEAR OF THE GAME AND LEAVING THE POINT "BLINKERS" 

This defect also shows itself among dogs suffering from nervousness and other particulars previously referred to, becoming evident in different forms. 

The feathered game, which gets up noisily and without warning, as is the case with partridge (our 

Mexican quail), etc., and generally with all gallinacious birds, is that which nearly always causes the most alarm among puppies and even full grown but inexperienced dogs, this fear being less frequent when hunting furred game because the dog then becomes keener, the alarming noise not being evident as in the former case. Nevertheless, there are times when the dog is afraid, even then.

Those dogs which desist from pointing, for any reason, as also those which are afraid of game, are called "blinkers" in the field trial vernacular. principal forms and causes of this "game shyness" are as follows:

The 

(1) The dog running off, with tail down, unnecessarily hastening from the spot where the game showed, stopping when at a certain distance and watching the spot from where he fled; returning again to hunt keenly. Cause:-The sudden scare which all nervous organisms suffer from any unexpected commotion and more especially so when not accustomed to it. It is noteworthy that when a trained though nervous dog, "se tape" as the French say of a dog which, without knowing it, gets among the game and startles it, he drops more rapidly when the game "takes wing" in this way than when it has been scented or pointed and therefore expected. (2) The dog becomes alive to the presence of the game by sight, scent or ear and at once discontinues the hunt, without any inclination to resume; and, in many cases, coming to heel. Causes:-Generally this is due to an error of the trainer, who, through desiring to make the dog "stanch" when pointing, or when the game takes wing, punishes him unduly or in an irrational manner. Also through having shot at the dog, as some will imprudently do, in order to punish him for having broken point or chasing the game; and finally, through being gunshy and scared of the detonation, for the dog realizes that upon the game being noticed, the noise of the shot follows as a consequence and he remembers the experience of past chastisements.

I may be mistaken but I am of opinion that dogs known as "blinkers," which do not desert the point from fear or some similar reason (merely abandon it temporarily and only show a lack of constancy) do so, it seems reasonable to think, through a disposition to work in the manner our predecessors called pointing and circling, and perhaps such dogs, with proper training, would return to this form of working, which, if not so showy as stanch point, can be of great service when the game is on the move and separated whilst feeding. 

The great arbalister, Don Alfonso Martinez de

He further states: "Here you see the different methods dogs employ in hunting. The best are those which point and also circle round and those which merely move around. But, on the other hand, if working with a good dog which points and then circles around, he is preferable to the others."

As has been seen by the preceding lines, the best dog was not thought to be the one which merely pointed. And from what I have learned by practice, for the present-day hunter, the point and circle dog would often be of enormous value, because even if he should not be so useful in finding the game crouched close to the ground, he certainly would be invaluable in facilitating opportunities of shooting game which runs swiftly, as has been my experience with our quail when running and my dog was a pointer called 

Prince W. Kent. In Yucatan, I have seen this dog point and, on finding that the birds ran, either because they had seen us or because they were feeding, Prince would desist from pointing, go to the rear and then making a detour, would appear ahead of them. 

At times he would make several rings around the birds, thus prevent their escape and, bunching them, he would continue to point motionlessly. To amuse my friends, inside a chicken run I would order this same dog, to round up all the fowls, which he would succeed in doing in a few minutes, cornering them at a certain place where he remained pointing and motionless so long as they made no endeavor to escape; but just as soon as they moved, he would cut them off and collect them once more. It can be said that he worked the same as a good sheepdog herding sheep. If he could do this with hens, why should it appear strange that he did the same with wild game? And often, owing to his skill, I have had occasion to get many quail. When working on the bordo* of a maguey plantation, I have frequently seen Prince point, then change his plan and, running along the same bordo-but from the opposite side, he would advance and get ahead of the quail, which were running along the same bordo-and thus stop them, for on seeing him ahead, the birds would stand still until I reached them. This had never been taught him; it came natural to him. When game stood still he would stand firmly pointing, as also when they took wing and at the shot. I have seen this trait in other dogs, but never so pronounced as in him. 

For these reasons I am of opinion that if a dog moves from pointing to work in this fashion, it is not only good but I wish all dogs had this trait.

METHODS 

Get the dog's health into good shape. Give him a drug should worms be the cause of his nervousness, afterwards repeating on each occasion when necessary and convenient. Always having in mind that firmness and patience are indispensable.

When the case is one of nervousness owing to inexperience, it will be ample to have him accompanied, when possible, by another bolder dog and thus give him the opportunity of familiarizing himself with the hunt. If the dog is not gun-shy, you may fire at your bird when he is in a position to see it drop, without ordering him to stand still, but on no account if he has showed fear, for the detonation would only serve to increase his terror and cause him to become gunshy. If there are any indications of showing fear of the gun, on no account should you shoot, for, as has been explained, it would only prove of fatal consequences. the game with a bold dog for a companion, will prove an efficient remedy. 

That the dog should notice the game and try to avoid it is a defect of greater consequence than the former, for in that case there was cause which produced the fear and in this case it must be studied from all sides in order to proceed with the greatest accuracy. If the cause has been that he is afraid of the detonation and he is aware that the game is the direct cause of this, it will be necessary to go out without a gun, accompanied, also, by a bold dog, tying them together if the trained dog is stronger and can pull the other. On seeing the game do not attempt to stop him from chasing, on the contrary, let him do as he likes. If you have no other dog to be a companion, the trainer should take him to where there is game and when the dog has realized or seen it, without saying a word he should endeavor to make it take wing and give chase, even though only a few steps, the dog, imitating, will little by little become keen.

It is a bad habit to insist persistently in trying to make him seek, because with many dogs this insistency may be the cause of its fear and the dislike of hunting, which may become aggravated. 

The dog which, upon seeing his master, desists from pointing and runs to heel does so owing to the harshness with which he has been treated when training him to point firmly, or because he has been shot at when giving chase to some game, which will be sufficient for such an incident to linger in his memory when he sees his master approaching him, and so he places himself at his master's heels. For such dogs a change of master and the companionship of other dogs may be a cure. It must not be forgotten that if this fear is due to the report of the gun, he will have to be given the treatment already described for this failing. There are dogs with such intellect that when hunting with other ambitious dogs that poach their companion's points, which though ordinarily steady, on seeing such dogs come near, will leave their work and return to their masters as though imploring protection and showing disgust for such companions. In such dogs fear is not the reason of their abandonment, but intelligence, for in this way they believe they will free themselves from having their work interrupted. These dogs, on being separated from such companions, will return to steady work.

The dog which, for the third reason just given, leaves his point, does not, in my opinion, merit any reproach; but if it is intended to break him of this habit it will be sufficient to make him lie down, at the spot where he stood pointing when interrupted, merely by word. Nevertheless, I am of the opinion that if the dog is intended for a hunter, he should not be broken of that habit, but encouraged in it. 

Old-time hunters, in order to teach the dog to circle round the game, used to drive a stake in the ground and tie two ropes to the dog's collar; one of which was also tied to the spike and the other was held by the trainer in order to oblige the dog to run with the trainer around a tasty morsel of food which was placed by the stake, allowing him to eat it after having given several turns and having approached it spirally. This same operation was afterwards gone through with game or a bird (in place of the food), which they killed after having given the necessary circles, making the dog stand still during the moment of shooting and ordering him to fetch the dead bird. 

Neither does it constitute a fault if the dog leaves the bird in order to return in search of the trainer and conduct him to where he left the game. 

An expert trainer can teach the dog to return in search of his master when the point has been beyond his vision, but this cannot be done by all, as the manner of training him for this is slow and requires the utmost care, the way being to teach the dog to leave his point on being called, generally by whistle. The first attempts are done by tying a long leash to the dog and, when he is pointing, call him, also pulling on the leash. When he comes up, pat him and then send him back to where he had just pointed. When he obeys well at short distances, gradually withdraw further away until he is out of sight and in this way the dog will understand the idea so that he will return, uncalled, when he perceives that his master is not near him, it being sufficient that one stands in a clear place so that the dog may see him.

This part of the training, I repeat, is not easy to do successfully, for it requires great patience and constant opportunities so that the dog learns and appreciates what is desired.

DOGS WHICH RUN AWAY OR WORK BEYOND RANGE, AND BOLTERS 

Dogs which run away when called are more numerous than might be imagined and this trouble is generally caused from fear of the master or trainer. 

Causes:-Generally, bad management on the part of the trainer, especially on attempting to give the dog one or another lesson without having the check cord on him to prevent his running away when having a fault corrected. Also owing to change of system; that is to say, from a trainer's previous teaching under the method of kindness to use of the whip when the dog has disobeyed or committed a fault, applying the latter when the dog was loose instead of the trainer having hold of at least his collar or hide so that on chastising him he cannot escape. Once this happens, the dog will endeavor to escape at every other recurrence.

Such dogs, after they have committed a fault, will stand at a certain distance from their master and, however much one may call to them, they will not come, merely awaiting developments and on the qui vive to run away at the slightest attempt to catch them, not stopping till they get back home; or it may even happen that they run away aimlessly, sometimes getting lost.

Treatment:-Provided they have been trained with the spike collar, if you put this on the dog with the check cord, it will often be ample, for them to realize that they must not run away, but in the majority of cases it will be necessary to teach them to lose all fear of their master. If they have not been thoroughly yard trained, this should be done, afterwards working them in the field, having a loose cord attached to the collar which they can drag along the ground, but on no account punish or scold them without having hold of the rope, or attempt to run up to them shouting. 

Another way of breaking the dog from attempting to run away or fear of the whip is this: Have the long check cord in your left hand and pretend to strike him with the whip in the other. Do not let him know you have hold of the check cord's extremity. 

When he has reached the end of the cord, pull him sharply, even though he falls over. Then call him and caress and pat him. Repeat this until he is cured and use the same methods to cure him of running from you when you run up to him.

Dogs which get out of range-that is to say, dogs which work too far away-are those which once loosened in the fields will commence hunting on their own, regardless of the trainer's presence. Among these there are various kinds, but they may be analyzed in three classes. (1) Those which while working near their trainer are obedient but just as soon as they get some distance away become disobedient and out of hand and give not the slightest attention to orders. 

Cause. Nearly always owing to defective training and of a very enthusiastic dog.

Treatment:-Give a thorough yard-training course and work them always with the spike collar and check cord in the fields for a long period. This also applies to a dog which has been well trained under the spike collar system in all its routine but later, owing to defective management on the part of his master, has developed this same defect. By putting the spike collar and check cord on him and giving him a reminder of his training days, it will usually be sufficiently effective. In proper hands the dog will very soon become as efficient and obedient as he was at first. (2) Those are dogs which, without being absolute bolters, are liable to start hunting independently and will not obey until they have tired themselves. 

Cause:-Extreme keenness and an independent temperament.

Treatment:-Continual work and the attention of a good trainer. (3) Bolters are hardly known in Europe. They are frequently the outcome of the way in which field trials have been conducted in the United States for many years and training they have been subjected to for several generation. They are taught much initiative, speed, resistance and bird sense (sentido de caza) in preference to discipline and obedience.

These dogs, as I have previously said, are of great action and have an enormous hunting enthusiasm, which is the reason that once they are loose in the fields, they make off at great pace in the direction they think the game may be found, and it frequently happens that master and dog may not see each other again for the rest of the day, or even several days later, if at the start of his escapade the dog unfortunately has not come across any game which might stop them.

It often happens that during field trials these dogs create a very favorable impression, for their great enthusiasm and their enormous ranging power draws · attention, especially if they have succeeded in quickly coming upon game. But how much is this to the contrary if they are not lucky enough to do so, for they then continue ranging of their own free will and any efforts on the part of the trainer to stop them are in vain. Some of these dogs, after finding game and after the birds have taken wing, return in search of their master, while others which have been taught to abandon the point will return to their master in order to take him to the game, as previously explained.

These bolters, as they are called, can never be relied upon and they are as likely to do the right thing as not, but it is generally the latter. 

Treatment:-A good yard-training under the force system, after which they should be taken to a place where there is plenty of game so that they are not forced to range far away from you. Take them out daily so as to deaden to a certain extent, their excessive ardor. 

Before closing on the matter of these dogs I will repeat what I have already said, that in Europe these dogs are scarcely known because the training there is more rigid in so far as discipline is concerned, and not so much attention is given to the development of natural instincts, which is the main factor in field trials in the United States.

DOGS WHICH LOSE THE HUNTING SPIRIT 

There are many dogs which, while puppies, seem to have great enthusiasm for hunting, but which later on lose it. It often happens that they fall into the hands of one who, one way or another, has slackened their enthusiasm, either by (1) lack of work or (2) through wrong training and too much discipline, which finally stamps out all keenness and creates a repugnance for hunting. There are full-grown dogs which have been out with guns and then lost all enthusiasm.

Causes of No. 1.-(a) Lack of work will cause deadening of the instincts; (b) an overrigorous training on the lines of the force system when puppies, will create an aversion and kill their ardor.

Causes of No. 2.-(a) Brutal treatment in the field which makes the dog lose all previous keenness; (b) an abnormal condition of the dog, such as being too fat, etc.

TREATMENT 

For No. 1.-(a) Reawaken his instincts by taking him out hunting, as well as with another good, keen dog as a companion. (b) Leave off training for a long period; a change of trainers and a course of hunting on his own, in company with other dogs. 

For No. 2. (a) The same as for "b" above-change of trainers and the stimulus of other dogs. (b) When caused by poor condition, get the dog fit by means of proper nourishment, exercise and taking out on shooting trips.

BREAKING SHOT AND CHASING THE GAME, AS WELL AS RUSHING OUT, BARKING AND FOLLOWING VEHICLES 

As a consequence of defective or entire lack of training, dogs will rush off at the shot and chase the game unnecessarily. This is especially noticeable when the dog belongs to a man who does not give him enough attention during shooting, but is entirely wrapped up in his shots and who will run to pick up the fallen bird without attention to the dog's movements, caring naught to keep the dog steady a certain while after the shot and obliging the dog to retrieve after having made him stay still the required length of time.

The dog should be given a certain amount of yard training if he lacks this. Out in the field keep the spike collar and check cord on him, which is an infallible cure. If the dog does not point stanchly, make him become so by giving him the training described in the chapter on "Pointing." If he points stanchly, put the spike collar on him without the check cord, allowing him to search with it on. When he points go up to him and put a check cord ten yards long on to the spike collar, taking hold of the other end with both hands; then cause the game to take wing. Do not order him to stay firm, but let him chase after the game until he gets to the cord's end, then give him an energetic tug and bring him up with a good round turn. Then, with the aid of the check cord drag him back to the identical spot where he had just pointed and order him "down," applying the whip if he disobeys.

Repeat this lesson as often as necessary, but you may be sure that after a few repetitions he will be effectively cured of rushing off in this way.

Afterwards use the leash instead of the check cord and tie one end to the spike collar and the other end to your special strap, so that both your hands will be free for shooting.

This lesson should preferably be given in open country.

Dogs, no matter of what breed, which rush out into the streets barking at people, coaches, etc., or which chase and kill fowl, pigs, etc., can be easily cured of this defect by using the spike collar and check cord on them in the way described above, having these on him and giving him a few lessons when occasions offer.

HOW AND WHEN THE DOG SCENTS THE GAME 

In this chapter I am going to point out to those who are beginning the task of training dogs, the manner in which it is probable that the scent of the game is spread and the way in which dogs are apt to notice it. It is my desire to avoid a misconception as to what are claimed as defects on the part of the dogs when really they are not, but are due to many circumstances which the person of little experience may attribute to the lack of sense of smell, bad training or unfitness on the part of the dog.

Any one beginning to know the aptitude and the work of a dog imagines that this animal must always scent the game anywhere it may be, without taking

into account the direction of the wind and many other causes that experience points out. In order to give more comprehensive idea of this matter, I have drawn the accompanying diagrams, which plainly show the places at which the dog will probably scent the game with the greatest ease.

Anyone Let my readers imagine that the odor given forth by the game may be likened to the smoke which proceeds from a heap of some burning material. can understand that owing to atmospheric reasons sometimes the smoke goes straight upwards, while at others it extends in all directions, but most frequently whenever the wind will carry it over an area which is greater or less according to the intensity of the smoke and the rapidity with which the wind blows. One understands, also, that the smoke is always more intense and narrower at the beginning and spreads over a wider space the farther it goes. Anyone who notices this will understand that there are many places where one may be near this column of smoke and yet not be able to smell it in any way, while there are other places where one can smell it more or less distinctly. The same thing happens with the dog. However good his scenting organs may be, it is impossible for him to scent the game if he is not at a place where there may be found some of the existing odor. Thus it is that in order that the nostrils of the dog should notice the smell, it is necessary that he should be within the cone where the emanation of scent is to be found, as can be noticed in Diagram 36, where the wind is represented by the large arrow, the game by the letter A, the cone where the scent is found by the two lines, and each dog by the small numbered arrows. 

Dog No. 1 goes before the game and is entirely outside of the cone; therefore it is impossible for him to scent it because the wind carries off such scent. No. 2 has gone outside of the cone, but perhaps may notice some of the scent when he is at a distance from the game, where the scent is almost imperceptible, and that is why he very often does not take it into account. Dog No. 3 is going to enter the cone in the same direction as the wind; therefore he will point in an uncertain manner and will have to return in order to be able to know surely where the game is. We must take into account the fact that the dog when in broken ground finds greater difficulty to scent, owing to the obstacles existing in such cases and the total lack of wind. When the game is in motion it is easier for the dog to scent it, because the odor arising from it not only is in the air, but also on the ground, on the bushes and on other things that the game may touch with its body, and this undoubtedly is a great help to the dog in finding the game. With regard to quails when in bevies, it is easier for the dog to scent them than when one is alone. There are moments when this bird gives forth less scent owing to some circumstance in which its breath is softer and it transpires very little. The dog not only scents the odor that comes from the pores, but also its breath, if we may so style it.

The well known French writer, Benoit, has said a great deal on this subject and has even stated posi tively that some game through their instinct of selfpreservation can very often retain their odor. I do not dare to judge his opinion, but common sense tells me that a bird which has been flying and then rests on the ground without moving, having dissipated its scent in the air at that moment, if taken unaware 1

will through the law of self-preservation gather in its wings and feathers to its body and keep back its breath. The dog that at such time goes near the place where the bird, is probably will not scent it.

All hunters having some experience may have noticed very often that in such a case even a dog with the keenest nose will go by near a quail or partridge (that perhaps the hunter is looking at) without noticing it. A little while afterwards the partridge will move and the dog will scent it, even though far away. This sufficiently proves that the dog had failed to scent it before, not through any lack of scenting faculty but owing to circumstances over which he has no control. 

Dogs do not find easily birds that fall on the ground when killed, as those that have left some trail.

The condition of the atmosphere is a matter of great importance for the scent of a dog. During rainy days the trails are scarcely noticeable and the same may be said of the scent given forth by the body of the game, owing to its being wet. Snow and very cold days are also bad for the scent of game, just as very dry or hot weather. A little rain on sandy soil helps the trail, but rain spoils it on clayish ground or ground covered with underbrush. Strong winds are bad, especially if coming from the North. The best time for a dog to scent is when the weather is not very dry, when there is some wind and in open country.

A rabbit or hare which has been running a little gives forth more scent than those that have remained without moving or have got too tired.

A great many opinions have been submitted concerning the difficulty of the pointing dog to find, by scent, nests and also fowls when brooding and their young ones, this being due, as they say, to the power of such animals to retain their odor by instinct of self-preservation, thus preventing the pointing dog from scenting them, no matter how near he may pass by. But I am of the opinion of the English author who says that the case is not exactly the lack of the spreading of the odor of such animals or their nests, but that this odor is somewhat different to the one which the pointing dog is accustomed to scent. I also add to this opinion that if the pointing dog was taught to become acquainted with this special odor he would no doubt learn in a short time to perceive it clearly.
