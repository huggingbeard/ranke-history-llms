THE PRACTICAL OPERATION OF THE NEW INDIANA SYSTEM OF TAXATION By PHILIP LUTZ, JR., Attorney General of Indi ana, Before the National Association of Attorneys General at Milwaukee, Wisconsin, August 

28, 1934, 11:00 A. M.

Indiana was confronted in the beginning of 1933 with very much the same situation as confronted other states of the Union-a rising deficit in all its taxing districts from the dwindling tax collections to defray the cost of government. 

This deficit was due to the failure of the usual ad valorem property tax to furnish sufficient revenue, which, in turn, was caused largely by decreased valuations and increased tax delinquencies. Similarly, although to a much greater extent, Indiana was re-experiencing what had occurred in 1849 when, following a period of liberal spending for what then was considered extensive internal improvements, property owners could not pay their taxes and one-third of them were defaulted and allowed to remain delinquent. 

When the General Assembly of the State met in 1933 it was apparent that the ordinary "so-called" property tax had failed. In addition to the enormous depreciation in real estate valuations and the growing tax delinquencies the financial situation of the State, as well as of the local governments, was further complicated by the enactment in 1932 of an act limiting the total levy on property in the State to $1.50 per $100 of valuation and limiting the rate for State purposes to 15c per $100. This enactment was the General Assembly's response to the almost universal demand of property owners all over the State for relief from what, with diminishing incomes, was proving to be a crushing tax burden too heavy to bear. Necessary as the relief undoubtedly was, its effect, combined with depreciation in valuations and delinquencies in collections, was to very materially reduce the State's available revenues as well as that of the various subdivisions of government. To illustrate: For many years prior to 1932 the rate for State purposes was approximately 29c per $100. (3) ww

The 1932 act reduced that almost by one-half. The local rates, of course, varied. However, in 1931-32 the valuation of property for taxation purposes throughout the entire State was $5,073,241,146. The 1933-34 estimate is $3,600,000,000. A one-cent levy under the former valuation would raise $507,324; under the latter only $360,000. By applying a 29c rate to the first valuation the total raised from such taxation is $14,712,396. By applying a 15c rate to the 1933-34 estimate the total which may be raised from such taxation is $5,400,000, a reduction in State revenues from such source in excess of $9,000,000. These figures, it will be observed, do not take into consideration relatively increased delinquencies which operate to further diminish the State's revenue from such sources. What is true in the case of the State is likewise true in case of local subdivisions.

Notwithstanding the application of a stricter economy in government, which was demanded from all groups, it was obvious that new sources of revenue must be found in order to adequately carry on the functions of government and meet obligations already incurred.

This, in brief, was the financial problem with which the State was confronted. How well it has met and solved this problem it is yet too soon to determine, but in my opinion through its new excise and license tax acts concerning which I am to speak there has been developed one of the finest and fairest systems of taxation supplementing, but at the same time relieving the old property tax system, that yet has been found.

I think I should say, before proceeding further, that the tax burden in Indiana has not been on account of the cost of State government so much as it has been because of the cost of local government, in which latter I am including local school expenses and the cost of the building and maintenance of highways outside of the State system. It is with pardonable pride, I think, that I am able to say that the system of State highways which threads the State, reaching every city and town of any considerable importance, is one of the finest systems to be found anywhere and that not a dollar of indebtedness exists against it.

In the case of local improved highways, however, the situation is different. Most of them were financed by bond issues. 

The same was true of many of the fine school buildings which

were erected during the period preceding the depression. 

These obligations, as well as the ordinary expenses of government, had to be met. The burden locally was heavy. The problem of how to reduce this local burden through State collections was paramount and pressing. The new license and tax acts of the State have met that problem in what I think will be found to be a constitutional method of redistribution of these collections to local units, which is itself unique, so that no question of unequal taxation can be successfully raised against it.

I have mentioned the school system of the State. I am proud of that, too, and proud also that I can say that, owing largely to the State's new tax acts, it is operating efficiently within the budgets provided. 

This record comes at a time when we learn that schoolhouses all over the country are being abandoned for lack of funds and that 2,000,000 children will be denied a school training in this country during the coming school year. In Indiana not one school has been forced to close for lack of funds, not a teacher is unpaid and no term has been shortened below that of eight months. Indiana is one of the three states only which can boast of this record. In my opinion this has been achieved by the daring service of men in our State who were willing to take an advanced step in tax legislation and school administration under the executive leadership of our able 

Governor, the Honorable Paul V. McNutt. 

We must remember that the property tax has failed as a fair and equitable tax-raising measure unless it is fairly supplemented by excise and license fees. In the North Dakota case, State, ex rel. Fargo v. Wetz, 168 N. W. 835, the court said: "Practically, the general property tax, as actually administered, is beyond all doubt one of the worst. taxes known to the civilized world. Because of its attempt to tax intangible as well as tangible things, it sins against the cardinal rules of uniformity, of equality, and of universality of taxation. It puts a premium on dishonesty and debauches the public conscience; it reduces deception to a system and makes a science of knavery; it presses hardest on those least able to pay; it imposes double taxation on one man and grants entire immunity to the next. In short, the

general property tax is so flagrantly inequitable that its retention can be explained only through ignorance or inertia. It is the cause of such crying injustice that its alteration or its abolition must become the battle cry of every statesman and reformer."

Before entering upon a discussion of the details of the new Indiana tax programs mention should be made of a law passed in 1933 that I regard as a valuable forerunner of the successful administration of the new tax plan. That is the 

Executive Administrative Act of 1933, which places in the hands of the Governor very broad powers in the administration of State affairs. Under this law one hundred and sixtynine units of State government-boards, departments and commissions-have been consolidated into eight administrative departments, each separately headed by an official, but all responsible to the Governor. These are the Executive Department, the Department of State, the Department of Audit and Control, the Department of Treasury, the Department of Commerce and Industry, the Department of Law, the Depart ment of Education and the Department of Public Works. The 

Governor has authority to hire and fire every officer, employee or servant of the Executive, including the Administrative Department, except constitutional officers and their first deputies. He is also empowered under the act to assign or reassign, transfer or retransfer any administrative power, duty or function to any one of the eight departments. He can also fix the compensation of any officer, employee or servant, but not exceeding that fixed by statute. You can see the vast power given the Governor by this act, perhaps the greatest power given any Governor in any of the states and the opportunity given him to establish a more economical and efficient administration of State affairs. Naturally, in a State like ours, one of the most politically conscious in the Union, a pivotal State where every Hoosier is a politician and statesman, this extraordinary power is the subject of bitter political controversy. But the same thing may be said of the extraordinary power given our President. I would not impose upon your courtesy extended me in giving me this place upon your program to advance any partisan position. I preface my remarks by admitting my high admiration for the sincere and high purposes and superlative ability of Governor McNutt.

I regard the new act as one made highly necessary at the time it was enacted because of the emergency conditions which prevailed in the first three months of 1933, and the results, under the administration of Governor McNutt, have, in every particular, justified this grant of power. The statistics show that he has reduced the cost of State administration from $43,166,888 in 1932 to $38,640,212.32 in the fiscal year ending 

June 30, 1934, showing a saving of $4,526,676.37, to which should be added $462,259.32 on account of administration of the excise law, the gross income tax and the intangibles law newly set up, showing a total saving of the State administration of more than $5,000,000. This, of course, is a small item compared with the savings in local government due to the tax policy of the State, involving economy and expenditures, for which the Governor is responsible, amounting to the amazing reduction of $41,000,000 in one year. I personally feel that while the State may take many advanced steps in efficiency and economy, authorized and possible under the Executive Administrative Act, the Governor as the business manager of the State's business has by his extraordinary accomplishments performed a service that justified the passage of the law.

Prior to the enactment of the new Indiana program in 1933 less than 5% of the intangible wealth of the State reached the tax duplicate. With a rate of taxes exceeding the gross income from intangibles there can be no wonder that owners made themselves liars and perjurers, and every State in the Union has had a like experience. With one year's experience the new law has created a larger return of taxes and has added to the duplicate twelve times the amount formerly listed. One bank in Indiana alone returned $56,000,000 of cash on deposit, which approximated the total of notes and cash formerly listed by all taxpayers for the entire State of Indiana.

Under our intangibles tax law intangibles are taxed annually at the rate of 5c for each $20 or 25c per $100. Intangibles not having a market value are taxed at the appraised value when presented to the county assessor. No taxable intangible shall be valid or enforceable until all taxes and penalties shall have been paid. Any sale, renewal or assignment of any such intangible without the payment of the proper tax shall be null and void. A separate law applies to banks and

8 building and loan associations, under which they may pay the taxes rather than have the depositor do it. I believe the law meets with popular favor and is having the effect of attracting to this State millions of intangible wealth from owners who desire to benefit from a low rate of taxes on securities. 

For many years there has been a cry in Indiana for a net income tax, and lacking constitutional authority for its passage an effort has repeatedly been made to secure approval by the voters of a constitutional amendment. Failure resulted through lack of interest in voting by the people. But finally 

Indiana accomplished the same result by the route of the Gross Income Tax Law. Our law taxes the receipts of gross income, which may be receipts from the sale of merchandise, real estate, securities or from professional service, or it may be salaries, interest, rents or commissions. 

Experience has taught in those states which have adopted the net income tax that the revenue was so deficient on account of reduced incomes that a supplementary sales tax was also necessary.

Included in these states are Arizona, California, Iowa, Mississippi, New Mexico, New York, North 

Carolina, Oklahoma, Utah, Vermont and Washington.

Great opposition developed in the legislature of 1933 when the gross income tax was proposed. It was little understood and the retail merchants of the State, always a very powerful political block at the polls, resisted the law on the theory that it was a sales tax. They were certainly against a sales tax. Now, however, that they have become educated to the different types of laws dealing with gross income, net income, mandatory sales taxes, consumers' sales taxes, optional sales taxes, occupational taxes, privilege taxes, etc., they are now organized to favor a sales tax law with a mandatory feature of passing on the tax. Merchants in Illinois and Michigan, two of our neighbors, pass on to their customers their sales tax of 2% and 3%, respectively, by agreement among the merchants. In Indiana the income tax may be added to the sale price, provided the tax is made known in advance to the customer. An insistent demand has arisen from many retail groups to amend the Gross Income Tax Law of 1933 to require a mandatory levy above the sale price, but my view of the law is that this would render our law unconstitutional, being a tax levy for private benefit. Experience has taught in other states that merchants are meeting such resistance in adding

taxes to sales that it is wiser to absorb the one per cent as a part of the costs of doing business. I personally believe that merchants fail to understand that it would require a sales tax of 212% to raise the same amount now produced by the gross income tax and that the repeal of the Gross Income Tax Law and the passage of a retail sales tax law would exempt manufacturers and professional income, salary, interest, rent, sale of real estate, securities, etc., which source is now supplying a large part of the amount being secured under this law. The Gross Income Tax Act provides for an exemption of one thousand dollars ($1,000) applicable to each taxpayer. The tax is levied in accordance with the following rates, which are applicable to all gross income in excess of one thousand dollars ($1,000) and which are as follows: 14 of 1% on gross income derived from agricultural, manufacturing, wholesaling or jobbing pursuits and 1% upon all other income.

The Gross Income Tax Law may not be a perfect law. My own idea is that its unpopularity comes from those who have heretofore largely escaped taxation and from a too strict interpretation of its terms, for which I may be partly responsible, but as our Federal income tax has been repeatedly amended so this law, in a new field among pioneers, must be changed to make the act more equitable and effective. 

Indiana has another law, passed in 1933, that seems to be without brothers or sisters in the other states and so floored the genius of the brain trust at Washington that almost every application to Secretary Ickes for public works allotments was rejected from Indiana. It is the Tax Limitation Law. While the so-called dollar and a half tax limitation law is new in legislative annals the idea is old in Indiana and has been a part of our cities and towns laws for many years, something which seems to have been long overlooked. It provides that the property levy in urban municipalities can not exceed $1.50 per $100, in rural municipalities $1 per $100, unless an emergency should occur. Under this law the Public Works Administration was never sure that the loan asked for might not involve a levy in excess of the limitation. The law has, of course, had the effect desired, namely, to reduce property taxes and thereby throw the burden upon other bases and sources. 

Property taxes actually collected in 1932 amounted to more than $135,000,000; in 1933, about $90,000,000, a decline of

revenue of $45,000,000, or a reduction of 33 1/3%. Property taxes levied in 1932 were $140,069,591, in 1933 were $98,165,109, or a decline of $42,000,000. This record so astounding has attracted the attention of all states of the Union and has won for its sponsors great praise from the owners of real estate, who have sought for years a method of shifting the burden of taxes from real estate to other sources of obtaining revenue.

There may be some severe criticism of some of Indiana's new tax laws, but I believe the results justify them. Except for their passage, Indiana's local municipalities could not have operated along the lines of their most necessary governmental functions. It has been accomplished by returning to the local municipalities these distributed taxes. We can not underestimate the need of school revenues from sources other than that derived from real estate. I can remember as a boy that it was urged that education was not a matter of local concern, but was one for which the State as a whole should provide. 

It was argued that when a boy was educated in the little red country school or in a small town school he was not trained for service in that community alone, but if he possessed intelligence and ambition he went to the larger cities of the country to give opportunity to his talents. The community could not bear the burden of his education from a tax upon real estate, every year becoming less profitable. Indiana has taken advanced leadership among the states of the Union in not only providing for a high standard of public education from the grades to the university, but in paying out of State collected funds a sum which will reach a maximum of $600 per year for every teacher in the State meeting a certain standard of requirements. This payment comes exclusively from the Gross Income Tax Fund, which is now collecting approximately $1,000,000 monthly.

Indiana has, I believe, the proper plan of raising a considerable part of its tax money through excise and license fees by a State agency and redistributing the same to local municipalities. This has, of course, been practiced on a limited scale for a number of years, but not until 1933 did the practice become a real-for-sure disease.

Before leaving the subject I think it is appropriate to call attention to the simplicity and ease of administration of the laws which produced this large fund. The intangibles tax is collected at a minimum cost through already existing agencies. 

The Gross Income Tax Act is featured by the simplicity of the return required and the ease and accuracy with which the taxpayer may determine the correct amount due. 

The above figures serve to illustrate the extent to which the burden of local property taxation may be relieved by the new forms of taxes. While taxation is always burdensome, in my opinion, the test of time will prove the fairness and adequacy of Indiana's new tax program. ww
