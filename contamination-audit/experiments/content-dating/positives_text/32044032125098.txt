WILLIAM AYLOTT ORTON, born in England in 1889, has been Professor of Economics at Smith College since 1922. 

As a young man he studied architecture and music. At Christ's College, Cambridge, he did honours work in history and economics interrupted by World War I, which took him through Gallipoli, Egypt, and the 

Somme, where he was badly wounded.

After postwar study at Cambridge he was appointed to the industrial relations staff of the British Ministry of Labour. 

In America he has taught at Amherst, Williams, and the University of California, in addition to teaching at Smith. He writes for leading American journals, lectures frequently for the Foreign Policy Association and other societies, and has published several books. Of these, Twenty Years Armistice, finished at the time of the Munich crisis, stands up extraordinarily well in the light of recent events. His latest is The 

Liberal Tradition: A Study of the Social and Spiritual Conditions of Freedom (Yale Uni versity Press, 1945), from which some passages are incorporated in the following essay by kind permission of the publishers.

THE CHALLENGE OF CHRISTIAN LIBERALISM 

Sir: I present you a small Treatise in defence of those Principles of 

Freedom which your exemplary Virtue hath so eminently contributed to establish.

O WROTE TOM PAINE, a century and a half ago, dedicating his Rights of Man to George Washington. In a way, it was an embarrassing dedication; for Washington knew very well that prudence and temperance are among the cardinal virtues, and poor Tom had little of either. But he had, almost to excess, one of the theological virtues: he loved ordinary people. In fact, like most lovers, he sometimes made a fool of himself over it. He was one of them, without privilege or pretension-but not without pride; and life had taught him the difference between wanting to do something for them and wanting to do something to them. Seeing that, he saw that there are indeed Principles of Freedom -not easily mastered, and still less easily adhered to—by which all innovation must be guided if it is to endure. His contemporaries discussed policy in terms of principle to a degree impossible to ours; and discussed it far more fruitfully.

Men of today have almost forgotten the existence of political principle in the sense in which Jefferson and Lincoln, Burke and Gladstone understood it; nor have they found in recent events, domestic or international, much to remind them. The political life of our time has shown a passion for quick returns. Since the century opened, popular attention in France, Germany, England, America has been increasingly focussed on short-run tangible objectives. Measures to bring specific material benefits to the nation, or to whatever groups or classes could secure control, have engrossed the public interest; raising the political temperature while they shortened the political perspective; so that along with considerable achievement there developed a growing instability. 

A new and dangerous temper pervaded public life as the ability to envisage policy in the larger and more permanent sense tended to disappear. We in the United States have been committed since 1933 to [ 5 ]

a regime of "experiment". For half a generation we have labored under a deluge of state action, most of it well-intentioned, some of it useful, but a very large part self-contradictory. Instead of a policy we have been offered slogans, improvisations and palliatives. And through all these years we have been so busy watching Uncle Sam reel like a drunken sailor from starboard to port and back again that we have scarcely considered where he was supposed to be going. Lacking a clear answer to that question, it might have been well to put him back in the brig of the good ship Constitution; instead he got into a fight.

It was a fight that had been going on, with rapidly decreasing intervals and increasing savagery, for about ninety years. At the moment, Uncle Sam said he was defending himself; but both before and after, he averred that he was bringing people freedom. Being a big fellow as well as a generous one, he came out on top; with the result that he is now much in demand (and more than a little tempted) wherever there is another fight in progress or in prospect. Yet abroad as well as at home, the results have proved disappointing and somewhat self-contradictory. 

To the world at large, as well as to its own constituents, the United States has a moral duty to rise from politics to statesmanship. And statesmanship is a matter of principle. 

Principle: "fundamental truth as basis of reasoning" says the dictionary: "general law as guide to action". "The greater part of the measures which arise in the course of public business”, says Burke, “are related to, or dependent on, some great leading general principles in Government."

For the English-speaking peoples, and by no mere act of choice, those principles are the Principles of Freedom. The "fundamental truth" and "general law" of our development are summed up in Acton's dictum: "Liberty is not a means to a higher political end. It is itself the highest political end." In the sense in which Acton understood it-and his understanding was both broad and deep-the liberal tradition is entailed upon us.

ODAY IT NEEDS DEFENDING against three main types of misunder standing, or abuse. First, the liberal tradition comes to us obscured by associations with anarchic individualism and laissez-faire by which, in the nineteenth century, it was in fact deflected from its course- [6]

though no more so than were other major traditions of social polity in that confused era. These associations have been deliberately emphasized and exaggerated by Professor Laski and his fellow-travellers whose avowed aim it is to supplant the liberal philosophy by a quite different philosophy in which liberty is no longer the highest political end. 

Second, Catholic priests and teachers in America, reflecting the strongly Latin background of their constituency, tend to associate liberalism with anti-clericalism, agnosticism, the Grand Orient, and aggressive atheism. It was not an association that bothered Acton and his fellowcatholics in England, nor Gladstone and other great exponents of Anglican liberalism; but it is a very natural association to those whose conception of liberalism comes almost entirely from non-British sources. The pity is that it encourages American radicals to represent the Church as the enemy of all liberalism; to ignore, or deny, those profoundly liberal elements which, stemming from Aristotle, lie at the heart of Catholic faith and action. Acton's insistence on liberty as the highest political end protected the refusal all Christians must make to render unto Caesarany kind of a Caesar-the things that are God's. 

Third, the term liberal has been widely misappropriated in America by persons of a type on which Herbert Spencer put a discerning finger one hundred years ago—"the subspecies Radical, who more than any other seems under the impression that so long as he has a good end in view he is warranted in exercising over men all the coercion he is able.” 

The modern tactics cover not only the economic coercion of the community, but "smear" journalism, falsification of news, and political blackmail; and they spring from a conviction easily acquired by those who come as strangers to our western patrimony, that the end justifies the means. We see this doctrine triumphant in certain phases of American action abroad, and by a natural contagion it increases the drive toward coercion at home. Liberalism, like Christianity, is a philosophy of means as well as ends. It can never grant that the end justifies the means, because it knows that the means will shape, not only the ends, but the minds and hearts of those who employ them. In fact, we must now recognize the emergence, on a very considerable scale, of a type of personality whose ethics and values are definitely other than those of Christian civilization: a type that tends in opposition to be conspiratorial, in office dictatorial, and always in intention, ruthless: a type that has ceased to believe-if [7]

ever it did in the power of the principles of freedom, acting within a humane tradition, to create a community of free men. 

What has produced this loss of faith in freedom? It is a commonplace that technical development has outrun the ability of free human intelligence to direct it: but why? From the invention of dynamite to the development of radar and atomic fission the effects have been overwhelmingly disintegrative rather than constructive: what was lacking?

Why, with the tremendous rise in the western standard of living this past century, has social cohesion grown progressively thinner and weaker ? 

In the long series of western wars in which this country also is now involved, we can discern for each separate outbreak a proximate cause --but never a final one: what chain of events prepared the German people for a Hitler, what made possible the fantastic foreign policies of a Palmerston, a Napoleon III? Take the ghastly procession as a whole, and it becomes evident that it springs from some fatal arrest, deficiency or vice in the culture itself, of which, if we would stop it, we must become aware. 

It is within ourselves that we must look, rather than to the multiplication of schemes and committees. Something is wrong with you and me, with our understanding and motivation and imagination: it is there that the diagnosis must begin, there that the seed of synthesis must first be planted.

II 

S WE ALL KNOW, what one can do is very much a matter of what one thinks one can do, or "feels like doing". Historically, what

AⓇ man had made of man is very much a matter of what man has thought of man and felt about man. Hence the responsibility of the intellectuals is very heavy, much heavier than they generally realize. For example, every great educational system, from ancient Greece to Nazi Germany, has aimed at the production of a specific type of integrated personality centered upon a specific value-system. Where the integration is successful the type is effective for good or evil, as the case may be; where there is no vision, the people perish. 

Insofar as the promise of freedom with which the nineteenth century opened remains unfulfilled; insofar as the principles of freedom have failed to produce a reasonably harmonious and expanding community of life: it is on the ideal plane that we must seek the primary causes. [8]

Says A. J. Toynbee: "A schism in the souls of human beings will be found at the heart of any schism that reveals itself on the surface of the society." Beyond question, the distinctively modern era begins with the loss of the vision, followed by the loss of the fact, of Catholic unity: the self-assertion of territorial groups and interests that cherish no longer the hallowed aspiration toward the life of a larger whole. Inevitably, the same disintegration presently affects those units themselves. We see it in the self-assertion of refractory groups within them-of labor and ownership, industry and agriculture-devoid of any innate communal impulse to which effective appeal can be made; so that it seems even to liberals, alike in the foreign and the domestic field, that integration necessarily depends upon coercion: a counsel of despair, repugnant even to those who offer it.

But the impulse to unity was rooted in the Christian doctrine of man-for there is nothing in materialism to protect the weak against the strong—and what marks the whole period is the shrinking of protestant and humanistic thought from the tremendous affirmations of the Faith. 

They are politely toned down, plausibly paraphrased, explained away to a point at which "we are all Christians now" (and to very little purpose). The Faith is represented as an unreasonable, or non-reasonable, incantation against nightmares; and seventeen centuries of the keenest thought man has ever achieved, are ignored in our "institutions of learning", while the weedy growths of a sour and shallow soil are extolled as "scientific".

It was this bastard scientism of the past hundred years which more than any other factor corroded the spiritual foundations of liberalism; especially in America. There were two phases of it. First, the success of the physical sciences led to a general exaltation of the "scientific attitude”, which was all to the good in so far as it fostered a dispassionate objectivity in the pursuit of knowledge. In that sense theology could still claim to be queen of the sciences. But by the 1850's the "scientific attitude” implied an assertion-explicit in many cases-that whatever could not be counted or measured did not really exist; or if it did, could be of no vital or practical concern to mankind. Some slight embarrassment arose when, under modern methods of measurement, matter itself declined to exist any longer; none the less, that extraordinarily dogmatic negation is still the orthodox credo of American thought. [9]

From this position, scientism rapidly advanced into its really dynamic phase. It asserted that the forces which determine the social and individual life of man (that was the sequence) are themselves analogous to those of the physical sciences: impersonal, ineluctable, predictable in their operation, immune to human wish, will or protest. To identify and describe them was the whole concern of "social science"; and they had to be just so, in order that social science could proudly claim to be scientific. The economists were the first, and the loudest, to insist on this dogma; it was effectively promulgated by the sociologists, under the aegis of positivism; and it soon infected psychology, ethics, philosophy, and even protestant religion.

He full effect of this creed was concealed from our fathers by the fact that it developed in an atmosphere of roseate optimism. The faith in "progress"-which was logically quite extraneousmasked the fact of nemesis. The economists taught both that their laws were inexorable, and that they would yield an optimum of wealth and distributive justice. If from that teaching the optimistic corollary be withdrawn, one is left simply with the inexorability. So in other spheres. As the actual course of events dissolved the optimism, what was left was an iron ring of determinism-economic, cultural, biological, psychological, even moral. On the entire gambit of positivism the intellectual career of 

Mr. H. G. Wells, in its concluding stages, offers an eloquent commentary. 

This misapplication of mechanistic concepts to the life of society has had an extremely debilitating effect on the philosophy of freedom. It has subtly and thoroughly persuaded the ordinary person, and especially the educated person, that the significance of his particular life and character, his particular moment, his particular generation, subsists only in reference to the condition and future of society as a whole; and that apart from that prospect, he and his here-and-now are devoid of anything that can really be called meaning. This is, in fact, one of those notions which, seldom rising into explicit consciousness, tend to color the outlook of a whole era; and it is probable that no other fallacy of our time has done more to corrupt manners and morals, to corrode assurance, and to destroy mental and spiritual health. The prevalent lassitude, negativism, apathy following the close of a victorious war are a sign of it. 

It would seem as if we lacked virility enough either to hope or to despair. [ 10 ]

For it is, by both liberal and catholic doctrine, a fallacy, and a cardinal one. It is one of the central tenets of both that every person great or small, cultured or lowly, every moment of time, every human generation, partakes of a significance that is absolute, second to no other; that to maintain the contrary in contemplation of a God who is supposed to be outside human time, or the divine Presence, which is supposed to be inside human time, is morally speaking, presumptuous and philosophically speaking, ridiculous. ag- To minds and wills so enfeebled, deterministic theories offer a blanket alibi for human failure; especially moral failure. Under the spell of nostic or atheistic teachings, modern life has become largely a technique for the evasion of moral responsibility: a phenomenon that now characterizes public as well as private life in an amazing degree. It may surely be said that the American people are somewhat uneasy as they contemplate the ruins of Dresden and Hiroshima, or the occupation policies in Europe, or the kind of law that has been improvised at Nuremberg; but they take refuge in the thought that years ago somebody else started a certain historical process, and it has to go on, riding roughshod over our doubts or dreams or wishes, and there is nothing we can do. Least of all can we take any chances or risk looking foolish. 

Similarly, in the personal life we welcome an alibi. Deterministic theories, sociological and psychological, are popular because they offer people a plausible excuse to go on fooling themselves and ignoring or denying their own experience. It is a simple fact—and everybody knows it-that when we refuse to fulfil the demands of our higher nature a sense of failure and remorse besets us: we feel and know that we have sinned and come short of the glory of God. Since this feeling is uncomfortable, an age that has made a god of comfort will not admit it; it is barred as far as may be from consciousness, and the word sin is banished from polite usage. Instead, we invent all sorts of elaborate disguises for the fact to which it refers. 

A deterministic dogma comes in very handy as an escape from responsibility. Sociology proffers a variety of comfortable excuses for human failure. You can blame, according to the school of your choice, either the "environment" (ignoring the circular argument involved); or the "culture"-that commodious ding-an-sich suspended in a logical vacuum; [ 11 ]

or your genes, hormones and chromosomes; or that poor old scapegoat, the "subconscious"-you can blame, in short, almost anything except yourself because you have been taught that you really have no self. If there is a quicker way to weaken the moral fibre of a democracy, it has yet to be discovered; for in all these subterfuges the root-impulse is to escape the more exacting and uncomfortable aspects of human responsibility, individual and collective. HE LOSS OF A LIVELY SENSE of responsibility to absolute moral standards has certain conspicuous results. One is the feeling that nothing matters very much; that however regrettable conditions here or there may be, there is no point in our getting too concerned about them even when we are directly involved. Thus the normal human impulses of pity, sympathy, compassion, brotherhood, are frustrated and atrophied for lack of hope and an outlet; and the false expediency of power politics becomes a shoddy substitute for constructive action. 

Another consequence is the opening afforded to the inherent narcissism of modern states; especially modern democracies. An aristocracy or oligarchy in control of affairs normally has tangible connections, of family or interest, with corresponding groups elsewhere; it knows by actual experience that there are other people in the world, and that ideas of policy have to rub shoulders. To a democracy foreign nations exist mainly in books and newspapers, where they are easily hypostatized and cast for subordinate roles in a drama of which our nation-whichever it happens to be-has, or at least ought to have, the lead. 

It is quite natural that the ordinary person should get a little egoinflation through identification with his group, especially so when the group is powerful and on the whole benevolent. But in the absence of clearly held moral principles, that attitude becomes totemistic; the distinction between relative and absolute good is blurred; national aims disguise themselves as eternal values; critics are hounded; the state sets up as judge of its own ends, with a veto on outside interference; and its leaders are encouraged to impose their ideas by coercion upon both their own and other communities, in the avowed confidence that the end justifies the means. 

Without absolute standards, independently affirmed and implemented, [ 12 ]

liberty will not remain the highest political end. When and where liberalism discarded the Christian criteria, it soon forgot its primary concern with international and domestic freedom. In both spheres power automatically usurped the place of principle.

III 

HE SWORD ALONE decides the destinies of nations", said Pilsudski; "a people which shuts its eyes to this fact would irretrievably compromise its future." "On the evidence of history," echoes Mr. 

Laski, "any class which has sought to redefine its position in the state has always had to achieve its ends by violent revolution. The facts give no warrant for concluding that our experience will be a different one." 

These two half-truths, resting on the same fundamental philosophy, complete the forecast of a world of violence. 

Insofar as the nature of nations and classes is thus correctly indicated, we must logically infer that we had better get rid of them; which is indeed, to that extent, a long-run aim of liberalism. (It is an old trick of the Marxists to talk about a "classless society" when what they are aiming at is the dictatorship of the proletariat; and we have even less experience of peace-loving proletariats than we have of peace-loving nations.) 

Indeed, it is well to recognize that while nations and classes are assumed to be primarily economic phenomena, their normal inter-relations are bound to be hostile. Simply to make the assumption tends to create the conditions under which it will be valid; and materialism propounds no synthesis. Liberalism therefore deliberately makes a different assumption, and in so doing claims a superior realism. 

It is not necessarily true that either nations or classes are primarily materialistic; but it can always, under certain conditions, become true, and when it is true there is the devil to pay. 

When the state, falsely identifying itself with the nation, presumes to play a positive part in economic enterprise, it steps from the sphere of justice into the sphere of material interest. In that sphere it cannot help backing one group against another, protecting or subsidizing some sections of the economy against the rest, according to whatever combination of theorists and lobbyists is in the ascendant. In the foreign field it appears as one embattled group-interest against others, each claiming its place in the sun; and in both fields the temptation to use those means of [ 13 ]

coercion which states possess, and private organizations do not, becomes irresistible. That is one of the basic reasons why liberals always seek to hold state enterprise to a minimum; and it entails the duty to protect the state machinery from capture by any particular economic interest, even the most numerous or the most powerful. 

There are deeper reasons. As a social philosophy, the secular individualism of the nineteenth century was woefully inadequate as Pius IX insisted eighty years ago; but there was this much to be said for it: It did in some measure operate to protect the ordinary human being from total submergence in mass organizations based merely on the appetites. 

It did to some degree-witness Bright, Cobden and Gladstone-defend him from the omnivorous advance of national arrogance and organized greed. Its weakness lay in its conception of the individual. Had it been able to proceed from its silly notion of utilitarian man to a more catholic concept of personality, it could have produced a genuinely social philosophy. That is, in fact, the supreme enterprise of modern liberalism. 

Similarly, there is something to be salvaged from the nineteenth century notion of "progress". On such shallow foundations, it was bound to collapse in the dismal dogmas of determinism; we may not therefore assert, as so many are now doing, that there is no ground for hope at all. 

Buried and forgotten below those foundations was still the living rock; and in such a shaking and shattering as we now experience it stands out clearer every day. It is still possible to build the city of man on the truth about man; and it is sheer ignorance to suppose that the Faith which affirms it, and the Society which upholds it, are solely concerned with some other life than this.

The practical principles of liberalism rest on older and deeper grounds than many of their exponents realize; and it may be that fuller realization is now the first requirement. Non-intervention in the affairs of other nations; freedom of trade and intercourse; distrust of coercion, at home or abroad; reliance on voluntary action and cooperation; extension of education along with political responsibility; equalization of opportunity for persons and peoples-all these, in their manifold applications, are grounded in the confidence that man, with all his weaknesses, is not wholly depraved; that the innate good, even of the natural order, is more dynamic than the evil; that the will and the passions as well as [ 14 ]

the brain can be educated; that free societies engender a growing sense of fairness; that, in short, order is not incompatible with spontaneity. 

To make these affirmations tends of itself, as in the opposite case, to create the conditions under which their validity is increasingly evident. 

IV 

OURAGE AND CONFIDENCE are today at a very low ebb. As a nation, we feel neither joy nor security in our recent victory; we are appalled rather than assured by our own might, scared of our own gigantic shadow. In Europe we pursue, under presidential orders, a largely negative policy that becomes increasingly cruel and futile, a source of disillusion and disgust to our own forces and to countless others; yet nowhere do we find vision and faith enough to break the vicious circle.

In force we trust, and yet we do not trust it; nor from the new international force-machine does anyone draw confidence in future peace. We now proceed to squander on a single demonstration of the atomic bomb as much as the entire cost of the social security program in 1945; yet we are afraid to use it, afraid to let anyone else use it, and afraid to refuse to use it. So we continue building our lethal stockpile, feeling that science has betrayed humanity, and yet knowing no other shrine at which to worship. Meanwhile from our returning soldiers, and from millions of simple people in every land, rises the inarticulate protest: By the God that made us, this is not good enough: it is too foul, too blind, too beastly· fools and weaklings we may be, but we are not as bad as this: give us a lead, a line, a policy, that does not blaspheme our common humanity.

That ominous protest, daily increasing in scope and intensity, portends revolution.

There are two possibilities, and two only. One-humanly speaking, the more likely-is an outbreak of aggressive materialism in its most ruthless form, to which all materialists, from Greenwich Village to Hollywood to Moscow, will find themselves forced to rally; and the next major depression will probably touch it off. The other is a rediscovery, on a scale comparable to that of the Renaissance, of the dynamics of Christian liberalism.

It is a remarkable fact that both the criticisms and the proposals of avowedly Christian organizations, as to both domestic and international [ 15 ]

issues, show a wide and specific coherence. And whether they come from

Rome, Paris, Stockholm, Berlin, London or Chicago, the coherence is that of the historic liberal tradition as it comes down to us from the primary synthesis of the early centuries. This can be verified by anyone who will take the trouble to study the modern pronouncements, many of which have been issued under joint auspices. The differences of doctrine and discipline do not produce major disparities of policy for the simple reason that Christian truth, wherever it is genuinely apprehendedwhether partially or deeply-cannot contradict itself. Some deductions go farther than others; but the main platform of Christian policy stands out in basic contrast to the teachings of Slavic or western totalitarianism, offering a pattern of freedom that is clear, concrete and consistent. 

We may well consider, as a practical measure, the embodiment of that policy in a type of organization more definite than has yet been attempted: a party or movement of Christian Liberalism that could work on the political plane in active liaison with similar groups abroad, and implement the vast amount of baffled idealism at home. Our old machines squeak and clatter, grinding out confusion. The enthusiasm that Hitler and Mussolini could evoke in the power of paganism need not remain unmatched by Christian men in touch with a Power that is stronger. 3
