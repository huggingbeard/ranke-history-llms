Fundamentals of U.S. Policy 

To achieve its basic objective of true peace, the United States pursues certain fundamental policies. 

Military Strength. In his state of the Union address President Kennedy observed that "we are moving into a period of uncertain risk and great commitment in which both the military and diplomatic possibilities require a free-world force so powerful as to make any aggression clearly futile." 

Collective Security. Since our own security is inextricably bound to the security of the rest of the free world, we have taken the lead in organizing a democratic coalition of nations committed to mutual defense. Under Secretary of State Ball, noting that the United States has joined with approximately 50 other nations in collective security arrangements, stated that several years ago it was determined that "we should not limit our assistance merely to those nations which were prepared to enter into alliance with us. If we could provide them with the means by which they could help themselves to build strong and stable economies, they could then use their energies constructively.

The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), originally proposed in December 1959 as successor to the Organization for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC), came into being on September 30, 1961. The new organization is composed of the 18 European nations that made up the OEEC-Austria, Belgium, Denmark, France, the Federal Republic of Germany, Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, and the United 

Kingdom-plus the United States and Canada, which participated in the OEEC but were not 

I members. 

Under the OECD convention the 20 member governments agree to contribute, individually and jointly, to the progress of countries in the process of economic development. Their objective will be to increase the flow of capital to less developed countries, to provide them with more technical and educational assistance, and to help them expand export markets. Aid will be increased, particularly by fostering consultation among those member nations able to provide a significant flow of grants and long-term loans for development purposes. 

In a message to Secretary General Kristensen at the time of OECD's entry into force, Secretary of State Rusk said that the occasion represented "the beginning of a new era in the long and intimate relationship between Europe and North 

America." The Secretary declared that the

OECD nations "must work together to encourage worldwide patterns of trade and investment that will not only be beneficial to our own peoples, but that will also meet the diverse needs of free peoples on every continent."

The Ministerial Council, at its first meeting at Paris in November, set a collective target of 50 percent growth in real gross national product for OECD members during the period 1960-70. In a communique issued after the 2-day meeting, the Ministers noted the substantial economic growth that had taken place in most member countries during the past decade and agreed on the desirability of establishing a target for further growth. "Each country will have to make its contribution to collective growth in accordance with its own special circumstances," the communique pointed. out. It added that the setting of a joint target for economic growth was itself recognition of the increasing interdependence of the separate economies of the member nations.

AFRICA The Republic of the Congo 

The sweep toward independence in Africa, which began after World War II, has brought sovereignty to 25 countries on that continent. 

Some of the new states already present a picture of stability and hope. During 1962 at least two more countries are scheduled to join the roster of new nations.

However, one of these new states, the Republic of the Congo-formerly the Belgian Congo-has not enjoyed the relatively smooth path to independence and self-government experienced by other African states.

Beginning with the revolt of the Force Publique within a few days after the Congo's accession to independence in June 1960, stability and order there quickly gave way to tribal clashes, secessionist attempts by several provinces and tribal areas, and a general deterioration in the economic and political structure of the country. 

When the United Nations on July 12, 1960, received an appeal for help from the Government of the Congo, the United States supported the U.N. 

Security Council resolution which authorized the U.N. Secretary-General to provide military assistance to the Congo until the Congolese themselves might be able to maintain law and order. 

The Soviet Union consistently opposed the U.N. operation in the Congo, refused to contribute toward its financial support, and eventually attempted to exploit the Congo crisis in an effort to depose the U.N. Secretary-General and promote bloc penetration in this vital heart of Africa. 

However, the prompt action of the United Nations, made possible partly by U.S. diplomatic support, the U.S. military airlift, and U.S. financial contributions, precluded a direct big-power confrontation, arrested the worsening trend of events, and helped avoid the dangers of a brushfire war in the heart of the continent. 

During the first 4 months of independence a series of political crises in the Congolese government resulted in the dismissal of Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba by President Kasavubu and a move by Army Commander Colonel Mobutu to suspend operation of the Central Government and establish a College of Commissioners to carry on its functions until the situation improved.

By the end of 1960 two major problems emerged from the Congo crisis: (1) the breakdown of political unity as manifested in the Communist bloc-supported regime in Stanleyville and the attempted secession of south Katanga and south Kasai, and (2) the absence of a parliamentary approved Central Government which at the same time could command the allegiance of the six provinces.

In 1961 two major steps were achieved which gave encouraging signs of improvement in the Congo situation. HE KITONA ACCORD 

The problem of the south Katanga secession was more difficult to deal with, because it involved the active participation of foreign mercenaries who had taken up arms against both the United Nations and the Congo government. So strong were the sentiments of nationalism of the Congolese people regarding the secession of south Katanga that it became clear that no government would survive in the Congo unless it demonstrated progress in reintegrating that province. It was obvious that failure to bring south Katanga back into the Congo would mean civil war and the ensuing chaos on which the Communists have capitalized in other parts of the world. 

It was also clear that the moderate strength of Mr. Tshombe and his party leaders and the economic wealth of their area were needed in the Central Government. U.N. officials as well as U.S. and European diplomats therefore made repeated efforts to achieve the reintegration of south Katanga through conciliation and peaceful means. 

One of the difficulties was that foreign elements, not responsive to their own governments, sought to convince Mr. Tshombe that through military force he could maintain his secession. 

The United Nations sought to remove the mercenaries, in accordance with the February 21, 1961, resolution, in order to clear the air for a peaceful settlement. Refusing to leave, the mercenaries cut off the United Nations lines of communication and resorted to acts of violence. The 

United Nations responded to protect itself and to establish conditions under which it could pursue its objectives with reasonable security. 11

While it deplored the loss of life and the isolated acts of barbarism that grew out of warfare, the United States supported the United Nations in its limited military action because the alternative was to acquiesce in south Katanga's secession and permit the civil strife that inevitably would sooner or later result in a big power clash. 

When Mr. Tshombe indicated a desire to negotiate, President Kennedy set in motion efforts which culminated in Mr. Tshombe's meeting with 

Prime Minister Adoula at Kitona and the resulting accord, reached on December 21, providing for the reintegration of south Katanga into a unified 

Congo state. Reintegration of the Katanga would represent a major step toward ending the Congo's political crisis and would enable both the Congolese and the United Nations to turn their attention from military efforts to the peaceful task of restoring the economy of one of the wealthiest countries in Africa. Red China Barred From United Nations

As in past years the Soviet bloc, with the support of certain other countries, again attempted to bring about the expulsion of the representatives of the Government of the Republic of China from the United Nations and all its organs and their replacement by representatives of the Chinese Communist regime. The question of Chinese representation has been raised each year since 1950 in various ways, not only in the major Councils of the United Nations but also in specialized agencies and subsidiary bodies. The United States has vigorously and successfully opposed these efforts to gain entry for the Chinese Communists into the United Nations or any of its component bodies. The United States has always considered the Government of the Republic of China the only rightful government representing.China and has always given full support to the position and rights of that government in the United Nations and its components.

On December 15 the General Assembly rejected a Soviet-sponsored proposal to give Communist China the seat now occupied by the Republic of 

China. There were 48 votes against the Soviet proposal and 37 for it, with 19 nations abstaining.

The Soviet proposal thus failed to receive a majority of the votes in the Assembly. Additionally, however, the Assembly decided that any proposal to change the representation of China was an "important question," requiring a two-thirds majority. This was passed by a vote of 61 in favor, 34 against, and 7 abstentions.

In a statement made before the Assembly on 

December 1, Ambassador Stevenson set forth some of the reasons for U.S. opposition to the proposal that the "aggressive and unregenerate" regime of Red China replace the Republic of China in the 

United Nations. The Chinese Communists, he said, have "for 12 years been in continuous and violent defiance of the principles of the United Nations and of the resolutions of the General Assembly, and deaf to restraining pleas of law- 21

abiding members." Moreover, Ambassador Stevenson reminded the delegates, the Chinese Communist leaders explicitly demand the expulsion of the representatives of the Republic of China before they themselves will "deign to accept a seat in the United Nations." He pointed out that the 

Republic of China's place in the United Nations, since its founding in 1945, has been filled with distinction "by representatives of a law-abiding government which, under most difficult circumstances, has done its duty well and faithfully in the United Nations."

On December 6 President Kennedy, in an address before the National Association of Manufacturers at New York City, declared that the 

United States urgently needed a new trade and tariff policy "if our exports are to retain and expand their position in the world market." The 

President emphasized that, if the West was to seize the initiative again in the economic arena as it did with the Marshall plan and point 4, the Trade Agreements Act "must not simply be renewed-it must be replaced." Mr. Kennedy made clear that, if American industry cannot increase its sales to the European Common Market and increase the U.S. surplus of exports over imports, "our international payments position and our commitments to the defense of freedom will be endangered."

In a message to Congress on January 25, 1962, the President submitted legislation for a 5-year Trade Expansion Act of 1962, which would permit the United States "to meet the challenges and opportunities of a rapidly changing world economy." The new trade bill would provide the 

President with essentially four types of new tariff authority: (1) general authority to reduce existing tariffs by 50 percent in reciprocal negotiations; (2) special authority, to be used in negotiating with the EEC, to reduce or eliminate all tariffs on those groups of products where the United States and the EEC together account for 80 percent of world trade in a representative period; (3) special authority to reduce or eliminate duties in relation to the less developed countries; and (4) authority to eliminate low duties.

In his message the President recommended, as an essential part of the new trade program, that companies, farmers, and workers who suffer damage from increased foreign import competition be assisted in their efforts to adjust to that competition. Accordingly, the bill provides the following forms of adjustment assistance to meet difficulties due to increased imports of like or directly competitive articles as a result of tariff concessions: (1) assistance to firms, including technical assistance, various forms of financial assistance, and tax relief in the form of special carryback of operating losses; (2) assistance to workers, including readjustment and relocation allowances where necessary, as well as retraining of workers for other types of employment; and (3) assistance to industries, in extraordinary cases where the foregoing types of assistance may be inadequate to mitigate the difficulties involved, through Presidential authorization to apply increased duties or other import restrictions.

The President told Congress that enactment of the new legislation "could well affect the unity of the West, the cause of the cold war, and the economic strength of our nation for a generation to come." The decision, he emphasized, "will either mark the beginning of a new chapter in the alliance of free nations-or a threat to the growth of Western unity. The two great Atlantic mar- 32

Market, will 'strike a blow' for freedom."

FOREIGN AID 

Mr. Kennedy told Congress in a special message delivered on March 22 that the United States must revise both its foreign aid organization and the basic concepts of operation. The President nati pointed out that weaknesses in the present program "have begun to undermine confidence in our effort both here and abroad." He then proceeded to outline a new set of basic concepts and principles in order that our foreign aid funds may be "prudently and effectively used," and among them mentioned the following: pers

THE 

Unified administration and operation-a single agency in Washington and the field, equipped with a flexible set of tools, in place of several competing and confusing aid units. 

Special attention to those nations most willing and able to mobilize their own resources, make necessary social and economic reforms, engage in long-range planning, and make the other efforts necessary if these are to reach the stage of selfsustaining growth.

Multilateral approach—a program and level of commitments designed to encourage and complement an increased effort by other industrialized nations.

Mr. Kennedy emphasized that long-term authorization, planning, and financing are the key to the continuity and efficiency of the entire program. "If we are unwilling to make such a longterm commitment," he declared, "we cannot expect any increased response from other potential donors or any realistic planning from the recipient nations."

For the fiscal year ending June 30, 1962, the 

President asked for monies in these categories: (1) $1.69 billion in appropriations, (2) $1.19 billion from loan repayments, and (3) $6.4 billion in borrowing authority for fiscal years 1963-66. 

On May 26 President Kennedy submitted legislation to Congress which would provide aid under two new acts-aid for social and economic growth under an Act for International Development, and military assistance under an International Peace and Security Act. The legislation provided that an Agency for International Development would replace the International Cooperation Administration and the Development Loan Fund, which would be abolished.

In submitting the legislation the President declared that "economic development assistance can no longer be subordinated to, or viewed simply as a convenient tool for meeting, short-run political objectives." He said that the proposed legislation would carry out the principal recommendations set forth in his March 22 message to Congress.

He explained that the Act for International Development sought authorization for appropriations of $1.69 billion for four major purposes: ● To assist and support nations whose independence or stability depends upon such help and is important to our own security. • To provide for our share in certain programs under multilateral auspices. • To provide grant assistance to less developed countries primarily to assist in the development of their human resources.

To establish a Presidential Contingency 

Fund to meet the unpredictable exigencies with which we will doubtless be confronted during the forthcoming year.

In addition, the President noted, the Act sought congressional authorization to make loans, repayable in U.S. dollars, to promote the economic development of less developed countries and areas "with emphasis upon long-term plans and programs designed to develop economic resources and increase productive capacities." 

Secretary of State Rusk, testifying in August before the Senate Appropriations Committee in support of the Act for International Development, emphasized that the foreign aid program is of key importance to our entire foreign policy. "We face a future which can either be relatively stable or dangerously chaotic. What we do now in terms of longer term planning will in no small degree determine which of these alternatives is likely to obtain," the Secretary asserted.

PEACE CORPS President Kennedy on March 1 signed an Exec utive order providing for the establishment, on a temporary pilot basis, of a Peace Corps, through which American men and women could volunteer for overseas service to help foreign countries meet their urgent needs for skilled manpower. Congress on September 22 authorized the Corps on a permanent basis. 

By the end of the year approximately 700 volunteers were serving in 12 countries in Latin 

America, Southeast Asia, Africa, and the Far East. 

The goal at that time was to have 2,400 volunteers in service or in training by June 30, 1962, and a total of 2,700 in training by July or August.

Projects were being formulated for 25 additional countries.

In establishing the Corps, the President said, "we intend to make full use of the resources and talents of private institutions and groups." He said that universities, voluntary agencies, labor unions, and industry "will be asked to share in this effort-contributing diverse sources of energy and imagination-making it clear that the responsibility for peace is the responsibility of our entire society."

The President told Congress that among the specific programs to which Corps members can contribute are the following: (1) teaching in primary and secondary schools, especially as part of national English-language teaching programs; (2) aiding in village development through school construction and other programs; (3) instruction and operation of public health and sanitation projects; (4) increasing rural agricultural productivity by assisting local farmers to use modern implements and techniques; and (5) participation in the worldwide program of malaria eradication. 

Volunteers, after a training period in the United 

States, at the Corps' training camp in Puerto Rico, and in the host country, work with their counterparts under host country supervisors. They re ceive a living allowance which enables them to live in a manner similar to their foreign counterparts but in any case under conditions which permit the maintenance of health and effectiveness. 

Each volunteer receives 30 days of leave a year, with a small allowance for travel, and is credited with a separation allowance equal to $75 for each month of service. The usual term of service is 

2 years, including training time.

CULTURAL EXCHANGE 

The U.S. Government took a series of important actions in 1961 to give increased emphasis and prominence to educational and cultural affairs as a major component of U.S. foreign relations. For the first time, for example, the Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs in the Department of State was directed by an Assistant Secretary of 

State. The Assistant Secretary, in addition to supervising the work of his own Bureau in this important sphere, is also responsible for the coordination of all U.S. Government activities with respect to international educational and cultural affairs. Within the Department he guides and coordinates the U.S. educational and cultural ex change program; the Cultural Presentations Pro gram administered by the Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs; educational, cultural, and technical exchanges with the Soviet Union; and activities of the U.S. National Commission for 

UNESCO. The Assistant Secretary also has responsibility for promoting the maximum cooperation between the Government and nongovernmental organizations and institutions concerned with international educational and cultural activities. 

New Legislation

The Congress acted to strengthen U.S. cultural relations with other countries by adopting the Mu tual Educational and Cultural Exchange Act of 

1961. This law, popularly known as the Fulbright-Hays Act, expands and consolidates authority for exchanges contained in previous legislation, such as the Fulbright Act of 1946 and 34

The Smith-Mundt Act of 1948, and provides for nore flexible administration and financing of the xchange program. 

Exchange of Persons

Educational cooperation with Africa during

OVERSEAS INFORMATION 

Under the leadership of Edward R. Murrow, the United States Information Agency last year expanded its facilities and improved the quality and persuasiveness of our overseas information work.

USIA now maintains 219 posts in 99 countries. 

Daily news reports and analyses are being broadcast over 87 radio transmitters, several of which are of 250,000-watt strength. More are now being built. 

On November 16 USIA announced the awarding of a contract for the construction of a highly mobile radio relay station, equal in power to four top U.S. broadcasting stations. The self-sufficient broadcasting facility will be air-transportable and can be made operational within 30 days after unloading. It will augment the flexibility of the 

Voice of America's 87 present transmitters at 17 locations throughout the world. It will be built to fit into truck-trailer type units, and will be capable of operating in temperatures ranging from 

20 degrees below zero to 120 degrees Fahrenheit.

The global radio network of USIA now tells America's story to the world in daily broadcasts totaling more than 100 hours in 38 languages. The programs originate in Washington, D.C., and are transmitted by short wave from various points in the United States. 

America Illustrated

The fifth anniversary issue of America Illus trated, USIA's Russian-language magazine, appeared on Soviet newsstands on September 22. 

Departing from its traditional use of text and photographs, this special issue was exclusively photographic.

Newsstand sales of the magazine have surpassed the three-million mark, but the Agency estimates that resales of old copies in markets and bookstores have boosted total sales figures by hundreds of thousands. In one instance, for example, a bookstore sold some old copies for three rubles each-six times the original price. Some issues are sold out virtually in an hour. One of these was the issue with a photograph of President

Kennedy on the cover. The magazine is sold in some 80 Soviet cities through an official Soviet distributing agency. 

Budget Increase 

Early in 1961 President Kennedy asked Congress to increase USIA's fiscal year 1962 budget by $11 million to permit further expansion of programs in Africa and Latin America. A White 

House statement pointed out that recent political instability in these areas has demonstrated the urgent need for larger scale information activities to counter increasing Communist-bloc activities and to "demonstrate the identity of U.S. foreign policy objectives and African and Latin American interests." Congress later appropriated approximately $6 million more than the Agency had been granted for fiscal year 1961. 

Commenting on the image of America projected by the USIA facilities abroad, Ambassador at 

Large Chester Bowles, the President's Special 

Representative, on January 16, 1962, said: "America and Americans are now being presented not as a self-satisfied, smug people who have solved all their problems but rather as members of a dynamic democratic society aware of their own defects and working to correct them, ready and anxious to cooperate as partners with other non-Communist peoples, proud of their great liberal traditions, strong and determined to resist aggression while always holding the door open to peaceful settlements."

Refugees 

In July 1961 President Kennedy asked Congress to approve legislation designed to centralize the authority to conduct, and to appropriate funds to support, U.S. programs of assistance to refugees, escapees, migrants, and selected persons. "This country has always served as a lantern in the dark for those who love freedom but are persecuted, in misery, or in need," the President said.

Mr. Kennedy explained that upon congressional enactment of the requisite authorization and appropriate legislation for these programs, the State Department will continue its responsibilities for overseas refugee activities. Under the proposed legislation, assistance programs for Cuban refugees in the United States will continue to be a responsibility of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. 37

Action on the bill is expected to be taken in 

1962. Since the end of World War II, the United 

States has spent over $1.2 billion on the refugee problem and we have admitted to our shores over 

800,000 refugees. 

Michel Cieplinski, Deputy Administrator of the 

Bureau of Security and Consular Affairs, on October 6 stated that Communist governments have recently been trying "to copy the Western Powers by sending relatively smaller but much more publicized shipments of food and relief goods" to refugees. They have succeeded in moving refugee children back behind the Iron Curtain. "The

Communists who create the political upheavals which in turn spawn the refugees are now posing as their friends and benefactors," Mr. Cieplinski said. "They are desperately trying to take away from the United States its historic role as champion of the oppressed and persecuted people." Antarctica The 12-nation Antarctic Treaty, which was signed at the conclusion of the Antarctic Conference on December 1, 1959, entered into force on June 23, 1961. This U.S.-sponsored treaty, which provides that the vast Antarctic Continent shall be used for peaceful purposes only, also provides for freedom of scientific investigation and international cooperation in science on that continent as practiced during the International Geophysical 

Year. President Kennedy said that the treaty, which has been signed and ratified by countries representing all of the world's six continents, constitutes "a positive step in the direction of worldwide peace." The first Consultative Meeting under the treaty was held at Canberra, Australia, July 10-24, 1961.

Passport Regulations 

The State Department has announced revised passport regulations providing that a passport shall not be issued to or renewed for any individual who the issuing officer knows or has reason to believe is a member of a Communist organization registered or required to be registered under the

Subversive Activities Control Act of 1950. The regulations provide further that the decision to deny a passport shall be based only on evidence which is made available to the applicant, who shall have the right to appeal, the right to be represented by counsel and to present evidence, and other guarantees.
