A WEDDING IN JUNE BY GEORGE S. O'NEAL 

AFTER more than twenty years of calm and contented married life in a small American town, Marian and John are preparing for the marriage of their daughter, Mary Frances. John's younger brother, Clarke, who went abroad just before the marriage of Marian and John, is coming back after all these years, and the reunion of the family group is joyfully anticipated.

For Marian, there is, however, a foreboding of trouble. A faithful and devoted wife to John ever since their marriage, she is forcibly reminded of her youthful infatuation with Clarke and her one indiscretion just before her marriage. Mary Frances is Clarke's child, and this terrible secret, long since absorbed in her devotion to John, assumes a new importance and becomes a crucial problem with Clarke's impending return. 

Clarke comes. To him, now married and settled down, the memory of that unfortunate incident has receded and diminished in importance. His absence has been his way of paying for his injustice to his brother. Yet his return creates a tense situation and precipitates a climax. 

In a dramatic moment, just before the marriage, John is suddenly made aware of the secret affair through an inadvertant remark. Marian admits her guilt. John is bewildered. He can not reason clearly or think sanely. His struggle is graphically portrayed and his solution finally comes from a calm fortitude and inner courage which spares others from his own destruction.

Clad in dotted Swiss, a blue field splattered with innumerable white specks, she was a slight and placid nymph, with a roguish expression about her eyes. "She said leaving a hat on the bed was almost as bad as breaking a looking-glass. I declare she thinks I'm going to faint tonight, mother, just like you did at your wedding.' "You won't faint," Marian caught her breath.

She was about to add, "You haven't any reason to 1

faint," but modified it to, "You aren't the fainting kind." 

A surge of queer, confused emotion swept through her, and for a moment she was frantic. 

She would have to do something right away to get her rebellious mind under control again-just anything to change the subject. So she reached into her darning basket and brought out a pair of stockings, which had already been inspected, and said, "Look, Mary Frances, here's a pair of stockings just as good as new. Don't you think you'd better take these with you?" And since it was urgent at that moment for her to have her hands occupied, she folded the toes of the stockings, and skillfully rolled the pair into a neat little oval bun, tucking in the edge. 

Mary Frances let an indulgent smile twist one corner of her soft, full mouth. "All right, if you say so. But I'm sure I have plenty already. Besides, if I run out while we're away, Howard can buy me some new ones. Golly, won't it be funny to have Howard buying my clothes for me?”

She strode across the veranda to the curving brownstone parapet, and stared out toward the bright green fairway of the Choctaw Country

Club, where there was a plume of silver birches on a knoll, twinkling in the sun. 

Aunt Sarah mopped her forehead-it was a scorching afternoon, the peak of the worst heat wave that had descended upon Red Valley in a decade. She sat bolt upright in a cabbage-green rocking chair, fastening shoulder straps on trousseau lingerie, her white head in sharp contrast to a cluster of flaming coral flowers that dangled from a nearby trumpet vine. "If Howard's down there playing golf," she said, "you better not catch sight of each other." 

Mary Frances swept around. "It wouldn't matter at this distance, would it?" "I don't know about that. All I know is you aren't supposed to see each other on your wedding day until you meet at the altar." 

Mary Frances appealed to her mother, and she had to repeat her question, for Marian was wholly absorbed by her own thoughts, as she stared at her hands, slowly twisting her engagement ring, a solitaire diamond in a plain heavy gold setting. When 

Mary Frances said the second time, "What do you think about it, mother?" Marian looked up with a startled expression in her splendid dark eyes, and gave a short peal of nervous laughter.

"As long as you've decided to abide by the old traditions, I think you ought to carry them all the way. Don't you? Of course, the traditions don't mean anything, not really. It's just the fun of it, and if you break the traditions, after you have started to keep them, you spoil the fun. Don't you think so?" "Yes, I think so. But poor Howard's not getting much fun out of it. I know he's just perfectly miserable."

Mary Frances gave a fleeting glance toward the golf course, as she groped in her handbag for her car keys. "If Uncle Clarke and Aunt Berenice arrive before I get back, tell them how dreadfully sorry I am not to be here. But I can't help it. You know yourself, mother, when a train starts getting late you never can tell when it will get in. And I've simply got to run over to see Betty Jane." "Darling, it's perfectly all right for you to go. I don't think it will make so terribly much difference whether or not you're here when they arrive. 

I told you that before." “All right—I'll be back as soon as I can," and with that, Mary Frances switched down the long

front veranda, and a moment later her blue roadster shot up the driveway. 

Betty Jane Allen was the girl originally chosen to be maid-of-honor, and she was now too ill to be in the wedding. By dropping in to see her as close as possible to the time to begin dressing for the ceremony, Mary Frances hoped to make her feel she hadn't been left out entirely. 

Aunt Sarah folded an orchid-tinted princess slip and put it on a pile in an adjacent chair, and then ran one hand over her white hair, which was streaked a mustard-yellow in spots from the fumes of an asthma remedy she had inhaled for years. "It's very thoughtful of Mary Frances to go over to Betty Jane's, I grant you that. But still I insist she should have waited until after Clarke arrives. I know he'll want to see her the very first thing."

Marian jabbed a needle into a red worsted strawberry, stuffed with powdered emery, to keep needles from rusting, and said: "Clarke has waited twenty years to see her. A few more minutes won't matter." 

Aunt Sarah's face was subjected to a sudden change, in much the same way the landscape

changes when the sun unexpectedly passes behind a cloud. Marian stooped to pick up a stray thread, which she rolled between her fingers until it was a small moist pellet. "What on earth made me say that?" she thought. "I must be going insane." 

Of course, Aunt Sarah didn't have an inkling of the truth about Mary Frances. In all the world there were only two persons who knew that, and those two were Marian herself and Clarke. The most Aunt Sarah could have suspected was that she had been very much attracted to Clarke, that time John brought him up to Shevlin. The thing that she and Clarke had done was so unspeakable it wouldn't even occur to such an innocent soul as Aunt Sarah as being within the realm of possibility.

Even if she should blurt the truth right out, and say that Mary Frances wasn't John's daughter, but Clarke's daughter, Aunt Sarah wouldn't be convinced, but would most likely come to the instant conclusion that the strain of wedding preparations and the heat had made her irrational and was causing her to suffer delusions. There was no denying that Mary Frances favored Clarke and had many of his traits to a striking degree. That was one thing that filled her with anxiety right

now, for in a very little while any number of people-particularly Berenice-would see them together, and might begin to wonder why they were so much alike.

But Clarke and John were brothers, so from the world's viewpoint, why shouldn't there be a family resemblance between Mary Frances and 

Clarke? No, even if she shouted out the truth this very minute, as she had so often been tempted to do, Aunt Sarah wouldn't believe her, but would probably decide that she had really been in love with Clarke all of these years, and that in brooding over it, had become unbalanced and imagined that Clarke was the father of her child. 

Being utterly guileless, Aunt Sarah would never suspect her of evil, and would righteously resist any such implication. But Aunt Sarah was evidently afraid she might still be nourishing romantic illusions about Clarke, and that explained the cloud of distress that had suddenly overcast the old woman's countenance. That explained why Aunt Sarah had her lips pursed and was glaring, with what seemed to be a deep, personal resentment, at the antics of a blue jay, sporting himself in a plum tree that grew up from the vegetable garden below the side terrace.

Even so, Marian told herself she had been extremely foolish to make that pointed remark about Clarke. It was symptomatic of a dangerous state of mind. There is no accounting, ever, for the wild, distorted notions that come bobbing up, even out of the most serene and orderly mindsand certainly, right now, her mind was woefully chaotic, woefully turbulent. All the more reason, then, why she should be on guard, ready to pounce upon any such revealing ideas and shove them deep back down into the murky subconscious, before they bounded out in front of someone not so innocent or guileless as Aunt Sarah.

Berenice, for instance. In the photograph of 

Berenice, which Clarke had sent from Honolulu, just before his marriage a little less than a year ago, there was a secretive, disturbing, "Leonardo" smile that Marian had instantly distrusted. It was the smile of the cat that had just eaten the canary -the smile that characterizes virtually all of da Vinci's lovely women. And the worse part of it was that Clarke had written that Berenice had posed for that picture "just to let you folks in Red 

Valley see what she is like." From that smile,

Marian was certain that Berenice must know that 

Clarke had once been in love with her. There was

no use trying to deceive herself-she was already afraid of Berenice, for Clarke's wife might very well be able to detect things no one else, so far, had discovered. As she thought of that her anxiety increased, and she told herself that after all she had endured it would be tragic to let a few heedless words betray her.

She had encouraged Mary Frances to call upon Betty Jane at this particular time, on the grounds that it was the considerate thing to do, but secretly she had been hoping that through that legitimate excuse Mary Frances would be absent when 

Clarke and his wife arrived. 

Trains in the South are often late, and she thought of the numerous occasions when she had been exasperated with waiting for one, but here was an instance when tardiness was a blessing in disguise. Mary Frances would hardly return in less than an hour and a half-since the Allens lived way over on the other side of Long Mountain, in Meadowhill Estates-and by the time Mary Frances did get back the first shock of seeing 

Clarke would be over, surely, and she would have had the opportunity to fortify herself for the additional shock of seeing Clarke and his daughter together.

Innumerable times she had visualized this approaching ordeal, for her imagination was vivid and her fancy active. Usually, in her mind's eye, it had occurred underneath one of the concrete canopies of the Terminal Station-and that's where it would have occurred if the train hadn't been late. She and Mary Frances had been dressed and ready for Wilson-he was the Latimer's combination butler and chauffeur-to drive them downtown in the limousine, when John called up from the store to say the train was already an hour late, and gave every indication of being later. 

There had been a terrific cloudburst somewhere up the line, beyond Nashville, and a trestle had been washed out. So John's secretary was keeping in touch with the station, and John was going to meet the train alone when it finally did arrive, and bring Clarke and Berenice out in his sedan.

Since the people up in Tennessee or Kentucky, or wherever it was, were getting more rain than they needed, it was a great pity Alabama couldn't have a little of it. The foliage of the elm trees was prematurely seared, the lawn was parched so badly it looked jaundiced-no amount of sprinkling seemed to do it any good-and the flower

garden was really pathetic, particularly the little skirts of the zinnias, which were all dusty and worn, like fantastic ballet costumes along toward the end of a hard season on the road. 

Marian listened to the rasping sizzle of the dry weather flies, or cicidas, which kept up an incessant shrilling as if steam were escaping from a thousand radiators. A shimmer of heat waves was rising from the dingy brownstone parapet, but even so, the side veranda was the only tolerably cool spot on the place, although it wasn't anything to brag about. Every once in a while there was just barely enough breeze to ruffle the scollops of the tan and yellow awning. 

Her head felt as if she were wearing a tight iron pot for a hat, and at intervals she experienced a terrifying, sinking sensation, which was mainly due to her anxiety, but in part also to her weariness. She inserted a small slick gourd into the toe of a sock and told herself she had no business sitting out here in the swing, going through the motions of darning, when there were still any number of much more important things that needed her attention.

For some little time, Aunt Sarah had been talking, but Marian hadn't been paying any particular

attention to her. Aunt Sarah had started out by telling her that the Red Valley Era-Tribune had called up while she was bathing, and asked permission to send out a photographer to take pictures of Mary Frances in her wedding dress. Then 

Aunt Sarah' said she wondered what had become of Mr. Rittenhaus, the photographer who used to spend part of each week in Shevlin and the rest in Elton. He had married one of the Crawley girls of Greenville. The mention of the Crawley girls led Aunt Sarah into telling what had happened to each of the Crawleys, and since there were a lot of them, Marian was left comparatively undisturbed with her thoughts for quite a while. Aunt 

Sarah required no encouragement and could talk placidly on through a thunderstorm once she got started on the genealogy of families she had known in her native South Carolina.

In a neighboring yard, the guttural crowing of a peevish rooster was raised in protest against the sultriness of the afternoon. A dispirited bee droned fretfully in from the flower garden, and after surveying the side veranda with one critical swish, darted off in a great hurry, as if afraid of being infected by the drowsiness that seemed to be settling over that corner of the world, like the

forerunner of an enchanted sleep in which all animation would be indefinitely suspended.

On such a day of essential activity-innumerable petty irritations and sweltering heat-this languid interval was a great luxury indeed, although Marian was too keyed up to make the most of it. 

She wanted to relax, and think of nothing at all, but she could not. Her mind kept right on grinding. At least, she could be thankful that Fred 

Hollis, the best man, had taken the bridesmaids and groomsmen out to the Redrock Club on a swimming party. They were such a noisy lot. Just after lunch they had descended upon the house, like a flight of agitated sparrows. Some of them danced on the veranda, with the radio going full tilt, and others ran up and down the stairs-lipstick on the towels, cigaret ashes in the soap dish, matches in the toilet! One wanted manicure scissors to clip a hang-nail and another needed something for sunburn. A snag in the hem of a skirt had to be mended. Two of the groomsmen invaded the kitchen, one after ice and the other to mix a dose of bicarbonate of soda. Apparently the only real excuse for an elaborate wedding is to provide an opportunity for the attendants to have a good time.

She smiled as she thought of the earnestness with which Mary Frances had set out to observe the old traditions. At her own wedding, she also had observed the old traditions, but she derived no ingenuous pleasure out of them. It had been more like passing through the crisis of a long fever, when a patient submits to any treatment, not so much because of a hope of recovery, as because of a lack of strength to resist, or perhaps because of a faint desire not to be any more disagreeable than absolutely necessary with those in attendance.

She saw herself standing at her bedroom window in her Aunt Sarah's house in Shevlin, waiting for her bridesmaids to come and tell her it was time to dress. It was late in the afternoon, and she was staring out into the back yard. The squat catalpa trees were shedding the last of their lilac flowers-mottled, trumpet-shaped flowers. The clean-swept area was spotted with them. The sunlight came through the new little spade-shaped leaves and wove queer, filigreed patterns on the hard ground. It was a thick, lemon-colored sunlight, with a greenish tinge in it. In the distance, the mountains seemed immeasurably remote, nothing more than dim shadows reaching across

the base of a pale, luminous sky. At intervals all that day-her wedding day-she had been suffering from attacks of nausea, and from that and other quite definite indications, she knew only too well what was the matter with her. But she was too stunned to make any decision for herself, and too terrified to confide in anyone. So she went through the wedding routine as a prisoner goes through the steps leading up to an execution-not in her right mind at all—and at the conclusion of the ceremony she fainted dead away. 

Clarke did not know the reason at the timehe did not even suspect it. She let him go away to the Hawaiian Islands without telling him, and it was months before she had the courage to write him the truth. Whatever opinions Clarke may have had about it then, or about her, he kept to himself. Of course, all the letters he wrote were family letters, to be read also by John, but at least he might have written to her privately upon an extra sheet of paper that could have been burned. 

To this day she did not know what Clarke really thought of her, and that was something she must learn before she could re-establish her peace of mind. With the passage of years that uncertainty had grown dull, but the prospect of seeing

Clarke again had re-sharpened it. She simply had to make him understand how and why it had all happened, as she now understood it. When she had done that, and Mary Frances was safely married, she could be serene again.

Clarke wrote sympathetic letters when she was so ill, after the birth of Mary Frances, and again when she lost the other child. But those letters were unnaturally restrained, and lacked the real understanding that should have been in them. 

Was that reserve due to diffidence, to a desire to remain strictly upon the surface of any emotional relationship that might still exist between themfor fear that if he went any deeper he might only increase her distress-or was it due to resentment, which he had never been able to overcome?

IN the living room, Mr. 

Caulkins, the window-dresser from John's store, was calling petulantly to his helper for a pair of scissors. Mr. Caulkins was converting the baywindow into an altar before which Mary Frances was to be married-white wicker floor baskets containing old-fashioned flowers and looped together with streamers of white satin ribbon. Mr. 

Caulkins was a shrivelled, cadaverous fellow, with a long nose, simpering smile and bad teeth-but very artistic! When they were all arguing over how the floor baskets should be arranged he had kept talking about the "fundamental principles of design," illustrating what he meant by waving his hands around like an adagio dancer. The helper was a youth in a pair of high-waisted duck trousers laced in the back and a pea-green polo shirt, and his face was broken out with acne. The last time she went in there to see how the work was progressing, the helper was peeling tinfoil from a badly squashed chocolate bar, and the sight and smell of the melted candy had made her a little 17

nauseated. She had cautioned Mr. Caulkins against filling the baskets too full, but he had merely nodded absent-mindedly and gone right on stuffing in flowers, perspiration streaking down his cheeks and dripping from his chin. 

The flowers were snapdragons, larkspur and foxglove. Mr. Caulkins had hunted everywhere for the kind Mary Frances wanted, and finally found them on a farm over in Slade Valley. For weeks he had guarded them, to make sure that the old woman who was raising them watered them properly every evening in order to have them exactly right for the wedding. When it got so hot, he dashed out and covered them with cheesecloth canopies to protect them from the sun.

A queer thing had happened right after Mr. 

Caulkins and his helper had lifted onto the veranda the big, brown packing cartons in which the flowers had been brought out to the house in one of the trucks from the store. Marian let her mind linger over the scene, and over her emotional reaction to it, in a kind of sentimental reverie, rather as if re-reading a cherished but disturbing letter. She recalled how Mary Frances was crouched beside one of the cartons, dipping

her fingers into the fresh, cool blossoms, her face glowing with delicately-refined, sensuous delight. 

Then a change came over Mary Frances. From the enthralling touch and color and fragrance of the flowers, her mind seemed to move on to more intimate reflections, to a deeper passion. 

The flowers became something more than mere flowers. They were a symbol for all that was going to be, and her eyes began to sparkle with a new light, seeing things that were not there. Her lips parted. She held her breath, as if to control a little burst of inner rapture: and Marian was filled with a grave wonder. It was just as if she had never seen her daughter before. Then the thought occurred to Marian, "She's truly a love child. I've become so accustomed to thinking of her as John's daughter, I'd forgotten that. Clarke should be here, too, to see her for the first time as she is right now."

But instead of Clarke there was the youth in the polo shirt having a good look at Mary Frances, a glimmer of awe replacing his usual expression of vacuous complacency. So Marian moved closer, to shield her daughter's too-revealing face, and let her hands slip down Mary Frances' shoulders, with a caressing pressure that caused the girl to

glance backward, with a shy, confiding smile. The next moment they were walking down the long, front veranda together, arms about each other's waist, like school-girls with a crush on each other -and Marian could not recall when they had done such a thing before. 

Only the other day Mary Frances had been chiding her, playfully, for spending so much time over the trousseau, making many of the things with her own hands when there was really no need for it—since John had been, as usual, considerably more than generous. That had brought back memories of the way she had devoted hours to rolling and whipping and hemstitching exquisite underclothing for her daughter's Christmas dolls, with an infinite care Mary Frances had been much too young to appreciate. There had been something psychopathic about the concentration she had expended on that vain activity, an activity she had justified by repeatedly telling herself it was being done merely to please Mary Frances-when she knew all the time it was for other reasons entirely. That was in the days when she was trying to keep herself from brooding over the loss of the other child, and the knowledge that she was incapable of bearing another.

21, From childhood, Mary Frances was undemonstrative and placid. Her huge blue eyes, gentian blue eyes, drank in all that went on around her --just as Clarke's did-but gave back little or nothing of what took place within her; and when 

Marian was, at length, well enough in mind and body to take a rational and joyous interest in her, the child had developed an aptitude for selfsufficiency into a lasting trait of character. If it pleased her to withhold herself, no one could possibly get beyond the surface texture of her prejudices and preferences-and in that respect, she was so very much like Clarke.

Whatever went on in her own little sphere was almost wholly a matter of speculation to Marian, always interesting but never authentic. As a child she seemed to have little precocious curiosity, and there was none of the usual inquisitiveness to embarrass Marian. So far as Marian knew, what

Mary Frances learned about those subjects which parents so often have difficulty discussing with their children, she learned without undue excitement or loathing, indirectly and gradually, from sources that were never fully disclosed. As the years went by, there was scarcely any outward indication of the deeper physical and mental

changes she must have been undergoing. On rare occasions, her eyes appeared to turn gray, with a troubled expression, as if she had caught a glimpse of something displeasingly familiar in her mother. But the next moment those huge eyes would clear up and become fathomless blue again, like the sea, after a swirl of wayward mist has passed away from the face of the sun. 

To Marian, Mary Frances and Howard had never seemed like lovers, not even on that heavenly, blue April day when they had followed her down to the rose garden, where she was at work with pruning shears (a pair of baggy canvas gloves protecting her hands) and announced in a matterof-fact and disconcerting way that they thought the time had come for them to get married. 

Marian had been so astonished she couldn't do anything for a moment except stare at them and snip the pruning shears in the thin air. The thing that had snapped her out of her trance was 

Howard's awkward manner of kissing her. Howard had recognized the importance of the occasion, and had behaved in what he believed was the approved manner; throwing one arm around her without warning, pecking her on the cheek, and knocking her dilapidated garden hat awry:

afterwards backing away, obviously relieved to have it all over.

Even then he still seemed to her to be merely the boy who lived next door: a boy with an uncanny power for divining when something tasty was being cooked up in the Latimer kitchen, and who was sure to show up at the right moment, to scrape an icing saucepan or contend for his share of hot tea cakes. She had watched him extend himself through his gangling youth into strapping manhood, yet to her he remained emotionally immature. He had a desk downtown and the title of transportation manager of the Wainwright Cast 

Iron Pipe Company-his father's firm-but he was liable to sniff around her stove any evening and invite himself to dinner if he liked what she was having better than what was being prepared at his home.

Even when it came down to buying and making things for the trousseau, Marian still could not believe she was preparing for a wedding. She felt more as if she were getting Mary Frances ready to go back to school, or working on Christmas surprises. At the pre-nuptial parties, when friends fluttered around her and spoke emotionally of how she must feel, "about to give up Mary Fran-

ces," she had been a little dazed. Several times she watched Mary Frances and Howard across a room, apart from the rest, whispering to each other, and she still felt that any conjugal relationship between them was impossible, because they were still children. But in that moment when she had seen the look which the flowers had brought to her daughter's face, she had realized that something momentous had been going on all the while in Mary Frances, and she had missed it entirely. 

An uneasiness had begun to stir again in the back of Marian's head, and at first she attempted to identify it with the fact that the lulling sound of Aunt Sarah's voice had ceased, but she soon discovered that some unpleasant feeling about 

Mrs. Wainwright, Howard's mother, was groping for expression. Mrs. Wainwright had no daughter of her own, and she just simply couldn't keep her hands off this wedding. She came from Richmond, so of course, her opinion on every point was the correct one, from the style of the invitations down to the flowers-she thought it was a great mistake to let Mr. Caulkins construct the altar. Marian was really quite fond of her, and while never intimidated by her, was nevertheless willing to go a long way to keep from offending

her, for Mrs. Wainwright was quite as formidable in her enmities as she was effusive in her friendships. She was a woman of strong character, always on the alert for any breach of propriety. While she gave every indication of being genuinely devoted to Mary Frances, what would be her attitude if she discovered the truth? The thought of such a prospect was almost enough to make Marian's heart stop beating.

Having exhausted the Crawleys, and in search of another topic, Aunt Sarah had reverted to Clarke, and immediately Marian became attentive. Aunt Sarah said that Clarke was a fine looking man when he went away twenty years ago, but she supposed that all of that sun down there in the Hawaiian Islands had dried him up, until by now he probably looked like a Chinaman. 

Then she said she wondered if Clarke had married the right sort of woman, and Marian felt forced to reply that Berenice's family was one of the most influential in Honolulu. "Clarke wouldn't be where he is today," she added, "if her brother hadn't taken an interest in him." "Maybe he married her out of gratitude," Aunt 

Sarah surmised.

"I hardly think so," Marian observed. "That doesn't sound like Clarke."

Aunt Sarah gave a sniff of disapproving surprise, and Marian thought, "I've done it again! 

This simply won't do-it's dreadful!" She got up and went into the living room, to inspect the completed altar, and after complimenting Mr. Caulkins-at the same time fluffing out the white satin bows a little and loosening up the flowers here and there-she called Wilson to clean up the mess on the floor. "And when you get through with that," she told the butler, "I have several other little things 

I want you to do. I want you to replace the burned out light bulbs in the dining room, open a window that's stuck in the guest room, and polish the punch ladle-you forgot to polish the punch ladle when you were cleaning the other silver." "Yessum," Wilson struck an attitude of injured innocence, "I knows it, but I'm supposed to take the big car down to the filling station and get it ready for the bride and groom. Mr. John, he tole me to be sho and not forget it." "I heard him tell you to do that the first thing this morning. Why didn't you?”

"Yessum, I know, but I had to cut the grass this morning." "You were supposed to cut the grass yesterday." "Yessum, but I had to wash them upstairs windows yestiddy." 

Marian was beaten, and knew it. This could go on indefinitely, covering everything Wilson had been told to do for the past month, and still' not get anywhere. So she said, "Oh, all right, but don't stand there talking all day! You'll never get anything done."

She went back to the kitchen where Carrie Hobson, the gaunt mulatto cateress, was folding pocketbook rolls and placing them in greased black pans; Bessie, the cinnamon-colored cook, was working at the sink; and "Aunt" Jinny had her great bulk ensconced in a split bottom chair and was disdainfully cooling herself with a palm leaf fan, as she watched what was going on, a look of stubborn disapproval written in every line of her sullen, seamy black face. "Aunt" Jinny was mad because "Miss" Marian had hired a cateress -for hadn't she herself baked the cake for "Miss" 

Marian's wedding? Her lip stuck out so far now "you could ride to mill on it." Marian was about

to ease the old servant's vanity by telling her to be sure and not forget to straighten Mary Frances' train at the last minute, when she caught sight of what Bessie was doing. "Oh, my goodness!" she gasped. “What are you thinking about Bessie? Here, get a dishpan." 

Bessie had all of Mrs. Wainwright's fragile after-dinner coffee cups piled up in the sink, and was slopping them around recklessly. Marian was so exasperated she was just about to do the job herself when Wilson called her to the telephone. "I won't have a moment's peace from now on," she thought, "I never should have left the side veranda." The kitchen was stifling hot, and just to be there a few seconds was enough to make her miserable. It was Howard on the telephone.

He was in such a nervous state he had stripped the gears on his car down at Mountain Circle, and while waiting for his mother's chauffeur to come for him, he simply had to call up to see if Mary Frances was all right.
