COMMENTARY PERSONAL PROPERTY: AVERAGE INVENTORY AND ASSESSMENT DATES Introduction 

At the November 27, 1961, meeting of the Executive

Board of the Legislative Research Council, several matters pertaining to assessment and taxation of property were discussed. 

One of these matters was the use of an "average inventory" method of taxing business inventories and merchandise. Interest in this method was stimulated by the following unanimously adopted by the South Dakota County Commissioners' Association on September 

20, 1961:

Whereas, the two date assessment law is the cause of many problems in assessment procedure, and Whereas, several attempts to obtain a uniform assessment date have failed, and

Whereas, several states are using an "Average Inventory" method in arriving at the assessment of certain types of Personal Property: 

Now, therefore, be it resolved, that the Legislative Research Council, through its Assessment & Taxation Committee be and are hereby requested to compile such information from the states using the "Average Inventory" method,

Be it further resolved that such information be carefully examined, so as to determine if such a method of assessment in South Dakota would be practical to be used in the State of South Dakota. 

Folowing a brief discussion of the matter, the Executive Board adopted a formal motion that study of the topic be referred to the Committee on Taxation.

At the February 26, 1962, meeting of the Executive Board representatives of the South Dakota Farm Equipment Dealers' 

Association discussed the problem of personal property tax relief. 

By formal motion, the Executive Board assigned this topic to the Committee on Taxation, inasmuch as the Committee was already studying the feasibility of an "average inventory" assessment and a business franchise tax, both of which had been suggested as possibilities by the implement dealers.

Personal Property Taxation Procedures in South Dakota The South Dakota Assessor's Handbook, issued by the 

Department of Revenue, Division of Taxation, defines personal property as follows: 

Personal property for taxation purposes includes not only all property of a movable nature, but also that property of a stationary nature which, because of its location on exempt land or other reasons, the Legislature has classified as personal rather than real property. Personal property of a movable nature includes: (1) all goods, chattels, and effects, and (2) all ships, boats, and vessels belonging to inhabitants of this state. Personal property of a stationary nature includes: (1) all improvements upon land the fee of which is vested in the United States, and all improvements upon land the title to which is vested in any corporation whose property is not subject to the same mode and rule of taxation as other property, and (2) all stock of nurserymen, growing or otherwise. In general, all tools, implements and machinery which are not permanently attached to the building should be considered personal property. All machinery permanently affixed to or connected with the building should be considered real estate. Assessors are warned against omitting in the listing of personal property, items which the householder is buying under various forms of conditional sales contracts. Such items are definitely to be assessed to the extent of their taxable value in the name of the purchaser. The situation is in contradistinction to that of the purchaser of real estate subject to a mortgage or under a real estate land contract. 

The dates of assessments are not uniform for all personal property in South Dakota. The South Dakota laws provide that real and personal property shall be listed and assessed annually during the first six months of each year, but that the value of such property is to be determined according to its value on the first day of May preceding the assessment. In cities of the first, second, and third class, however, the value is determined according to the value of such property according to its value on January second preceding the assessment. The effect of this distinction is that all personal property located within incorporated cities or towns is assessed as of January second, while all other property is assessed as of May first. It should also be noted that the procedure for assessing livestock varies somewhat from that of other personal property.

Property owners are supplied self-listing blanks by the various county directors of equalization in South Dakota. After the information has been supplied by the owners of the property, the blanks are returned to the director of equalization (or appropriate assessing official) who then computes the absolute amount of assessed value.

All taxes are due on the first day of January of each year following the assessment, levy, or extension except where the date may be accelerated by proceedings authorized in cases of removal of personal property. On the first of May all unpaid personal property taxes become delinquent and on the first day of each and every month thereafter, a penalty and interest of two-thirds of one percent is added until the taxes are paid. If any person pays one-half of the amount of personal property taxes due from him on or before April thirtieth following the assessment, however, the other half is not considered delinquent until the following 

November first. Personal property tax assessing methods of the various states are shown in Appendix I. 

The Two Date Approach 

The Concern about the two date approach to assessment of personal property has been evidenced for a number of years. 

1954 report to the Executive Board of the Legislative Research Council Committee on Assessment and Taxation stated that the principal reason advanced for adopting a uniform assessment date for all property in the state is to prevent property from escaping taxation by its being moved from cities to rural areas to avoid assessment.

This same report noted that an assessment system which provided for non-uniformity of assessment dates between cities and rural areas within a state might tend to encourage the transfer of property to avoid assessment. The extent of this practice in South Dakota to avoid taxation is unknown. Certain flagrant abuses of the split assessment day have, however, been cited by responsible local officials. The loss of revenue as a result of such practices is in the opinion of these authoritative sources, considerable. 

After an examination of the present system, the 195354 Committee on Assessment and Taxation recommended the establishment of March 1 as the assessment date for all real and personal property in South Dakota only in the event that the county assessor proposal became law.

At the time this recommendation was made, rural property was assessed as of May 1 and urban property as of 

March 20. No action was taken during the 1955 Legislative session regarding the two date system. In 1957, however, the 

Legislature did modify the assessment dates by providing that urban property should be assessed as of January 2 (to allow sufficient time for assessment) and rural property as of May 1, still maintaining the two date system.

As has been mentioned above, the two date system may allow persons to escape paying taxes on personal property merely by shifting the location of that property. In addition, there is the consideration that inventories of many merchants are very low on January 2, thus the tax revenue may be lower than if the date were changed. Conversely, shifting the rural assessment date to January 2 might have the effect of levying

tax against a very high inventory in some industries--notably seasonal stock feeders.

Average Inventory 

The average inventory concept, recommended by the County Commissioners' Association as a substitute for a single assessment date for all personal property and the South Dakota 

Farm Equipment Dealers' Association for personal property tax relief is said to be an equitable means of determining the tax base. This approach is not based upon the value of the inventory in the hands of a businessman at any given date during the year, but rather the average amount invested in the inventory. The amount need not be limited to the amount of cash investment, but may also include any credit obligation for inventory possessed by a person. Comments on the average inventory are reproduced in Appendices II, III, and IV. 

The average inventory would be determined by the assessor based upon the number of inventories from which the average amount is determined. For example, the inventory might be computed at sixty percent of full and true value if only one inventory is submitted, fifty-five percent of value if four inventories are submitted, and fifty percent of value if twelve inventories are submitted.

Adopting an average inventory method, however, does not necessarily lead to better equalization. Objectors have stated that this method does not take into consideration the volume of business, rate of gross or net profit, or the overhead expenses of the business. Even businesses having the same amount of average inventory may vary with reference to these factors. In addition, the turn-over rates for various businesses varies widely. A recent 

Revenue Department report showed that the average annual inventory turn-over for jewelry stores as 1.2, restaurants 35.5, drug stores 

4.0, meat markets 53.3, farm equipment dealers 3.4, and grocery stores 13.6, among others. There might also be marked differences between the inventory turn-over rate of the same class of businesses located in different cities or towns. The figures are, of course, averages and should be used as an indication of the difference in turn-over rates rather than the absolute rate for any single class of business.

Report of Oregon Study Group 

During 1959-61, a legislative interim tax study committee in Oregon examined property taxes in that state. At the time, Oregon used the single date property assessment procedure. As a result of this study, the group recommended repeal of the tax on inventories for the following reasons: 

1. Many types of inventories, intangibles and services had been removed by statute from taxation and some of the remaining inventories escaped taxation through problems of administration. 

2. The inventory tax was said to be difficult to administer.

3. Most other classes of property were taxed in such a way that the tax varied with the value of the income from the property throughout the year. 

4. The tax on inventories frequently penalizes a business when its income is being reduced and collects a lesser tax when income is on the increase. By way of example, during a poor business year, inventories might be high, while inventories might be lower during a good year. 

5. The committee determined that certain manufacturing companies go through a major curtailment of operations in the closing months of the year and some actually shut down completely in order to reduce the manufactured goods on hand, thereby reducing the personal property tax. 

6. The effort on the part of many owners of inventories to curtail their inventories on January 1 was said to have an unhealthy economic effect on the state. 

7. The Committee determined that replacement revenue derived from a 1.5 percent tax on all business net receipts would cause some persons to pay a tax who, at that time, did not own inventories--this last remark apparently aimed chiefly at services. One

The Oregon committee pointed out that the net receipts tax would be very easy to administer, requiring only an additional line on the personal income and corporate excise tax returns. member of the Committee took exception to the general findings, however, and stated that a change from inventory taxation to net receipts would create as many problems, or more, than it would solve. 

A 1959 Subcommittee of the South Dakota Legislative 

Research Council Committee on Assessment, Taxation, and Finance reached somewhat the same conclusions. In their report, this 

Subcommittee recommended among other things, that further consideration be given to the adoption of a franchise tax in lieu of personal property tax on retail businesses. Studies have revealed, the report continued, that on any given assessment date, certain types of retail businesses have an unusually high inventory, while the inventory of other retailers may be unusually low. This situation has proved inequitable in the distribution of the tax burden. order best to insure fair treatment of all taxpayers, the Subcommittee believed consideration of a business franchise tax, based on a schedule according to turnover and average inventory in lieu of personal property tax on retailers is merited.

COUNTY DIRECTORS OF EQUALIZATION: 

Introduction QUALIFICATIONS, TENURE, SALARY 

At the November 27, 1961 meeting of the Executive Board of the Legislative Research Council, several matters pertaining to assessment and taxation of property were discussed. One of these matters was the county director of equalization situation, including their qualifications, security, method of appointment, and salary. This topic was brought to the attention of the Board by 

Commissioner of Revenue Bruce Gillis and Hughes County Director of Equalization James Mulloy. After a brief discussion, the topic was, by formal motion, assigned for study to the Committee on Taxation.

County Director of Equalization 

In 1957 the South Dakota Legislature repealed Chapter 422 of the Session Laws of 1955 which authorized counties of the state to choose between two forms of assessment procedure; and replaced this chapter with Chapter 477 of the 1957 Session Laws, reproduced as Appendix VI. The latter law related to the assessment of property for the purpose of taxation, created and established the office of county director of equalization, and prescribed the necessary powers and duties to carry out the provisions of the act. 

The new act stipulated that the office of county director of equalization was created for each organized county in the state. 

The board of county commissioners were required to meet and appoint a person to fill the office within thirty days after the effective date of the law or as soon thereafter as this might be accomplished. 

If a county contained a single municipality containing more than fifty percent of the population of the county, the mayor of that municipality was authorized to sit and vote with the board of county commissioners in making the appointment.

The law further stated that the director of equalization should hold office at the pleasure of the appointment authority subject to termination upon thirty days notice. Unless terminated, the tenure of office is two years. Vacancies in the office are filled in the same procedure as used in the original appointment. 

The qualifications listed for holding the office are that "...the director of equalization shall possess knowledge and training in the field of property taxation, and his appointment shall not be dependent upon any residence requirements, but he shall reside in the county for which he is appointed during the tenure of his office". 

Salaries for county directors of equalization are based upon the population of the counties as determined in the latest federal census, and the population of an unorganized county is added to the county to which it is attached for judicial purposes in the computation of the director's salary. The salary schedule, which has not been changed since 1957, is shown in the following table:

The Board of County Commissioners is empowered to fix the salary of the director within the limits set by law. In fixing the salary, the Board is instructed to take into consideration the number of deputies appointed in townships, municipalities and unorganized counties; the number of first, second, and third class cities within the county; the number of real and personal property assessments made during the previous year; the character of the services; amount of labor to be performed; and such surrounding circumstances as affect the cost of living and supporting oneself at the county seat, compatible with the dignity of the office. 

The county director of equalization is also entitled to necessary traveling expenses as fixed for county officials, and before assuming office the director is required to file an oath and bond with the county auditor.

In addition to the county director of equalization, the school board of an unorganized county and the governing body of any organized township or municipality may appoint its own assessor or assessors, to serve subject to the recommendations of the director. 

These additional assessors serve at the pleasure of the appointing body.

The county commissioners may also appoint such other assessors as may be required. Any additional assessors are appointed subject to the recommendations of the director of equalization. Strangely, the law stipulates that all assessors appointed by the county commissioners "shall have the same powers and duties as the director of equalization."

The Office of Assessor 

Viewing the nation as a whole, one writer (Clyde F. 

Snider, Local Government in Rural America, 1957, pp. 409-410) has stated that effective administration of the property tax is hindered by small assessment districts and the methods by which assessors are chosen. Professor Snider observed that small districts make it necessary that assessors serve on a part-time basis, and the provision for popular election was not conductive to the selection of qualified persons for the position. The assessor, chosen by popular election, is often a local politician who is not prepared to perform the difficult and technical task of assigning valuation to real and personal properties of every conceivable kind, the writer added.

From an examination of the nation as a unit, Dr. Snider has concluded that satisfactory assessment would seem to require that full-time assessors be employed and that the selection of such persons be made under a system of examinations designed to secure qualified personnel. Such a procedure is applied in some of the states, and Kentucky requires that elected assessors qualify through examination. In other states, the qualifications for the position are loosely stated. Washington, for example, requires only that the assessor be a citizen of the United States and a qualified voter of the county. In sharp contrast, Iowa requires that assessors be chosen on merit by a board of composed of representatives from the board of supervisors, school boards, and municipal councils of the county.

South Dakota has taken steps, through the passage of the 1957 county assessors law and subsequent amendments, to provide equalized assessments within the various counties. Progress has also been made on upgrading the qualifications of persons who are assessors through the annual school for assessing officials held at the State University for the past several years. In addition, the Commissioner of Revenue has prepared a manual for these officials which contains not only a compilation of the laws governing assessors, but also additional supplemental information. 

Recommendations from the Commissioner of Revenue The Commissioner of Revenue has recommended that some means be made available for determining the qualifications for holding office as an assessor. One means, of course, would be to have all candidates for the office take a qualifying examination. second means would be to encourage persons to attend the annual assessors school at the State University. Satisfactory completion of the course offered at this school would help insure that assessors had a basic knowledge of proper procedures and also make them aware of possible new sources of information regarding assessing. If the assessors were encouraged to attend the school and required to take an examination, the Commissioner of Revenue might also prepare a listing of the candidates for office showing the relative competence of each. Or, after carefully considering centralized control and local control, one or all of the above could be made mandatory. That is, persons could be required to attend the school, take an examination, and appointment from a list of qualified candidates required of the county commissioners. 

Another recommendation from the Commissioner of Revenue is that tenure be accorded to persons appointed as assessors that they be removed only for cause. At the present time, the assessors serve at the will of the appointing body, but they must be reappointed at least every two years. 

The reason given for a longer term of office is that two years is not long enough for a qualified person to effect a program of re-evaluation and/ or equalization within the county. To set up, and put into effect, such a program usually requies several years. In addition, the insecurity might prevent well qualified persons from entering the profession. and The Commissioner has also recommended that the authority for appointment of assessors be changed. One reason for this suggestion is to provide a more accurate reflection of the various interests involved in the appointing body. At the present time, about sixty percent of the revenue from property is expended for school purposes. Yet, the school boards are not directly represented on the board appointing the director of equalization. A second example would be a county where the greatest portion of the assessed property lies within several municipalities, and the county commissioners appoint the director of equalization without the direct participation of city mayors and county members.

Finally, the Commissioner has recommended that the salaries of county assessors be increased. One reason advanced

for such an increase is that many assessors are forced to take extra jobs at the present time and cannot devote their full attention to assessing. The pay scale, if much too low, might also discourage qualified persons from entering the field. This recommendation is, of course, closely related to better qualified persons. Simply stated, if better qualifications are desired, a higher salary will probably be necessary.

In support of the Commissioner's recommendations, the assessors believe that the director of equalization is being charged with increasing responsibility, and that better qualified persons should be enlisted to bear this responsibility. Assessment is becoming more important and complex, the assessors contend, and as this process continues, good original assessments and equalization become more necessary.

BRANDON SCHOOL DISTRICT: "WINDFALL" TAXING BENEFITS Introduction 

At its November 27, 1961 meeting, the Executive Board of the Legislative Research Council considered several matters pertaining to taxation. One of these matters, brought to the 

Board's attention by Representative Ellen Bliss and elaborated upon by the Commissioner of Revenue, is the present property taxation in Brandon school district of Minnehaha County. Northern 

States Power Company has located a large plant in Brandon district and thereby provided the district with greatly increased property tax base. Although the Plant serves Sioux Falls chiefly, Brandon school district receives the benefits from its taxable valuation. 

The situation has resulted in low taxes on other property in the Brandon school district. Because there will be a second large power plant located in Brandon school district in the near future-the Pathfinder atomic plant--the Executive Board determined that the topic merited further study. The study of possible equalization of property taxes levied against public utility property when concentrations of such property result in "windfall benefits" to the district in which they are located was assigned to the Committee on Taxation.

Tax Levies in Independent School Districts of Minnehaha County There is little doubt that the tax problems facing Brandon school district and Sioux Falls are vastly different. 

The former is an area of relatively low population while the latter is a densely populated urban area. The location of the 

Northern States Power Company plant within the Brandon school district (independent district 145), plus the fact that public utility property is assessed by the State Department of Revenue (in contrast to other property, assessed locally), have resulted in a "windfall" for owners of other property in district 145. Briefly, the revenue received from the utility property provides a large portion of the total money necessary to operate local government. Consequently, taxes on other property can be reduced considerably. For example, the Department of Revenue estimates that the Northern States Power Company property represents about 65 percent of the total taxable property in the district. This property is valued at sixty percent of its full and true value by the state assessors, while at least a portion of the other property is taxed at a good deal less than sixty percent of its full and true value. (See also Appendix XI).

In addition to contributing to inequality in property taxes, the situation is said to influence people to move from Sioux Falls into district 145 because of the low property taxes.

Possible Solutions 

Because there may still be a good deal of inequality even after school district reorganized, it has been suggested that some other means of equalization be considered. One pos- sible solution would be to pro-rate the revenue from taxation of utilities among the various consuming districts. That is, if the Northern plant serves Sioux Falls primarily, the tax money collected would be distributed to Sioux Falls in proportion to the amount of total power generated that is consumed in that city. The principle upon which this is based is that the plant constructed chiefly to serve Sioux Falls and that the tax base would not exist if it were not for the city. was It

Such a procedure might work very well in Minnehaha County, and in fact, in many other areas of the state. might also produce greater inequities in some areas, and the net effect could be that one area would benefit at the expense of another. For example, cities such as Aberdeen, Watertown, and Mitchell would be forced to share a portion of their revenue from taxation of utilities with the surrounding areas. 

If it is true that the cities are now hard pressed for revenues, there is little doubt that any further reduction would result in additional hardships on them. 

A second consideration regarding pro-rating of revenues from taxation of utilities is that it is very questionable whether this could be applied only to electric power companies. Indeed, it might be reasonable to assume that it would be unconstitutional if the procedure did not apply to all public utilities in the state. Thus a change would effect the entire tax structure of South Dakota. 

A partial solution might be affected by strict enforcement of the present statutes. Requiring that all property be assessed at sixty percent full and true value would have the effect of shifting a portion of the present burden. from the Northern plant and distributing it across all the property in the district. Although an increase in the valuation of some property might result in slightly lower mill levy, it would accomplish some equalization.

AMENDMENT OF THE OLD AGE SURVIVORS INSURANCE LAWS 

At the February 26, 1962, meeting of the Executive

Board, Lloyd Jorgenson, state director of the Old Age and Survivors Insurance Division, discussed the problem of inadequate provisions to enforce the payment of the FICA taxes by political subdivisions of the state. All public employees of the state and its political subdivisions were covered under the Federal Social Security Act by Chapter 100, Session Laws of 

1951. Since that time there have been few means by which the state office, which is liable for the payment of the tax for all state employees and employees of the various political subdivisions, could insure that all the tax has been or is being paid. County and municipality employment does not, however, constitute a problem. The difficulty lies in the fact that since 1951, a number of small school districts have gone out of existence under the provisions of the school reorganization laws, some of which did not pay enough or any of the FICA tax for which they were liable. Former employees who were employed by school districts have applied for OASI benefits, and it was discovered that not all the employment taxes were paid for these employees. So far, no serious problems have arisen because the division had been able to make collections from the delinquent political subdivisions. It should be recognized, however, that as school district reorganization continues, more and more small school districts will go completely out of existence. 

In order that the state not be forced to pay the FICA employment taxes if a delinquent political subdivision is no longer in existence, the director of the Old Age and 

Survivors Insurance Division said additional authority for his office is needed, the text of which request is reproduced as Appendix XIII. The Executive Board, by formal motion, referred this matter to the Committee on Taxation. 

At the April 16, 1962, meeting of the Committee on

Taxation, Lloyd Jorgenson again discussed these problems. 

An intensive educational program undertaken since February by Jorgenson, however, had considerably eased the situation. 

It is evident that the problem does not arise because of an intentional desire to violate the state law, but more simply because of ignorance on the part of school district clerks of what the state law provides. Difficulties in enforcing the collection of these employment taxes, however, do exist.

RECOMMENDATIONS 

The Committee recommends against the adoption of taxation based on an average annual inventory of personal property or a business franchise tax in lieu of personal property taxes.

There were several considerations which prompted the Committee to reject the average inventory assessment procedure in lieu of personal property taxes. The most compelling reason was that the unofficial opinion of the Attorney General indicated that there were serious doubts as to the constitutional validity of two proposed measures, an average annual inventory for all businesses or an optional average inventory for the use of those businessmen choosing to use it (opinion reproduced as Appendix V). In essence the proposals seem to violate the provisions of Article XI of the South Dakota Constitution which requires that taxes be uniform on all property of the same class. 

The violation of this uniformity provision occurs when certain property is taxed in a manner different from that in which the same property would be taxed were it in the hands of persons other than businessmen. A second consideration is that property in the hands of non-businessmen would be assessed according to its value on the assessment day, while property in a business inventory would not necessarily all be assessed on that day and would thus be "exempted, " in violation of Section 7 of 

Article XI. 

It

A primary reason for the interest in an annual average inventory was to provide personal property tax relief. is recognized that certain kinds of businesses are at a disadvantage vis-a-vis other types of businesses under the present assessment situation. It must be recognized, however, that a change in the present situation might simply reverse the disadvantageous situation, requiring tax relief later for another segment of the business community. As one business representative remarked, "Business has learned to live with the present situation, but something new might create more inequities than presently exist."

In rejecting the average inventory approach, the Committee was influenced by a third factor: the fact that the system, without a doubt, would be more expensive to operate, both for the businessmen and for the state agency collecting the taxes, since the localities would not be equipped to handle the complicated procedure. Numerous spot checks would be required. More than one inventory each year would be quite costly to the businesses. Because of the high cost, there is reason to assume that the businesses involved might simply provide "rough guesses" of an inventory held, in violation of the law. 

Enforcement problems would be compounded.

Finally, there is no reason there is no reason to assume that tax collections with an average inventory procedure would be sufficiently increased to provide for the increased costs of enforcement and collection. A number of states have enacted the average inventory assessments, and, subsequently, repealed the measures. 

The reason most frequently given was increased costs of collection and the difficulties encountered by the businessmen themselves. 

It is, therefor, the belief of the Committee that the average inventory assessment process should not be adopted. 

In making this recommendation, the Committee is knowledgeable of the problems faced by the farm implement dealers whose Association brought the request for this study in the first instance. However, despite its understanding of, and sympathy with, the tax relief needs which prompted the request, the Committee cannot make a favorable recommendation on this approach. 

Regarding the second request that the Committee consider a business franchise tax in lieu of personal property taxes: the Committee was advised by the Attorney General that a business franchise tax would be constitutionally valid (see opinion, Appendix V). No states have ever applied business franchise taxes to all businesses in lieu of personal property taxes. In essence, a business franchise tax is nothing more than a gross receipts or income tax. This form of taxation has been used in some state where the value of property is difficult to establish, i.e., large corporations, public utilities, and businesses in which profits are derived from a service requiring a relatively small investment, such as insurance companies. 

A business franchise tax would have to be administered at the state level, since Internal Revenue Bureau records are not available for use as a cross-check by local political subdivisions, thus decreasing local control of the level of taxation. Critics point out that a business franchise tax approach does not take into account the amount of capital invested or the cost of operations. The return or profit from a low-profit-to-investment ratio business is taxed at the same rate as the return from a highprofit-to-investment ratio business; the ability to pay is quite different. All the arguments in opposition to income taxes can be used with regard to the business franchise tax. The Committee does not recommend this approach to provide tax relief.

It was the consensus of the Committee that up-grading of county directors of equalization is needed. In order that the county directors of equalization need not return to the Legislature to amend salary scales, the Committee decided to recommend the elimination of any statutory salary minimums or maximums; the Committee believes that these limitations are not necessary since the boards of county commissioners are elected officials. The Committee, for the same reason, does not recommend longer terms of appointment or removal only for cause. If well-qualified county directors of equalization are recruited, selected and paid a good salary, the recommendations of the SDAAO to improve tenure do not appear to be needed. 

The Committee recognizes that additional adjustments in these several provisions may be required in the future but believes that the changes here recommended should go far toward solving problems which have arisen at the present time.

3. 11 The Committee recommends that the Thirty-eighth Legislature consider the adoption of: a. April 15 of each year as the uniform reporting date for public service companies, and b. a uniform method of hearings for all public companies before the Commissioner of Revenue, with provision for appeal of his decision to the State 

Board of Equalization. 

The present systems of reporting dates of regulatory and tax reports and various methods of appeal for those public utilities known as service companies have literally "grown like Topsy. Legislation providing for the regulation and taxing of the various public service companies was adopted at widely varying times, some as early as 1897 and some as late as 1939. There are, therefore, great differences in the time required by the laws for these companies to make reports of business conditions and earnings for changes in assessments. There are also great differences in the manner by which these same companies may seek redress from arbitary official rulings of the Department of Taxation and Revenue. 

Currently, it appears that inadequate time is allowed for preparing reports to the Commissioner of Revenue. Both the representative of Northwestern Bell Telephone and the counsels for several private electric companies testified that insufficient time is allowed for developing reports. In addition, the different reporting dates create a workload problem for the Department. The lack of uniform appeals procedure constitutes a different problem, inasmuch as it affects the rights of public utilities with regard to appeals from arbitrary dicisions rendered by the Department. 

The present law creates much confusion. Both the public service companies and the Commissioner of Revenue agree on the desirability of the two changes. The Committee therefore recommends serious consideration of these suggestions.

The Committee recommends that the Legislature take no action with regard to the Brandon school district and Minnehaha county property tax inequity problem. 

The inequitable property tax situation in Brandon school district in Minnehaha county grew out of the Northern States Power's decision to locate two power plants in the school district. 

One conventional power plant is currently in operation, and the Pathfinder atomic power plant is under construction. Because of the high value at which these installations are assessed, the Brandon school district levies a very low mill rate. At the same time, other school districts in Minnehaha county are levying very high mill rates, but are paying for electricity generated in Brandon school district. Many people regard the tax resources available to the Brandon school district as "windfall" benefits which should be shared with other less fortunate districts. 

It is the belief of the Committee, however, that any attempt at legislative solution of this Minnehaha county problem would cause inequities in other parts of the state, where municipalities would then be required to share tax collections of utilities located within the town and serving other nearby communities. 

Because part of the problem has been solved through school district reorganziation, no action should be taken on the state level. 

In the opinion of the Committee, this is a local problem, capable of solution locally.

The Committee recommends that the Legislature adopt provisions to require certification by the county superintendent of school of all operating school districts and their employees. to the OASI director. The Committee also recommends that the 

Interest Fund for the OASI Division be renamed the Indemity and Interest Fund and the provisions of the law be amended so that the function of the funds fits the new name. The Committee recommends further that authority be granted to deputize county superintendents of schools to collect FICA taxes from delinquent school districts, the cost of the travel for which is to be paid by the deliquent school district. 

The potential cost to the state of paying FICA taxes for political subdivisions which have gone out of existance is great. Several millions of dollars are annually collected and remitted to the federal government. As school reorganization progresses, the likelihood that these small school districts will cease to exist materially increases. No matter how effective an educational program is instituted, there will always be school district clerks who are unfamiliar with FICA taxes, and the change exists that they will fail to pay the taxes. The county superintendent of schools, however, has clear knowledge of all employees of the various school districts and is in the best position to insure that all employment taxes are properly paid.

Appendix II CORRESPONDENCE CONSIDERED BY THE COMMITTEE IN RELATION TO ITS STUDY OF PROPERTY TAXATION BASED ON AVERAGE INVENTORIES OF MERCHANTS COPY FEDERATION OF TAX ADITI NISTRATORS

December 3, 1956 

Dear Sir:

In our conversation in Los Angeles you asked me about the advisability of substituting an average basis of valuation for the valuation as of a given assessment date as the basis for assessing inventories of manufacturers and stock in trade of merchants. On the whole, there are, I think, as many difficulties involved under the one system as under the other.

It is well recognized that some businessmen may have especially high inventories on the assessment date and that others will have unusually low inventories on the same date. Nevertheless, tax officials and others who prefer the single assessment date basis take the view that the shifting of inventory and stock in trade among various levels of manufacturing and mercantile businesses is inevitable and that the only way to insure that all property within the state on the tax date is taxed to someone, is to maintain the single date assessment basis. In this connection they point out that whatever inequalities there are in the situation do not ordinarily affect competitors in the same line of business but rather that disparities in the inventory situation occur among manufacturers or merchants in different lines of business. For example, coal merchants' inventories might be highest at a time when the stocks of a fertilizer or agriculture equipment dealer are lowest, etc. This point was the subject of a comment in the 

Bulletin of the Assessment and Taxation Division of Oregon State Tax Commission, dated April 30, 1954, as follows: 

This same situation holds true as to the January 1 assessment date whereby some establishments have high inventories, others have low ones, and hence an apparent inequity. Here again such apparent inequity is between unlike and noncompeting businesses. Concerns in the same line of trade will all be taxed much the same because of the quantity of holdings on January 1.

In the latest compilation which the National Association of Assessing Officers has published, 17 states and the District of

Columbia are classified as jurisdictions where inventories and stock in trade are assessed on an annual average basis. In 

Michigan and Oregon an average monthly valuation basis is optional and, with one or two exceptions, all the other states where inventories or stock in trade are subject to tax, the single assessment date basis is used.

Most of the states which use the average annual assessment basis do not lay down strict rules for determining or supporting the monthly average value which the merchant or manufacturer is presumed to compute. Many of these laws and regulations provide only that the merchant or manufacturer shall estimate the amount of inventory for each month in the preceding year. A few states do lay down some standards to insure that these "estimates" of monthly inventories are backed up by some figures. For example, in Maryland, an actual inventory or values taken from the books of account must be used. In Colorado, the assessor is instructed to value the inventories at a higher percentage of full value than he would ordinarily use, in any case where the average monthly figure is not supported by at least four actual inventories while in Alabama the statute provides that the average value on an estimated basis may not be less than the capital actually invested in the business.

I understand that in many states where the annual average method is employed it is the administrative practice to accept an average of what ever inventories are taken during the year. Where the taxpayer takes two, or three or four inventories they are averaged. However, in cases where the taxpayer takes only one inventory a year, and I think this is not uncommon, the practical effect is that you will get either the December 31 inventory or one taken at a time when stocks will be at their lowest. A fiscal year federal taxpayer may take an annual inventory at the end of his fiscal year, and, from the standpoint of convenience and ease the date might very well coincide with a low point in the inventory cycle. Incidentally, there are many indications that the practice of using a fiscal year for federal tax purposes will continue to grow.

While a number of states have maintained some sort of an average basis for the assessment of inventories and stock in trade, there are other states where this plan, apparently, has not worked out satisfactorily on a mandatory basis. For example, Utah instituted the average basis in 1937 and repealed it in 1939. 

Iowa has moved back and forth a couple of times. South Carolina, which once used the average monthly assessment, has gone back to the single assessment date. Kansas is another state where, I believe, the monthly average basis was once in use but is no longer. 

The mandatory average basis has considerable appeal for merchants or manufacturers who would be favorably affected by it.

but at the same time there are many others who fare better under the existing system. Moreover, if satisfactory results are to be obtained, the laws, regulations and administrative policies relating to the basis of computing the so-called average inventory would have to be stricter, it is recognized, than those presently found in most states. Businessmen who favor the retention of a single assessment date point out that they are accustomed to it and that it is compatible with their present practices concerning the taking of inventories. Adrian Kragen, counsel for the California Retailers' Association, presented the retailer's viewpoint before a legislative committee in California in these words:

I should say in this regard that we are against averaging. We are against averaging for a number of reasons. 

First, we think it is administratively difficult from the standpoint of the assessor. It offers all sorts of possibilities for--for those who haven't the proper responsibility for checking and moving around their inventory. It also is very costly for single store operations. It is expensive because you have to adjust inventory each period, whenever the periods happen to be. It is--in branch store operations, it is nearly impossible to adjust that many times without a very expensive operation, and in addition, we believe that in the retail field especially and in the wholesale field, the jobber field, that it would result in such poor merchandising policy that we think we could show it would increase the cost of merchandising immeasurably to the retailer, to the consumer, rather, that we would have substantial increases in various types of merchandise because now you buy on the basis of a long-range program, and if you were trying to meet tax dates, you would be adjusting, and as a result, the costs would go up. You would have more freight problems, you would have other problems to measure against the tax costs. We think it would be very bad.

Other opposition to average inventory valuation came from the warehouse industry and also from farmers and distributors. 

Those who supported the proposition of average inventory included manufacturers of building materials, fertilizer, apparel, steel, lumber and wood industries. The local assessors who testified appeared to have no objection to the use of an average basis rather than the single assessment date. A summary of the testimony at the hearings held by the Interim Committee on State and Local Taxation of the California Senate appears in the committee's report entitled THE TAXATION OF PERSONAL PROPERTY IN CALIFORNIA (Part II), 

January 1955.

After considering the evidence that had been laid before it the committee said this in its statement of recommendation:

With respect to improving the assessment of one type of personal property, via, business inventories, the committee has for some time favored the use of an average inventory value method. With this method, the value of business inventories would be determined by averaging annually the ending inventories for each month, or for each quarter. However, pending more adequate data as to the effects of this method of inventory assessment and unanimity of support by business and industry, the committee, which favors it largely for reasons relating to the improvement of assessment practices, cannot unqualifiedly recommend its adoption at this time.

A step in the right direction might be taken by providing the alternatives of assessing inventories either as of a fixed lien date, or as an average (monthly or quarterly) of the preceding calendar of fiscal year. Selection of the latter method of inventory valuation by a taxpayer would be contingent upon his maintenance of adequate and reliable records. Moreover, in the interests of ease of administrat on, the latter method of inventory valuation, once elected by the taxpayer, should become the permanent method by which the assessed valuation of his inventories is determined. The case for the use of an average inventory assessment as an alternative to assessment on a fixed lien date is well set forth in a recent report of the Legislative Interim Tax Study Committee of the State of Oregon. Their views are presented in Appendix A.

The recommendations of the Interim Tax Study Committee of Oregon and also a statement on a series of inventory checks conducted by the Oregon Tax Commission are included as appendices A and B respectively of the report of the California 

Interim Committee.

In Oregon the Interim Committee took the view that from a theoretical standpoint the monthly average inventory assessment is more equitable than any other, but pointed out that from a practical standpoint the case for assessment of inventory based on a monthly average is not so clear. In this respect the Oregon 

Committee said: ...So far as the research conducted through our own State 

Tax Commission has shown, there is not a single example of a truly effective application of the average inventory method in any of the states which are required or permitted to follow such assessment practice. Your Committee believes that, for the present at least, a general requirement for assessment of inventory based upon the monthly average, is administratively impracticable. Only a very minute number of total taxpayers presently maintain records which would be sufficient to provide monthly inventories. Taxpayers, as a whole, should not be required to undertake extensive and complicated bookkeeping procedure in order to satisfy the require-

ments of an average inventory statute. On the other hand, we would not approve a statute which required the assessor to accept monthly estimates furnished by the taxpayer, in the absence of any adequate proof. 

For practical reasons, we recommend against the adoption of any general law requiring the assessment of all inventory property by the monthly average method.

The Oregon Committee went on to recommend a permissive or optional use of average inventory method by those taxpayers who maintain or who are willing to adopt records adequate to support property tax inventories on a basis of a monthly average. The committee recognized that its recommendation would be appealing only to those taxpayers whose January 1 inventories are unreasonably high and that the adoption of the optional use of the monthly inventory basis would not make any change in the tax situation of those property taxpayers whose inventories as of January 1 are at a low level.

The recommendation of the Oregon Interim Tax Commission was accepted by the legislature and, in general, the Oregon treatment of inventories now follows the pattern set by Michigan when it adopted an optional monthly average basis in 1950, I believe it was. 

In Oregon the tax commission has laid down a number of rules governing the procedures to be followed where inventories or stock in trade are to be assessed on the monthly average basis. Article 8292.1 

C-1 provides that the monthly average value shall be ascertained by taking the true cash value of all stock, contemplated by the statute, within the county, and owned or held by the taxpayer on the last business day of each month of the preceding year, adding such monthly values together and dividing the total by the number of months the taxpayer was engaged in business during that year. The result is the average value. Section C-3 provides that special categories of stock will take the unit values established by the commission and with respect to reports of goods in these categories the taxpayer includes only the measure of quantity.

Paragraph D states that adequate "records" are those which will enable the assessor to determine the taxpayer's stock on hand at the end of each month and the true cash value thereof. If a taxpayer "costs" his sales monthly or maintains perpetual inventories, the monthly inventory which is to be averaged can readily be determined from these records. If this information is not available the taxpayer may determine the monthly ending inventories for all months except the month in which the accounting period ends, by the application of percentages of gross profit. The regulation goes on to specify the precise procedures to be followed when this method is utilized.

From the comments made in the report of the Oregon legislative Interim Tax Study Committee it appears that its action in recommending an optional monthly average basis for inventories was in great measure due to the situation in the lumber industry where tremendous inventories were built up during the summer and the fall to sustain winter operations, with the result that on January 1 inventories were very high.

Where the use of a single assessment date is prejudicial to an important segment of the economic life of a state this optional monthly average basis is undoubtedly as good a means as any other in providing a measure of equity to the property taxpayer. Moreover, if the use of the optional basis is made contingent on the maintenance of records that are sufficient to support the monthly average inventory and these requirements are strictly enforced, it is probable that the use of the optional monthly average method will be, for all practical purposes, limited to those who would derive a real benefit from it, namely those taxpayers who are now in a sense penalized because, for one reason or another, their inventories are unduly high on the assessment date.

In writing to you yesterday about the use of a monthly average basis for the assessment of inventories and stock in trade I completely forgot to include a comment on the developments in

The mandatory average basis was enacted by the legislature at the 1955 session and on November 26, 1955 the attorney general wrote a formal opinion holding that the statute was unconstitutional. The attorney general said that it violated the uniformity clause of the constitution in so far as the inventory and stock in trade of those persons and corporations defined as merchants would be taxed by a method that was different from that employed for taxing other forms of property. The attorney general pointed out that even within the class defined as merchants (which includes manufactures) there was a violation of the uniformity clause because merchants properly so called were taxed on the basis of the average value of their inventories while manufactures properly so called were taxed only with respect to the average value of the raw material components of their inventories. 

The attorney general went on to say that this statute also violated the section of the constitution dealing with exemptions from property taxes inasmuch as the property in the hands of some classes of citizens on assessment day was taxed with respect to its value on that day whereas another class, namely those defined as merchants, were not taxed with respect to the value of property which they held on that same date. It was the opinion of the attorney general that in this respect the statute also violated the privileges and immunities clause of the Nebraska constitution.

Dear Ernie:

I am sorry that time does not permit me to secure the necessary approval to write Senator Risty direct and express our Company's position upon the tax matters covered in the Senator's letter to our Mr. Patterson, dated August 18.

Instead I will pass on to you, as expressed herein, my personal observations as expressed in our telephone conversation in Chicago Friday. 

The ad valorem Tax should continue to produce its share of the revenue. 

This tax, however, should be kept well below the point of where it would become confiscatory or a hardship, otherwise it would discourage property ownership and result in an undesirable increase in the number of tax payments becoming delinquent. Thus, in a manner of speaking, this tax has definite limitations and may not be increased indefinitely. Therefore, other types of tax must be resorted to if it is necessary to raise more revenue. I submit, however, that such other taxes are "additional" rather than "property tax relief measures."

In the interest of legal equity, all taxes should be uniform as to subjects and to the objects and transactions taxed. It seems that it would be a mistake to unnecessarily narrow the base of a tax by classification or exemption. When there is preferential treatment of any class of transactions or objects taxed, the remainder must carry "Z loads" as the whole tax burden must be carried.

In our conversation you par ticularyly requested that I discuss my experience in the midwestern states with respect to the subject of merchandise inventory assessments. Some of these states such as Illinois have Constitutional requirements of uniformity in property assessments and taxation. In these states inventories, as personal property, are valued upon the same basis as all other property with respect to rules for determining value, arbitrary assessment dates, etc., This treatment may be predicated upon the theory that, by and large, there is substantially no greater fluctuation in inventories upon a year to year basis than with some other types of property. Also consideration may be given to the fact that inventory items which are sold or consumed are subject to other forms of taxation, such as sales, use, income, etc.

Some of the States have adopted one form or another of averaging inventories. Some of these forms are: (a) average monthly inventories during the previous year, (b) average inventories for three (3) or more inventory periods, (c) average cost or investment in inventories for previous year to be determined in some manner, (d) greatest amount on hand between January 1 and May 1, (e) taxpayers option of an arbitrary assessment date or monthly inventories.

In nearly all of these methods there are two (2) grave faults. One is the additional burden upon the taxpayer of taking, counting, valuing and reporting. In some cases this may be just as burdensome to the individual druggist, grocer, or general store proprietor as it is to the corporation with several different locations scattered throughout the state. The other major fault is the administrative headache caused by the difficulty in administering this type assessment. 

The average assessor's office does not have competent personnel to audit and evaluate this type of tax return.

It is doubtful that the additional tax revenue, if any, resulting from the average inventory method will cover the additional cost, expense and muisance involved in collecting it. Perhaps an exception should be made of some of the larger cities, particularly where enabling legislation has empowered the assessor to examine and audit all of each taxpayers private books and records. After some experience with this method of assessment, many jurisdictions have unofficially (and perhaps illegally) reverted to some more practical method. In Iowa, assessors generally shy away from the average inventory and have invented a procedure whereby they arbitrarily determine that an inventory at a bulk oil station is equivalent to one half of the capacity of the storage tanks. Other assessors attempt to determine the annual sales volume and divide the aggregate amount by a fictitious turn-over factor. In Colorado a penalty method is invoked thus; if four (4) or more acceptable inventories are submitted, the valuation is calculated at 50%. Otherwise 65% or a greater percentage may be taken by the assessor. Ohio reports must be "per books" But the taxpayer is saved considerable expense by being authorized to file a single return with the state rather than with each assessor if he does business in more than one county. Other taxable jurisdictions, even in states requiring average inventories, prefer that an equal amount be taken as the assessment each year in order to prevent greatly fluctuating values. 

From the above, you can see that I favor the arbitrary assessment date method, however, I believe that the same date should apply to all over the state in order to prevent possible tax dodging.

Appendix V UNOFFICIAL OPINION OF THE ATTORNEY GENERAL REGARDING CONSTITUTIONALITY OF PROPERTY ASSESSMENT ACCORDING TO AVERAGE INVENTORIES AND IMPOSITION OF BUSINESS FRANCHISE TAXATION COPY STATE OF SOUTH DAKOTA OFFICE OF ATTORNEY GENERAL Dean E. Clabaugh 

May 29, 1962

Director of Legislative Research State Capitol 

Pierre, South Dakota

Dear Mr. Clabaugh: 

The Attorney General has requested that information be supplied you as to possible constitutionality of certain measures now under consideration by the committee on Taxation of the State Legislative Research Council. Three questions you propose have to do with methods of assessing property for the purpose of ad valorem taxation. The methods are; assessment on an average level of inventory over the business year, an optional average inventory and a business franchise, in lieu of part or all ad valorem taxes.

Our Constitution provides in Section 2, Article 11 that taxes shall be uniform on all property of the same class and also in Section 6 and 7 of the same Article are declared certain exemptions from assessment and taxation and a prohibition against exempting any other properties.

In view of these Constitutional provisions it is my opinion that your proposed methods 1 and 2 would not be proper as it is my belief they would violate the uniformity clause in attempting to tax the stock in inventory of certain businesses in a manner different from that in which the same property in the hands of other persons would be taxed. These methods may be unconstitutional for another reasons that being property in the hands of citizens on assessment day would be valued according to its value on that day, while property in inventory would not necessarily all be assessed and would thus be exempted in violation of Section 7, Article 11. 

It is my further opinion that a business franchise tax as suggested on your question 3 would possibly be constitutional. There as several such franchise taxes on our statute books at this time including the taxes on banks and financial corporations as well as certain utilities. Since the basis for this tax is not the value of property at any time but rather is determined by the business done by the taxpayer, it is not an ad valorem tax and does not come under the Sections of Article 11 above noted. There seems to be no prohibition against prescribing such a tax and providing the same shall be in lieu of all other taxes and licenses, state, county and local except taxes on real property. 

Very truly yours, /s/ 

JOHN DEWELL 

Assistant Attorney General

Pierre. All applicants who pass said examination will remain eligible for a period of two (2) years, and those who fail shall be ineligible to retake the examination for a period of six (6) months. A current list of passing applicants shall be kept and made available to all County Boards of Commissioners from which they shall appoint Directors of Equalization. 

An assessor appointed by a Municipality, should come under this qualification.

Appointment: 

Appointment should be made as it is now, but only from the above mentioned eligible list. Present assessors could

come under a grandfather clause, or be given the opportunity to comply within a period of say one (1) year from the effective date of the bill. Tenure:

We suggest a tenure of office appointment to be four (4) years, subject to reappointment without re-examination, and subject to removal for just cause after a public hearing, if requested in writing by the office-holder. 

Salary: 

We suggest that the present salary schedule be increased in the following way:

In the counties with a population of less than 5,000the present minimums and maximums be increased 20%. 

In counties of 5,000 or more, the minimum and maximum be increased 15%, except in counties of 20,000 or more, that the maximum not be changed.

We feel that a few comments are in order in regard to salary ranges. Since the creation of the office of 

Director of Assessments back in 1955, the salary has been somewhat tied to the salary of County Auditors and Treasurers. This, we believe is wrong, and cannot be substantiated in logical reasoning. The duties of the 

Auditors and Treasurers are set out in laws and regulations, resulting in no more than an administrative job of seeing that predetermined figures are placed in proper place and proper ledgers are kept. The Director's job, on the other hand, in our growing state and economy, is becoming more and more complicated and on the executive order. The good assessor must not only find through his own diligent work the value of property, but then must explain and justify that valuation, not only to the property owner, but to any taxpayer, or in some cases the courts of our state. Legislative Research Council 

Pierre, South Dakota 

Dear Mr. Clabaugh, December 7, 1961

COPY 

The division which I head is responsible for the collection of Federal Insurance Act (Social Security) 

taxes, from all covered employees of the State of South Dakota and its political sub-divisions. 

The Federal State Agreement under which the OASI Division operates provides that the State of South Dakota is liable for all FICA tax due from all of the political sub-divisions in the State of South Dakota. 

The Federal Government does not deal directly with political sub-divisions. It deals with the State of 

South Dakota through the Division of Old Age and 

Survivors Insurance. Our annual collections of FICA 

tax approximate $5,000,000.00. Our potential liability is therefore substantial. 

Under existing laws we do not have adequate authority to enforce collection of FICA tax from the political subdivisions in the State.

In view of the fiscal liability involved and the responsibility I have as Director of the Division, I propose for consideration by the Legislative Research Council, the following items of legislation which will improve our control over the collection of FICA tax` from the political sub-divisions in the State and will assure collection of the tax due.

I consider these proposals necessary for effective administration of this Division. I have not attempted to include detailed discussion of the merits of these proposals in this transmittal to you. However I would welcome the opportunity to discuss the proposal in detail with a Legislative Research Council Committee, if the proposals are accepted for study by such a Committee. 

Thanking you and the Committee for your consideration.

Facts about the Legislative Research Council The State Legislative Research Council is a permanent, joint committee composed of every member of the South Dakota Senate and House of Representatives. Its purpose is to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature. To achieve this purpose, the 

Council, during the interim between sessions, gives advance study and consideration to problems expected to confront the next Legislature. 

The work of the Council is conducted by committees and subcommittees and by a research staff, under the direction of an Executive Board. The Council staff serves the Executive Board and committees and subcommittees in development of a program for consideration at the next legislative session, and serves every legislator in an impartial and objective fact-finding capacity. 

In its work, the Executive Board: 1) acts as a clearing house for ideas on current legislative problems through receipt of proposals for study; 2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all topics accepted; 3) disseminates advance information on these problems to legislators and the general public by means of reports and newsletters and by discussion at public meetings; and 4) reports directly to the Legislature in advance of regular sessions, making recommendations in the form of bills or otherwise, and summarizes the material prepared for use of the Legislature in considering the Council program. 

Since 1951 the State Legislative Research Council has provided an open forum to which legislators, administrators, citizens, and organizations have submitted their proposals for consideration. 

As a result of Council activities, problems of South Dakota state and local government receive continuous attention and study by members of the Legislature.
