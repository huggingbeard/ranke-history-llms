FOREWORD 

HIS little book attempts to give memories of a Quaker childhood, which began more than three-quarters of a century ago on a West New Jersey farm. 

There has been no endeavor to picture the lives of the grown-ups, except as their influence touched the children about them. R. N. T.

HE shall be called Elizabeth. That is

SH not her real name, but the name she thought she ought to have when she arrived at the age when one criticizes the deeds of one's parents.

If the innocent little soul could have chosen the place of her arrival in this wonderful world, we might say she used discretion. It was, to be sure, in the year 1857, a time of great financial depression, but she did not know anything about that until more than fifty years later. She came to a part of the country where there were no floods, no famines, no earthquakes.

No wars and no mobs were to disquiet the neighborhood. It was one of the most fertile and well situated farms in West New Jersey. 

Redeemed from the primeval forest, it had for many years been cultivated and tamed into submission by careful Quakers, and showed the effect of their faithful and intelligent work.

In the last half of the seventeenth century, West New Jersey passed by purchase into the hands of members and organizations of the Society of Friends, mostly from England and Ireland. It was laid out in sections by London Quakers. This was before William Penn came over to take possession of his grant in Pennsylvania but he had much to do with the planning of it. We know his fondness for straight lines which was perhaps a general result of Quaker psychology, and well illustrated in the plan of old Philadelphia. 

It may well be that it was Penn himself who took a map and ruler and divided West Jersey into rectangular tenths. Therefore, in the neighborhood about Elizabeth's home, all the boundary lines were as straight as could be made. Her father's farm was straight in all its borders. It lay at the junction of two old highways. on the farm were straight. The fields were laid out in five and ten acre plots, between straight lines of fences. A crooked furrow was a disgrace. The Master of the Farm was an expert in such matters and his ploughmen were carefully chosen and trained. It was said of him that, in his early days, he

A straight row of trees, placed carefully at exact distances apart led up to the rectangular house, through which ran a wide hall, with rectangular rooms on either side. Everything in that house was in its proper and appointed place. The Master of the Farm would say, as he looked around to see if any picture was hanging crooked, "I like things ship-shape.' 

Inside and out, all things were kept in strict order. If a rash weed dared to raise its head, it was not for long. There was no sagging gate or broken fence. No farm machinery was allowed out of its proper shed when night came on; and horses, wagons, and machines seemed to last forever. Seldom indeed was rain allowed to fall on sheaves of wheat or piles of hay. A cloud in the sky, and they were all swept into the barns. A very tidy world it was that greeted Elizabeth's eyes. 

She was a plump and rosy baby. The Master of the Farm was very proud of his first daughter. In an incautious moment he showed her to Great-aunt Priscilla. Of all

Quakers this good lady was devoted to "plainness of speech, behavior and apparel." She allowed no superfluities, no idle words, no vanity. Therefore she looked severely at 

Elizabeth and said, in tones of sadness, "Her jowls are very fat, but she will get over it." 

Fortunate it was that the Master of the Farm had a sense of humor. He loved to recall this event. His next exhibition worked better. By that time, Elizabeth had learned to say a few words. He displayed her to a group of young aunts and others more inclined to admire than Aunt Priscilla. One of them asked pleasantly, "What is thee made for, Elizabeth?" The baby answered promptly, "Made for tissen."

Such frivolity would have been a shock to her great-aunt. 

Great-uncle Isaac was a pleasant, social man, but he was obliged to satisfy the scruples of Aunt Priscilla. Therefore he laid aside his red topped boots, painted black the silver handles of his carriage, and conformed to her ideal of Quakerism. By the time Elizabeth was born these extremes began to fade away, but they clung long in remote country neighborhoods.

11 snapshots, etched on her consciousness as separate occurrences. One of these recalls the face of her elder brother, John. She can see him plainly now after all these many years. He looks at her through a window against a background of snow-covered shrubbery. His face is framed by a warm cap and comforter. His large brown eyes are shining, his cheeks are red. Elizabeth was for the first time conscious of beauty, and for all her life she was to hold precious the beauty of the human countenance.

The religious influences about Elizabeth were very strong, but her earliest thought was given to Satan. Goodness to her was a matter of course, and she was entirely satisfied about it. She found no problem there. But why were people bad? Satan, of course.

Well, she was ready for him. Occasionally, Satan seemed to get after John. Then would she take her little broom and clear out every corner of the nursery, driving Satan into the stove, where he belonged. After this performance there was naturally an improvement in John. It has rather irked Elizabeth since that modern religion seems to ignore Satan. She can't help thinking that there is

something somewhere that makes people bad. 

It was natural, in that immaculate household, that Elizabeth should rely upon her broom, and that her earliest little ditty, sung with enthusiasm, should be: "Myja, with her brush and broom, Makes a very tidy room."

Some early fears disturbed the mind of Elizabeth. As she lay alone in a dark bedroom in the second story she thought it very possible that there were lions and bears in the hall below. She said her prayers, of course, but she also wanted some intellectual assurance. She hoped that lions and bears could not walk up-stairs, but she could not be sure. Finally she found peace in the thought that as her grand-parents and parents had never been eaten by lions or bears, it was more than probable that it would not happen to her, and so very reasonably concluded that she was safe. She now finds herself falling back on a line of reasoning somewhat similar, as she considers the destructive influences of the present day.

In her next memory she sees herself facing an immense turkey-gobbler. His spread tail, his red wattles, his menacing looks appall her. Ah, here comes the all-powerful Master of the Farm. He beats off the gobbler with a dried corn-stalk. How excellent is a corn-stalk for defense from a turkey-gobbler! 

Then came wider adventures. Elizabeth is visiting cousins. They have a wooden velocipede and skim along over the brick path

at an amazing rate. She is conscious of a powerful ambition to ride on that velocipede. 

She is placed on the seat, which is in the form of a little arm-chair, but the velocipede does not move. Nothing that she can do will make it go. Oh, the stupidity, the mortification, the shame! It is years afterward, as this ineffaceable experience arises in her mind, that she suddenly realizes that it was not stupidity but the shortness of her legs that caused the dreadful situation, so now she is comforted. 

The next experience brings the joy and sorrow of a great adventure. She goes to visit her Aunt Hannah, the most delightful and wonderful of aunts. In the first act, Elizabeth recalls herself walking up an unknown street, past a gate, with a stone dog reclining on each gate post. Someone says, "When those dogs see their master they run to meet him." She is greatly confused by this remark. An explanation is given, but her mind cannot grasp it. Today as she sometimes passes those stone dogs, a shade of the old mystery comes over her.

Aunt Hannah was a source of great thoughts and also of great pleasures. Elizabeth always learned something from her.

ANIMALS 

HE animals about the farm were a great source of interest to Elizabeth.

There were the calves in their stalls, the big, angry bull, the cows coming back from the fields and gathering around the water-trough. Banquo, the great St. Bernard dog, was the children's companion. His special gift was driving out the cows, herded along the road past the front gate on their way to market. These loved to rush in and crop the soft green grass of the lawn. Then the children would call, "Here Bank, here 

Bank," and the dog would dash out and drive the cows back to the road. This was often a difficult matter as frequently the cows would put down their heads and threaten the dog with their horns. They never touched him. but the sight was thrilling to Elizabeth. She never cared for thrills. The daily round was exciting enough for her.

One of the most fascinating features of the farm was the pigs. They walked about a large farmyard, and were white and clean as pigs on such a farm should be. Their hair waved attractively, and they had long, curly tails. Elizabeth, watching them, was stirred by a great desire. How delightful it would be to catch a pig by the tail! For some time her efforts were in vain. The pigs seemed to know when she was coming, and moved on at the crucial moment. At last the opportunity came. Under a great cherry tree was a large and happy pig, cracking cherry stones

with his jaws, and oblivious of all else. Very quietly Elizabeth stole behind him and grasped his tail. It was a terrible shock to the pig. As we all know, pigs cannot see behind them, but it seemed imperative that this pig should do so. 

Therefore he spun round like a top. Elizabeth, in a sudden panic, instinctively felt it was safer to hold on than to let go, and so for a time that seemed endless to both parties, they spun around together. At last Elizabeth could hold on no longer. She let go and fled. She ran one way, the pig ran the other. Her ambition was satisfied and she escaped with her life. 

John made little bows and arrows to play hunter with the pigs. This was great fun and not particularly disturbing to the pigs. 

One day little red-haired and freckled Davey

Bispham, afterward the famous singer, went hunting with the children. They thought him a wonderful shot.

She is standing in the road looking up the mountain side opposite the boarding house.

There were no inns, no lodges, no clubs, not even a hot-dog stand in the mountains in those days. A lumbering old stage brought you over a stony and dusty road to a small, hot room in a dismal looking house known as a "boarding house," but you had a lovely time, and didn't mind.

Up on the mountain side, tethered to a stake, was a lamb. Now Elizabeth had never seen a lamb, but she had read about them.

She knew all about Mary's little lamb, with its snow-white fleece, and its loving heart.

She knew about little Bo-Peep, and her lost sheep, and how all ended happily when they came home with their tails behind them. In fact, in one picture book she saw those very tails, washed and hung out on the clothesline, and had given considerable thought to the matter. Then there was the gentle maiden feeding her lamb, who said, "Drink, pretty creature, drink.” 

And now she was going to see a lamb!

What a great moment for a little, trusting child!

A group of children were going up the hill to bring down the lamb for its supper in the barn. Of course, Elizabeth joined them.

They were older than she, and looked on her rather doubtfully, but Elizabeth thought of nothing but the lamb. To be sure, its fleece was not as white as snow, but it must have a loving heart. An older girl began to untie the rope. "Oh, please, please let me lead it," begged Elizabeth. "Oh, you can't, you can't! 

You are too little," cried the children. But she plead so vigorously, that at last the rope was placed in her hand. In another moment the lamb was speeding down the rocky road with great leaps. She had said she could lead the lamb, and she was bound to do it. Her little legs seemed to fly straight through the air. But the wild race did not last long. 

Down went Elizabeth, her breath entirely knocked out of her.

Another serious event was John's first cigar.

Smoking was at that time much objected to by the Quakers and many other religious bodies, but, such is human nature, it was looked at longingly by small boys. A cigar stub was a tempting discovery, and in the gravel road, one was picked up by John. He concluded he would try it. He therefore retired to the "chicken yard." This was mostly empty of chickens, but a favorite resort of the children. It was nearby, a long strip of land, concealed from the house on one side by fir trees, a high fence, the coal bin and

the carriage house. On the other, the fields stretched slightly upward toward the horizon, giving a sensation of space. 

No anxious parents could overlook it, and it was generally empty of interfering humans. It had many attractions, among them a board pile, furnishing material for houses which the children built from time to time, and where they went through many imaginary experiences with Indians, or brought up families. Elizabeth remembers her joy in a swinging door, with hinges cut from an old boot, rescued from the ash heap. 

But to return to the cigar.

John retired to the chicken yard, and the faithful little sister followed. There, under the walnut tree, was one of those small houses which, before chickens were regimented, were the homes of happy hens who were bringing up their own children. 

John threw himself on the slanting roof of one of these houses, and lay, his face pointing over the front edge, and then was very ill indeed. Elizabeth was paralyzed with amazement and without resource. 

Fortunately, John soon recovered, and it was a long, long time before he smoked again.

CIVIL WAR 

HE Civil War was an event that greatly stirred the minds of the chil- TH dren. They saw no soldiers or warlike preparations. The spirit of the good 

Quakers about them was absolutely opposed to war. The children, however, heard much talk about it, and now and then saw an illustrated paper, although in those days pictures were comparatively rare.

Little Elizabeth, having been duly instructed in the Quaker doctrine of peace, was deeply concerned with the situation. "Jeff"

Davis being her enemy, she was determined to love him. She could not understand why her parents admired Abraham Lincoln so much when he was engaged in war. She begged her father to go and tell him it was wrong to fight. That anyone would do wrong when they once knew better was not understood by Elizabeth. As her father excused himself from undertaking the mission, Elizabeth added to her prayers 26

CIVIL WAR 

27 the request that her Heavenly Father would teach Lincoln that it was wrong to fight, adding politely, "If Thee thinks it suitable."

John was stirred by the war. He made swords out of barrel staves, and guns out of broom sticks. Belts were provided and to them hung little bags of dust, representing gunpowder. Elizabeth followed him, regardless of her peace principles. They hid and fought in the cornfields, and escaped from prison by jumping out of the windows of barns.

In the evening they sat for supper in little yellow chairs, one at each end of a small blue table, and they were not themselves, but Captain Kund, and Captain Hanker. Elizabeth thought these were beautiful names. Every night they went to the moon and ruled over the people. They had slaves, too, and a rebellion. In two little blank books, they drew pictures of events in the moon. These seemed very wonderful pictures, but when they came to light in later years, not much could be made out of them. 

Affairs went on quite happily until Elizabeth heard that her father was drafted. She knew he would not fight, but feared he might

be put in prison. She went off to a lonely part of the farm and suffered agonies. From her earliest years she met the terrible problems of her life in solitude. However, relief came. There was a neighboring farmer who had a "pull," and so was excused from the draft on the pretext of a sore foot. His friends were rather fond of laughing about it. When Elizabeth finally asked her mother if her father would be required to go to war, her mother, not realizing how deeply the child was stirred, said jestingly, "No. He has a crooked toe." This greatly comforted 

Elizabeth. At any rate, her father never was called to the army, whatever might have been the reason. She took a sly peek at his crooked toe, and saw it was certainly there. 

As the war went on, she grew to share the great admiration of the Quakers for Abraham Lincoln as far as her capacity permitted, and overlooked her peace principles, as did some others.

She was visiting her Aunt Hannah at the time of Lee's surrender, and the family all went to the neighboring town to see the illumination celebrating the event. The next morning, as the children were busy

BAKER'S CORNER SCHOOL 

ET LIZABETH learned to read early, possibly before she went to school. She remembers sitting in her little yellow chair by her mother, and reading words of one syllable out of a small pink book. It was a sad story she read. "Poor Fan lost her Ma, and she ran up and down the street and cried, 'Ma, Ma.'" What more dreadful could happen to a little girl! Elizabeth dissolved in tears. 

She was delighted and proud when the time came to go to school. The small unpainted schoolhouse was about a quarter of a mile from the door of her home. It was situated at the crossroads, and known as the Baker's Corner School. The benches and desks were unpainted, and worn very smooth. Elizabeth's grandfather had gone to school in that house probably about 1800. A large stove in the center warmed the one room in winter. Drinking water was carried in a bucket from a neighboring well. There was one tin 30

Elizabeth is glad that her education began in this typical American way, and in later life, her brother John, who had all the advantages of boarding school and college, used to say that he learned most of what he knew at

The school was attended by children from neighboring farms, both white and colored. 

Elizabeth was probably the youngest child there. The teacher gave her a yellow spelling book, and taught her to spell "ba, be, bi," and other strange words. If she could read she did not mention the fact. She accepted what came. She was very shy, and the tears rolled down her cheeks. How ashamed she was. But later she grew happy and composed. 

Walking home with John, she saw the

Master of the Farm in his hay field, riding on a wooden hay rake, probably one of the earliest inventions of the kind. Elizabeth loved that hay-rake. Sometimes she was allowed to ride with her father, sitting on the side with her legs hanging down. The hay smelled very sweet; birds and butterflies and

bees fluttered over it. The strange wooden fingers which rolled up the hay were a mysterious and fascinating sight. As the children passed the field, the Master left his machine and climbed the fence to hear how his little girl liked school. She told him with pride of her spelling lesson, but John interrupted. "She cried all the time," he said.

Elizabeth was covered with shame, and her happy story spoiled. She was proud, but, Oh! how often her pride had a fall.

There were some episodes at school of which Elizabeth did not approve. Sometimes the white children teased the Negro children. and called them "Black Crows." John and 

Elizabeth, with their good Quaker training, were horrified at this. 

Then there was Retty Christoe, a little "Taken-girl," from the poorhouse. She was ostracised. Elizabeth asked why. The children said that Retty told a lie. Elizabeth decided to be kind to her. How much that meant to Retty she had no idea, nor could she tell why Retty brought her presents.

Bits of china were collected for dishes and imagination did the rest. Elizabeth was apt to be chosen as the baby, and petted by the older girls.

Later on John went to school in the village about a mile and a half away. This was taught by a Quaker of strict conformity in dress, language and mentality. He was a tall, thin, sober man. John sometimes rode a horse to the school, and sometimes went on the children's donkey. If John was late, the schoolmaster would say, "Did thee come on horse back or ass-back?" If the answer was "ass-back," John was excused.

Sometimes afterward, Elizabeth also went to school in the village. In those days school had two sessions, morning and afternoon, and adjourned at twelve o'clock for noon-day dinner.

Their Grandfather Nicholson with his two unmarried daughters lived not far from school and the children were always welcomed there to dinner. Their little cousins came with them, and they had delightful plays. One thing distressed Elizabeth. As the little Quaker girls walked to their Grandfather's, there was a group of boys who used to call after them, "Quaker, Quaker mashed potato." Elizabeth told her mother of this. 

Her mother was great at solving problems. 

She said, "When they call that after you say,

'Dutchy, Dutchy, don't you touch me.'" This was perhaps not the Quaker way of turning the other cheek, but it was effective, and the boys never troubled them again.

T was perhaps rather hard on little girls to be brought up in ignorance of the social world, with peculiar dress and language, and books severely limited. But in the 

Quaker home there was much lovingkindness, and fine character training, which had its charm, and what was done was done well. 

One great concern was to prevent all vanity, or excessive interest in clothes, and compliments were very rare. Elizabeth remembers four of these in her early years, one of which was a dismal failure. The first was the only one which would be recognized as such at the present day. Elizabeth remembers herself following her mother into Quarterly Meeting. Like her father she combined joy with religion in a delightful manner, and was a fountain of energy and full of enthusiasm for new ideas. Later on, as H. W. S. she wrote numerous religious books which have been translated into many languages, and one of which, at least, may be considered a classic

of its period. Her table, piled with books, was of great interest to Elizabeth on whose life and thought Aunt Hannah's influence was very strong.

Elizabeth remembers two expeditions led by this unquestioned leader. She insisted on having a riding party composed of the older group of cousins. The party bumped along until their leader turned into a small wood, without roads or paths. Perhaps the other horses did the same, but Elizabeth was dealing with her own problems, and not looking at those of others. 

All got safely out of the woods, and she is the

Near the Cedars was a tide-water creek, flowing down to the Delaware. There was a deep channel in the middle, with banks of black mud on each side, and spreading out beyond was shallower water, filled with tangled water plants. The only place to swim was in the deep channel and the black mud of the banks would stain feet very black indeed. A dilapidated old row boat was the children's dependence, and out of that they tumbled into the deep water. Arrived at that point, Elizabeth thought she was certainly drowning. Then she remembered hearing that when drowning your past life came. back to you, but as hers did not come back, she took courage and siezed a floating fence rail. It is only now, after many years, that her past life is coming back to her, and it seems to do so without the necessity of drowning.

The four cousins went along quite comfortably until they reached the Delaware, the boys taking off their shoes to pull the boat through some shallow rapids at the entrance to the river. Here they began to feel hungry, and believing the town of Gloucester was nearby, went up the river, and in a short time reached a black and forlorn beach with a saloon in the distance, and concluded they had arrived. The boys went to forage, and returned with a few crackers, which seemed all that the place afforded. 

It was now getting late and time to return, so the cousins started back again. Suddenly their boat began to rock, and looking up, Elizabeth saw a gigantic ferryboat making its way vigorously up the river. The waves, stirred by its immense paddle wheels, seemed to rise mountain high. It looked and felt as if those four foolish children and their leaky little rowboat would be swept away, and why they were not Elizabeth cannot understand. The ferryboat passed on while the passengers stared from the deck.

It was a weary journey up the creek and

back to the Cedars. What were the parents doing all that time? Elizabeth cannot remember whether they ever knew anything about it. They were such kind, good parents. 

They did not bother their children unduly in vacation time. Aunt Hannah, who believed in liberty for children, was probably their friend at court. The grown-ups always had a perfectly delightful time together, discussing everything under the sun, especially religion and the state of the church. 

Years after, when Elizabeth last visited the Cedars, it was indeed a sad sight; the house, lacking paint and out of repair, the garden gone, and many of the trees also, and only one woman coming out of the kitchen door, forlorn and shabby. Everything looked uncared for, and over what once were green fields appeared rows and rows of hideous bungalows.

At this tragedy Elizabeth has much to feel but little to say.

Tall, heavy iron railings surrounded it, but the gates were always open, and there Elizabeth could roll her hoop in safety. Around this square were the houses of old Philadelphia families. They were generally of the approved Philadelphia type, made of brick, and trimmed either with brown stone or immacu- 79

late white marble, most of them four stories high, with large windows, shaded within by green blinds and white curtains.

An experienced observer could tell instantly whether a Quaker or one of the "world's people" dwelt within. The 

Quaker curtains would be of sheer white muslin, while the worldly minded would have lace. Many of the houses had side entrances and large yards at the back, where the light came in freely to the "back buildings," so characteristic of Philadelphia.

Filbert Street was paved with cobble stones, and at long intervals a dejected looking horse car came slowly along it. Where the Masonic Temple now is was a great confused mass of stones and bricks and rubbish.

A little cousin, well versed in Biblical lore, after gazing at it, asked her mother, "Is that the wilderness where they sent the Scape Goat?"

It was in 1851 that Grandfather Whitall built his house in Filbert Street and other 

Quakers gathered there. Elizabeth remembers the names of Cope, Lloyd, Longstreth, Leidy, Yarnall, Pearsall Smith, and Wistar.

Early in the day from those dignified

houses came forth quiet Quaker men, in collarless coats and black beaver hats, mostly walking to their places of business. Later, Grandmother Whitall's heavy carriage, with large black horses, and a majestic German coachman, stood at her door, and she would come down the steps in her Quaker bonnet, her array of drab silks and a heavy gray shawl, beautiful and serene. To Elizabeth, in her early youth, all this seemed as if it always had been and always would be. 

Entering the front hall of number 1317 you looked along the long entry to the stairs which went upward at an easy grade, but what seemed to Elizabeth a great distance. 

At the right were two large parlors, the "front parlor" and the "back parlor." The front parlor was consecrated to dignity and proper behavior. In the back parlor was more relaxation and youthful overflow. 

Green carpets, haircloth and walnut furniture, and dove-colored paper made a not altogether attractive color scheme. There were no pictures on the walls, and few ornaments on the simple marble mantels, under which large black radiators poured forth heat. 

There was a mirror between the front win-

dows, before which was a table covered with black marble, and supporting a high vase of Pampas grass. It was a typical Quaker parlor, and to see anything remotely resembling it now-a-days gives Elizabeth's heart a thrill. 

From the landing at the head of the stairs you reached the dining-room, and there all entered willingly. Three large windows let in the light, and what seems to Elizabeth's memory everlasting sunshine. A large dining table occupied the front part of the room -a table which seemed always full, but yet there was always room for one more, and that one more was very likely to arrive. Two o'clock was then the customary dinner hour in Philadelphia. This sounds a long time to wait, but the enormous breakfasts eaten in those days were sufficient to support life for some time. The Quakers generally walked home to dinner from their offices, and a hearty feast was enjoyed by everyone.

Sitting against the back wall of the diningroom was a large mahogany sideboard, as innocent of wines or liquors as any other occupant of the house, where happy hearts and good digestions furnished all the gaiety needed.

By the sideboard was a white door into the pantry and such a pantry! Painted with white, well lighted, and full of attractions. 

First, you saw the dumb-waiter. Roomy and tempting, no child could see it without wanting to ride, but Elizabeth cannot remember anyone going so far as to do more than discuss the matter. 

Beyond the dumb-waiter was a door.

Open it, and the heart of the pantry was revealed. There, on low shelves were treasures-white mountain cake, jars of ginger, pieces of pie, jams and jellies, oranges, lumps of sugar, and other things too numerous to record. You went in there, and nobody said anything. You came out and nobody said anything. The children were generally moderate, and refrained from eating everything up. In fact, they were too well fed at Grandmother's table.

Elizabeth made up her mind she would be like Grandmother Whitall and have a pantry just like that; but she has never succeeded, sad to say. Do children get their rights now, or only a balanced diet?

At the far end of the dining-room was a conservatory. A table placed in front of the

conservatory windows had a gas lamp upon it and a rocking chair at each end-one for Grandmother and one for Grandfather. 

Each had their own Bible and spectacles, and Grandmother generally had some fresh letters from her devoted daughters. That peaceful scene is a precious memory to Elizabeth. 

On the second and third floors in the front of the house were large bedrooms furnished with great beds and bureaus, and wash-stands with beautifully decorated pitchers, basins, and foot-baths, a sight to thrill antique lovers of today. The fourth story, almost equally vast, was reserved for the servants. Back over the dining-room was what was called the nursery, where groups of children gathered. Beyond, far back, was a dark, mysterious room, little used, and in the corner of it a bathtub, closed in with shutters, and looking very strange to childish eyes.

Many memories of this house are connected with Elizabeth's wonderful cousin Carey.

She decreed that there should be a ghost in the room beyond the nursery. That ghost lived in the bathtub. Carey concocted a strange drink consisting of salt, pepper and

She also would gather the older children in a ring, sitting on the floor in a dark room, and whoever could come on a dreadful ghost story would tell it there in mysterious tones.

The home at 1317 Filbert Street became known for its hospitality. Quakers came from all points of the compass. England furnished guests in shining broadcloth. They were supposed to be superior beings, and Elizabeth viewed them as visitors from a higher sphere. From the far West, then much undeveloped, came quaint figures, carrying carpet bags. Women preachers, tired out, stretched themselves on the sofas. One, to the delight of the children, took off her shoes and warmed her feet at the radiator. 

Grandmother fed, comforted and refreshed them all, and once Elizabeth caught sight of her quietly pushing a little roll of bills into a needy hand. It was a customary thing, apparently, thus to help Quaker preachers on their way.

There was one instance of Grandmother's tact that caused some delight in her adoring family. She had in her parlor a beautiful sofa pillow, a gift from one of her friends. 

It had a brilliant wreath of flowers worked upon it, which made a bright spot in that rather dull colored room. One day a weary

Quaker preacher came in and prepared to throw himself down on the sofa, his wellgreased hair upon the pillow. As the family viewed the approaching catastrophe with. alarm, Grandmother, just at the right moment, appeared with another pillow in her arms, saying in her sweetest way, "Perhaps thee will be more comfortable with this pillow," and deftly removed the treasure, leaving the good Quaker happy with her kind attentions.

There was one visitor from the West who came more than once. Him Elizabeth can never forget. He was a successful evangelical preacher, of great size, wearing a thick, black beard, which made him unpopular with the conservative Philadelphia Quakers, who regarded beards, then fashionable, as very worldly. Grandmother, however, was not checked in her hospitality by any outward feature. Elizabeth can see him now, knife in one hand, fork in the other, cutting up something on his plate with abounding energy. Beside him sat John, just big enough to have his head knocked off by one of those enormous elbows, and contorting himself

curiously in the attempt to escape the danger which threatened him.

Small boys have a way of avenging themselves, and the next morning John, standing a little back of the vigorous guest, devoted himself to counting the incredible number of sausages the visitor consumed. The oldfashioned sausage was tremendous compared to the dainty affair which now adorns our tables. When the meal was completed, John beckoned to Elizabeth and whispered mysteriously, "Thirteen.”

On New Year's Day it was the custom to have a large family party at 1317 Filbert Street. All were seated at a long table in the dining-room which was covered with appropriate delicacies of the season. The large blue plates were filled with a sample of everything and a second helping was expected. Between dinner and desert the youngest child able to walk was placed on the table to promanade its length. On one occasion a large meringue on a high-stemmed glass dish looking like a little stool was brought out rather too soon. John saw his chance, and as the baby reached it he called out, "Sit down." This the obedient child prepared to do, and was

Another time as all the party, very full of turkey, sat waiting for desert, John began to gobble in a most realistic manner and so appropriately that no one could do anything but laugh.

As Elizabeth thinks of those who gathered around that table, the line comes to her: "All, all are gone, the old familiar faces."

HE day at last came for Elizabeth to go to school in the city. She went in shyness and fear, yet with courage.

She was now to enter on what seems to her memory the most delightful period of her life, with precious friendships, and great freedom from care and responsibility. She loved to learn, but she doubted her capacity. If

Quakerism had allowed her even a wee bit of flattery, she might have had a little more faith in her own brains.

As the first step, you entered through a green gate in a high brick wall. The highest wall Elizabeth had ever looked upon. Then you came upon a large brick-paved court, open to the sun, and bounded on each side by the walls of adjoining buildings. 

In front of you was the schoolhouse, built of brick, with gray stone trimmings. It was three stories high with an ample basement.

Such large windows she had never seen before, and she believes her grandchildren of this day are hardly likely to be more impressed on entering the great Empire State

The most important thing, however, was the door. A number of stone steps led up to a colonial porch with its white pillars and wide entrance. I wonder if any old scholar can ever think of that door without seeing 

Teacher Margaret Lightfoot standing within. it. In her Quaker cap and kerchief, her brown dress, which, in those days of crinoline, was slim and sweeping the floor, her left hand plucking at it in order that she might not trip, and in her right hand a large dinner bell, Teacher Margaret made a picture worth preserving. Elizabeth feels that she was a born teacher. She never seemed to have any difficulty in keeping order, and although she was determined to keep a grave and dignified demeanor on all occasions, one could see sympathy and humor in her eyes.

When Elizabeth entered the schoolhouse she was taken to the hall-way on the second floor where a little group of girls were gathering until they should be called to the collecting room. She gazed in awe while they greeted each other, until in came Harriet, and Elizabeth knew that she was the friend

of all others for her, but her humble little soul doubted if she could ever aspire to such companionship.

Harriet was as immaculate and correct in all things as a little Victorian girl in a picture book. She stood erect, as straight as an arrow. Down her back was a heavy braid of coal black hair, incredibly smooth. Her dress was what was greatly admired in those days—a large black and white shepherds plaid, with a tight waist, and skirt spreading out a little. Her hat, which was particularly fetching, was a brown straw, flat crown, and spreading brim, beautifully scalloped around the edge. Two long streamers fluttered out behind. Harriet struck deep into Elizabeth's heart. She is the only girl Elizabeth remembers on that day.

She was kindly shown over the school by some of the pupils. The rooms were large and light, the ceilings high. There were no pictures on the walls, no unnecessary adornments anywhere, but all was clean and shining and in perfect order. Last of all, the girls said, "You must see Teacher Rebecca's room."

They ran up to the third story and opened the door. Here was a contrast to all other

schoolrooms Elizabeth had ever seen or heard of. It was bright with flowers. Pictures hung on the walls, and a beautiful aquarium stood in front of the window. 

Teacher Rebecca Shinn loved beauty and no doubt many little Quaker girls of that day needed to be taught to consciously observe it. 

Elizabeth cannot recall what she learned from books in that room, but two simple suggestions have always remained with her. One day before school she saw Teacher Rebecca arranging a bunch of roses in a vase. With them were some little grasses which had partly gone to seed, and had beautiful feathery heads. She put them in among the roses, making the whole look very attractive and fairy-like. How often Elizabeth thinks of this even now, as she makes a bouquet. 

One thing did Teacher Rebecca say which

Elizabeth did not like at all. She was talking about Arabia, which was in those days supposed to be the source of all figs. They came pressed tight, in small wooden boxes and sometimes with the fruit was a stiff bristle of camel's hair. The teacher said, "I like to see this bristle of camel's hair. It makes me think of the Arabs riding across the desert."

Elizabeth was horrified with this. She did not wish her food mixed with camel's hairs, and she lost her appetite for figs. 

Teacher Rebecca was a gifted and progressive teacher, but her failing health compelled her to leave the school not long after Elizabeth came there.

The next teacher to enter the school was James Price. The girls were told he was a very learned man, and could, like Giotto, make a perfect circle with one sweep of the arm. He was a tall man with a very bald head and a strict Quaker garb, including a large beaver hat with a very broad brim. 

This he would place on a near-by desk while he gave his lessons. He always stood it upon its top, and Elizabeth found it a tempting place in which to drop ends of chalk. Unfortunately she was never there to see him. put it on, and so missed his reactions. Very possibly he was too learned to notice such trifles. He taught Latin and it rather seemed that the greatness of his brain kept him from understanding the empty little heads before him.

Latin grammar was one of the mysteries. to which Elizabeth had to resign herself. She

saw no reason why people should not call a table "mensa," if they wanted to, but why they should go on so about it she could not understand. She did her best, and can yet see the pages of that Latin grammar. She was probably a pretty satisfactory pupil, but she walked in darkness.

One of the great adventures which occurred to her at Select School was in connection with James Price. The class was over and 

Elizabeth was quite provoked with the teacher for some unremembered reason. As she walked upstairs to the collecting room she said with all the fierceness of which she was capable, and that was not much, "I'd like to do something to that man." She was soon to repent this wicked wish.

Far up in the center of the high ceiling of the collecting room was a large skylight. The light came from a window in the roof and entered the third-story rooms by large windows on the sides of the light well. It also served for ventilation, at times, and the rooms all seemed very pleasant. Under this skylight a little group of girls gathered, chattering away in happy fashion, when came an awful crash above their heads which scattered them

in all directions. From a safe point Elizabeth looked up, and there in the light well hung James Price, clinging in desperation to the middle bar. Elizabeth felt as if she had done the deed. She, with James Price's daughter, rushed to a nearby closet, where there was a step-ladder. They seized it and placed it under him. Alas, it was too short. 

The poor man groaned. This was truly terrible. The two girls rushed downstairs and brought a longer ladder, and then he was helped down. When Elizabeth next dared to look, he was stretched out on the long recitation bench, his head lay in the lap of a 

Quaker lady, correctly clothed in a brown. shawl and bonnet. Elizabeth saw only her back. From whence this ministering angel had so miraculously come she knew not, but was encouraged to believe that under such care he would soon come right and, in fact, so he did.

The one remarkable thing about Elizabeth was the fact that when she sinned no one ever found it out. She and her little group used to go to Teacher Margaret's chemical closet and make lovely fizzes, but it was never discovered. On one occasion as she and Hannah

were standing at Teacher Margaret's desk looking over a paper with her, Elizabeth very quietly began to tickle her friend. Hannah naturally writhed and squirmed and Teacher 

Margaret said severely, "Hannah, don't make Elizabeth rude." After that time, when the girls were pushing each other about you might have heard the slogan, "Hannah, don't make Elizabeth rude."

On Fifth days the girls marched down Arch Street to the great meeting house. The boys did so, also, but the sexes were not supposed to communicate with each other. Elizabeth at this stage of her existence thoroughly despised that section of the human race known as boys. It was very reprehensible at that time for a Quaker, boy or man, to give “hat honor" to anyone. One of Elizabeth's schoolmates told her that you could sometimes get "tips" from the boys on the way to meeting. Whatever were her sins, Elizabeth thoroughly believed in keeping rules, and any boy who was weak enough to tip his hat had no attraction for her. Neither was her informant admitted to her friendship. 

After meeting the girls were allowed a few minutes to go up to Eighth Street, the great

shopping neighborhood, and make some purchases. This was the great shopping street for women. It was before John Wanamaker had made his great progressive step in fixing prices. Every store had its own. price and you were supposed to "jew down" the merchants, as is still done in some European countries today. With the better stores this was not generally necessary. It was on 

Eighth Street that Elizabeth was introduced to a cream-puff. Elizabeth had never encountered anything so delicious. It was beyond all dreams. This and a somewhat similar adventure makes Elizabeth always think tenderly of Eighth Street, for it was there also that her mother took her into a store and treated her to lemon ice. Do children feel that way now, I wonder? 

Elizabeth found the joys of friendship as delicious to her heart as these material delicacies were to her appetite. The little group of girls who were her inner circle each contributed much to the development of her character. She was like a tight little rosebud when she went, but she gradually began to unfold, slowly, indeed, but full of wonder, as in a strange country.

Harriet had elegance, reserve, self-control, and she and Elizabeth had one deep and secret desire, regarded in those days as foolish and reprehensible. They wanted to go to college, but fathers were almost universally opposed, and no way seemed open. Harriet later attained her ambition, but Elizabeth entered the great school of life, where she is still learning. 

Hannah was the cleverest one of the group.

She seemed to have more brains and humor than the others. Lucy, although she was at the school but for a short time when Elizabeth was there, remained a life-long friend. 

She was gay and sympathetic and social, but capable of those deeper things which Elizabeth had so near her heart. Sally was bright and amusing in a very original manner, and full of mischief in her own odd way. Her great ambition was to make Teacher Margaret laugh in meeting, and in other serious moments, and this Teacher Margaret was determined not to do. Her face would grow very red, and she would move her hand over it from forehead to chin, as if trying to brush off all smiles. Sally was truly delighted when she brought this about. Elizabeth should

here confess that she found some pleasure in making Lucy laugh in meeting, but then Lucy enjoyed it more than Teacher Margaret did.

CONCLUSION 

ANY years have passed since those gay and childish days. Few indeed who shared any portion of Elizabeth's early experiences now remain. 

As she looks back over the long scroll of her life she sees the world as a school. There are lessons to be learned, some over and over again. There is training in all the virtues, love, faith, sincerity, self-sacrifice, patience, generosity, self-control, and many others, large and small. There are failures and successes, laughter and tears, but none of these are wasted and futile unless we will have it so. We can win from them, if we choose, a finer character, a greater breadth of vision, and a glimpse of something greater still. She now knows that mankind must always be learning, never in this world attaining absolute truth. Looking backward she can say with Whittier, the beloved Quaker poet, 104
