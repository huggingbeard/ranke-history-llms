The Commonwealth of Massachusetts SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

This report discusses the proposal made in House Bill, No. 3069 of 1957 that the statutory compensation payable to injured, unpaid civil defense volunteers for lost earning capacity be increased to the compensation payable under the Workmen's Compensation 

Act, as has been recommended by the State Commissioner of 

Administration. 

Unpaid civil defense volunteers in Massachusetts who are injured are now eligible under the Civil Defense Act to receive a maximum of $35 per week for lost earning capacity plus $2.50 per week for each dependent. Under the Workmen's Compensation

Act, these maximum amounts would be increased in some cases to $40 per week plus $4 per week for each dependent; moreover, they would automatically be further increased whenever the law is amended to raise the maximum. 

Injury Payments in Massachusetts. 

To qualify for compensation in Massachusetts, an injured worker must first establish that he (1) is a volunteer, (2) is unpaid, (3) has taken a loyalty oath, and (4) has registered or otherwise signified his intention of joining the civil defense organization. Thereafter, he must prove that his injury or disability was legitimately incurred; a civil defense claims board acts on all claims. Restrictions imposed by other States are similar or more stern than those of Massachusetts.

In the six years during which the present Civil Defense Claims Act has been in force, 68 claims have been filed. Of these claims, 54 have been approved at a cost of $6,041 to the Commonwealth. 

About one quarter of this sum can be disregarded, since one claim of that size did not involve a payment for lost earning capacity. 

Compensation Elsewhere. 

Such compensation for paid civil defense volunteers in other States for lost earning capacity varies considerably. California pays a maximum of $50 per week; Wisconsin, $40 per week; Vir-

Of 38 States providing coverage for civil defense volunteers, five have done so by amending the Workmen's Compensation Law. The remainder have amended their civil defense act or have enacted special legislation. In some States, certain benefits provided under 

Workmen's Compensation Laws are smaller for civil defense personnel than for regular workers. Fourteen States provide compulsory or elective coverage for all types of civil defense workers, whether the accident occurs in training, in practice, or on duty. 

Other States limit coverage to specified civil defense personnel. In a few States coverage is limited to injuries received during an actual attack, or while on actual duty, or while performing specified service.

Most States classify the covered volunteers as state employees, or give them the same rights as state employees, for purposes of the 

Workmen's Compensation Law. 

Opinions relative to Proposed Change. 

Opinions of civil defense officials vary as to the effect of the statutory rate of compensation and the extent of coverage on the recruitment of civil defense volunteers. Some officials feel that little or no compensation is a handicap in attracting and holding personnel. Other officials, however, consider the effect of the amount of such compensation upon civil defense programs to be inconsequential.

Locally there is concern that in the absence of a medical examination as a prerequisite to civil defense enrollment, the increased compensation proposed may stimulate claims which are falsely laid to civil defense duty. Advocates of increased injury compensation point out, however, that the rate would still not be high enough to attract many false claims. In any event, the Massachusetts claims board reviews all claims carefully, and has thus far denied 14 claims, including two for death benefits.

Chapter I. Introduction. 

LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND. 

The Commissioner of Administration, in a special report to the General Court, dated May 3, 1957, recommended passage of House 

Bill, No. 3069, to increase the compensation payable to injured

I civil defense volunteers for lost earning capacity. The proposal, which is reprinted as Appendix D, was referred to the Committee on Public Safety and later to the Ways and Means Committee. 

The heart of House, No. 3069, is in the clause, “. . . indemnification for loss of earning capacity shall not exceed for any one week the maximum weekly compensation payable under section 34 of chapter 152 of the General Laws . plus, for each person wholly dependent on such person," the sum specified in section 35A. Simply stated, this clause would give qualified civil defense volunteers the same rate of compensation that is applied under these two sections of the Workmen's Compensation Act (i.e., G. L. c. 152). 

The Ways and Means Committee reported out an omnibus order, House, No. 3217, on June 18. This omnibus order (which is printed on the inside front cover) included a requirement that the Legislative Research Council investigate and study House, No. 3069; it was adopted in concurrence on June 24, 1957.

GENERAL ASPECTS OF BENEFITS AND COVERAGE. 

The Federal Civil Defense Administration in its 1956 Annual Re port indicates that 36 States have adopted legislation to compensate civil defense workers who may be injured while in training or on duty or under specified conditions. Replies to a Legislative Research Bureau questionnaire sent to all States add at least two more States which have provided such coverage since the federal publication was issued.

[Apr. 

Of the 38 States which are therefore known to provide compensation for injuries, 5 have done so by amending the state Workmen's 

Compensation Law, (California, New York, Ohio, Rhode Island and Virginia). The other 33 States have included coverage in the state civil defense act.

In general, where benefits under a state Workmen's Compensation Law are applied to civil defense workers they are usually covered to the same extent as other covered employees. But in 

California and New York, where coverage for special workers is provided by amendment to Workmen's Compensation Laws, the benefits are less in some respects for these workers than for others. 

New Jersey has a special benefit system for civil defense workers. 

In 14 States compulsory or elective coverage is provided for all types of civil defense workers whether in training or on duty (California, Colorado, Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, Michigan, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Oregon, Rhode Island, Washington,

Wisconsin and Virginia). Other States have limited their coverage to certain specified civil defense workers, such as members of mobile support units, individuals employed outside local jurisdictions, or police, fire and rescue personnel. In some States, coverage is limited to workers injured during an attack or while on actual duty or while performing specified services. Since most States now include natural disaster relief in their civil defense operations, coverage is extended to include this activity. 

Most statutes classify the covered volunteers as state employees, or at least give them the same rights as state employees for purposes of the Workmen's Compensation Law. A few States classify such personnel as employees of local political subdivisions. 

The foregoing text has indicated the general coverage and some of the differences among state laws relative to civil defense workers. 

There follows a discussion of the basic civil defense act in Massachusetts, and subsequent amendments thereto which have broadened coverage of personnel and increased payments to qualified workers. 

Chapter II. Civil Defense Compensation in Massachusetts. 

THE CIVIL DEFENSE ACT OF 1950. 

The Commonwealth created a Civil Defense Agency within the Executive branch seven years ago (Acts of 1950, c. 639). The 

Agency is administered by a Director, appointed by the Governor,

.] 11 

with the advice and consent of the Council, who serves during the pleasure of the Chief Executive. The Director has the full-time task of co-ordinating the activities of all civil defense organizations within the commonwealth, and of co-operating and maintaining liaison with similar agencies of other States and with the federal government.

Civil defense includes the preparation and execution of those emergency functions which are outside the responsibility of the military forces and the national guard, for the purpose of minimizing and repairing the damage resulting from enemy action or from natural disasters.

In this service, civil defense volunteers assist in fire-fighting and policing; medical aid and rescue services; evacuating persons from stricken areas; restoring communications, transportation and engineering services; providing welfare services; and utilizing special weapons of defense such as radiological or chemical agents. 

Civil Defense Personnel. 

These workers consist of paid and unpaid personnel, and local employees, as follows: 

(a) Paid Personnel. The basic Civil Defense Act states (sec tion 2) that "experts, clerks and other assistants" who are hired to carry out the program of the civil defense agency are not subject to the civil service laws (G. L., c. 31). It also provides (section 5) that the Governor may proclaim a state of emergency utilizing every agency and every member of the departments and divisions of the government of the Commonwealth. Such members are not only entitled to the protection of otherwise applicable law, but to the protection afforded by the Civil Defense Act. 

(b) Unpaid Personnel. — In addition to these government employees who can be mustered into emergency service, the basic civil defense act provides (section 11 (a)) for the appointment of such ". . . unpaid auxiliary firemen and auxiliary police and other volunteer, unpaid public protection units as may be approved by said civil defense agency.

These appointees are specifically excluded from the application of the Civil Service Act, the Retire ment System and Pension Law, and the Workmen's Compensation 

Act (G. L. c. 31, 32 and 152, respectively).

PROPOSED COMPENSATION FOR INJURED VOLUNTEERS. 

As a result of testimony in 1950, before a special commission on civil defense (created by Resolves of 1950, c. 56), the Commission recommended legislation in its final report (House, No. 2500 of 1951) to indemnify volunteer unpaid civil defense workers in the event of injury or death. Injured volunteers were to receive the benefits specified in the Workmen's Compensation Act. For this purpose, the compensation payable was to be based on the average weekly earnings of the volunteer worker in his normal occupation. If he was unemployed, the average weekly wage was to be determined "at the weekly minimum amount which shall entitle him to the maximum weekly benefits" under the existing Workmen's Compensation Act.

THE CIVIL DEFENSE CLAIMS ACT OF 1951. 

The above bill, part of House, No. 2500 of 1951, was scrapped by the Military Affairs Committee, in favor of a more detailed proposal (Senate, No. 676 of 1951) setting up a claims board and providing $30 per week to injured, unpaid volunteers for lost earning capacity. 

Senate, No. 676 subsequently was enacted as an amendment to the civil defense act of 1950 (Acts of 1951, c. 547; see Appendix B).

Compensation under 1951 Act. 

Under the provisions of the 1951 legislation, every unpaid volun teer appointed under the Civil Defense Act of 1950 (Paragraph 

11(a)) is protected by (a) Indemnification up to $50 for property damage sustained during training or duty when no personal fault or neglect is involved. (b) Indemnification up to $30 per week for lost earning capacity resulting from personal injury, in addition to indemnification for reasonable hospital, medical and surgical expenses incurred, plus $2.50 per week for each dependent of the injured volunteer worker.

Compensation Changes Proposed in 1957. 

As indicated above, when the civil defense claims act was drafted, compensation figures were based upon the Workmen's Compensation Act then in force. Subsequently, when weekly compensation was increased under the Workmen's Compensation Act the claims act had to be amended similarly to increase compensation payable to eligible civil defense workers.

Since the last amendment to the Civil Defense Claims Act, the Workmen's Compensation Act has been further amended so that the maximum now payable is $40 per week under some circumstances. Similarly, payments for dependents of eligible workers under the Workmen's Compensation Act have been increased to $4 per week.

The purpose of House, No. 3069 was to make this type of adjustment automatically, so that compensation for lost earning capacity which is payable under the Civil Defense Claims Act, is revised without any special action when the maximum weekly compensation payable under the Workmen's Compensation Act is changed from time to time. By providing in the claims act that compensation equal to that specified in sections 34 and 35A of the Workmen's Compensation Act shall be paid, the necessity of going to the Legislature for periodic revision of the law is eliminated. Such is already the case with respect to payments for injuries received which are specified in section 36 of the Workmen's Compensation Act.

Determining Compensation. 

CO From answers to a Legislative Research Bureau questionnaire sent to the Civil Defense Directors of the 47 other States, it is apparent that there are three basic legislative approaches which decide the compensation for lost earning capacity to be given unpaid civil defense workers. One method is to apply the rate of compensa-re tion specified in States' Workmen's Compensation Acts, by amending the act to include the civil defense workers. A second method per which seems to be most widespread is to include a certain rate of compensation in the State's civil defense legislation, as in the case pri

Compensation Act. The third approach is to enact special legis-

The source of benefits may be a State's Workmen's Compensation Fund, a State's General Fund, or a Special Fund, as in the case of New Jersey's Insurance Law. 

Coverage under Various Workmen's Compensation Laws. 

(a) California. -In California all civil defense and disaster service volunteers are covered by workmen's compensation when they are engaged in activities authorized by the California Disaster Act. Volunteers in training are included under this provision. 

Coverage is provided by the State of California, without payments of premium, and is administered by the State Compensation Insurance Fund. Each claim is adjusted by the State Fund, on an individual basis and on its own merits, following the same procedure as is used in their adjustment of premium paid workmen's compensation claims.

Compensation granted amounts to 65 per cent of a workmen's average weekly earnings, but may not exceed $50 per week in the case of a temporary disability, nor $40 per week in the case of a permanent disability. For each 1 per cent of permanent injury, the injured receives four weeks of disability payments; where permanent disability is 70 per cent or over, a life pension if provided. (b) Connecticut. LEGAL RESTRICTIONS ON PAYMENTS. 

Massachusetts. As indicated above, the General Court amended the Civil Defense Act of 1950 to provide compensation for unpaid volunteers. The 1951 amendment specified that only those injured workers who met specific requirements were eligible for compensation. With respect to an injured civil defense volunteer with lost earning capacity or medical or hospital or surgical expenses, he had to establish that (a) He had been appointed under section 11(a) of the 1950 act as: (1) a volunteer, (2) unpaid, (3) either an auxiliary policeman, an auxiliary fireman or a member of a locally authorized public protection unit approved by the civil defense agency. (b) He was participating in authorized training or performing authorized duty within or without the State. (c) He was neither personally at fault, nor personally negligent. (This requirement need not be established in a death claim.) 

The law also required the injured volunteer to file his claim within six months of the date of injury or death; this period was reduced to 90 days by Acts of 1955, ch. 607. By executive order he must show evidence of having taken a loyalty oath as prescribed by section 18 of the 1950 act.

In 1956 the law was further amended so as to modify the requirement that a claimant establish that he had been appointed under section 11 (a) of the 1950 act. The Civil Defense Claims Board, under the 1956 amendment, was given sole discretion to approve claims in accordance with section 11A (the 1951 claims act) where claimants had not been appointed under section 11 (a) of the basic (1950) act, provided that each claimant can establish (1) that at the time of injury he was actually participating in civil defense training or duty as an unpaid volunteer under an authorized superior; (2) that previous to the occurrence of the injury, he had "enrolled, registered or otherwise previously signified his intention of joining the civil defense organization concerned." 

Other States. Most States granting compensation have similar restrictions at least as stern as those of Massachusetts. Pennsylvania, for example, requires that payments may be made only to individuals who, prior to the time of injury, have been duly enrolled in a state, county or local civil defense organization. Payments are made only to those not eligible to receive benefits under the

FEDERAL GOVERNMENT IMMUNE FROM LIABILITY. 

The federal government is not liable for property damage, personal injury or life due to the act or failure to act of a federal agency, official or employee during the course of duty in civil defense emer gencies. (Fed. Civil Defense Act of 1950, § 304.) In the event of injury or death federal employees would, however, be eligible to collect under the Federal Employees' Compensation Act, or, in some cases, special federal pension legislation. 

COVERAGE IN ADDITION TO LOST EARNING CAPACITY. 

Proposed House, No. 3069 of 1957 was concerned only with increasing compensation for lost earning capacity. But civil defense workers also receive other important benefits, including: medical, hospital and surgical services; annuities for certain survivors; and burial expenses, which are discussed below. 

Medical, Hospital and Surgical Benefits. 

In Massachusetts, an injured civil defense worker who qualifies for compensation is indemnified by the State "for the reasonable hospital, medical and surgical expenses incurred by him or in his behalf by reason of such injury."

New York has a $3,000 ceiling on medical care in a case of total disability, or of permanent partial disability or of total disability with subsequent or prior permanent partial disability. It also pays up to $500 in a case of temporary partial disability without subsequent or prior total disability.

In California the aggregate payments for the cost of medical, surgical and hospital treatment which are provided a disaster service worker may be made up to a maximum of $2,500 for any one injury. 

Oregon also has a maximum of $2,500 on these three items, along with a $7,500 maximum on any one claim for medical, surgical or hospital expenses, compensation and rehabilitation. New Jersey and Pennsylvania pay a maximum of $750 for "medical and hospital" expenses for any one injury.

Survivors' Death Benefits; Burial Expenses. 

The 1951 Massachusetts Civil Defense Amendment provided that the widow of a civil defense worker who is killed or who dies from injuries suffered while on duty or in training, shall receive an annuity of $1,500 per year as long as she remains unmarried. For each child under eighteen, there is an additional $312. The widow's annuity ceases upon remarrying, but the allowance for the child under eighteen increases to $520. The widow's annuity becomes payable to the child, or in equal shares to the children upon her death. If no widow or child survives the deceased, a $1,000 annuity is payable to other dependents qualifying under c. 547, Acts of 1951. 

Pennsylvania pays $1,500 for accidental injury which directly causes death. New Jersey pays a $1,500 benefit to the spouse of the volunteer or in equal shares to his children; otherwise it goes to his estate. In Washington, a widow or invalid widower receives $125 per month during the life of the surviving spouse or until remarriage. Payments for children vary from $12 to $30 per month per child with a maximum of $216 per month. There is a "forthwith" award of $350 to the surviving widow in addition to monthly payments. Upon remarriage a widow gets $1,500; monthly payments then cease but children's payments continue. 

In California, the maximum death benefit is $10,000 except in the case of a surviving widow and one or more dependent minor children, in which case it is increased 25 per cent, but not to exceed $12,500 as to original injuries incurred after September 7, 1955. 

New York pays to the surviving spouse with no child under 18, 40 per cent of the average wages of the deceased during widowhood or widowerhood not to exceed two-thirds of wages nor to extend for more than five years. To the surviving spouse with child under 18, there is paid 30 per cent of the average wage of the deceased during widowhood or widowerhood plus 20 per cent of such wages for each child under 18.

Reimbursement of actual funeral expenses vary from $300 to $500 of those States reporting with Massachusetts and Washington in the maximum category.

Recruitment.

The emergency preamble to House, No. 3069 states that the purpose of the bill is to "encourage the immediate enlistment of volun-

teer civil defense workers, urgently needed to assure adequate civil defense protection. . . 

There has been considerable publicity throughout the nation relative to the lack of response to many appeals for civil defense volunteers. It is difficult to determine whether this lack of action by the public is due to an indifferent attitude, to the lack of enthusiasm for routine drills during peacetime, or to the reluctance to assume hazardous duty connected with some civil disaster operations. However, the feeling in some quarters that more adequate compensation would stimulate recruitment has not always been borne out when it was increased, or provided where it previously was absent. 

Moreover, the opinions expressed by civil defense officials on the value of compensation as a means of attracting volunteer personnel vary from one extreme to the other. 

Pros and Cons of Civil Defense Officials. 

Officials in Kentucky, Nebraska, New Hampshire, Oregon and

Oklahoma and locally in New York feel that compensation has a beneficial effect upon recruitment programs, and conversely, that the lack of compensation, or inadequate compensation, has a detrimental effect. The opinions of a selected group of officials, printed below, will indicate the wide differences which occur. 

On the one hand, there is the feeling that lack of, or inadequacy of, compensation is a "definite handicap", a "detriment to . . . reenlistment," a "very definite deterrent to such programs," and that provision of benefits has had a "substantial effect on holding people who might otherwise have left the program." On the other hand, there is the bald statement that the provision of compensation has had "no effect on our recruitment program" coupled with such cautious remarks as "no indication that the rate is of great significance;" "unable to detect any specific increase in our recruitment program," "almost no effect upon recruitment."

Arguments against Increased Compensation. 

One of the principal fears of many individuals opposed to extending the payments to unpaid civil defense volunteers as proposed by the bill under discussion, is the fact that no medical examination is required to become a sworn member of an approved and accredited local civil defense organization. Thus, there is no protection against the individual who, with a concealed injury 'suddenly" becoming disabled while participating in a civil defense drill, operation or training exercise. 

Since many civil defense operations involve hazardous duty, opponents argue that there is greater likelihood that injuries really suffered elsewhere can be covered up. Furthermore, some volunteers without prior injury, but physically unconditioned, may be assigned to duty, or may be ordered in emergencies to perform tasks that result in injuries.

Another fear is the relationship of the character of duty and the age of the civil defense volunteer. In the case of the very young teen-age volunteer, there is apt to be carelessness from enthusiasm or lack of caution on routine operations and high probability of injury, while many of the older volunteers are easily susceptible to disabling conditions brought on by excitement or sudden physical exertion.

While there is genuine concern in some quarters that increased compensation will bring about a wave of sudden disabilities, the 

Massachusetts Civil Defense agency, which supports House, No. 

3069, points out that the weekly compensation is still so comparatively small with reference to average weekly wages, that no regularly employed worker can afford to feign a claim so as to collect compensation.
