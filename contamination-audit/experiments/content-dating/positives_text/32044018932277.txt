THE PARISH IS CREATED 

THE official letter appointing me Rector of the new parish in Harrowgate is dated October 1, 1919. 

On that day the Chancellor of the diocese, Dr. 

Fitzmaurice, now Bishop of Wilmington, sent for me and announced the good news. My formal acceptance was made the next day. Of course I was delighted. To be a Pastor is the dearest ambition of almost every priest. To be a good pastor is to reach the greatest glory of the priesthood. There are higher ecclesiastical ranks and wider responsibilities, but none is more honorable, none affords better opportunities for a happy life. 

Two weeks previously I had with some trepidation reported my return from foreign military service to his Grace the Archbishop. I had been away so long and had found it so difficult to be demobilized that there was expectation of searching questions if not an actual reprimand. A Monsignor who was leaving the episcopal residence at the moment of my entry gave me a deferential greeting. He mistook the insignia on my British uniform for that of a

Major-General of the U. S. A., but when he recognized me he laughed and shook hands warmly. His [ 1 ]

Grace received me, as he has always done, with the welcome of a father, and despite my protestations, insisted on my taking a vacation. As I was leaving the room he called me back and reminded me that a Retreat was required of the returning Chaplains. 

Mine was passed under the hospitable roof of Father 

Wachter at Pottstown. 

Returning to Philadelphia on Friday, September 

26, it was a happiness to see Cardinal Mercier once more. The last time I saw him was at St. Gudule in 

Brussels. He was then in company with the King and Queen of the Belgians, and with the famous 

Mayor Marx. De Valera followed him as guest of the city a little later; indeed, on the day on which the parish was founded. I caught a glimpse of him in the pouring rain as he passed down Chestnut 

Street to Independence Hall.

The parish was yet unnamed. For me there was only one desirable name—a name beloved of every soldier who had been at the front. It was a possibility; but not a probability. Saint Joan of Arc had not yet been canonized although the canonical process was complete. Moreover there was a rumor afloat that those of the twelve apostles who had not yet been recognized by parochial dedications were to be honored first. The new parish would in all probability be called Saints Simon and Jude. I did not dare to make a direct application in accordance with my wish, but I asked the Sisters of the Holy Child to make it the object of a novena. Was what followed a mere coincidence? A few days later on 

October 21 during a meeting of the consultors of the diocese the name of the new parish was needed. The archbishop had left the room and it occurred to the [ 2 ]

Chancellor to ring me up and obtain the desired information. I told him without a moment's hesitation that its name was "St. Joan of Arc"; and this it became from that moment. 

There were difficulties. The properties which covered the site already chosen, six parcels of them, had not yet been purchased. It was important that no hopes of enhanced values should be aroused in the minds of the owners. It was therefore dangerous to visit the location although the desire to do so was almost overwhelming. One dark night a friend took me to what he thought was Harrowgate and we lost ourselves somewhere near the Boulevard. There was, however, no danger run by calling on the three pastors who were relinquishing territory. I did this by daylight and my reception was kindly. The new parish contained portions of the Nativity, the Ascension and St. Joachim. The site chosen was the geometrical centre of their church buildings. It was also at the point where their boundaries touched. 

Many years before the spot had been selected as a desirable one for the German parish of Our Lady Help of Christians; but nothing came of the matter. 

More recently an English-speaking church had been suggested so that the selection, when known, caused no surprise. [ 3 ]

II 

THE PARISH BOUNDARIES ON October 11 Father Nash, Rector of the 

Epiphany, gave me the parish boundaries which he and Father Donovan of St. Agatha's had made. He gave them to me with some hesitation knowing that there was cause for objection. That objection was reasonable was proved by the change that was made some years later. Father Nash advised me to accept them as they stood and I took his advice. The church was to be on Frankford Avenue close to the corner of Venango Street. The purchase of this site was in the hands of the real estate agent already. 

The southern boundary was only two blocks away at Ontario Street; the eastern only one block and a half away at Sepviva Street. On the north the new parish ended at Buckius Street. Only on the west could the line be said to be drawn halfway between the adjoining parishes; here the boundary was at "J" Street. All these boundaries except the one at "J" Street were revised on February 23, 1928. The accompanying map will show how they are drawn at the present moment.

Within this comparatively small space the inhabited houses are arranged in interesting groups. [ 5 ]

None of them would now be considered modern, with the exception of a few on Pike Street provided with garages which have been built more recently. This disposes of the assurance that was made to me by the Chancellor that the parish was situated in a growing neighborhood. He pointed to the Elevated Railway, not yet in operation, as an encouragement to the housebuilder. Alas! there was no space for the housebuilder to fill, except the large lot, not then in the parish, on which the North East Catholic High School was soon to be erected. Every other open space, and there were very few at that, was to be covered with mills during the early years of the parish. More houses have been torn down than built; and the Elevated Railway is the chief means of taking away parishioners who are by its means able to reach newer districts in the same time that they had taken to reach old ones. The district is, in fact, an old one; it contains many of the oldest houses of the original parishes.

The uptown parishes are constantly being fed from downtown. The young people who want modern conveniences are attracted to the new districts. The older families who have improved their circumstances also pass us by and go further north. Those who have large families and small incomes are the only ones who are likely to move in. This is proved to be the case. The changes have all been in this direction. We lose those whom we educate when they marry or obtain better employment than the district affords; we balance our losses by those who have little to spare. This is a serious situation when a whole parochial plant has to be erected. But there are compensations. [ 6 ]

The chief compensation is homogeneity. It is a great advantage to be a priest amongst people who have decided that they must be content to put up with what they have; that is, to live on the life line. They are not critical; they do not separate into cliques; and they find their chief happiness in the practice of their religion. Adversity creates close friendships and the greatest friend is the priest. In such a place one can do work with many consolations and be sure that some way will always be found to meet any needs that arise. All through the long years of depression, and we have survived two such periods -there has been little complaint. The parish has become closely knit together with spiritual relationships, and these tend to keep it intact. 

In a mill district such as ours the houses cluster in groups. There are nearly a dozen such groups in the parish. There are amicable rivalries between them, but always a spirit of give and take. We have never had any such thing as a "parish row" and we know each other too well now, to believe that such a thing is possible. We have dozens of ready workers who are as rooted to the parish in their interests as a peasant is rooted in the soil. [ 7 ]

III GETTING STARTED 

THE properties that had already been chosen as the site of the parochial buildings were on Frankford 

Avenue between Atlantic Street and Venango Street. 

They consisted of two large parcels of ground, the one to the north, vacant, the other containing three habitable houses in the rear and one in a ruinous condition on the Avenue. Between these two parcels were four small houses and a blacksmith shop. The properties were absolutely necessary and there were six individual owners. The situation was delicate. 

It was practically impossible to make the purchases simultaneously. Thomas J. Eagan our real estate agent, laid out a plan of campaign: we must acquire the large parcels first and do so without arousing gossip.

On October 27 the first parcel of land-that which lay to the north of the group-was purchased for the very reasonable sum of $10,500. It measured 100 feet on the Avenue and it had a depth of 150 feet. 

This was the proposed site for the school. The transaction was easily kept secret since the seller was a private Corporation having no connection with the district. The few who did hear of it supposed [ 9 ]

that a moving picture theatre was to be erected. 

This had been talked of for sometime. 

Nothing could now be done until the other large parcel of land was secured. Here we faced a difficulty. The owner did not want to sell. He was said to be holding the lot expecting that the city would buy it as the site for a fire-house. The fate of the parish lay in his hands. Mr. Eagan was in favor of waiting. I disagreed with him. I urged him to tell the owner the truth believing that we should receive fair treatment. This proved to be correct. Mr. 

Medford, well-known in the district, instantly agreed to meet our wishes. We could have the property at the same price for which he had held it for many years-$26,500. He was very ill at the time and died soon afterwards. We should consider him as one of our benefactors. Had he increased his price or refused to sell, we should have been compelled to dispose of the lot which had already been purchased. 

The agreement was signed on November 6th, 1919. 

The next day the blacksmith shop and four of the adjoining houses were purchased without difficulty at the equitable sum of $17,000. One house remained unpurchased. It was held for a prohibitive price for the next fifteen years; it was eventually bought for what had been offered in 1919. At the present moment the Church possesses all the land that is necessary for the future building.

There was another difficulty. It was impossible to obtain immediate occupancy. Houses were not to be had. Where was the Rector to live? Where was he to hold services? Standing at the corner of 

Ontario Street and Frankford Avenue in the middle of November he received the final word of the agent: [ 10 ]

As there was nothing purchasable within the boundaries of the parish which could be occupied at once. this ultimatum was delivered he looked over the speaker's shoulder and saw on the opposite side of the street an empty shop in a three-story house. 

Warning Mr. Eagan not to look around he described the premises. "You could not live there! That has been a 

Chinese laundry". "Certainly I could. It would be the very place.

Buy it at once if you can. We have done so well that we need not haggle over the cost." 

Mr. Eagan was able to purchase the property without difficulty at a cost of $5,000. In that house I lived for the best part of the next year and sold it at a small profit. It was 3403 Frankford Avenue. 

There was no further obstacle to our starting work. 

A few dollars would make the place habitable. 

What fun it was! There was furniture to be bought; an altar to be made; pews to be begged; and much cleaning to be done. The Church occupying the store was ready in a week. The property was purchased on November the fifteenth and the first informal mass was offered on Sunday, November 23. 

It took another two weeks to get the rectory ready. 

It was to consist of the two upper floors. 

The church would seat about sixty people with standing room in the private hallway. On the first 

Sunday there were only two masses. At 7:30 everything was crowded; at 9:30 people were clamoring to get inside, and failing to do so contented themselves with looking through the bulk window. Something had to be done. It would be far worse the next 

Sunday at the formal opening-it was worse! For- [ 11 ]

tunately the weather was fine and we could open the doors and windows. The crowd filled the back yard, the passages, the stairs and even penetrated into the living rooms above. So large was the assembly on the sidewalk that many passerbys thought that a tragedy had taken place. The curious alighted from the street cars to see what was wrong, whilst the zealous ushers passed the collection baskets around. 

Three weeks of this and it was necessary to get help. 

Father John F. Dodwell of the Holy Ghost College at

Cornwells came to us for Sunday Masses, but even with four of them the crush was still great. There was fear that the bulk window would be pushed out; it was used as a gallery. Sitting was out of the question. Every room including the bedrooms was invaded.

We did everything possible. The floors were inspected to be sure that they would carry the weight; non-parishioners were exhorted to hear Mass in their own churches; but even the normal attendence would have been too large for the space available. Each 

Sunday many came out of curiosity to see how Mass could be conducted under such conditions. In order to preserve a moral unity in the congregation, the electric bells throughout the house were rung at the various parts of the service, and the priest who was not officiating stood in the hall and recited the Mass in English. The Gospel and the notices were read on each floor.

Before moving into the new quarters, the Rector accepted the hospitality of Mr. and Mrs. James 

Meagher, the parents of the Reverend Francis Meagher. They lived on Frankford Avenue near the temporary chapel. By Saturday December 6th [ 12 ]

all the furniture, dishes and so forth were procured and the Rectory was ready for occupation. Everything was very comfortable.

The first sick-call came between the masses on the first Sunday. It was followed by the first deaththat of Mrs. Elizabeth Dillon. As it was impossible to hold a funeral in the chapel, for the time being all these services were conducted in neighboring churches. All other ministrations took place in the store. The first baptism was on December 7, that of 

Thomas Joseph Hughes. The first Holy Hour was held on November 28, a devotion that has since been continuously observed. The First Sodality meeting was on December 9, which was a Tuesday, and the night has never been changed. The first Holy Name meeting took place on December 11. Both Societies were canonically erected as soon as possible. The 

First Holy Communion for the children followed later, during the Octave of the Ascension Day. In after years our First Communions were always made on Ascension Day. Ascensiontide is sacred to St. Joan.

The crowded conditions on Sunday morning led to a search for some Hall within the parish where Mass could be said temporarily until the blacksmith shop was available. There was not a single Moving Picture Theatre in the district. There was however a large room at the corner of Erie Avenue and Glenwood Avenue, but its area proved to be smaller than that of the store. There was also a Fraternity Hall close by, but it could only be reached by a doorway next to a saloon, and it was thought inadvisable to hold religious services here at a time when Prohibition was coming into effect. We were compelled to accept the situation. [ 13 ]

IV FIRST PLANNING 

THE first weeks in the new Parish were spent in visiting the people. For a number of them it was a matter of sadness. There were families, which, whilst living in the same house, had been called upon to build the Nativity and the Ascension Church successively. Now they were asked to build a third one. They were naturally attached by many ties to these older parishes. It was hard for them to give up long and happy associations. They were obedient Catholics, however, and I can only remember one or two who expressed their disappointment openly.

They were all kind to me and did their best to help me.

I had two plans in view. The first was for adequate temporary buildings; the second for the permanent ones. It was not thought possible to open a school at present; the blacksmith shop might serve the purpose of a temporary Church. The permanent school and church building must be built as soon as possible. 

Pressure was brought upon the blacksmith, Mr.

Weisner, to vacate his premises. He could not do so until he had built others. He commenced to do [ 15 ]

this at once. where the horses waiting to be shod often got into the way of the trolley cars. It would take some months for him to move and his contract with us gave him until June. He would not be able to vacate any sooner. He expressed his satisfaction in a change which helped us both, and being a man of strong religious belief, added some words that remain in my memory:

He was glad to move off the Avenue, "This house (meaning the dwelling which was destined for a Rectory) has always been conducted in a moral manner, and I am sure that it will always be so conducted by you. I think that the Lord has rewarded me by sending you here." I discovered that he was referring to his total abstinence convictions.

I already had my architect for the permanent building. When I was at St. Edward's as a curate before the war, it was my happiness to meet Mr. 

George Audsley, the distinguished architect of that magnificent building. I promised him then that if I ever had to build a Church he should build it for me. 

My residence at the St. Edward's Rectory after my return from France reminded me of this promise. 

I made no mistake although he was now an octogenarian; but what an octogenarian! He had the soul of a child. I wrote to him at once. After Christmas he came to stay with me at the store. He had already a plan in his mind. We decided to visit a number of new schools in order to get ideas, amongst others the Nativity, St. Agatha's and St. Edward's.

We interviewed the Sisters of various Orders to discuss ventilation, cloak rooms and other problems. [ 16 ]

We even picked out the bricks that seemed most suitable.

Mr. Audsley entered into the undertaking with boyish eagerness. His visits were frequent. He was versatile. Architecture was not his original profession. He had been a skilled reproducer of ancient documents and designs. He was the author of many books. His chief hobby was building organs. An organ of his, which won a prize at the St. Louis 

Exposition, is now a part of the large instrument in Wanamaker's Store. He could talk on many subjects. He had the most naïve opinions which he held with the utmost tenacity. For example, he refused to believe in the existence of sound waves. He had a magnificent idea of color, and a fine appreciation of music. We talked by the hour. Amongst other accomplishments he was a skilful cook. One night he put on an apron and prepared a dish of scalloped oysters. He brought me bottles of a sauce prepared from his own recipe-one that was too costly to be marketable. If I did not have time for conversation he would go alone to the movies. His mind was never at rest and his health was magnificent. When asked the secret of his longevity, he would answer that it was due to his working at night when his family could not interrupt him! He went to sleep when he was tired and never arose until he awoke naturally.

Mr. Audsley was not a Catholic but he had a Catholic mind. He had known many members of the neo- 

Gothic School in England and he lived imaginatively in the Middle Ages. He had a strong belief in the 

Real Presence although I could not find that he went to Church. I remember his walking one night in St. [ 17 ]

Edward's Church (he avoided it in the daytime as far as possible because he detested its cheap windows, placed there without his advice) and his speaking to me as I was leaving the confessional. "What a privilege", said he, "to have built this house to enshrine the Presence of God". He died at the age of 87, a few hours after writing the last page of a book which has been considered one of the best on the construction of organs.

At night in the room which served as parlor, dining and sitting-room he and I used to build the future Church of St. Joan of Arc in fancy. He would have liked a Gothic building; something that would recall the chivalrous days of its patron saint. It was to be emblazoned by way of decoration with the shields of all the cities connected with her short career of glory. 

Alas for such dreams? Since we sat dreaming the fabric of society has been changed and such dreams belong to an age that has passed away. We did not suspect the collapse that was so near. The millions of dead on foreign battlefields were yet to take their vengeance. Will the Church ever be built? Certainly not as Mr. Audsley and I planned it. The age of the Gothic Revival is over. A new generation demands something that it can understand. 

Meanwhile the architect was busy on the plans for the School which was also to serve as a temporary Church. It is amazing to know that this octogenarian had finished his work and received the approbation of the Diocesan Building Committee by the 4th March 1920. He and I had submitted them on 

February 13, to Father Nash who promised to bring them before Monsignor Sinnott and Father Daley, the other members of the Committee. We were [ 18 ]

highly complimented on the work. In returning the plans to me at the later date Father Nash said that they were the best that had ever been offered to him. 

The Committee had no criticisms to make nor any changes to suggest. [ 19 ]

V 

HARROWGATE: A COUNTRY DISTRICT 

THIS seems to be the place to say something about the neighborhood in which we live. I intended to write only a few words, but the interest grew with discovery. As this chapter would otherwise be far too long, I will divide it.

The name Harrowgate, or Harrogate (for it is spelt both ways) is attractive. I connected it with the famous Spa in Yorkshire and, before I visited it, had visions of pump-rooms and pleasure gardens. 

The truth was somewhat discouraging, especially on a damp day when the heavy atmosphere brought to earth the smoke and smells of manufacture. I looked in vain for any evidence of an ancient village. 

There was to be found no meeting-house, no school, nor even a tavern which suggested any antiquity. A few groups of brick houses here and there of the kind what are sometimes called "corners" all turned out to be rather late comers. Moreover the eastern part of the district was not called Harrowgate; it went by the name of Aramingo. Here, too, there was a complete absence of historic landmarks such as give evidence of village life. All that was really old were some stone houses; one called Chalkley [ 21 ]

Hall, another Cedar Grove Farm, and a few smaller ones which were little more than ruins. They were scattered. What, then, was the secret of the name "Harrowgate?" Why did a number of the inhabitants call themselves "Harrowgaters?" and speak of having grandfathers who called themselves similarly? The old maps gave the explanation. 

In 1777 a survey of the environs of Philadelphia was made by N. Scull and G. Heap. On this it is easy to find our location. It lies between two creeks designated on the map by their present names: 

Frankford Creek and Gunner's Run. The last is shown to have had a tributary running through the middle of what is now our parish. Later and more accurate maps divide this tributary into two branches. Three houses are marked within our boundaries. It is easy to identify two of them. 

They are the ones already mentioned. Chalkley Hall was then owned by Abel James. He was the husband of Rebecca, the oldest daughter of an early settler Thomas Chalkley. The other house was then owned by Joseph Paschall, he likewise had married the daughter of a first settler, Thomas Coates, and her name was Elizabeth. The third house is assigned to a party named Worthington. There is a house still standing near the position marked on the map, but I can learn nothing about it from local tradition.

The two small rivers are prehistoric. Both of them were navigable for some distance at high tide to sea-going ships of the period-1777. From other maps I find that there was a coal-wharf at the bridge where our single ancient road crosses Frankford Creek. This creek was called the Quessionomink, [ 22 ]

i. e. "Eel Skin" by the Indians who lived beside it as late as the time when the Swedes were establishing themselves on the Delaware. The aborigines lingered in the district for years afterwards and sometimes camped on the banks of Gunner's Run in the early eighteenth century.

The mention of the road crossing the river brings us to the days of the white man. The road is still the chief route between Philadelphia and the ancient and once Indian village of Frankford. It is a grand old thorough-fare, attesting its age by its winding course; even in the small part of it that runs through our midst, it has several slight changes of direction. 

Its irregularity has caused many to think that we have built our school askew. They must blame the original wagon track and not the architect. This road goes on beyond Frankford to Bristol, to Trenton, to New Brunswick until it reaches New York.

It was called the King's Highway. The foundations of our buildings have gone deeply into the undisturbed soil that borders on its historic course. 

On this road near the Frankford Creek the British and American armies were in contact during the 

Revolutionary War. Along it came Lydia Darragh carrying news to the outposts of General Washington at White Hall of an intended British attack. Her excuse for the journey was her need of flour from 

Frankford Mill and she went through the military lines, it is said, with a pass signed by General Howe. 

She returned to her house at Second and Spruce 

Streets without being suspected.

It is likely enough that Allen McLane, the guerilla horseman, may have raided in this district the provision wagons that constantly brought food to the [ 23 ]

troops in Philadelphia. There is a story of a freebooter, named Doale, who after rendering chivalrous help to a woman on Frankford Road, escaped from a patrol of fifty soldiers by swimming the Delaware River.

Chalkley Hall itself was the scene of an amusing incident when the redcoats entered through the front door and ate the meal prepared for American soldiers who had just time to escape by the back door. 

This stately mansion, now used as a warehouse, was quite new at that time. In 1776 Abel James built it with cream-colored freestone, brought from England as ballast. His father-in-law, already mentioned, had settled here and built a smaller residence in 1723. The latter was a seafaring man who owned part of the ship on which he was Mate. He had intended to retire, but like many another sailor, he could not remain content ashore, at what he called his plantation at Frankford; he made more voyages, dying on one of them in 1741 at the West Indian island of Tortula. He was a fervent Quaker who is still remembered for his religious and charitable activities.

Cedar Grove Farm was much nearer to our church property. I say was because it has been bodily removed to Fairmount Park since I came. It could once be seen from the front door of our present Rectory. Indeed, the second oldest road in the parish, developed from a path which connected the farm with Nicetown Lane on the north and with Frankford Road on the south, is the present Atlantic Street.

The farm had a beautiful site when Thomas Coates settled here. It was built on the highest ground for [ 24 ]

miles around. It was surrounded by woods through which the three branches of Gunner's Run meandered. In the distance could be seen Fairhill and 

Nicetown, a little nearer Rosehill and Strawberry

Hill and other high points. The Delaware River then running free from factory chimneys stretched across the landscape to the south-east. 

Below the farm the old maps mark a mineral spring from which one of the tributaries of Gunner's Run took its rise. It is this mineral spring that brought a new development after the Revolution. This was the building of a hotel called the Harrowgate which gave its name to the lane which ran past the present Rectory. It is a curious and satisfying fact that our parish site is at the meeting of the two oldest roads in the district.

I have tried to reconstruct in imagination, with the aid of old maps, the appearance of Harrowgate Lane before the middle of the last century. It crossed 

Frankford Road where its successor, Atlantic Street, does now.

At the south-east of the crossing there was a toll house which caused a jog in the Lane. 

To the north-west from the same crossing it ran between two properties, each of about thirty acres; the one to the right was owned by Mr. Spohn, the one to the left by Mr. Polk. I think that the present 

Fisher house marks the location of the Spohn farm. These two properties were bounded by one of the tributaries of Gunner's Run somewhere near the present Jasper Street. You must try to forget all the quadrilateral blocks (which probably took their bearings from Harrowgate Lane) of a much later date. Across this very small stream, that passed under a bridge, was Harrowgate Hotel. It [ 25 ]

was on the map long before any of the present streets were in existence. Its owner in 1849 was Mr. C. Weyn.

The Lane after passing the Hotel went on its course until it reached a new road which appeared about 1800. This road was a bending of old Front 

Street. It was made by a Company who wished to capture some of the traffic that had hitherto passed up the Frankford Road. It went close to Cedar 

Grove Farm and brought two taverns to the corners where it crossed the Lane. They were called by the delightful names "The Stag" and "The Star". I like to think that some old houses, still standing when I came, were the remains of these inns and their stables. By this time a hamlet has sprung up in the neighborhood of the farm. This is Harrowgate. 

Hardly a vestige of it remains and the Lane itself which ended at Nicetown Lane has been completely destroyed. On what is now "K" Street (horrible name) and also on the present Venango Street you can find buildings that belonged to the later days of Harrowgate hamlet. There was never any meetinghouse, but there was that most important factor in civilization, a blacksmith's forge. Still later there were greenhouses and two small cemeteries, one for the burial of Jews of the most orthodox congregations in Philadelphia. The succeeding years were to change the whole character of the neighborhood. [ 26 ]

HARROWGATE: AN INDUSTRIAL DISTRICT 

It was the fate of Harrowgate to lie on the direct route to New York. This brought the railway in the first half of last century. The Philadelphia and 

Trenton Railway drew a straight line below Frankford Avenue, cutting off Chalkley Hall and other residences from the main highway. With the railway came the factory. Industrial enterprises begin to appear on the map of 1849. A chemical works is now situated near Harrowgate Lane across from the Hotel; a cotton mill, an iron works, a dyehouse and another chemical works appear near the railway where it crosses Frankford Creek, but a canal that had been proposed in this neighborhood is not constructed. It was to have connected Gunner's Run with the Creek.

A new hamlet springs up in the neighborhood of the now isolated Chalkley Hall. Its name is not yet definite; it is called by turns Roseville, Doverville and at last Aramingo. These names suggest the transformation of the neighborhood. The first was descriptive of the departing rural prospect, the second was derived from a resident, the third which has remained until now was taken from the chemical [ 27 ]

works, whose ruins can still be traced. This hamlet was situated at the junction of Point Lane (now called Wheatsheaf Lane from an old tavern) and

Frankford Avenue. On the map of 1849 there are many residences marked. There is a school, a tavern the Black Horse, a doctor, a butcher Mr. Buckius, and several parallel streets. This was all to be swept away a little later by two new developments.

The first was the Plank Road. In the last chapter 

I spoke of the rival to Frankford Road-the extension of Front Street bent to the East from a point near Hart Lane. A much more dangerous rival was to appear in 1860. A new road perfectly straight was cut from York and Front Street to Frankford. 

It is now called Kensington Avenue and it is the route of the present Elevated Railway. On this avenue there ran for many years what was known as the "dummy," a train of street cars drawn by a steam locomotive. Kensington Avenue eventually became an important throughfare, disputing with 

Frankford Avenue the monopoly of northern travel.

When the whole district was surveyed for streets, those to the east were drawn at right angles to Kensington Avenue and parallel with Harrowgate Lane. 

This Plank Road passed to the north of Harrowgate Hotel.

The second development was to follow quickly. 

Before the Civil War had begun, the Philadelphia and Trenton R. R. was joined to the Pennsylvania 

R. R. by what was known as the Connecting Railway.

When it was built Old Front Street almost disappeared, Harrowgate Lane was cut into pieces and partly renamed, and the hamlet of Doverville was [ 28 ]

obliterated, except for a house here and there. The name Aramingo survived in a vague and indefinite way.

Factories now sprang up on all sides. Harrowgate was turned into a Railway Junction at which the lines to the Docks, to Atlantic City, to New York and to Philadelphia were to meet. A quarter of our parish area is now railroad yards.

The physical features were also changed. Gunner's Run was completely sewerized; Frankford

Creek was turned into an outlet for factory waste. 

The rolling country was slashed, filled, leveled and divided into quadrilateral blocks. A cemetery which had a short existence in the neighborhood of the present Jasper and Emerald Streets above Venango

Street was entirely removed and its site almost forgotten. The tiny tributary of Gunner's Run which flowed under Harrogate Lane passed through this spot. There are still one or two houses built on the old level upon which you can look down from Jasper Street.

Schools came but no churches; saloons took the place of the old roadside taverns. The neighborhood was surrounded by new boroughs but it remained until the county of Philadelphia became coterminous with the city of Philadelphia, a part of the unorganized Northern Liberties. It never belonged to Oxford, to Frankford, or to Richmond. It now overlaps two wards, the forty-fifth and the thirty-third. [ 29 ]

VII THE MILITARY MASS 

Is it an extravagant fancy to see a fitness in the dedication of a church to St. Joan of Arc on a thoroughfare that witnessed the struggle between 

America and the mother land? It has been said that the loss of France was the beginning of the strength of England. The Maid of Orleans always prayed for her enemies calling them, innocently, the “goddams". It is likewise true that the loss of the thirteen colonies was the beginning of a successful colonial Empire. It was also the chief cause of 

Catholic Emancipation. The English Government sensed the danger of alienating its Catholic subjects. 

In 1919 the Great War was just over and it was inevitable that the first church in America erected to the honor of a French military hero should be associated with its recent memories. A Military Mass to mark the canonization was arranged. The date chosen was May 23, 1920, one week after the solemn ceremonies in Rome. Since the two parcels of ground that had been purchased were each too small for the purpose, it was decided to obtain the use of Harrowgate Square. Early in April we began to make preparations. They were on a large scale; we [ 31 ]

have never attempted anything since on a larger one. 

Of the many Committees Mr. Robert Hepburn was appointed General Chairman. It would be impossible in the space at my disposal to name all those who helped. Practically everyone in the parish did something. The ushers were assisted by over seventy service men in uniform. Father Joseph L.

N. Wolfe sang the mass assisted by two other army chaplains, Rev. Joseph Hayes and Rev. Martin 

Joseph Lynch. The preacher was the Rev. Joseph Whitaker.

Final arrangements were made at a most enthusiastic meeting of the Holy Name Society held on 

May 9th. A few days afterwards the architect Mr.

George A. Audsley arrived. He designed and made the canopy of white material powdered with gold fleurs-de-lis which was placed over the altar to be erected in the Square. Unfortunately he could not stay with us for the celebration.

The great day arrived. As the sun arose it was unable to pierce the thick clouds and before nine o'clock a slight rain was falling. We were all alarmed. Would the parade take place? Would the crowd consent to stand on the wet grass? Suddenly the weather changed. The sun came out strongly and the temperature rose. The arrangements were perfect.

The procession started from Allegheny and Frankford Avenues at 10:30. First came the police escort with the marshal and his mounted staff followed by the police band. Next came the clergy accompanied by a squad of cavalry. Then the various military organizations which made up the first Division. 

Amongst them were representatives of many veteran [ 32 ]

posts, with color guards and bands. There was also a battery of artillery. This division was in uniform and contained about one thousand men. The second division was composed of civilian and religious societies, amongst which was the Holy Name Society of the Ascension Parish led by Father Hugh Dale who afterwards acted as master of ceremonies at the mass. When the parade reached Harrowgate Park by way of Emerald Street and Tioga Street an enormous crowd was awaiting it. The children at the gospel side of the altar sang hymns in honor of 

St. Joan of Arc under the charge of Sisters Cyrenia and Sacred Heart. A large choir accompanied by brass instruments and directed by Sylvester Keul and Cecilia Heaney sang the Missa de Angelis. 

Monsignor Crane, who presided, accompanied by the ministers of the mass, approached the altar as the parade moved into place. On the platform there were distinguished visitors including the French 

Consul, Dr. Ernest Laplace, Professor Pierre Giroud and Rev. Dr. Leon Legrain; the last named added a few words in French to the sermon. After the ceremony the guests were entertained at a luncheon given by the parish at the home of Mrs. J. J. Ratigan.

It was hoped that a military mass might become an annual event, but this was found to be impractical until 1926, when an opportunity was presented which may be recorded now.

The officers of Capron-Allyn Post of the Veterans of Foreign Wars wishing to hold their annual Church Parade in our Church, usually an evening affair, readily changed their plan for an attendance at mass [ 33 ]

when they discovered that this was more convenient for us.

There were several other reasons for having another military mass. A Boy's Brigade had been established in the parish; the French colony wished to have a public observance of St. Joan of Arc's feast; and there was a statue, which had arrived. some months before from Oberammergau, the donation of Mr. James McGuigan, that awaited an occasion for its solemn blessing. A military parade preceded the mass. Several units marched, notably the British War Veterans who had taken a leading position in the celebration at Harrowgate Park. 

The service was held in the auditorium which was filled to capacity. Many of the soldiers were non- 

Catholics and they may have found the services very long since we were celebrating the Forty Hours on the same day.

The military parade and mass became an annual event. This was not only due to the assistance given to it so generously by nearly all the military organizations in the neighborhood, but also to the coincidence of the feast of St. Joan of Arc with Decoration Day. In 1929 the French Consul, M. Rene 

Weiller, kindly consented to be present at the mass and he and his successor, M. Marcel de Verneuil, have always been with us since. Other distinguished visitors come, and what began on a small scale is now a looked-for neighborhood event. At this Mass we always recall the names of those from the parish area who died in France: James Costigan, Harry 

Crossley and Anthony Glowka. 

There have been other military affairs. On May 

12, 1929, the French colony in Philadelphia observed the 500th anniversary of the beginning of the career [ 34 ]

of St. Joan of Arc. In France celebrations spread over three years, were to move from place to place following the great events in her short military life. 

The Philadelphia observance took place in the afternoon in Fairmount Park at the statue of St. 

Joan of Arc. Our parish was invited to cooperate. 

The response of our people was unanimous. Ten full trolley-car loads of adults and children were carried to Girard Avenue where a parade was formed. There were eight hundred parishioners in line headed by the Boy's Brigade band. I was also able to secure the co-operation of a number of military units and the affair grew to large proportions. 

The Mayor of the city was present and also a number of distinguished citizens. There have been other celebrations at this statue on Decoration Days which were followed by a picnic in the Zoological Gardens. 

These were arranged by Mr. Michael Francis Doyle who has for many years been a promoter of the cultus of St. Joan of Arc. On May 25, 1931 we had a parish supper to mark the end of the round of celebrations.

This is a good place to mention our Boy's Brigade. 

It was organized in 1925 by Father Kohl assisted by 

Sergeant-Major Peter Burke, late of the Marines.

The Brigade is still flourishing. It has taken part in a great number of parades and has won many prizes in competitions arranged by the city. At first it was intended to be a military organization; but it very soon became a band. Everyone wanted to play something-chiefly a trumpet or a drum. For several years there was a fife division, but it languished. 

A fife does not make enough noise for the average boy and it is hard to play. Father Kohl and his [ 35 ]

assistants worked incessantly. They were able to outfit about one hundred boys with khaki uniforms and to buy the whole instrumental equipment. In the past twelve years there have been many devoted helpers. I mentioned the name of Frank Grace first because he was faithful to the day of his death. At present George Brogley, John Mulherin and John

Steinberg are active officers with long years of service. After Father Kohl left us, Father Bourne and 

Father Bonner have had charge.

There was one event in the history of the Brigade Band which stands out strongly. It was a summer camp for the boys. It was made possible by the hard work of Peter Burke and Patrick Esmond, assisted by four of our devoted women workers, Mesdames Allen, Ettner, Hornung and Murphy.

This experiment was mighty nice for the boys butfor the others, it was a different story. It was our intention to have a camp on other years, charging a small fee, but no one ever seemed to have the fee. 

So the matter has dropped. [ 36 ]

OUR PROPERTIES IN 1920

VIII THE BLACKSMITH'S SHOP 

At the beginning of June the blacksmith gave us possession of his house which together with the one next door was to be our Rectory for nine years. 

They were small cottages, each of six rooms, badly built and having no entrance halls. They were almost ready to fall apart, being constructed of salmon brick with the plastering directly on the exterior walls. The windows and doors were of various sizes suggesting that second-hand lumber had been used in their construction. When it rained dampness showed on the wall-paper and the slightest wind made itself felt through the ill-fitting woodwork. The two houses were attached, but detached from a similar pair a few feet to the south. Their front walls were built parallel to the road, and reproduced its irregular angle; their side walls were parallel to the side streets; in consequence no room had square corners and the impression made on some of our visitors was alarming. They thought that they were suffering from giddiness. It was difficult to fit the rugs and the furniture. It was to avoid this that we built the front wall of the new school perfectly square, despite many criticisms.

In front of the two houses there were delapidated [ 37 ]

stoops of different design. These were torn down in order to replace them with a long built-in porch that would give the building a little dignity. There were other structural changes necessary. Everything was done by voluntary labor. A number of bricklayers came on the second Saturday of June and in a few hours the base of the porch was built. The carpenters completed the job later. On the same day the smithy, which almost touched the Rectory on the north, was vacated. Other men began at once to excavate the dirt floor to a depth of several feet—a very necessary thing after its long use. They then laid sleepers in a cinder fill on which was placed the wooden floor. The men enjoyed their work and there were a number of mysterious thirst quenchings in the back lane. 

Formal possession was taken of the new premises on Sunday, June 13, when Benediction of the Blessed

Sacrament was given on the lot on which the new school was to be built. We re-erected the altar which had been used in Harrogate Square. There was a large attendance. 

On June 27 the smithy was used as a chapel for the first time. It was totally inadequate; if anything it was worse than the store. It was only eighteen feet wide and sixty deep. The heat was intense despite the wheezy wooden electric fans, brought from a dismantled saloon. Fortunately the windows, (with which the walls had now been pierced) could be opened, and through these most of the congregation, standing on the adjoining lot, heard Mass. 

The priest vested in the former kitchen of the rectory and entered the chapel through a side door; on rainy days the sexton stood ready with an umbrella. [ 38 ]

One thing was certain, the chapel had to be enlarged as soon as possible. This had already been foreseen. Our friends the carpenters and bricklayers were willing to help. We could extent the smithy about forty feet to the rear and build an "L" behind the Rectory, incorporating the kitchen used as the sacristy. The buildings could be heated by a large furnace in the basement of the Rectory. This was arranged for at once. I digress to say something about this furnace. In order to heat the chapel with its thin walls and tar-paper ceiling it was necessary for the sexton to get up steam at five o'clock in the morning. There was no need for alarm clocks. Everyone in the Rectory was halfroasted before mass began. A temperature of seventy degrees in the chapel meant one of about ninety in the house. Fortunately at the later masses no heat of any kind was necessary. 

On two more Saturdays the men worked hard and the walls were almost finished when a difficulty arose. 

I was summoned to City Hall and told that we were putting up an illegal building. The walls should be three bricks instead of two, because the width of the chapel was over eighteen feet. It was useless to show that in no place did the span of roof exceed the lawful width. It was the "L" that caused the trouble. The "L" was parallel to Frankford

Avenue to the extent of thirty-six feet if one included the width of the chapel itself. The rafters were, however, at right angles to its walls and there was no upper story. The Inspector was obdurate. The law, he said, had nothing to do with the span of the rafters, it referred solely to the actual width of a building parallel to the street. I mildly suggested [ 39 ]

that it was at right angles to the neighboring side street. In reply I was referred to the letter of the law. We must make the walls four inches thicker. 

The bricklayer, Patrick Esmond, then had his say. 

It would weaken the existing walls to hang more material on them. The Inspector answered that he did not care whether the walls were stronger or weaker-the letter of the law must be observed. 

One of those present thought that he saw the Inspector wink, and on the strength of this probably imaginary wink we did nothing and our further progress was not hindered. It was an exhibition of interpreting the letter of the law by preserving its spirit.

On July 9th we moved into the Rectory. Henceforth our address was 3552 Frankford Avenue. The house had been made very comfortable. The northern part which the blacksmith had occupied was reserved for the priests; the southern part, which was only connected on the ground floor, was used by the housekeepers. The space available was very small. 

During the last year of occupancy three priests were huddled together in three minature rooms, one of them measured nine feet by five.

The chapel remained in use until October, 1922, that is, for a period of a little more than two years. 

After then most of its site was used for the new central heating plant. A small portion, however, was allowed to stand until 1934. It was used by turns for a school room, a drill room and a store room. This portion was the oldest. From its walls we could never paint out the horse shoes that proclaimed its original purpose. Perhaps they brought us good luck. [ 40 ]

IX THE RAG-SHOP SCHOOL 

ON the corner of Frankford Road and Atlantic

Street there stood a dilapidated three-story building. When the lot was purchased this building was regarded as a ruin. The windows were out, the roof leaked and the floors were dangerous. No one had the least thought of its being of any value. It had been built after the year 1849 for it does not appear on the map of that date. It was used as a feed store, and no doubt did a thriving business when the toll gate stood directly across the street. In later years it had become a ragman's dump. The lower floor was the only part that was used, although a block and tackle still hung from the top window in the rear. 

The yard was filled with unsaleable junk. The tenant, who had promised to clean up, saw difficulties ahead, and departed to an address unknown. The whole lot was surrounded by high bill-boards. 

When Mother Isador of the Sisters of the Immaculate Heart of Mary called to see me with reference to the future school she happened to notice this wretched building. From the beginning of the parish she had sent us two Sisters, Cyrenia and 

Sacred Heart, from Frankford to take charge of [ 41 ]

our Sunday School. I took her to see the purchased properties to convince her that it was impossible to start the school which the Sisters had already contracted to serve. The ruin on the Medford property had already attracted her attention. "There's your school," she exclaimed.

I was dumbfounded. She insisted on entering the building which was a risky matter, since the lower floor was actually fallen in places and the cellar was full of vermin. She gathered up her skirts and went over the premises, even going up to the top of the third floor. She assured me that a school of the six lower grades could, and therefore should, be started at once. 

This was on July 23.

There was no time to lose if a school was to be started in the Fall. We had only just finished the work on the Rectory and Chapel. I knew that it would not only be unfair to ask for more voluntary work; it would be quite impossible to do all that was to be done in such a way within the time limits. We needed money at once. A monster carnival on the school lot provided $8,000. With this I paid for the improvements that were necessary, hiring the labor and acting as treasurer. It was an expensive way to build as I soon found out. The whole sum was needed before the ruined house was rehabilitated. 

The brickwork was pointed; the joists were pulled through the walls and replaced; new floors were laid where necessary; electric light was supplied; plumbing and under-drainage was provided; a steam-heat system with furnace was installed; then there was roofing, painting and plastering. It would have been cheaper to have pulled everything down and started afresh. This I found out afterwards. Nevertheless [ 42 ]

I profited by experience. The school was ready by 

September 8th. It opened with 182 children.

Meanwhile the school lot had to be cleaned up. It was a terrible job. Night after night a number of men assembled with shovels and rakes. We had piles of newspapers to burn. They were pressed into the earth to a depth of several feet and represented the accumulation of years. There were huge stacks of rubber horsepads which were found to be quite worthless. We had to load them into carts.

The cellar was the first job that we tackled; here we disturbed the dwelling of numerous rats which were all destroyed. Fires were smoldering every evening on the lot. I do not know what the neighbors must have thought. Perhaps they were glad to see an eyesore disappearing. The bill-boards were removed. 

Eventually everything was made tidy and by the time that the children came to school there was a very convenient yard for their recreation. 

The old building stood for many years yet. It served as a school until the Fall of 1923 when the permanent building was ready for use. As early as 

September 1920 a Catholic Club met on the top floor which was not needed for classrooms for the first year. This Club after a temporary banishment from 

1921 to 1923 (when it assembled at the south-west corner of Emerald and Tioga Streets) returned in the latter year and took over the whole building. 

For about seven years the Catholic Club had a struggling existence. At times it experienced prosperity and even achieved fame in athletics, especially in long-distance running. Amongst its devoted supporters were Neil Crosson, Harry Hughes and 

James Ratigan. Unfortunately it was never self- [ 43 ]

supporting and it became a financial burden, depending on various money-raising activities. After it ceased to exist the building remained unoccupied until 1934 when it was demolished together with all the other buildings on Frankford Avenue. 

There were a number of young people who mourned the disappearance of the old house. Within its walls they had started school; from it our first class was graduated. Nevertheless it was not demolished one day too soon. It was discovered that its bricks were of very inferior grade and in some places the walls were powdering into dust; dry rot had even attacked the new joists and many of them were hanging to the walls by a few fibres. 

Peace to its memory. It stood us in good stead and enabled us to start a school three years earlier than would otherwise have been possible. [ 44 ]

THE NEW SCHOOL 

SOMETHING has already been said of the planning of the School. It was, of course, hoped that its building would commence almost at once. The depression of 1920-1921 stopped us. The price of steel was prohibitive; the bricklayers were on strike. 

Another reason for delay was the temporary school which was found sufficient for present purposes. 

When all the plans had been drawn and had passed various scrutinies, they were laid aside. Amongst those who approved of them was the Rector of the Nativity Church, himself a school builder. He was enthusiastic about them. We were in the second year of our parochial existence when a letter arrived from the architect, on September 18, 1921. Mr. Audsley had consulted with his brethren of the profession and had discovered that immediate building would capture satisfactory prices.

Two summers in the Blacksmith Shop had convinced us that every possible effort must be made to build. Permission was applied for and it was re- 

Iceived on October 5th. We chose our contractors carefully sending them the plans and specifications on October 24. The bids were to be submitted on [ 45 ]

November 16th. It was decided that the whole building should be erected but that only the auditorium and the stairways should be completed. As the school rooms would not be needed until the fall of 1923, the two upper floors could be left in the rough without partitions.

There was much excitement when the bids arrived. 

None were opened until they had all been received. 

Mr. Audsley was the soul of fairness. The lowest bidder was to be awarded the contract since no one was allowed to bid who did not come up to his standards of work. There were five bidders and the range of prices was not very large. The contract was awarded to Melody and Keating at a price of $129,120. There were no extras, indeed, there were several reductions in regard to waterproofing and other details. 

Mr. Audsley was delighted with the decision since he had already been associated with this firm when it built his church of St. Edward. A few days later the Continental-Equitable Trust Society agreed to finance the venture and nothing prevented work but the brick-layers strike which had run into many months. It was, however, decided to put in the cement foundations before the frost set in. The contract was signed by the Cardinal on November 18th and on the following Sunday the ground was formally broken at 4:15 in the afternoon in the presence of a large gathering of the parishioners. 

Day by day we awaited the arrival of the steam shovel. After what seemed an endless delay it arrived on December 13. The cement footings were laid at once before the ground, which was of the firmest brick clay, had any chance of falling in. [ 46 ]

FIRST ALTAR IN BLACKSMITH SHOP

Then came the frost and we had to wait once more.

The winter was very mild and it was possible for the stone masons to lay the heavy foundations in the early part of January. They brought them up to ground level.

The first plans provided that the auditorium should be about eighteen feet below the ground level. 

The City Hall, however, raised an objection. If this arrangement was to be followed it would be necessary to provide ramps instead of stairways for the exits; and this in turn would mean the building of a retaining wall that would have increased the price by many thousand dollars. Mr. Audsley met the difficulty by raising the whole building and providing plans for a new front elevation to harmonize with the alteration. It was a satisfactory change. 

The proximity of the neighboring houses really made it desirable to place the school-rooms as high as possible. The basement auditorium was changed into a temporary Church. This is the explanation of its character. We had previously intended to hold the services temporarily on the first school floor. It was well that this difficulty occurred, for it was not many years before we needed more space for school rooms than one floor would have provided. 

On January 24 the steel began to arrive. The building was to be completely fire-proof; even the floors were to be nailed to a new cement without the use of sleepers. Practically no wood entered into the construction, for the partitions were built later on from gypsum. Early in February the bricklayers strike was settled. Our building was the first to profit by it; indeed, it was by pursuading Mr. 

Melody to withdraw from the Masters Builders [ 47 ]

Committee that we were able to bring about this happy solution of a very serious situation. 

Throughout the month of February the brickwork progressed like magic, whilst the great steel columns and beams were being erected. I tried my hand at rivetting and was able to put in a few bolts under close supervision. I learnt then what heavy work it was to sit aloft and bear the constant vibration of the compressed air rivetter. The cement of the first floor was poured on March 16th. I remember the occasion very well. From our elevation we could see the Delaware river and there was a fresh breeze blowing. My friend, the building inspector, with whom I had once had a controversy, was watching the proceedings and he expressed his admiration for the strength of the construction.

We left a portion of the wall at the south-western corner below level so that the solemnity of the corner stone laying might take place in fair weather. 

Bishop Crane officiated on Palm Sunday. Before the ceremony the Bricklayers Union requested the pleasure of making him an honorary member of their guild to record the settlement of the strike. 

Bishop Crane was compelled to refuse this kind suggestion for reasons that are obvious. Mr. Audsley appeared in his best and superintended the laying of the beautiful stone that he had designed, a gift of the stone contractor. In it were placed various articles destined to prove the date of the event. An address was delivered by our old friend Hon. 

Michael Donohoe; Father Meagher, a boy from the parish, said the prayers. Amongst the notables were the rectors of St. Joachim and the Ascension. 

We also had with us the newly-made Monsignor Rt. [ 48 ]

Rev. Thomas F. McNally. It is interesting to note that our building is dated by the recent election of our present reigning pontiff, Pius XI, who was placed in the chair of St. Peter on February 6 of the same year.

By the end of May the brickwork was finished and in another month the roof was on. The plastering began at once. On July 2 the first mass was said in the new building on the middle floor. For one month the Sunday masses were celebrated in this inconvenient place, the floor being of unfinished cement. The plasterers completed their work in the 

Auditorium by the end of July. On August 6 mass was said here, the floor being still unfinished. 

Everything was practically in order by October 8.

Meanwhile a difficulty had arisen concerning the boiler house which occupied the rear portion of the blacksmith's shop. The general contractor, Mr.

Melody, was not able to undertake this work as had been expected. It was not part of his contract. 

Through the kindness of the heating inspector, Mr. 

Berkley Hackett, we were able to rush this job through in time to meet the approach of winter. A very deep excavation had to be made, involving the under-pinning of the Rectory and the construction of a heavy subterranean wall to carry the thrust of the School building. Unfortunately an accident occurred at this time. The sub-contractor, an elderly man, was struck by some falling earth which he had failed to shore-up. The injury was not considered serious but its neglect resulted in his death. 

The boiler-house was started on the 23 August and the job, including the erection of the stack, was finished by October 21. Then came a hurried rush [ 49 ]

to furnish heat and light needed for the mission that started on November 12. That mission was given by Father Doran assisted by Father Collins of Albany, both of the Apostolic Mission House in Washington.

The school was still unfinished. The class-rooms were not yet divided. Furnishings, however, were added to the floor used as a church. Father Doran erected the stations of the cross which came from the recently demolished house of the Christian Brothers in the parish of St. Michael. In the spring, as will be noted elsewhere, the scenery was put in place.

Bids were made for the completion of one floor of school rooms at the beginning of March and the contract was given to Royd-house Arey Co. They started work without delay early in April and everything was ready for the school opening in September.

The Sisters were delighted with the new building. 

For three years they had been working under difficulties, opening a new grade each year. The old rag-shop contained four class-rooms for the first year; five for the second when the Catholic Club found other quarters; and for the third year, by using the remains of the blacksmith-shop after the construction of the boiler-house, i. e. the front part (now demolished), six class-rooms were provided. 

The first graduating class was from the old school. 

The event was signalized by the presentation of class-pins to all the students. The members of this class have kept up their friendship, although most of them have left the parish. [ 50 ]

For several years the top floor of the school remained unfinished. In 1927 a contract was awarded to Dennis O'Dea for the completion. 

Whilst the work was going on the auditorium was decorated by Kase and Son of Reading. It was thus that the long task of building the school was over within eight years of the founding of the parish. 

The total cost was considerably less than $200,000 -a very reasonable figure for a fireproof building of its size erected when materials were at high costand erected sectionally. Owing to the hard work of our people the parish debt was never more than a little over half of this amount. We were ready to begin the work of the new convent and the Rectory the next year.

I must now say something of our School children. 

We opened with an attendance of 182 in the old building. The numbers gradually increased during the first three years. When the new building was opened in 1923 we had an attendance of nearly 400 children. We now had the eight grades divided into six classrooms. Next year it was necessary to use the committee room in order to make a better division of the children although the numbers remained at 385. In 1925 the numbers increased, seventy-five new pupils were registered at the commencement of school. One floor of classrooms was found to be inadequate. In 1926 the numbers had reached 515. Before the end of the year we were at our wits' end to find accommodation for them in the seven rooms available. There was only one thing to do unless we wished to take some children back to the old school. We must complete the top floor. We were enabled to take the last alternative [ 51 ]

by a successful drive and when the school opened in the fall of 1927 we had ten rooms available without the Committee room. This still left us half the top floor as a Recreation Hall.

It was in the fall of 1927 that a uniform was adopted for the children. It was a valuable arrangement which had to be modified to some extent during the years of depression. It is to be hoped that it will once more become the rule. It distinguishes our children and assists school discipline. 

Moreover by the purchase of a large number of costumes a saving of money is made for the parents. 

The summer costume of light blue, the color of St. 

Joan of Arc, was much admired.

At commencement in the summer of 1929 the numbers of the children had risen to 563 with nine rooms in use. This was due to the enlargement of the parish. In 1930 the enrollment increased again to 583. The larger first grades now began to tell and in 1932 our numbers were 627 with ten rooms in use.

This was the largest enrollment. From then the numbers have been falling off until we reached the low mark of 550 at the beginning of 1937.

A very large proportion of our children go to Catholic High School after they have graduated from the eighth grade which constitutes the entrance examination. The numbers have been increasing steadily for several causes. The law now compels children to remain in school until they are sixteen. 

Before this age they can obtain partial employment if they have passed the sixth grade. The depression has made such employment difficult to obtain and it has become the custom to allow everyone to go to High School who completes the parochial course. [ 52 ]

Of the pupils at High School many are only wasting their time. About twenty per cent of the boys graduate from High School, the others leave during the course. The average for the girls is somewhat better. During the past few years we have had about one hundred children at Catholic High Schools, the parish paying their fees, a few of which, very few, are returned by the parents. [ 53 ]

XII 

OUR RECTORIES 

I HAVE said something of the First Rectory in the 

Chinese Laundry at 3403 Frankford Avenue. It was only in use for seven months. Some memories linger. The first Christmas was a happy one. Confessions were heard in the kitchen until late, then came the task of decorating. A small crib was arranged on a table near the altar and room was found somehow for Christmas trees. It is wonderful to say that the Collection amounted to about $1000. The Easter Collection was even larger. 

Where did the money come from? It was brought by at least one member of each family for it can readily be seen that few families failed to contribute. 

On the floor above the chapel there was a large room which served at dining, sitting and writing room. Here all the parish meetings were held. Individual callers were received in the unused kitchen at the back of the altar. On this middle floor there was also a sleeping room for the Rector and a temporary kitchen. The furniture was meagre. There was no space for more than what was absolutely necessary. When large meetings were held, chairs were borrowed from an undertaker, to add to our six dozen folding ones. [ 59 ]

Our gas was supplied by a quarter-meter and we were sometimes left in darkness by a failure to supply the necessary coin. This meter was almost buried in a pile of coal which was far larger than was needed and of a size often too big for the furnace. One had to climb over the coal to reach the meter. We had to learn everything. The furnace was a perpetual trial. It was always going out. 

When we moved to the new Rectory we felt that we have risen in the world. It seemed enormous. 

It had twelve rooms and two bath rooms! It also had a large colony of rats which supplied lots of sport for the parish cat. Each priest now had two rooms. After the school was built in 1922, what had been the "L" of the chapel became what we called the Radio Room. The Radio had just arrived. 

Night after night the young men who were interested in the new sport of dial twisting came to build radio sets. We have a huge assortment of cats' whiskers, condensers, variometers, coils, head-sets, batteries and what not, which were hooked up in endless ways. The night that we were able to get faint noises from Cuba was memorable.

In the same room we counted the money on Sundays and sorted the envelopes. Here we arranged moon-lights, picnics, and entertainments. Many of my own hopes and disappointments are closely associated with the memory of that Radio Room. It seemed to be the ante-chamber of a new world. Here we followed the famous Democratic Convention of 1924 with its momentous results-the growth of a new bigotry that was to deprive America of a great President. It was in this room that there came a [ 60 ]

realization of the birth of a new age filled with dangers and also high possibilities.

We lived for nine years in this Rectory and by doing so saved money to build the new one which has never added anything to our debt. It was not a dignified residence and some of the parishioners were not slow to say so, but it served its purpose. 

My days in it were happy ones and I spent more there than in any other place in which I have lived. 

Despite its size it was large enough to accomodate the priests who came to us on the occasion of the Forty Hours although we had to sit close. It was also large enough for the entertainment of the parish Collectors who used to meet once a year to eat sauerkraut and doggies and listen to songs by

John Ettner and others. It was with regret that I saw its last bricks hurled apart by the wreckers and its site levelled for ever.

With the third Rectory we ascended another rung of the social ladder. We lived for the first time in "the big house." The planning of the Rectory was careful. After the death of Mr. Audsley I made the acquaintance of a young architect named 

William Burke. His home was in Pittsburgh. He was associated with a local firm of architects to which he introduced me when we needed supervision of the last work on the School. Mr. Audsley did not live to see the completion of his plan. He died very suddenly in 1925. A few hours before he had written the last page of a monumental work on organ building. The last time I saw him was on 

October 22, 1924. He looked hale then although he was past his 86th birthday. Mr. Burke accepted his plans for the school and carried them out perfectly. [ 61 ]

It was during this summer of 1927 that we talked over the Rectory.

The Rectory was built exactly to my wishes. It is so arranged that it will some day be connected with the Church and in the same style of architecture the Italian romanesque an inexpensive and adaptable style. I insisted on a garden wall much to Mr. Burke's amusement. He met me in every point and after eight years of residence in the new house I find that there is little to criticize. The diocesan Building Committee passed the plans on June 

15, 1928 and bids were opened on November 7 the day after the disastrous defeat of Al Smith. The contract was signed by the Cardinal on November 

20 with William Dougherty the contractor. On 

Sunday December 2 we broke ground at 4:00 P. M. using the same spade that had served the same purpose when the School was started. 

Mr. Duffin arrived with his steam shovel on the next morning, December 3, and finished his job in two days. One of the first things that was done was to pull down the old stable that had stood for so many years against the wall of 3555 Joyce Street.

In that stable there had been a horse whose tenancy had brought us a small monthly income. In the same stable our rectory cat "Bill" passed many a sleepful hour beside his large friend. The destruction of the stable was the beginning of a campaign of housewrecking that demolished three houses on Joyce Street in 1933 and all the buildings to the south of the School on Frankford Avenue in 1934. 

The completion of the Rectory still left us cut off from the school by our inability to purchase 3550 Frankford Avenue. We were compelled to use [ 62 ]

Joyce Street as a means of reaching our sacred altar. It was in 1934 during the novena of the 

Sacred Heart that the end of this inconvenience came. For many years, indeed, since the beginning of our holding this Novena I had been in the habit of asking the children to pray for my secret intention. This intention I did not even dare to whisper to the reeds. I even forgot the intention myself on the memorable morning of the Novena when I had a hunch that was to lead to its fulfillment. The intention was that I might be able to purchase the obstructive property at a reasonable price. The hunch turned out to be right. The property was bought for the sum of $2400. I was able to tell the children all about it before the Novena closed. No one but myself appreciated the serious situation that we had been in. Our properties were cut in half, and what, I think, no one else realized, our valuable central heating plant would have been useless for the church. 

The previous owner of this property has recently died. He was always a pleasant neighbor. I wonder if he ever realized my own anxieties in regard to him, or perhaps I should say to his legal heir. It was once in his power to have ruined the lay-out of our parochial buildings unless we were willing to pay a high toll.

In our new Rectory we have surrounded ourselves with flower gardens. We are waiting for the happy day when we shall be able to break ground for the Church to be built in honor of our blessed Saintthe Maid of France. How long shall we have to wait? Will it be my happiness to see that building rise and to say my Nunc Dimittis. [ 63 ]

XVI THE HOUSEHOLD STAFF 

ONE of the problems of a priest's house is the housekeeping staff. In this matter we have been relieved of anxiety. Miss Alice Keller came to take charge of the Rectory when the parish was founded and she has been here ever since. She came as a favor leaving her business as a dressmaker. She did not know what was ahead of her or she might never have come. The first year she did all the work because there were no accomodations for other help. To most people the Rectory beside the Blacksmith shop would have been a trial. It was quite unfiitted for its purpose. Yet, strangely enough, it was this Rectory that she still looks back to with pleasant remembrances. It was small, stuffy and in the last stages of senile decay—yet no one can say one word against it in her presence-nor against the old chapel either.

The housekeeper is strong for liturgical reform and reads her missal with great exactitude. She is rarely absent from daily mass, although she is very critical of almost any kind of piety. It was one of the conditions under which she came that she should have charge of the altar, a work that she had done for some years at St. Edward's Church, where I [ 79 ]

first met her. She is scrupulous in her search for dirt and disorder, and woe betide the offender. She has made all our altar breads since the start. She is an expert needlewoman and delights to provide the church with linens and other necessities. 

Locally she is known for her interest in the prevention of cruelty to animals. We have had a succession of famous cats most of which perished under the wheels of trucks. One of these cats was the actor in a moving picture film; his memory is ever green. She knows every cat and dog for squares around and a vast collection of sparrows have made their home with us, knowing that they will never starve in the winter. [ 80 ]

XVII OUR DEVOTIONS 

THE first object of our devotional life was to emphasize the importance of the Blessed Sacrament. 

The Holy Hour was therefore started at once on 

Friday, November 28, 1919, in the chapel in the store. 

Except for a few nights when the blacksmith's shop was intolerably hot, and during the time of Missions, the Holy Hour has been observed weekly on Fridays without any break. The attendance is often disappointing; an hour seems a long time to many people; yet the devotion is a very sincere one, and a very unselfish one, and the faithful few would not miss it for anything.

Beginning with the annual Calendar I will give a list of the devotions that have become established. 

In January we have always kept the Church Unity Octave from the 21st to the 28th. This devotion began in the Anglican Church through the influence of the Greymoor Friars who afterwards became Catholics. Knowing Father Paul when he was an 

Episcopalian it was natural that I should wish to make this Octave a parish observance especially since it has been indulgenced by the Holy See. 

Someday it may be placed on the same footing as the 

Month of May. [ 81 ]

The Novena of Grace began in 1928; previously we had observed the Novena to St. Joseph about the same time. Ten Jesuits priests have conducted the devotion, all of them had spent some time in the Philippine Islands, and they were therefore able to give us the advantage of their experience in the field. 

Some of them are still in that far off mission. It was my pleasure to meet several at the time of the Manila Congress. Their names are still remembered. They run in this order: Fathers Gallagher, Pollock, O'Connell, Downey, Doucette, Kirchgessner, Morning, Murray, Delany and Welch. This

Novena is always well attended. It is necessary to have two sermons, one at five thirty and the other at eight. 

It goes without saying that we observe the Month of May, the Novena of the Holy Ghost and the Month of the Rosary. These are ordered by the Holy See.

It has been customary to have a short address at the 8.15 mass each day at which the school children are requested to be present. In the evening there is 

Benediction at 7:45. 

The Forty Hours was held, at first, on the last

Sunday of May. As this conflicted with our annual military Mass it now takes place on the first Sunday of June. It was placed at this rather inconvenient time because it is near the feast of our patron saint.

In the early days of the parish a special preacher was engaged; the attendance was so small that this had to be changed. The Forty Hours opens with a procession after the last Mass at which the children of the school accompany the Blessed Sacrament. It closes on Tuesday night with a procession of our men. This is one of our earliest traditions dating [ 82 ]

from 1921. It was copied from the practice of St. 

Edward's Church.

The Novena to the Sacred Heart begins each year on the feast of Corpus Christi and ends with a reception of promoters of the Apostleship of Prayer on the feast of the Sacred Heart. These promoters each have districts. They distribute the monthly leaflets and Messengers of the Sacred Heart, a magazine that should find a place in every Catholic home.

Every two years a Parish Mission is held either in October or November. The first two Missions 

1920 and 1922 were conducted by Father Doran. In 

1924 the Redemptorists came. Fathers Conway and

Knecht are still remembered. The pledges that the men took to go to Holy Communion on the second Sunday of every month were kept faithfully until the next Mission. In 1926 we had a Passionist 

Mission; in 1928 one by the Jesuits; in 1930 Father 

Peter Quinn, a Marist whose home was in St.

Edward's parish, and who is well-known in Philadelphia, was assisted by two of his confrères. In 

1932 the Paulists came; in 1934 the Augustinians; and again in 1936 we returned to the Redemptorists and were happy to have with us the famous Father Turner. 

A Novena in honor of the Immaculate Conception intended to increase the Sodality and to be a preparation for Christmas is held before the feast of Our Lady on December 8th. It is always well attended. 

There are our seasonable devotions. During the past few years we have had devotions every Sunday night at 8:00 in honor of the Miraculous Medal. 

The start was made on Sunday evening December 9, [ 83 ]

when Father Fitzgerald, C.M. preached. So great was the crowd that for sometime it was necessary to arrange for a second service at 5:15 on 

Sunday afternoons. Unfortunately services of this kind have a temporary appeal for most Catholics. 

The novelty wears off. The Novena has a permanent value, however, since it offers a short service on Sunday evenings for those who wish to attend the 

Benediction of the Blessed Sacrament. During the worst days of the depression a collection was taken up for those who needed help. The Vincentian 

Fathers who promote the devotion have been very generous to the poor.

In addition to the above special services we have some other devotional traditions. On the so-called "Mother's Day" we sell carnations for the benefit of the C.Y.M.A. at all the masses. The occasion is offered for a blessing of babies in the afternoon of the same day. We must not forget the blessing of candles at the feast of the Purification at which we hope someday to have the proper procession. The throats of multitudes are blessed on the feast of St. 

Blaise. We impose the ashes at the beginning of 

Lent and bear our palms on Palm Sunday. The children annoy our good neighbors by selling Easter 

Eggs and by going through the streets shouting "upper". We do not forget Thanksgiving Day, and we cheerfully put up with the bombardment that takes place before and after the 4th of July. The sane and safe celebration does not seem to have reached us yet. On All Souls Day practically everyone remembers the dead at the altar of God. There is a Catholic atmosphere in our district that has swept in from the neighboring parishes. [ 84 ]

In recent years the devotion to Christ the King has been promulgated by the Holy See. A world wide consecration to Our Lord was ordered on New Year's Eve 1925-6. We observed the occasion with a crowded Church. Since that date a feast has been established on the last Sunday of October, when a similar consecration is commanded. This devotion brings to our mind the great struggle which is going on in so many countries between the forces of atheism and the Church. At the same time the prayers after Mass were ordered to be said for the faithful in Russia.

On Christmas 1933 in order to mark the anniversary of the crucifixion of Our Lord a midnight Mass was permitted. Our church was crowded to capacity. A special Jubilee year was also announced. 

The indulgences to be obtained the first year by a pilgrimage to Rome were extended to the world until the spring of 1935. The parish made the jubilee in a body to the Shrine of St. Michael at Torresdale on 

April 7 of that year. Like all other parishes we conformed to the orders of the Pope to open the Holy Year with a universal Holy Hour on the 

Thursday of Passion Week 1933.

XVIII 

OUR PARISH THEATRE 

BEFORE the days of the Moving Pictures almost every parish had its Dramatic Society. Ours was no exception. We have had a series of Societies called by different names which all had a common origin and a connected history, for they all depended upon the initiative of one man-Robert Hepburn. There has never been a parish play in which he has not been an actor, a stagemanager, or a promoter, nor is it possible to think of having one without his assistance. This was not only due to his efficiency and enthusiasm but also to his loyalty to the ideals of the clergy. It has been his constant aim to avoid anything that was not consistant with parochial dignity. It would be difficult to give a list of all the plays that have been staged. One or two were given in other parishes before we possessed a stage; but when the stage did come in the spring of 1923 the first performance on April 3 was of a quality that we have never surpassed and rarely equalled. The play was Mr. Cohan's "Fifty Miles from Boston", the author lending us his MS. Other plays followed during the next year: "Clarence " and "Mary". A new note was struck by Father Kohl in 1925. Under his direction an amateur production "The Minia- [ 87 ]

ture Lady" and two of his own compositions "Sunbonnet Sue" and "The Sweet Shoppe" brought the history of our drama down to the spring of 1926. 

These last three plays introduced ballet dances which were executed with great skill and refinement. 

He was assisted very loyally by Mrs. Martin 

McDonald as pianist After this the drama was of the comedy or problem type and singing and dancing were dropped; they were too difficult to stage. 

In recent years we have had few plays; there is no support given to them. The spectacular and exciting character of the Motion Picture Drama has practically destroyed all interest in the old parish productions which were often the training schools for actors of the highest grade. Perhaps things will change again. Nevertheless one type of drama has continued to persevere and this has been kept for the closing word. The late Monsignor Benson did a great deal to create interest in the religious play. 

Some of his plays were produced in America especially his "Upper Room" and "The Nativity".

When I was a curate at St. Edward's Church, before the war, the parish produced these two plays with great success. For some reason they did not revive after the war, although other parishes continued the tradition. It was natural that I should want to produce them at St. Joan of Arc. In the spring of 

1924 the "Upper Room" was put on for the first time during the Lenten season. We were fortunate to have the help of several of those who had taken part in the earlier performances at St. Edwards' and especially the assistance of Mr. William C. 

Hayes who acted as stage manager; with him our own people worked loyally. Of those who took part [ 88 ]

in that first performance two are still in the present cast, for the play has run almost every Lent since then, and is now in its eleventh season. Indeed it has become, as it was my wish, a parish tradition. 

Nothing has been varied. We have the same makeup man; the scenery, dresses and the stage effects are as they were from the first. In the course of the years we have developed leading personalities. The part of "Samuel" has been continuously taken by John McLoughlin, and that of "Magdalene" by Gertrude Cooper, now Mrs. Flood.

We have had several Maries, Johns, &c. &c. Only once did we attempt to stage the Nativity play. It was a most beautiful performance but it was found to be impracticable. You cannot get people to attend rehearsals just before Christmas and you must give the play at the very time of the Nativity or it will fall flat. On two occasions we played "Everyman" in place of the "Upper Room." But it was a little too difficult for the leading man, and was not so appealing as the "Upper Room." We had the high honor of presenting the play to his 

Eminence in 1936.

We have tried moving pictures, and minstrels but they always tend to vulgarity. The censorship and tastes differ.

During Lent 1933 Hilary C. Pepler, an associate of Belloc and Chesterton, stayed with us and taught our boys how to mime the Stations of the Cross. 

This led to a more ambitious endeavor, the Mime of St. Joan of Arc, which was produced under Mr. 

Pepler's direction, with the aid of Edward Mann of our parish, on May 29. A distinguished audience, interested in this form of drama, was present. [ 89 ]

XIX 

OTHER SOCIAL ACTIVITIES 

DURING the eighteen years of our existence we have tried every plan for bringing people together with the further object of meeting our financial obligations. Apart from plays, concerts, reviews, minstrels and movies there have been many other social activities.

We have had several large parish dinners. The first was given on February 10th as the opening of a drive to complete the school. On that occasion— 

1927-about 500 men, who knew beforehand the purpose of the affair, assembled in the auditorium. 

Father John P. Greene of the Precious Blood Church was the chairman on that occasion. The drive brought about $20,000. This was the actual amount paid. A few pledges which were not met would have brought the total sum to several hundred dollars more.

We had a dinner on the occasion of the martyrdom anniversary of our patron Saint on May 25, 1931. 

Then Mr. Clare Fenerty spoke. 

On May 27, the occasion of the Rector's jubilee, the chief speaker was Rev. John Ewens, C.M.

There have been a number of drawings. One stands out in my mind. It had a rather unexpected [ 91 ]

ending. We drew for a horse, a pig and a gold coin. 

The horse which had been represented as a magnificent animal worth several hundred dollars was won by a non-Catholic. He was much pleased at his good fortune and promised to pay ten per cent of its value as a donation to the Church. He brought me $1.00. After seeing the horse he sold it for $10.

I was perfectly innocent of what was considered to be a fine joke. I am glad to say that the winner was willing to regard it in that light also. The pig was won by a boy. Delay in the drawing had caused the pig to reach very large dimensions. The boy did not know what to do with it and he too sold it for $10. The coin was a genuine $5.00 piece. 

We had a great clock contest. To make it completely successful we had to sell something like 

86,400 chances at 10 cents a chance. Every hour of the day and night was covered by seconds. Two watches were sealed up and allowed to stop naturally. This drawing took a long time to complete. 

It was finished at last and the night of the drawing arrived. The prizes were two automobiles. One was won by a local manufacturer who had purchased a few tickets; the other by a man whose name had been put on a ticket by his sister without his knowledge. We hope that he was grateful for this.

We have had block parties innumerable. The largest have been given by groups at Joyce Street, Albright Street, and Wheatsheaf Lane. There was for several years a keen rivalry between these groups. We are not going to say which won. We do not forget the other parties, many of them arranged by one or two people. It is found that no [ 92 ]

matter where the party is held there are always generous people who help.

The great annual carnivals went on for years. 

They were held in August and frequently lasted over two weeks. The first three or four were never surpassed. Before the school was built, i. e. in the summers of 1920 and 1921 its vacant site was perfect for the purpose. These two carnivals were our largest. We made about $8,000 at each of them. 

They were attended by vast crowds. The tables that made the most money were those which chanced off hams and groceries. In those days there was plenty of money about and it was spent liberally. 

Our non-Catholic neighbors were amongst our best friends. I have often wondered at their willingness to put up with the noise. A few were rather scandalized.

One night a Minister engaged Dr. Carr in a conversation on the subject. "How can you teach your people to gamble?" he objected. objected. "Gamble," answered Dr. Carr, "this is not gambling. When you gamble you have a chance; here you have none."

These carnivals have defenders and objectors. It is safe to say that all who spent money on the lot had come to make a contribution to the Church, but they wished to have as much fun out of their contribution as possible.

After 1921 the Carnival was removed to the lot at the corner of Atlantic Street around the old school. It did fairly well for a few years but it has ceased to be an attraction and street carnivals have taken its place. No one can recall our carnivals without imagining that the voice of Frank [ 93 ]

Plick is deafening their ears with the cry of "Ten cents a chance.”

Another social event is the annual card party, held the first year at Moose Hall, on February 12, 1920. 

Unfortunately we had quite misjudged its size. We had only engaged one floor of the building. Of those who attended only about half ever reached the tables. They spent the evening trying to put their wraps into the cloak room and get them out again. 

The crush was enormous. The receipts after paying all expenses amounted to $2400. After then we engaged both floors of Mercantile Hall and with one exception this annual event has since been held there. The one exception was a trial of our own 

Hall. It fell flat and we returned to Mercantile. 

The attendance has dropped steadily. Card playing is not as popular as it was. It is impossible to omit a reference to the prizes. We have always had magnificent prizes. This is due to our women who work enthusiastically under the direction of Mrs. 

John J. Ratigan. Mrs. Ratigan's ability to organize our workers, and her own untiring activity, are matters of parish history. 

We have tried junk sales, radio games, and blanket clubs, with varying success. No matter what we undertake we always have a splendid body of workers. 

We must not forget our annual Christmas Party which is arranged for our children. No admission is charged, The hosts are the High School pupilsand there are tableaux and playlets, a Christmas 

Tree-at which John Koller is Santa Claus-candy and small gifts. [ 94 ]

Amongst the many who have never failed to assist in our social affairs some have died-Mary Rigley, Patrick Rush and Jeannie Daley; some have left the parish-Victor Eshbach, Mrs. William Keane and

Mrs. Frank Hefferman, and some have been prevented in later years from doing all the work that they once did-Mrs. James Mallon, Mrs. James A. 

Murphy and Mrs. William Hornung-of the many others still active mention is made elsewhere of some of them. [ 95 ]

John Koller. Of all who have given regular and continuous service the name of Charles Mallon is outstanding.

Here mention should be made of an organization recently started-the Catholic Girl Scouts. There are two bands under the direction of Kathleen Taylor, who is also a diocesan officer, and Mary McGrath.

We have a band of men known as the Collectors. 

In the early days of the parish they did actually collect a weekly offering from house to house. This arrangement ceased when the duplex envelopes were introduced on the first Sunday of November 1923. 

It was never satisfactory. Some Collectors were devoted; others had good intentions. It was also annoying to the people. The envelopes increased the collection very greatly. Their purpose is the same as the house to house Collection-to give contributors credit for their donations. Some argue that such contributions should not be published. I respect this feeling. It is the ideal. Nevertheless, the payment of a definite sum to religion is not an act of charity but an act of strict justice. We can do our charity privately; we ought to perform our acts of justice openly.

The Collectors remained with us after they had ceased to collect. They now distribute the Monthly 

Calendars and they keep a record of arrivals and departures. These Monthly Calendars were first published in March 1920. They cost the parish nothing for the advertisers provide the means to cover printing and editing. On the other hand they bring no revenue to us. The margin of profit is often very small, especially in times of depression [ 101 ]

such as we have twice experienced. The more advertisements the publishers are able to secure the larger is the monthly number. We are under obligations to those who advertise for it would be costly to replace the Calendar by a privately printed sheet such as was often done in former days. 

In 1920 we started the Christmas Saving Fund. 

At first it was an activity of the Catholic Club. It continued to be such for many years. The amount distributed at Christmas reached on one occasion as much as $15,000. When the depression arrived it was impossible to pay any interest on deposits and the Fund was placed in the hands of the Beneficial 

Saving Fund Society in 1930 where it has since remained. It was difficult to make people understand that the Church could not derive any income from such an arrangement even with voluntary help. 

We simply handed on to the members the interest paid by the Saving Fund in which the money was deposited.

Here it may be mentioned that a Building and Loan Society which adopted the name of the Parish never had at any time any connection with it. A proposal on our side to pay the expenses of a change of name was not accepted. It is contrary to the laws of the diocese to call purely social or financial organizations by the name of the Church. XXI OUR CHURCH MUSIC 

FROM the beginning of the parish it has been our constant effort to conform to the regulations of church music laid down by the Holy See. We had efficient help from our Sisters. Mother James, the

Superior of the Sisters Servants of the Immaculate Heart of Mary when our parish was created, established a musical supervision of the parochial schools where this Order provided the teachers. From time to time Sister M. Mercedes visited the various schools and gave instructions in liturgical chant. 

This accomplished teacher afterwards came to us both as a music teacher and later as superior of our convent. She laid the foundations of a tradition that has survived. Our children sing the music of the mass very successfully. For a short time they provided the singing at funerals; this had to be abandoned because it interfered with the routine of the school. The custom has grown up of singing the mass every Wednesday morning at 8:15 when all the pupils are expected to attend. At Christmas, during Holy Week, and at the Forty Hours, the children provide most of the singing. At the military Mass in honor of St. Joan of Arc they sing the liturgy of the day with all the propers. They have [ 103 ]

won high praise from those who understand the principles of church music.

On Sundays at High Mass the music is now provided by a boys' choir under the direction of Cecilia Heaney. This choir has replaced, for sometime, a previous men's choir organized by Miss Margaret Mary Marshall. At Christmas and on special occasions this choir is re-enforced by instrumental music under the direction of Edward Griffin, who also helps us with the production of the "Upper Room" which needs incidental and interact music.

For funerals the music is supplied by men's voices provided and accompanied by Miss Marshall. We have always had strictly liturgical music at this function in strong contrast with the sentimental trash which has for many years characterized the funeral music in most of our Philadelphia churches.

We have had the greatest co-operation from Miss Heaney and Miss Marshall, who have both been associated with me from the beginning of the parish. 

This co-operation has relieved me of that constant source of annoyance in so many parishes, the lack of sympathy between the clergy and the choir. 

For our devotional services Cecilia Heaney has given loyal and constant assistance. She has worked under great handicaps. We have never had a pipe organ to give her scope for her talents; and she has been content to serve our interests for the meagre remuneration provided by such church weddings as she is able to attend. 

We hope some day to have a pipe organ, or what may be a possible substitute-an electric organ. 

The Choir has always been voluntary. It is difficult to get a voluntary choir that is willing to sing [104]

liturgical music. The popular taste inclines towards the sentimental. It has been a problem to discourage the very natural desire to sing songs in church which are entirely out of place. Slovenly customs have grown up in Philadelphia churches which it is hard to change. We have set our faces against solo singing and what are ridiculously called "offertories." On the whole our people have been very loyal to what, perhaps, they regard as my own stupid tastes. It is not to be expected that they would accept the rulings of the church which are so widely scouted by prevailing practice. The influence of our schools, however, is being gradually felt. The next generation will have a better appreciation of the glorious wealth of traditional music which is now being given to the Protestants as a gift, whilst we have been copying their worst productions. 

Most hymns in general use are atrocious. There is no excuse for this. The hymnology of the Church is immense, but, alas, only known to musical experts.

In place of it cheap airs and worse poetry has had popular approval for generations. The change will come but it will be gradual. 

In mentioning the choir it is only right to say something of our altar boys. We have excellent altar boys. Every stranger notices this. They are reverent and they are polite. We never have any trouble with them. They provide their own cassocks and surplices, and this insures proper care being taken of them. It is the rarest thing for any boy to come to church with a torn cassock or a dirty surplice.

I made a rule from the start. The boys are expected to leave the altar when they leave school, [ 105 ]

unless they have signified their desire to study for the priesthood. There are two good reasons for this.

The boys are taking the place at the altar of the lower orders of the ministry. They are honorary acolytes, and the acolytes are next in the hierarchy to the sub-deacons. The Church allows them to act as acolytes by reason of their youth and their freedom from evil habits. It would be a great scandal to the faithful if it were known that a boy who serves at mass was not innocent in his private life. The risk of this happening is lessened if the boys are still in the school under the supervision of priests and sisters. 

There is another reason. An altar boy must leave the altar at some time. As he grows larger he becomes out of place in the sanctuary with boys of lesser stature. It is difficult to tell a boy to leave. 

It is better that he should leave by reason of some rule, and the best rule is that he should leave the school and the altar at the same time. On the other hand a boy who seems to have a vocation is in a different category. He should leave when his vocation has been proved to be imaginary. [ 106 ]

XXIII 

THE LAST CHAPTER 

I am afraid that this book is going to be a disappointment to many of the parishioners. Their memories and mine are not the same; their valuations may not always agree with mine. I have striven to satisfy myself with the belief that anyone who tries to please everyone usually pleases no one.

I have asked for help in this compilation a number of times. If my critics did not respond they must blame themselves.

As I come to the last chapter I realize that I have left many things unmentioned. I have not, for example, said one word about the Seminary Solicitors who have remained almost the same in personnel during all these years. They have kept the rules of the diocese by calling from door to door and making their requests from everyone. They all help the parish in many other ways, but this is one of the best ways. All the various Collectors will be listed hereafter. Remember, however, I am not writing a history of the parishioners, but a history of the parish.

Someone told me that I was expected to give lists of school graduates. Here I draw a line. No one wishes to be dated. Amongst our girl graduates there may be some who would not thank me for giv- [ 109 ]

ing their ages, so I refrain. I am taking a risk in publishing the baptismal register but this can be done fairly safely; hardly anyone objects to be just eighteen or less.

I add some memories. Why they survive whilst others have flitted it is hard to say. They are not in chronological order.

One day an unknown benefactor sent a picture of the Little Flower before she was canonized. It was the present of a Sister who requested that the representations of the two saintly French girls be placed side by side. We hung them at the door of the old blacksmith's shop, and later on placed statues of them in the Church. It is said that they assisted one another in their processes of canonization. Our parish was probably the first in Philadelphia to recognize the popular devotion that was soon to spread far and wide. I received from Rome, through the kindness of a priest, a relic of her sacred bones, taken from the cemetery at Lisieux where she lay buried for twenty-five years.

Despite the rigors of the Philadelphia climate we have had a number of octogenarians. I can still see 

Susan McGurk as gay and active as a girl; Sarah

Devlin walking to mass at the rate of four miles an hour; and other aged friends who preserved their youthful interest in life: Mary Hughes, the mother of Harry Hughes; Catherine Murray "Aunt Kate" a gentlewoman who had seen better days; Margaret 

Mulherin, who was always hoping to be able to see the new Church; John Coleman, who died somewhere under a hundred; Henry Medernach, who liked to talk French with me; Margaret Boyle, whose family came from the Isle of Man; Sarah McKeever, [ 110 ]

from the best in old St. Michael's; James and Rose

Meagher, the parents of Rev. Francis Meagher, who opened their house to me when I needed a roof; 

Catherine McNamara, a remarkable personage, who lived as an invalid almost the whole time of our existence as a parish; and Margaret Bennett, another shut-in, who remembered seeing Cardinal 

Manning when she was a girl.

I remember especially those who came to Mass every morning: Harry Gindele, who, watch in hand, contributed to the punctuality of the clergy; John and Ellen Sherry, old St. Michael people, and strong in their attachment to that celebrated Church; 

Eleanor Heaney, often with her little niece; and

Emily Sullivan, mother and daughter of the same name, whose piety was as refined as it was sincere; these are only a few of the faithful who love to assist at the holy sacrifice.

The most poignant recollections are of death-beds. 

Thank God I cannot remember a single instance where those who sent for a priest were denied this privilege through our neglect. I recall the sad night just before Christmas when I realized that John J.

Ratigan was dying. He, and all his family, had given me my first welcome to the parish and they have been loyal to this day. I remember the terrible sufferings of Thomas Johnston and the long illness of Florence Faunce. One day I was summoned to give the last sacraments to Alfred Marshall. He had just returned from the South where he had been persecuted because his wife was a Catholic. He came back to die and wished to die in the faith of his family. He was one of many sincere convertsseveral of them received on their death beds. Some [ 111 ]

of these conversions have never been made publicsuch things are commonplace in a priest's life. 

We have lost some young people. Of school girls: Louise Eschbach, Veronica Hetherington, Sarah Corrie, Jane Keough, Rita Lynch, Catherine 

Baubunas, Dorothy Glowka and Rita Zachoraski, Gladys McDevitt was killed on our dangerous streets; so were Robert Hays and Joseph McNulty. 

One boy, John Myer was killed by a base-ball; two were drowned: Francis Cook and David Kane. A sad loss was William Tiernan, a pupil of the North

East High School; another was Cornelius Crosson, a candidate for the Naval School at Annapolis. 

I shall never forget the morning when Norah 

Morgan, thinking that a priest had been insulted, returned her Friday fish, with some precipitancy, to the surprised salesman. The incident had an amusing beginning and no one was more astonished than the priest. Then there was John Finney, whose strong voice led the short-lived men's choir. 

Another character was Michael Gallagher, the fireman, who had sailed the seven seas and retired to take devoted care of his sister. I have pleasant remembrances of Mrs. Baker, and her convert husband. At one time she was afraid of my having a revolver (a pure imagination) with which to shoot trespassers on the school lot; in those days she lived near us. Michael Doyle, the policeman, was a stirling character who gave you confidence. You felt that if all the force were like him there would be no danger to life and limb. One of our school windows reminds us of Barbara Snyder and of her brotherin-law, Frank Kech, old residents of Harrogate. 

Another memory brings back John Baptist [ 112 ]

Louvet, who worked as a boy in a glass works in Belgium which our army passed on its way to the Rhine. Strange to say he had never made his first 

Communion. It was my joy to bestow this sacrament, and to see him at every Holy Hour with his family. He gave me a beautiful cut-glass decanter made in the celebrated glass works of St. Vallery. 

David Deegan, was another of my early friends who has passed from us. He was a very militant Catholic as became a policeman. Then there was the cockney, Michael Harrington, whose accent was like the strains of "Home Sweet Home "; his goodhearted wife and two boys are still with us, but he was called to rest suddenly. 

I have spoken of those who are dead. What shall

I say of the living? The parish is small enough for the priests to know everyone, and to know them very well. During the early years I think that there was not a soul whom I did not know. Many of these are still here. Their names cannot all be mentioned. 

If I should trust myself to make a selection I should be doing a dangerous thing. Of those who have left the parish there are the kindest remembrances of 

Mary Myers who gave us our handsome altar hangings. There is Magistrate Dougherty who must have found it difficult to put up with me, and probably forgave me for being born in the wrong country because his girls were popular in our school. Then there were the Keanes, especially Mrs. Keane, who specialized in apples on a stick. There was the good-hearted Mrs. Strecker, who would have laid down her life for us. Helen Seitter was another good friend who comes back to see us sometimes.

The interest that a number of our girls take in the [ 113 ]

week-end retreats is due to the influence of Gertrude Hellstab; and we miss the refined presence of Mrs. 

Walter Taylor.

In the days when radio was a novelty one of the fans who spent his time constructing and pulling apart various sets was William Dougherty, one of the most faithful members of the Catholic Club. At that time John Doughten, now a lawyer, was one of the few amateur broadcasters; later Joseph C. Bailey,

Jnr., became interested in astronomy and made his own thirteen inch reflector, grinding the mirror himself. It was the total eclipse of the sun in 1925 that made a number of amateur astronomers, and I myself purchased a second-hand telescope which supplied us with a certain amount of diversion until the electric lights came to Frankford Avenue and made star-gazing difficult.

One of the hardest things to get used to is the speed with which our children grow up. They seem to be in school one week and married the next. For a long time Gloria Schaeffer seemed to be the exception. For a good many years she was able to squeeze her feet into her wooden sabots, and, in Alsatian peasant costume to, present the French consul's wife, or mother, with a bouquet at our annual military mass; but she, too, has outgrown her clothes and will doubtless be grown up before it seems possible.

This chapter is already too long. One last word about those whose names may not be in this book. 

They have too many household duties to be church workers, or they may be retiring and shrink from public notice. I can think of dozens of them who do their Christian duties faithfully. History is made [ 114 ]

up of incidents not of routine. We read of battles, revolutions, famine and disaster. Time seems to be marked by such events. Even a history of a parish can never tell of things that count the most in the sight of God, and are often known to Him alone. 

To those who do their part without expectation of reward I especially dedicate these last pages. [ 115 ]

APPENDIX A A LIST OF OUR PARISH PROPERTIES I. IMMOVABLE PROPERTY 1. Combination School and Church Building 

A three-story structure of fire-resisting construction with brick outer walls and concrete floor slabs supported by skeleton steel framework and slag roof. To the rear there is a balcony entrance to a fire tower, while at the front there is a stairway enclosed at each floor with tile, having wood doors on the openings. The interior walls and ceilings are plaster-finished and nicely decorated in the lower floor used as a church. The floors are hardwood throughout. The building is heated by low pressure steam supplied from a central heating plant and lighted by electricity. The second and third floors are used as classrooms and there is also on the third floor a large recreation room. There is complete stage lighting and a motion picture booth with opening to the roof on the lowest floor. Also a lateral room intended to serve the purpose of a kitchen, two toilets, two dressing rooms and two cloak rooms, all on the lowest floor. The dimensions of the building are 150 feet by 100 feet over all.

This building was erected in 1921-1922 on a lot purchased from the Howell Estate in 1920 and transferred to the Archbishop in trust on February 5th of that year. 

The deed is recorded on February 10th, 1920, in deed book 

J.M.H. 741, page 203.
