How could the states encourage owners of these motor vehicles to have them repaired? Are there current state laws that would require an owner of a registered motor vehicle subject to a defect recall notice to have the vehicle repaired? 

This Commentary will review state laws that might authorize requiring owners to comply with a defect recall notice. It will cover Uniform Vehicle Code provisions providing for withdrawal of registration if a vehicle is found unsafe to be moved or operated upon the highways as well as provisions making it unlawful to operate an unsafe vehicle. REGISTRATION 

One of the difficulties with the current federal program of recalling motor vehicles possessing specified defects is the failure to achieve compliance by the vehicle owners. This is due primarily to the voluntary nature of the program with no apparent basis upon which to impose any sanctions on the owner for his failure to comply. 

In an effort to stimulate compliance by owners of vehicles containing defects subject to recall, attention is being focused on the role of the states. Within the parameter of this role, attention is directed to those areas of statutory authority that would permit a state to mandate (or at the least, provide the state with the requisite discretionary authority to require) compliance by the owner in a defect recall campaign. - 1 -

One way this could possibly be accomplished is the threat of loss of registration. The Uniform Vehicle Code (UVC) provides in § 3-704 (2), general authority for a state to withdraw registration " (2) When the department determines that a registered vehicle is mechanically unfit or unsafe to be operated or moved upon the highways." The UVC provides for additional authority to withdraw vehicular registration in the areas of inspection and equipment violations. The Commentary will review state laws on registration with particular emphasis on the loss of registration for an unfit or unsafe vehicle.

Before this analysis is undertaken, the Commentary will briefly examine state laws on the periods of registration and the manner in which states have established the registration periods for renewal. These laws will be compared with § 3-412, the applicable UVC provision.

For a defect recall campaign to be implemented at the state level, a state should have statutory authority to exert pressure on owners to comply with defect recall notices. One basis of authority could be the threatened loss of registration for noncompliance. The Uniform Vehicle Code provides for suspension or revocation of registration in §§ 3-406 and 3-704 whenever a vehi cle is determined to be mechanically unsafe or unfit to be operated or moved upon the highways. Loss of registration could also occur through rejection of an application for registration as well as failing to meet a state's safety inspection requirements or required equipment provisions. This Commentary will examine the comparable state provisions relating to loss of registration involving an unqualified vehicle.

Re- The question that must be addressed initially is whether a vehicle subject to a defect recall notice is a "mechanically unfit or unsafe vehicle to be operated or moved upon the highways." search of judicial decisions at the state level has not revealed any interpretation of the quoted phrase. There are, therefore, no judicial guidelines or standards established upon which to determine what is meant by an unsafe or unfit vehicle. It is suggested that the rationale for the issuance of a defect recall notice should be utilized as the basis for stating that a vehicle subject to the notice is an unfit or unsafe vehicle if not corrected within a specified period of time. The registration of that vehicle could then be suspended or revoked.

This portion of the Commentary will explore several legal issues relating to a state becoming involved in a defect recall campaign, such as 

1. Does a state have authority to participate in a defect recall campaign? 

2. Could a state enter a contract providing for the state to notify owners to have their vehicles repaired? 

Rules And Regulations

Before a state utilizes existing statutory powers to withdraw registration, consideration should be given to whether a state has any authority to involve itself in a defect recall 

14

campaign. While there is no specific statutory authority relating to defect recalls, general language exists in several states that could be interpreted to permit state involvement with defect recall campaigns.

One basis upon which state involvement could be authorized is through the issuance and enforcement of rules and regulations. 

Administrators of motor vehicle departments are provided with the authority to adopt and enforce rules and regulations that are necessary to carry out the provisions of the motor vehicle laws. 

Thus, for example, regulations could be issued requiring the owners of motor vehicles to submit their vehicles for inspection as part of a defect recall campaign. Failure to do so could result in a loss of registration. Also, by regulation, a motor vehicle could be considered "mechanically unfit or unsafe to be operated or moved upon the highways" if a safety related defect has not been corrected. Failure to comply with a defect recall notice could result in the withdrawal of registration.

Another way for a state to become involved in a defect recall campaign would be to enter a contract with the manufacturer of the defective vehicle. The state would notify owners and encourage them to have the defect corrected. The state would spend public funds for this program but would be reimbursed by the manufacturer. 

In addition, the manufacturer would supervise correction of any defect by its dealers. Though the state would, in effect, be assisting the manufacturer in fulfilling its moral and legal obligation, the state also would be performing an important governmental function by reducing accidents, injuries and deaths. 

Similarly, a state could become involved in a defect recall campaign by entering an agreement with the National Highway Traffic 

Safety Administration. Using funds provided by the Administration, the state would notify owners and encourage them to have their vehicles repaired. 

Does a state have the authority to enter into agreements of this type? The answer is yes.

Generally, in the absence of any constitutional or statutory limitation, a state has the power to make contracts. 

The power to enter into contracts to advance its proprietary or governmental interest is absolute and unqualified. A state may set aside its sovereignty and like a private individual or corporation enter into agreements with other governmental entities or with individuals or corporations, and be bound accordingly as a private person would be bound under a similar contract. Under such a contract, a state is held to the same rules and principles of construction and application of contract provisions as govern private persons and corporations in contracting with each other. 

A state contract must ordinarily rest upon some legislative enactment and in this respect is distinguishable from contracts 

17

between individuals.

Generally, the legislature delegates contracting authority to the appropriate executive agency which derives its authority from either the legislation creating the agency, the state constitution or legislation relevant to a particular subject matter. The legislature, although it cannot impair the obligation of the contract, has the ability to avoid payment of the obligations of the state by a failure or refusal to make the necessary appropriation, and creditors accepting obligations of the state are bound to know that they cannot enforce their claims against the state directly when no appropriation has been made for their payment. Unless there is an appropriation, courts are powerless to enforce a contract of a state, even though there is no doubt as to its validity.

Liability Of State For Breach Of Contract 

Assuming that a state has the authority to contract with a manufacturer or the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, what liability, if any, would inure to the state if it failed to perform its obligations under the contract? For example, if a state fails to notify an owner of a vehicle subject to a defect recall and that vehicle is subsequently involved in a crash due in substantial part to the defect, is there any liability on the state for the nonperformance of its duties?

The line of demarcation between liability and no liability to a third person not a party to the contract has been drawn by the courts between the nonperformance of any action or duty required under the contract and the negligent performance of the required action or duty. Failure to perform an act would not lead to the imposition of liability whereas liability would most likely be imposed in a situation where a duty has been assumed by contract. 

For example, in a situation involving three people A, B, and C, if A contracted to accept employment with B, in work which would affect the safety of C, and then entirely failed to appear for work and never entered upon the employment, A may be held liable to B for breach of contract, but there would be no liability to C.

Additionally, there have been a large number of cases holding that a company which contracts with a city to supply water to the public is not liable to a private citizen when the service fails at a critical moment and a house is destroyed by fire as a result. 

If, however, water that was supplied was contaminated, there would be no difficulty in finding liability for this negligent act. 

Therefore, where there has been only complete nonperformance of the contract, without even an initial action, liability has been denied.

Liability has been found where there was some act performed in such a way as to conclude that a duty was assumed by the conduct of that act. Liability then arises wherever the act has been negligently performed. 

19 -

Thus, in the hypothetical situation involving a contract between a state and a manufacturer in which the state agreed to notify the owners of vehicles affected by a defect recall notice, the state would most likely not be held liable for damages resulting from a crash caused by the defect in question, if the state took no actions at all in notifying the owners. However, if the state undertook to notify the owners and, because of its negligent operations, the owner of the vehicle involved in the crash was not notified, then it is conceivable that, under present law, liability could be imposed upon the state. 

Aside from the liability question, the question of immunity should briefly be addressed. For many years it has been an established principle of law that the state, as a sovereign, could not be sued in its own courts or in any other court without its consent and permission. Until a state abrogated its privilege of immunity by waiver, no individual could maintain a lawsuit against the state, regardless of the merits. 

This principle of law has been modified somewhat in some states where the courts have separated the business or proprietary enterprises of government from the purely governmental functions and have held that a state can be held liable for engaging in an industrial or business enterprise. Other state courts have clearly rejected this test for determining state liability, taking the view that a state, from its very nature, can act only in a sovereign, governmental capacity and is therefore always immune from liability in the absence of a waiver of the immunity. All authority possessed by a state is that conferred on it as a sovereign and the state can act in no other capacity.

If a state constitution contains an immunity clause, the legislature will have no power to circumvent this provision by passing a law providing for a waiver or consent to a lawsuit. 

Without a constitutional restriction, however, a state has the discretion to waive the privilege of immunity and consent to be sued. Consent may result from the enactment of a statute if it has not already been authorized in the state constitution. It should be remembered that the waiver of immunity does not concede liability in favor of the claimant nor does it recreate a cause of action that did not previously exist. - 20 -

CONCLUSION AND OBSERVATIONS 

If a vehicle is considered to be mechanically unfit or unsafe to be operated or moved upon the highways because of a defect cited in a defect recall notice, there are state laws that provide the authority to require owner compliance. The threatened loss of registration can be an effective weapon to use against an uncaring, indifferent owner. 

The key, of course, is whether a defect cited in a defect recall notice places a vehicle in the unfit or unsafe category until correction is made. Unfortunately, no case law has been uncovered that would shed some light on how a court of law would define the phrase "mechanically unfit or unsafe to be operated or moved upon the highways." To be most effective in aiding recall compliance, the state registration laws or regulations should be amended to clearly define this phrase as including defects cited in a recall campaign.

Detection of defects can be accomplished through the mechanism of a state's inspection program that utilizes police officers, transportation officials, and inspection stations to uncover the defects cited in a recall notice. States maintaining a periodic inspection program could most effectively provide procedures for the detection and correction of the defects. Failure to do so could lead to suspension of registration.

There is also some authority in the dealer licensing area for a state to effectively control a defect recall campaign by requiring dealers to coordinate their activities with the state or face possible suspension or revocation of their license to operate a dealership.

In most states, using a vehicle that is so unsafe as to endanger any person is a crime. A majority of states also proscribe the operation of a vehicle unless it is in good working order or in such safe mechanical condition as to not endanger any person. If a particular safety defect makes a vehicle so unsafe as to endanger any person, these state laws could be powerful weapons for use against owners who do not have their vehicles repaired. Needless to say, there would be enforcement problems and, to be effective, their enforcement could involve substantial time and money. - 21 - 

---

State agencies have the authority to enter into agreements for the advancement of its proprietary or governmental interest provided this authority is derived from a legislative enactment or the state constitution. This authority, if properly granted or delegated, is unqualified and absolute. Like a private individual, a state agency may contract with individuals, partnerships or corporations of the private sector or with other governmental entities and be held to the same rules and principles of the laws of contracts. 

Most likely there would not be state liability to third parties who are injured as a result of the state's failure to perform its contract obligations in a defect recall campaign. 

Resolving a hypothetical question with a definitive answer based on judicial decision is almost an impossibility because each court case ultimately depends on the facts of that case. However, a general review of the court decisions in this area would be supportive of an answer of no liability.

Finally, there is authority for states to disseminate information to the public as to defect recall campaigns since several states provide the public with traffic law digests and point system pamphlets to alert their citizens as to current changes in the law.

It should be unlawful to operate a vehicle that is unsafe under the laws of all 50 states and departments of motor vehicles should have authority to suspend the registration of any such vehicle. Provisions in the Uniform Vehicle Code on this subject should be reexamined to assure their adequacy and the development of a model law should be considered. Following these efforts, each state should study its own laws and adopt any necessary amendments. 1/
