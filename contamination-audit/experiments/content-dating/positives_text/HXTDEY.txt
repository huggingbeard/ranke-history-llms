The Beginnings of the First 

Church of Christ in Wethersfield

EUGENE M. BUSHONG 

ARLY years of the church in Wethersfield are obscured by a dearth of contemporary chronicles. 

There were no separate church records before 1697, when the Rev. Stephen Mix began a diary noting chiefly the church roll and vital statistics. Entries in the record of town votes, starting with 1647, concerning religious activities of the parish, minutes of the General Court of the colony, and occasional references to the Wethersfield church and its leaders by early chroniclers of New England are our now available sources of information. 

Tradition adds some picturesque details which are not always so accurate as interesting. The evidence is sometimes contradictory, but a careful analysis yields an outline picture of the beginnings of this church which we will trace, endeavoring to discriminate between facts and hearsay where possible.

Land hunger, pioneer restlessness, political, religious and even personal differences were important incentives for the settlement of the three river towns of Connecticut by colonists from Massachusetts. The plea of residents in the already crowded Bay towns for permission to remove to the fertile valley of "the long river" was debated and denied for several months by the Massachusetts court, but on May 6, 1635, that body passed the following order. "There is liberty granted to the inhabitants of Watertown to remove themselves to any place they shall think meet to make choice, provided they continue still under this

government." The settlers of Wethersfield were thus the first to receive official consent from Massachusetts Bay Colony for settling in Connecticut. The migration, which had been launched without sanction in 1634 by the adventurers under the leadership of John Oldham, now began in earnest. There is no certain knowledge of the number who came that summer of 1635. Among them, however, were the founders of a new church in the wilderness, the first to be organized in Connecticut, although not the oldest of the churches in the river towns. The 

Windsor and Hartford churches were gathered in Massachusetts earlier and transplanted in August, 1635, and

June, 1636, respectively, when considerable numbers of the congregations at Dorchester and Newtown emigrated under the leadership of their pastors. 

A difference of opinion has existed about the date of the founding of the Wethersfield church. Three dates, 1641, 1636, and 1635, have been cited in various histories. A principal reason for this publication is to determine, if possible, which of these dates is correct. 

The statement is found in an Historic Sketch of the First Church of Christ in Wethersfield, Given from the Pulpit, July 9, 1876, by A. C. Adams, pastor of the church at that time, that "No church was organized in 

Wethersfield for the first seven years (1634 to 1641), and the people were, during that time, a fragment of the church in Watertown. That this assertion was made without knowledge of the earliest records concerning the church in Wethersfield seems assured. In the Records of the Colony of Connecticut, Vol. 1, 1636-1649, in the archives of the State Capitol, on page 1, among the minutes of the first session of the General Court, which governed the new Connecticut plantations by authority delegated to it from the Massachusetts General Court, meeting in Newtown (Hartford) on April 26, 1636, the following entry is found: "Whereas there was a dismission

graunted by the C(h:) of Waterton in the Masachusetts dated 29th of Ma() last to Andrewe Warde, Jo: Sherman, Jo: Stickland, Rob'te Coo, Rob'te Reynold & Jonas

Weede, wth intent to forme anewe in a Ch: Covennte in this River of Conectecott, the saide prties have soe accordingly done wth the publicke allowance of the rest of the members of the saide Churches, as by certificate nowe prduced apprs. It is therefore in this prsent Cort ratified & confirmed, they prmissing shortlie publicquely to renewe the (said) Covennte uppon notice to the rest of the Churches."

From this record of dismission of these six men it is apparent that they were not "a fragment of the church in Watertown" until 1641, but were organized as a church in Wethersfield, then known as Watertown, some time prior to April 26, 1636, when their work of establishing the church was "ratified & confirmed" by the first session of the Colony's court. It was in 1641 that the first settled pastor was installed, and this event has apparently been confused with the founding of the church, which was, in fact, not only organized but involved in bitter controversy before 1641.

How long prior to this meeting of the court in April, 1636, was the church founded in Wethersfield? That it was before that date is beyond doubt from the past tense verbs used throughout the minutes: "there was a dismission graunted. . . . wth intent to forme anewe in a Ch: 

Covennte in this River of Conecteco tt, the saide prties. have soe accordingly done."

Just when was the dismission from the Watertown church granted? The record of the court in its present condition reads, "dated 29th of Ma() last". Very unfortunately the latter part of the name of the month, which came at the end of a line, has been obliterated by the much handling which the book received. The month might have been March or May. Which it was is impor-

tant, because it makes a difference of eleven months in the date. As the meeting of the court was held in April, falling between March and May, if the partially illegible month was "March last" it was the preceding month, March, 1636; but if it was "May last" it was in the spring of 1635 that the Watertown parish dismissed from its fellowship the fathers of the Wethersfield church. 

The History of Ancient Wethersfield, Volume I, was for the most part written by Sherman W. Adams, but after his death in 1898 his manuscripts were edited, enlarged and published by Henry R. Stiles in 1904. Here the above record is interpreted, on page 135, as follows: "Whereas, there was a dismission granted by the C(hurch) of Watertown in the Massachusetts, dated 29 M(arch) last... On the basis of this interpretation it is asserted "there was, properly and legally speaking, no church of Christ, and no church parish in the plantation for over a year after its first settlement. When it was formed in the Spring of 1636, it was through the action of six men, members of the Watertown, (Massachusetts) Church, from which, however, they had, up to this time, received no formal dismission." The italics were the author's.

This statement appears to have been the chief basis for the subsequent general acceptance of 1636 as the date of the founding of the church in Wethersfield. It is so positive in tone, and conspicuous by its position in the section on "Church Beginnings", that those who have accepted this work as an authority and quoted from it have either overlooked or discounted conflicting statements in other chapters. Elsewhere there are no less than eight references (pp. 24, 27, 54, 146, 147, 291, 303, 310) to the dismission of the founders, all of which give the date as May, 1635! It is hard to explain this discrepancy unless it is due to the editorial hand of Dr. Stiles or a change of opinion by Judge Adams. If the latter had lived to finish

his projected history, the result would probably have been more consistent on this and other points, for he was a painstaking student. Comparison of his manuscripts with Stiles' publication based on them might clear up this point, but the writer has been unable to locate the originals, if they are still in existence.

The original minutes of the "Corte" are now carefully preserved in the archives at the State Capitol. They were consulted in the preparation of this monograph, and sections from pages one and four are reproduced in the illustrations opposite our first and last pages. 

From page one we have reproduced the paragraph which refers to the Wethersfield church. It is difficult for the uninitiated to read, because of the unfamiliar spelling, the handwriting, and the discoloration of this first page due to age and handling. Perhaps you can decipher the paragraph by comparing it with the transcription of it on pages 2 and 3. At the end of the second line are the words "dated 29th of Ma-". It is about the last that we are most concerned. The letters "Ma" are distinguishable slightly more clearly in the original than in this reproduction but no other part of the word can be detected. 

It might, therefore, have been either "March" or "May".

A close study of the minutes written in this same hand suggests two conclusions which throw light on the problem as to which month was written by the scribe. 

In the first place, let us compare the legible portion of the word in which we are interested, on page one, with "March" and "May" as written elsewhere by the same hand. On page four of the original minute book, in the section reproduced in our second illustration, is a heading which reads, "Att A Cort Att Harteford, Mrch 28th, 1637". The record of that session was brief, and below it is the next heading, "The first day of May, 1637, Genrall

Corte att Harteford". A comparison shows no similarity between "Mrch", as written on page four with an abbre-

viated spelling, and the part of a word we have on page one. It is possible, of course, that the word "March" might have been abbreviated on page four and written in full on page one, but this is highly improbable as we shall point out below. On the other hand, the similarity of the two visible letters to the same letters in the word "May" on page four is striking.

In the second place, a careful measurement of the pages shows that the scribe used for the first two letters of the name of the month approximately the same amount of space as originally remained, to the edge of the paper, for the rest of the word. There would have been room for the letter "y" completing the word "May", but hardly sufficient room to complete the word "March", and if the word was the latter it was not abbreviated because the vowel would not have been written in that case. With space thus limited at the end of the line it is highly improbable that the writer spelled in full here a word which he elsewhere abbreviated. Stiles indicates that only the letter "M" is visible by bracketing the word thus --- "M(arch)". This is not the case, however, and the legible vowel eliminates the possibility of a shortened form. 

Therefore, if the word was "March" it was written in full, under conditions which made crowding of the final letters necessary, and in spite of the scribe's custom of abbreviating the word. And finally, the letters now visible do not appear to be cramped, being written as broadly as in the "May" we find on page four. These considerations point to the conclusion that the date written was “29th of May last".

In the search for further light on the question all known sources which might yield information have been checked. 

There are no contemporary accounts of the dismission of the six founders of this church other than the court minutes. The parent church in Watertown has no records for that period as the records of the church were included

in the annals of the town. A search of the town records reveals no trace of the dismission of these men. 

In the Genealogies of the Families and Descendants of the Early Settlers of Watertown, Mass., Volume II, which 

Henry Bond published in Boston in 1855, are references to each of the six men who were named in the dismission from the Watertown church. In every case the date for the dismission from the Watertown church to Wethersfield is given as May, 1635. Bond did not give the source of his information when he wrote, about the middle of the last century, and it is unknown whether or not he had access to some documents which are now lost. It is quite certain that he knew of the entry in the minutes of the Connecticut court on April 26, 1636, because he quoted from that record on page 950 in the reference to John Strickland.

Even if Bond had no other information than this record, he strengthens our case for the earlier date because he interpreted it as May, 1635, writing nearer the time of the original event than Stiles by half a century. Inquiry in 

Watertown, consultation with Mr. Albert H. Hall, state archivist for Massachusetts, and correspondence with the Massachusetts Historical Society, all failed to yield any further significant documentary evidence in that state. 

Another bit of evidence which supports our thesis is in the records of the General Association of Connecticut, which has statistics for the Congregational churches of this state back to 1831. Therein the date of the founding of this church has always been given as 1635. It is not possible to determine the source of this information, but it probably harks back considerably further than the beginning of the existing statistics. Presumably the date was originally put down as 1635 on information supplied by the Wethersfield church or from some other historical source. 

Turning back to the court record of April, 1636, reflection on the circumstances surrounding the dismission

and on the wording of the minute confirming the action of the six founders of the church suggests three further grounds for dating the dismission May 29, 1635, instead of the following March. In the first place, we know the six men named were in Wethersfield in the summer of 1635, as soon, probably, as removal could be made after the Massachusetts court gave permission on May 6. If they did not receive their dismission until March 29, 1636, there was only an interval of 28 days before the first session of the Connecticut court, April 26. During that period they must have secured the "publicke allowance" of the other Bay colony churches for the new society. 

They produced written evidence of this permission before the court at Newtown (Hartford). Then the documents had to be brought to Connecticut by a journey consuming several days, and the church organization effected in time to lay the matter before the governing body. That all this took place in 28 days, if they had already waited almost a year before taking action, is not very plausible.

Events did not usually transpire with such dispatch in those times.

It seems much more probable that when the Watertown people gained the desired leave to migrate, a group of them, with a mind to the spiritual welfare of their projected settlement, received their dismissal "wth intent to forme anewe in a Church Covennte in this River of Conectecott". They also conformed to custom and obtained the assent of the other churches to their plan. 

Thus prepared, they set out on the trek to Connecticut. 

In the second place, the language of the court minute, probably suggested by the wording of the dismission itself, hints that the dismission was granted before these men knew exactly what their new location would be. It says the dismission was granted them "wth intent to forme anewe in a Church Covennte in this River of 

Conectecott". If the dismission was granted in March,

1636, after they had been settled for nearly a year and the town named, it seems that the dismission would have been more specific as to the location of the new church than simply "this River of Conectecott". The language suggests that here are men setting out to establish a new settlement without absolute certainty as to their future location or the name it would be given. They prepared to found a church by carrying a dismission from their parent organization worded in broad terms concerning the location of the new church.

In the third place, it is difficult to explain why the court should have asked the members of the newly organized church to promise "shortlie publicquely to renew the (said) covennte upon notice to the rest of the Churches", if the covenant had been entered into and the church organized within about two weeks, after the dismission had arrived. To renew the covenant seems wholly unintelligible if we take the position forced upon us by dating the dismission in March. On the other hand, if the dismission was granted in the spring of 1635, and the church was organized shortly after the arrival of the settlers, the request of the court that it now be renewed seems more reasonable.

It has been repeated many times since Stiles' work appeared in 1904 that whereas Windsor and Hartford were each settled by the migration of a church with its pastor, the case in Wethersfield was different because here there was no church in the beginning nor for about a year after the first considerable settlement in 1635. But from the evidence given above it is clear that the nucleus of a church did come in the spring of 1635. The pastor at 

Watertown, the Rev. George Phillips, did not remove, but the emigrants from his church to Connecticut were affectionately dismissed to establish a church wherever they made their homes in the new colony. They also received the "publicke allowance" of the other churches

in the Bay colony that their new religious venture might be organized in the approved manner according to custom. Having made such preparations there is no reason to doubt that they proceeded to form in a church covenant without delay upon arrival in Pyquag and to hold religious services. From this beginning the history of the Church of Christ in Wethersfield runs its course across three centuries to our own time.

Leaving the rather involved question of dates and their supporting evidence, we turn to a delineation of the religious life of the new community at the site called Pyquag by the Indians, first named Watertown by the white settlers, and since February 21, 1637, officially called

Wethersfield. When the final name was adopted it must have been unfamiliar to the secretary of the court for he spelled it "Wythersfeild" and "Weathersfeild" in the same day's minutes. It has been variously misspelled ever since.

The first extant record in the town votes pertaining to church affairs is under date of April 22, 1647. The first meeting house was in course of erection. On this date 

Joshua Jennings "barggened" with the townsmen (predecessors of our present selectmen) for putting up seats in the meeting house with wainscot, the seats to be "acording to the paterne of the Harford seats". Perhaps they had used temporary benches up to this time. Pay was at the rate of three shillings a yard, nine pounds in hand and the rest when the work was finished, which was to be before the last of the next March. But the pay was in wheat and pease, to be delivered, "sound and dry and well drest," to any house Joshua named in the town. 

The work of finishing the meeting house advanced slowly. On September 26, 1647, Mr. Park was ordered to see to underdaubing and clapboarding the building, which was probably built of hewn timber. But over four years later, March 8, 1652, we find this vote recorded: "Sam: Smith is apointed by the towne to se that Hen: 1 IO

Palmer clapboard the meeting house, betwixst this and the first of May next; & if he do it not, having clapboard layd by him, the said Sam: hath power given him by the 

Towne to take a warand & sew (sue) him that he may force him to do the worke." Apparently they were determined to get the job done at last. 

If we may judge by the progress of construction after 1647, it must have been at least a couple of years before that when work was begun on the building, so the date 1645 has been generally accepted. It may well have been earlier. There is no reason to believe that another meeting house preceded it. Probably religious services and town. assemblies were held for the first few years in some dwelling or other available building. The existence of an earlier meeting house whose usefulness was past in less than ten years is incredible.

Concerning the growth of the church membership in the early years we know very little. There were no rolls until the pastorate of Mr. Mix. Governor John Winthrop of 

Boston, writing in his Journal in 1639, says there were seven members of the church. If this is correct only one had been added to the original number in four years. The strife which marked the life of the church, to which we will refer more fully, may have kept its membership small. However, small numbers were not exceptional. In his History of Connecticut, page 287, Trumbull says, “In several of the first churches, there were not more than eight, nine and ten male members".

One explanation for the slow growth of the young church lies in the greater voice which those not members of the church had in the local government in the Connecticut colony. In Massachusetts church affiliation was a prerequisite to admission as a freeman and only freemen held the franchise. In the new colony men could be admitted by the towns as "inhabitants" if they were of reputable life and conversation, and could vote on all 

II

town affairs, irrespective of their church relationship. 

This is strikingly different from the theocratic political system of the Bay colony. 

In theory, state and church were separate in Connecticut from the first, but in actual practice they were knit together as flesh and spirit. Church business was the care of the entire voting population and the air of religion pervaded all civil action. In the early town votes regarding church affairs in Wethersfield, customary forms of record were, "At a towne meeting at Weathersfield it was voted", or, "It was voted and agreed by the church and towne.' Committees appointed by the town meetings acted for the church even in affairs so vital as the settling of a new minister. The limits of town and parish were the same. One meeting house served for all civil and religious functions. 

The absence of a settled pastor before 1641 may be attributed in part to the small church membership and probably to the presence of ministers in the parish who conducted religious services although they earned their livelihood by secular pursuits. The first of these to arrive, and one of the founders of the church, was the Rev. John 

Sherman. He was a man of fine ability and in all likelihood the first minister to conduct services in Wethersfield. 

Space limitations forbid biographical sketches of these clergymen except as incidents pertaining to them reveal something of the early life of the church. Mr. Sherman went to Milford in 1639 and was one of its settlers with the Rev. Peter Prudden, who is also said to have preached in Wethersfield although he was never a householder here. Other residents of the town were also among the planters of Milford. 

The date when the Rev. Richard Denton came to

Wethersfield is uncertain, but was perhaps 1638. Like 

Mr. Sherman he was a preacher of ability. The bombastic (and very inaccurate) Cotton Mather, in his History of 12

New England, page 360, says the following of Mr. Denton. "First at Weathersfield, and then at Stamford, his doctrine dropt as the rain, his speech distilled as the dew, as the small rain upon the tender herb, and as the showers. upon the grass. Though he were a small man, yet he had a great soul. . . . I think he was blind in one eye; nevertheless he was not the least among the seers of our Israel." The occasion of his removal from Wethersfield in 1641 was a dissension which divided both church and town. The cause of the "unhappy contentions and animosities" is not known, but because of them the church gained an unenviable repute in its early years. 

Trumbull attributes the difficulties, in part at least, to the lack of a settled minister. This may well be the case for in Hartford all was peaceful so long as the Rev. 

Thomas Hooker lived, but after death removed his irenic influence, the church there became involved in bitter controversy.

The ministers of the neighboring towns were unable to compose the differences in Wethersfield. News of the quarrel spread and the mother church in Watertown, having some brethren here who had not been dismissed from her watch and care, sent two ambassadors with the hope of establishing peace. But they availed nothing. 

Then Mr. Davenport and members of the New Haven church came to Wethersfield and in the interest of harmony offered a suggestion different from those before made. One of the factions should remove. Some liked the advice, but for a time neither side would agree that they were the ones to move. However, on November 4, 1640, Andrew Ward and Robert Coe completed arrangements with the New Haven colony for the purchase of Rippowams (Stamford) by a group from Wethersfield. 

They migrated the next spring, with Mr. Denton, who became the first pastor of their new church. Mr. Adams says, "It is supposed that the church and the town 13

records went with the secedents". There is no reason for supposing any separate church records before the secession because none were kept for many years following, and the town records before 1641 were probably scanty, judging by those we have from 1647 on. 

After it was decided that the party under Mr. Denton's leadership was going to Rippowams, the church here, in 

February, 1641, installed the Rev. Henry Smith as its first settled minister. With his family he had come hither in 1638 or 1639 from Charlestown, Mass., where he arrived from England in 1637. It is quite likely that in the controversy of the period he was a member of the party opposed to Mr. Denton and the group allied with him. Indeed, the presence of so many clergymen may not have been an unmitigated blessing, nor conducive to harmony.

After 1641 Mr. Smith was the sole minister in the church, as Messrs. Sherman, Prudden and Denton had all departed, but he still had his troubles. Clement 

Chaplin, the ruling elder of the church, was a man of wealth and influence, but also of a contentious spirit. 

He gave his pastor little peace and was involved in quarrels with others. He engaged in a controversy with 

Matthew Mitchell, the town clerk, which resulted in a court order in 1640 for Mitchell to apologize publicly. 

By 1643 some dissatisfaction with Mr. Smith had arisen and a petition, evidently regarding the removal from Wethersfield of the disgruntled group, came before the General Court, which appointed a committee to investigate. Their report to that body is written in the minutes for April 13. They found the cause for dissatisfaction was "want of lotts, and other considerations" in addition to dislike of Mr. Smith and the "present quarrells". They were "of the opinion that the best way for recovering and presearveing the publique peace is that Mr. Smith lay downe his place, if it may be done according 14

to God". The recommendation was not followed and the court caused a further investigation. The result was that on November 10 the ruling elder was censured and fined by the court for writing and signing with others a declaration "tending to the defamation of Mr. Smith". At the same time it was ordered that a statement should be prepared and openly read in the several towns clearing the minister of blame, and a stiff fine of ten pounds set for anyone convicted of repeating the calumny. We would like to know the character of the charges made by Mr. Chaplin, but no hint is given. In any event, the minister was fully vindicated and thereafter Mr. Chaplin's influence seems to have waned. The only further accounts of him in the court minutes are records of suits he instituted against others in the town. 

In the early town votes the second entry concerning church affairs is a vote in general town meeting on 

September 17, 1647, "that Mr. Smith's maintenance shal be raised from 70 pound to eyghty pound for this prsent yeare 1647". His land was also exempt from taxes toward his own salary. His house was the first north of the meeting house and burying ground, on the east side of 

High (now Main) Street.

His son, Samuel, wrote a letter in 1699 which gives a description of his father as five feet and ten inches tall and of medium weight, fair of skin with long curling hair, having a merry eye and sweet smiling mouth, "tho he coulde frowne sternlie eno' when need was". He died of a fever in 1648, leaving a remarkable will which is in the Colonial Records, Vol. I, disposing of his modest estate of 

370 pounds value. He concluded the testament with this request. "And I desire the Church, whose servant I now am, to take the care and oversight of my family, that they may be brought up in the true feare of God; and to see that this my will be faithfully performed."

We have evidence that the church accepted this charge, 15

for Widow Smith was voted ten pounds a year, which probably continued until she married again. She became the wife of John Russell, father of her husband's successor, the Rev. John Russell, who was installed as the second minister of the church in 1650. He was a graduate of Harvard College. His ministry was peaceful until about 

1656. A doctrinal controversy that began in the Hartford church disturbed the whole colony for several years. In 

Wethersfield the situation was further complicated by a personal issue between Mr. Russell and Lieut. John

Hollister which grew until it caused a division between town and church and contributed to the second major emigration from Wethersfield.

Mr. Russell gave testimony under oath in a lawsuit involving Hollister, to which the latter and his friends took exception. A quarrel ensued and in the spring of 

1658, Mr. Russell, already considering a removal to 

Massachusetts as leader of a group of doctrinal dissenters in the three churches of the river towns, sent a sharp note to Hollister's faction, saying that they should “provide for themselves lest they be destitute". They interpreted his ambiguous statement as a resignation and petitioned the General Court, August 18, 1658, for liberty to settle another minister more to their liking and comfort. The petition was signed by eleven members of the church, apparently a minority, and thirty-seven voters of the town.

In reply the court urged a patching of the troubles. The answer of Mr. Russell and the majority of the church was to excommunicate Lieut. Hollister without giving reasons. 

The town voted, December 7, 1658, that they had no settled minister and should look for one. Thus the town and church were in opposition over the minister, the latter still recognizing Mr. Russell. 

Lieut. Hollister's next move, March 9, 1659, was to petition the court again, and they ordered Mr. Russell 16

and the church to put into writing the charges upon which the Lieutenant had been excommunicated and convey them to him. They also urged again that they seek to end their sad differences. But on the 18th of 

April, a group of fifty-nine men from the three towns met in Hartford and agreed to remove with their families out of the jurisdiction of Connecticut into Massachusetts. 

The government of that commonwealth had already granted them permission to settle at Norwattock (Hadley). Mr. Russell left shortly after, for a town vote on 

May 2 (or 20, the day is not clear) seems to indicate that he had recently departed, "whereby the towne is wholly destitute", and a committee was appointed to "procure, if they can, a minister to preach to us till we can procure one of our owne to setle amongst us". This indicates that, although the town did not regard Mr. Russell as their settled minister after December 7 of the preceding year, he had continued to exercise the functions of a minister in the community until his departure. 

In June the General Court acknowledged the situation in the following judgment. "This Court doth judge it to be the duty of the inhabitants of Wethersfield to prvide for the Towne of Wethersfield, in reference to the ministry." But this was not easy to do. The secession to 

Hadley took considerable of the strength of the Wethersfield church. Ezra Stiles, writing in his diary in 1788, said that all but five members left. These, with two who were added, carried on the church and admitted others; but they were vexed by some who said that they were no church, because of their fewness. To determine their status, they requested the General Court to review their case.

The decision is important because secedents to both Stamford and Hadley claimed that they were the church of Wethersfield, and it was therefore removed. This judgment denied their claims and legally established the 17

fact of the continuous existence of the church in Wethersfield from its first gathering in 1635. The court's decision, rendered March 14, 1661 (1660 according to old style dating), is copied below, with the spelling modernized for the help of the printer and the reader. "Whereas, it is well known to the Inhabitants and Churches in these parts that there was a Church orderly gathered at Wethersfield, by the full approbation and allowance of the Court and Magistrates then in power, and by the consent and approbation of neighbor Churches, and whereas there are divers of the members of the said Church removed from thence without any notice given to, or allowance and approbation from this Court or the Magistrates of this Jurisdiction or the Churches within this Jurisdiction or the neighboring Churches, so as the number of the members of that Church is lessened thereby, and upon that occasion some, through misapprehension of the true state of that Church, there still resident and remaining, have taken occasion unjustly to question the station and being of the said Church of Christ as some of that Church do complain, and yet none have charged any offence or irregularity upon the said Church or their proceed in their Church estate; and whereas the said 

Church did manifest unto the former Session of this Court, which heard and examined their case openly, the reality and truth of their continuance in the same membership, Church estate and station as formerly; This 

Court doth therefore hereby declare that the said Church is the true and undoubted Church of Wethersfield, and so to be accounted and esteemed, for anything doth yet appear; yet this Court doth also declare that if any just charge be brought in against them, or anything be made to appear that may justly disapprove or call in question their Church estate before the 14th of May next ensuing, and shall orderly declare and regularly prosecute their charge against them, then the Court will attend the hear- 18

ing of such charge against them and accordingly judge of their estate as shall then appear." No protest is recorded and the case was thus closed. 

From the end of Mr. Russell's service in 1659, to 1668, the church was served by various ministers for short periods, but none was settled as permanent pastor. 

These supplying clergymen included first the Rev. John 

Cotton, son of the eminent divine of the same name in 

Boston, who was here from 1660 to June, 1663. His remuneration was fixed by a town meeting in 1661 at seventy pounds a year, house rent free, use of five acres of meadow, and his wood cut and carted to his house. 

The Rev. Joseph Haines, son of Governor Haines, ministered from September, 1663, to the next June. At that time an invitation was conveyed to the Rev. Gershom 

Bulkeley, who had some difference with his church in New London, to become Wethersfield's minister. He did not accept this offer and, on August 15, Mr. Buckingame (Buckingham) was voted twenty shillings a week, and expenses for himself and horse, for supplying as minister. 

The next incumbent was Jonathan Willoughby who came from Massachusetts in the fall of 1664 with a view to permanent settlement. He decided, however, in September of the next year, not to accept settlement and the church again began looking for a permanent minister while he continued in temporary service until May, 1666. 

Apparently discouraged by their failure to secure a satisfactory minister to settle among them, the town chose a committee October 24, 1665, to consult the leaders of the neighboring plantations about their problem. 

They also voted "that there should be a day of humiliation kept by the whole towne, to seek to God, for guidance and succes in the provision of a sutable able minister for the towne".

Their prayers and persistent efforts eventually won the services of the Rev. Gershom Bulkeley, the third settled 19

pastor. He began his ministry here in the summer of 

1668, apparently, after almost two years of negotiations.

The Wethersfield people had determined to secure him. 

He was an able man, trained in medicine and law as well as theology. During King Philip's war (1675-6) he was an army surgeon, and received the "hearty thanks" of the war Council "for his good service to the country". 

In October, 1676, he informed his people "that it was too hard for him and beyond his strength, by reason of the weakness of his voice, to carry on the whole work of the ministry". The town, therefore, willingly sought another "to assist him and to be a comfort and help to him in that work". The assistant soon took over all the work and Mr. Bulkeley retired from the ministry, but he remained in the community as a practicing physician and a strong influence in town affairs. 

Here our narrative must end. The church seems to have entered an era of greater peace and prosperity. 

Each of the next five ministers who were settled by the church and town served until death wrote "finis" upon their work. Their names and dates of incumbency were: 

Joseph Rowlandson (1677-8), John Woodbridge (1679-91), Stephen Mix (1694-1738), James Lock ood (1739-1772), and John Marsh (1774-1821). The last three served a total of 127 years a remarkable record of service by a triumvirate of distinguished men. 

To tell the full story of the past three centuries would require a volume many times the size of this, and the present sketch ends with the wish that someone will, ere long, set down the complete history of the First Church of Christ in Wethersfield. 20
