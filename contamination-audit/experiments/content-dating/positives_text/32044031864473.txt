DEFINITIONS AND CLASSIFICATIONS. A right has been defined as the power or the capacity of one to influence the action of another. A moral right is merely one derived from the moral sentiment of the community but it becomes a legal right where it is derived from the government and enforceable by law. Physical rights are based merely on superior force or the right of might. The law, however, is concerned only with legal rights. 

Jurisprudence divides obligations in law into those ex delicto and ex contractu. An obligation ex delicto or a tort duty arises from the violation of a preexisting right and does not depend for its existence upon any volition of the parties concerned to create it but arises independently of consent or agreement, being imposed by law upon members of society. Accordingly, X owes a delictual obligation to Y not to commit any tort against him such as slander, assault, false imprisonment and the like. Should X violate this tort obligation a new right immediately springs up giving Y a right in the law of torts to recover damages. A tort right is a right in rem signifying that it is not a right in personam against any particular person but against the whole world. 

An ex contractu obligation, unlike one ex delicto, arises between the parties by virtue of their own volition, conduct or consent; it does not depend upon any preexisting rights and it is a right in personam and not in rem. X and 

Y enter into a contract of partnership. At that moment there were no preexisting contractual rights and duties between them but the instant the contract was made new rights and duties were thereby created voluntarily. The contract rights of the parties are in personam and not in rem viz., X's right in the contract extends only to Y and Y's right in the contract extends only to X. This thought is evidenced by the familiar rule of law that no one has any rights in a contract except the parties thereto. 

Blackstone defines a contract most accurately: "A contract is an agree ment upon sufficient consideration to do or not to do a particular thing." This definition has been upheld by Chief Justice Marshall speaking for the Supreme 

Court of the United States in 4 Wheat. 122.

More practically speaking a contract necessarily consists of the following elements and should one or more be missing there can be no contract: 1. Offer, 2. Acceptance. 3. Consideration. 4. Competent parties. 5. Legality of object. 

6. Formal requirements, if any (statute of frauds, etc.).

The offer and the acceptance constitute the agreement whereby the parties mutually assent. Every contract must be based upon a valuable consideration. A consideration to be valuable need only be sufficient, not adequate. A "good consideration" will not support a promise although it is a sufficient consideration to support a covenant to stand seised as between parties related by consanguinity. 

Blackstone states "A good consideration is such as that of blood or of natural love and affection, when a man grants an estate to a near relation; being founded on motives of generosity, prudence and natural duty; a valuable consideration is such as money, marriage, or the like which the law esteems an equivalent given for the grant and is therefore founded in motives of justice." The only purpose for which a "good consideration" may be effectual is to support a covenant to stand seised to uses in favor of a wife, a child or a blood relative and no one else. 

Competent parties are necessary to constitute a valid contract by which is meant that the parties must be contractually sui juris. Undoubtedly no agreement involving an illegal object can result in a contract because such would be contra 593190

An example of a formal requirement is that a deed to real estate must be sealed; another example is the law requiring a valid contract for the sale of lands to be merely in writing, regardless of a seal, The expression "aggregatio mentium" literally means a "meeting of minds". 

It has been negligently laid down that the true test of a contract is to determine whether or not there has been a meeting of the minds, This is incorrect for the reason that the undefinable expression itself is a mere conclusion or a deduction that upon all the facts and circumstances there is a contract. It is impossible to tell whether there is or is not a contract merely by trying to determine whether or not the minds of the parties have met, It is true that there is a "meeting of the minds" in most contracts but there are many situations where there is a meeting of the minds and yet no contract results. The true test of a contract is to determine whether the elements enumerated above are present considered in the light of the attendant circumstances and of what the parties intended. 

According to the etymology of the word, "contract" is derived from the Latin "contraho" which means a drawing together. 

Contracts have been classified as (1) formal, (2) quasi-formal and (3) simple. A simple contract is also called a parol contract. A formal contract is one that depends for its validity upon its form. There are two kinds of formal contracts: (1) Under seal and (2) of record. The so called contract of record is a judgment of a court which is not a contract at all because the law regards a judgment as a debt and not a contract. A contract under seal, also called a specialty or a deed, is a written obligation or promise deriving its validity from the seal. A quasi-formal contract is one partly dependent on form and partly on a consideration. It is better to consider such contracts as simple contracts an example of which is a promissory note. All other contracts whether they be oral or in writing not under seal are designated simple contracts otherwise known as parol contracts.

Contracts have also been considered as (1) express, (2) implied and (3) quasi contracts. An express contract is so stated either orally or in writing so that its contents are completely known to the contracting parties. X promises to sell Y his radio for $10 and Y in exchange for X's promise gives his promise to purchase the specific radio for the said amount, the mutual exchange of considerations to take place eo instante. This is an express contract because the terms are fully expressed. There are two kinds of implied contracts; (1) Implied in fact and (2) implied in law. Contracts implied in fact are true, genuine contracts wherein the law implies a contract not fully expressed as where one results from the conduct of the parties, no words being necessary. A newsboy holds out a copy of The 

Evening Star, X places three cents in the vendor's hand, takes the paper and goes on. This is a contract (as well as a sale) implied in fact although no words were spoken. Implied contracts in law are termed in modern jurisprudence "quasi contracts." Quasi contractual rights were not recognized by the law anciently but were cognizable in chancery. Later the law took recognition of and enforced such rights particularly after the Statute of Westminster II created the common law form of action on the case in 1286, As a result equity released its jurisdiction on quasi contractual obligations because after 1286 there was a plain, complete and adequate remedy at law. Quasi contractual rights are enforceable through the form of action at common law known as general assumpsit via the common counts.

· · The substantive right in a quasi contract is based on the theory that no man shell be unjustly enriched at the expense of another. 

Whereas the measure of damages in an express contract or a contract implied in fact is based upon the contract price, yet it is differently with a quasi contract where the measure of damages is based upon what the goods or services or use is reasonably worth, there being no contract price. A quasi contract is not a contract but is merely an obligation purely equitable based on good morals and conscience enforceable at law.

Contracts are either (1) executed or (2) executory or (3) partly executed or (4) partly executory. A contract fully performed by both sides is wholly executed. Where performed on one side only it is partially executed as to that side but executory as to the other. Where a contract is unperformed on either side it is wholly executory.

Contracts are also designated as (1) bilateral and (2) unilateral. A unilateral contract is a misnomer for the reason that the elements requisite to a valid contract or some of them are missing. In a unilateral contract there is a promise on one side only calling not for a promise, but for an act to be performed by the other side. As there is no promise for a promise there can be no contract, As soon as the act is performed an acceptance takes place and as soon as this eventuates a contract is born resulting in instantaneous death before it can draw its first breath. Thus it is more properly said that a unilateral contract is merely a promise for an act resulting by its execution in a sale. A offers Dr. B $100 if B will cure A of hay fever permanently. In this case A's promise did not call for a promise but for an act. B therefore could only accept by an act and not by a promise. This is the situation usually designated a unilateral contract, evidently no contract because there is no promise for a promise. B may accept the contract merely by performing the act in toto or at least by substantial performance and in no other way. Until he accepts in that manner there is no contract between A and B and A has the right to revoke his offer at any time before acceptance is completed. Should A do so B could not sue for any breach of contract no matter how far he has progressed toward acceptance but in some cases B may recover merely in quasi contract on the theory of unjust enrichment provided he can show not merely that he has suffered detriment but that A has profited thereby at his expense.

The only true contract recognized by law is the bilateral contract which is defined briefly as a promise for a promise, each being a consideration for the other. Where there is a promise for an act there is no consideration for the first promise and no contract results. Suppose A promised Dr. B $100 if Dr. B would promise A to cure A of hayfever within one year permanently. Dr. B so promises. 

He thereby accepted A's offer so that there is now a promise for a promise based upon a valuable consideration (each promise being a quid pro quo for the other) and a true bilateral contract results. 

The bilateral contract is characterized by reciprocal promises involving something to be done or not to be done on both sides. A true bilateral consists of mutual executory promises. Thus where X promises to paint Y's house for $50 and Y promises to employ X to do so for that sum, there is a bilateral executory contract based on a promise in exchange for a promise, A bilateral contract executed on both sides is termed a sale,

Contracts are also designated as (1) void, (2) voidable and (3) unen There is no such thing as a void contract because if it is void it is no contract. The term really means an agreement of no legal effect such as a contract between X and Y to kill Z. A voidable contract is one giving a party the right to reject or affirm the transaction. The leading example of a voidable contract is where an infant contracts with an adult. The infant may reject or disaffirm or avoid his obligation ordinarily but the adult is bound. Where both parties have the right to avoid then there is no contract to begin with, Where two infants contract either or both may avoid. A voidable contract is a perfect contract until voided. The difference between a voidable and an unenforceable contract is chiefly a difference between the substantive and the adjective law, A voidable contract is voidable as a matter of substantive right but an unenforceable contract is unenforceable because of lack of remedy or noncompliance with form or by virtue of a statute. Thus an unenforceable contract may be a void contract or one unenforceable because it is not in writing and involves the sale of land.

A contract is aleatory or hazardous when the performance of that which is one of its objects depends on an uncertain event, such as insurance, etc. 2 

C. J. 1030.

OFFER AND ACCEPTANCE 

An offer as an element of a contract is merely a proposal to make a contract. An offer may be by words or by signs; either orally or in writing; by conduct, or by a combination of either. It may be made either personally or by servant or agent, but in whatever way it is made it is not an offer in law until it comes to the knowledge of the offeree, Thus an offer to sell merely contemplates the proffer, proposal, presentation or exhibition of something to another for acceptance or rejection. The promise of an offeror is the offer and the promise of the offeree is the acceptance. The offeror and the promisor in a contract are the same; the acceptor, promisee and offeree are the same. 

As an offer must be communicated to the offeree from the offeror, it is impossible for one to accept an offer of which he is ignorant. The offer must be directly communicated in order that it may be accepted. The variety of ways in which an offer may be communicated is infinite, The offer as communicated must be complete so that it may be accepted without addition to its terms by the offeree; otherwise, the latter is really the offeror. X wires Y an incomplete offer to sell him a carload of beans naming no price. This constitutes no offer but merely an invitation to make one. Y wires back that he will take the carload of beans at 50¢ per bushel, This is not an acceptance by Y but constitutes a new offer because there was nothing to accept due to the incompleteness of the purported offer. 

An offer must be seriously intended. An offer made in jest and known by the offeree to be in jest cannot be accepted for the purposes of contract. On the other hand even where an offer was not seriously intended a contract my result by estoppel. Where a man whose $50 radio was stolen shouted that he would pay $500 to anyone who would catch the thief, it was held not to be an offer to pay a reward but merely an emotional explosion of wrath against the culprit. But if it could have been shown that the offeror intended his offer to be accepted then the

900 wi

offer would have been serious and thereby acceptable to form a contract. 49 Ill.. 

App. 459. The rule is that if a reasonable man would be justified in treating the offer seriously and the offerec in reliance on the offer acted to his injury, the offeror would be estopped and consequently bound by a resultant contract. 

Unless otherwise specified, an offer lives a reasonable time. What is a reasonable time depends upon the facts and circumstances of each case. A reasonable time may vary from a few minutes or seconds in some cases to a much longer time as weeks or months in other cases. Where there is no acceptance or no revocation and an offer fails to specify by its own terms how long it shall endure, the rule is that it endures or is open for a reasonable length of time. An 

Offeror may withdraw his offer at any time before acceptance. Rejection kills an offer and so does an acceptance; in the latter instance the acceptance in killing the offer gives birth to a contract but the rejection of an offer merely destroys it without any resultant legal liabilities. Where an offer to sell olive oil was made by wire and acceptance was wired about 24 hours later it was held that the offer had terminated before the acceptance was made on proof that olive oil fluctuated rapidly in value at the time. 

A contract to keep an offer open for a certain length of time is termed an option. An option is a perfect bilateral contract and is based upon a valuable consideration. It must not be confused with a mere offer which by its own terms promises to be good for a fixed time. X offers to sell Y ten Southern Railway bonds. Y pays X a consideration of $25 to keep the offer open for six months. 

This is a contract (option) whereby X has sold his right to revoke before the end of the specified period. Y may accept the offer at any time within six months. 

This agreement or transaction was the purchase by Y of an option from X. On the other hand suppose X offered the bonds to Y in writing or orally and stated that the offer would be good for six months. This is merely an offer and not an option. 

The offer would be open for six months in spite of the fact that Y gave no consideration for it on the ground that the offeror has a right to fix a time limit for his offer. The difference between this situation and the option is that where no option is bought X has the right to revoke his mere offer to Y upon Y's receiving either direct or indirect notice of the revocation, provided X's revocation is received by Y before Y's acceptance takes effect. On the other hand if X had offered to keep his promise open for six months and said promise was under seal, the offer would have constituted a contract (option) in the absence of any consideration because a promise under seal dispenses with the requirement of a consideration to make a promise binding and consequently X's offer would be irrevocable. Adams v. Lindsell, 1 B. & A 681. This case holds that an offer is good until it expires by its own terms or is revoked directly or indirectly. An offer terminates when the time fixed in its terms for an acceptance has passed without acceptance by the promisee.

Death of either the offeror or offeree destroys the offer by operation of law. An offer cannot be accepted by one's executor or administrator and similarly where an offeror dies before acceptance his personal representative cannot be bound. Insanity will also terminate an offer. 

An offer must be accepted in the manner provided for by the offer. 

Acceptance not in terms of the offer destroys the offer and the acceptance constitutes a new offer which may be either accepted or rejected by the original offeror. X, in Harper's Ferry, sent a letter by wagon, containing an offer to buy flour to Y, at Mill Creek, about 20 miles away, requesting Y to answer by

- letter by return of the identical wagon on its way back to Harper's Ferry the next day. Instead Y supererogatorily sent his letter of acceptance by post intending it should reach X sooner than requested. But instead of addressing the letter of acceptance to X at Harper's Ferry, Y sent it to him at Georgetown which X eventually received at Harper's Ferry but after & longer period than it would have taken had the acceptance been returned by the specified wagon. Meanwhile the wagon having returned without any acceptance, X purchased flour elsewhere. Y failed to recover against X for breach of contract. The Supreme Court of the 

United States held that no contract resulted on the ground that the offer specified that acceptance should be made at Harper's Ferry and mailing it to X at Georgetown materially changed the terms expressly stated in the offer and thereby destroyed it. An acceptance communicated at a place different from that pointed out by an offeror, and forming a part of the offer, imposes no obligation binding upon the offeror unless the offeror acquiesces therein which in this case X declined to do. On the other hand it was entirely unimportant whether the acceptance was sent by the wagon specified or another wagon or in any other manner provided it was sent to Harper's Ferry and was not delayed beyond the time contomplated by the offeror in specifying the wagon that he did. The rule is that where an offer specifies a place for acceptance to be sent, no contract will result if the purported acceptance is sent to another place, unless the offeror acquiesces. 

It should be noticed additionally that time was of the essence in this case and before the acceptance was actually received X had changed his position due to the failure of Y to accept in terms of the offer. Eliason v. Henshaw, 4 heat. 225. 

A termination of an offer occurs without the act of the offeror, but every case of revocation of an offer calls for an act on the part of the offeror, except where there is revocation by operation of law as in death, insanity, etc. 

To revoke an offer revocation must be communicated or brought to the knowledge of the offeree directly or indirectly before acceptance or termination. Of course where an offer terminates or expires it is too late to revoke hut the effect is the same, On July 1 X offers to sell Y a peck of peaches, the offer to be good through July 3. X changes his mind on the night of July 1. He must notify Y that he has revoked the offer; otherwise, if Y accepts within the time specified X will be bound. A mere mental determination to revoke uncommunicated to the promisee is ineffective. But suppose that Y while on the way to X's house to accept the offer meets 2 who tells him that X had revoked his offer to Y. This indirect knowledge of X's revocation received by Y is effective to destroy the offer and it is too late for Y to accept, provided Z was a person reasonably under the circumstances to be believed. Y would not be bound by an indirect revocation coming from an unreasonable, unreliable, or untrustworthy source. A mailed B an offer August 1 which was received August 10 and accepted by mail on that same day. On August 2, however, the offeror mailed a letter to the offeree revoking the offer but the letter was not received by B until August 12. There is a contract. The offer became effective when it was received on August 10. The revocation was not effective until the 12th when it was received, but the acceptance took effect on the 10th when it was mailed. Since the acceptance took effect before the revocation, the revocation was too late and a contract resulted on the 10th. 

An offeror may make his offer in such terms as he wishes and for this reason he may reserve the right to revoke without notice or he may so specify the terms and conditions of his offer that it is impossible to accept. Where a railroad company advertised that the timetables were subject to change without notice, no recovery was permitted for changing the departure of a train. On the other hand where a railroad company did not reserve the right to revoke without notice

recovery was allowed because the change of notice in train time was not advertised as extensively as the original timetable had been advertised. 14 Allen 433. Generally an offer may be revoked by advertising such revocation as extensively and through the same medium as the offer had been advertised. Shuey v. U. S., 92 U. 

It is futile to revoke an offer after it has been accepted or where there is an option to keep it cpen. An option renders an offer irrevocable but terminable per se.

A continuing offer is one that is capable of several acceptances either by the same or different parties. Each new acceptance of the offer results in a new contract but a continuing offer may be revoked before any subsequent further acceptance.

A counteroffer destroys an original offer and amounts in itself to a new offer so that the first offeree becomes an offeror and the first offeror becomes an offeree. X offers Y his horse for $2.98. Y counteroffers to pay only $2.00 for the beast. The counteroffer of $2.00 is a new offer and destroys the original offer of $2.98. X may reject Y's new offer but if he accepts it contract reşults. Two offers will not make a contract as where X writes Y a letter offering him $100 for his blue-nosed mule and at the same time Y writes X a letter offering to sell the identical mule at that price. The letters cross in the mail and are simultaneously received. No contract. A mere inquiry does not constitute a counteroffer as where X offered to sell corn drippings to Y and Y merely inquired if X would grant 90 days time to pay. Y's inquiry will not destroy the offer. 

On the other hand a mere inquiry cannot be accepted and become a contract. 

A wires B if he would care to sell his racoon coat for $100. B cannot wire A that he accepts the proposal and thus form a contract because A's mere inquiry was not an offer and therefore not acceptable. Thus

X offers by wire to sell Y his mule and cart for $100. Y wires back that he will buy the mule alone for $10. X wires back an acceptance for the mule. 

Y's counteroffer having been accepted by X, a contract results. But X's original offer was revoked by the counteroffer.

Am sending you $6,000 herewith by telegraph money order." Hold: No contract. If a customer enters the store and offers to buy that particular suit at that particular price his offer may be refused by the merchant. On the other hand there is no doubt that a positive offer may be made by general notice or by advertisement provided the publication contains words of promise so as to constitute a technical offer. 207 N. At an auction sale it is the bidder and not the auctioneer who makes the offer. The auctioneer merely invites the making of bids. A bid is an offer and as such is not binding and may therefore be withdrawn anytime before acceptance by the auctioneer. The auctioneer usually signifies his acceptance by letting the hammer fall and knocking the article off to the bidder. Such acceptance creates a contract. Before the fall of the hammer or other acceptance a bid may be revoked.

Accordingly, an auctioneer is under no duty to accept any bid. 

Where one sends an order to a dealer for goods such order is an offer to buy but no contract results until accepted or some act is done by the dealer on the faith of the offer as by delivery or shipment or any other act evidencing intention to accept. 381.

A contract becomes effective as of the date of acceptance. 1 H. L. Cas. 

Where an offer contains on its face the terms of a complete contract the acceptor is not bound by any other terms intended to be embraced therein unless he knew of those terms or should have known of them. Where one accepts a railway or steamship ticket containing certain stipulations it is for the jury to determine whether they constitute reasonable notice to the holder in order to determine whether he assented to such terms in the absence of a showing that they were expressly called to his attention or that he knew of them. One who buys a ticket for transportation on a common carrier today is charged with constructive notice of all the common and statutory law pertaining to such contract and his ignorance of the law is no defense.

An offer sent by letter is considered as being made during every instant of time until it has reached the offeree and the law will grant a reasonable time after receipt thereof in which to accept or reject such offer in the absence of a stipulation therein to the contrary. While the rule is that an offer to be good must be directly received and that a revocation must be directly or indirectly received, nevertheless an acceptance takes effect when the acceptor parts with control over it, regardless whether the acceptance is received or not, provided the acceptance is made in the manner requested or contemplated by the offer. When acceptance is made other than according to the tenor of the offer it takes effect only when received. X in washington mails an offer to Y in London. As the offer specified no medium of communication for the acceptance, it is the law that the acceptance should be made in the same way the offer was received on the ground that the method used by the offeror impliedly indicates the same method to be used by the offeree. Suppose Y mails his acceptance but it is lost in a sinking at sea and therefore never received. There is a good contract because the acceptance took effect when the letter was mailed in London and the acceptor resorted to the same medium as the offeror had. On the other hand if Y cabled an acceptance a contract would not result unless and until the cable was actually received by X. 

It is clear therefore that the acceptor may accept using a medium of transportation different from the one called for but with the result that his acceptance will not be good when he parts with control over it but only when it is actually received.

As to rewards, the rule is that the offer of a reward must be known and acceptance must be made with intention of complying with the terms of the reward. 

Hence, where A offers a reward for the return of his stolen dachshund, B who recovers it without knowledge of the reward may not collect. There are, however,

The entire law of rights in contract is based on what the parties themselves intended. 

An acceptance of an offer must be an act which is manifested externally so that the offeror may know or realize that one has been made. A mere uncommunicated mental determination to accept is insufficient. Acceptance may be made by express words or by conduct or by both, such as by a nod, a twitch, a raising of a limb, a wink and the like. An implied acceptance is where an offer is made and neither expressly rejected nor accepted, but the offeree proceeds in the matter and derives benefit or profit from it or asserts rights over the res in regard to which the offer is made. Where the offer calls for an act to be done the doing of the act may constitute an acceptance. X writes Y that he will sell his bull for $4 and if Y wishes to accept he should indicate such acceptance by wearing a red necktie at church the following Sunday. Y may accept by wearing the. red necktie.

Silence, however, does not constitute consent unless the offeree agrees in advance that his silence may constitute consent or there has been such a course of dealings between the parties as to render silence equivalent to acceptance. 46 

N. Y. 467. One has no right to impose upon another an obligation or penalize him for silence. X writes Y, "Unless I hear from you within 10 days, I will take your silence as an acceptance of my offer mailed you last month." Y's silence constitutes no acceptance or consent.

Although circulars and advertisements to the general public are usually construed merely as invitations to make an offer as has been pointed out above, cases have arisen where such publications constituted offers because they contained words of promise and in other cases because there was a showing that they were intended as offers. A patent medicine company offered a reward to anyone who had contracted influenza. The offer contained in a circular promised a reward to anyone who after using the medicine was not completely cured provided they used the medicine for a certain period of time in accordance with directions and had complied with various terms of the circular. The company had placed the amount of the reward in a bank and the court considered that act as indicating that the circular was intended as an offer. P used the medicine in compliance with the offer and instead of recovering from his influenza contracted double pneumonia, The company was held liable for breach of contract. 

Where, after a series of negotiations, it is the intention of the parties to reduce the terms of the proposed agreement to writing, it is the rule that

10no contract results until the writing is executed. This is based on the rule that a contract is the expression of the mutual intentions of the parties. Accordingly, where parties have come to a complete agreement and understanding but contemplate the privilege of withdrawal until the contract is reduced to writing, then there is no contract until the writing is signed and until that is done either may change his mind. It is important to remember the difference between a contract and the mere negotiations leading to a contract. It all depends on the intention of the parties.

CONSIDERATION 

Con- Every simple contract must be based upon a valuable consideration. sideration is legal detriment which moves from the promisee to the promisor at the promisor's request in return for the latter's promise. Consideration is something done, forborne or suffered, or promised to be done, forborne or suffered, by the promisee in respect of the promise. 

It has been said in many books that a consideration is a "benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee" but this is not correct. It is immaterial whether there is a benefit to the promisor or not, All that is necessary to constitute a valuable consideration is to find that there is a detriment to the promisee given in exchange for the promise. The word "detriment" means a legal detriment and no other kind.

In an action on a contract the promisor is the party who makes the promise. He is the party sued and is called the defendant. The promisee is the party suing and is called the plaintiff. On the other hand, if the defendant in the first action were to sue the plaintiff of the first action in a countersuit on the same contract then the party who was originally the promisor (defendant) will become the promisee and plaintiff in the counteraction. The party who was the promisce (plaintiff) in the original action will then become the promisor (defendant) in said counteraction. In other words, in every bilateral contract there are two promisors and two promisces; in order to determine the promisor and promisce it is necessary to first have a suit filed. In any action or suit the plaintiff is always the promisce and the defendant is always the promisor who is being sued for breach of his promise to the promisee. 

A case exemplifying legal detriment is Devecmon v. Shaw. 69 Md. 199.

In that case an uncle promised his nephew that if the latter would take a pleasure trip to Europe he, the uncle, would pay all his expenses. In reliance on the uncle's promise the nephew took the trip and upon his return payment was refused on the ground that there was no consideration for the promise. The expression "nudum pactum" means a naked pact, a bare agreement; a promise without consideration. The phrase "quid pro quo" means "what for what" or "something for something" and really denotes the giving of one valuable thing for another. It is nothing more than the mutual considerations which pass between the parties to a contract.

Where X promised to support Q for life without any legal detriment being suffered by Q in exchange for the promise of X the agreement constitutes no contract because it is nudum pactum. But

Motive and consideration are different. Motive is the personal reason one has for entering into a contract; it is the cause behind the transaction. consideration is that legal detriment suffered by the promisee in exchange for the promisor's promise which gives validity to the agreement. 

A valuable consideration is merely legal detriment to the promisee.

A promise not under seal by X to make a gift to Y is nudum pactum, but any promise under seal is enforceable regardless of consideration.

A promise forbearing to sue or not to press a claim may constitute a valuable consideration. Cases involving this rule usually fall into three classes: (1) 

Where the claim is well founded there is no doubt but that forbearance to sue or press the claim constitutes a sufficient consideration. (2) Even where the claim is doubtful in law or in fact it is universally agreed that forbearance is a sufficient consideration. (3) Where the claim is unfounded two situations arise: (a) where P knows his claim is unfounded and (b) where although it actually is unfounded, he reasonably believes it is well founded. Where one knows that his claim has no foundation and such is the case, forbearance is not a consideration. But where one has a reasonable belief that he has a good claim, forbearance is a good consideration even though his claim is actually unfounded. Accord and satisfaction is an agreement between two parties to give and accept some thing in satisfaction of a right of action which one has against the other, which when performed is a bar to all actions upon this account. An accord alone, however, is not a bar to an action on the old agreement. X has a claim against Y for $100 arising out of an executed contract to paint Y's garage. Y has no money to pay the debt. Where one is already under a duty imposed by law, a promise to perform that same duty constitutes no legal detriment and the consideration will be deemed insufficient. Such is the case where a policeman makes an arrest in his general line of duty; he cannot recover a reward for such arrest as he was duty bound to do what he did. But where one goes beyond and does some thing which is not a part of his duties he may recover on the ground that doing more than he was supposed to do constitutes a legal detriment and is therefore a sufficient consideration for the promise. An example is where a fireman recovered a reward by entering a burning office building and carrying out a person to safety where he was under no duty to so unnecessarily risk his own life. Similarly, no legal detriment is suffered where a wife or husband promises merely to perform marital obligations, where a ward promises to obey his guardian, where an infant promises to obey his parents, or where a mother promises to support her illegitimate child. 9 Cyc. 348.

Accordingly the promise to perform an existing contract is no consideration as the promisor is merely agreeing to do what he is already in duty bound to do. it is different where X abandons a contract because, for example, he is losing money in its performance or ran into difficulties not contemplated by the parties when the agreement was made. Many courts hold that under such circumstances a promise by Y to pay X an additional sum if he will go on with his contract is enforceable although X promised nothing that he was not already bound to do. 

61 Minn. 482. In personal service contracts, however, in order to constitute a

Had the employee promised to do something additional to his original contract, he could have recovered. Thus where a master offered the crew extra compensation to remain on an unseaworthy vessel, recovery was permitted on the ground that they were not bound under their original contract to remain on a vessel not seaworthy.

When they ended the Bank still had the stock and the defendant's note. 

Defendant had her husband's worthless note and nothing else. The agreed statement of facts proved that the company was insolvent. The stock then had no book value.

THE DISTRICT OF COLUMBIA STATUTE OF FRAUDS 

The English Statute of Frauds and Perjuries was enacted by Parliament in 1677, the 29th year of the reign of Charles II. It was designed to prevent the perpetration of frauds and the engineering of rackets and the temptation to lie on the witness stand as to the making of contracts. The 4th section of that statute has been adopted substantially in every state and the District of Columbia. The 

17th section relating to the sale of goods has not been so universally adopted. 

The District of Columbia statute of frauds corresponding to the 4th section is found in D. C. Code, S. 12 - 302. It is as follows: "No action shall be brought (1) whereby to charge any executor or administrator upon any special promise to answer damages out of his own estate. (2) or whereby to charge the defendant upon any special promise to answer for the debt, de fault, or miscarriage of another person, (3) or to charge any person upon any agreement made upon consideration of marriage, (4) or upon any contract or sale of lands, tenements, or hereditaments,

-- 
- or any interest in or concerning them, (5) or upon any agreement that is not to be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof, unless the agreement upon which such action shall be brought, or some memorandum or note thereof, shall be in writing, which need not state the consideration, and signed by the party to be charged therewith or some other person thereunto by him lawfully authorized."

The only difference between the District statute and the English statute (as to the 4th section) is the clause "which need not state the consideration" inserted in the last part of the District statute. The import of these words is that the memorandum or other writing under the District statute need not state the consideration whereas under the original English statute of 1677 the consideration had to be stated.

The effect of the operation of the statute is to prevent the enforcement of a contract unless the terms of the statute are met. Compliance with the form required by the statute does not constitute the contract because the statute always presupposes a valid contract in existence. If there is no contract then the necessity for a memorandum or note thereof is out of the question. The statute therefore affects only the remedy that may be brought on the contract and not its intrinsic validity. The effect of the statute is to render a contract merely voidable and not void. The statute of frauds operates as a bar to all actions brought upon oral contracts falling within its terms. Accordingly an action to recover damages for a breach cannot be maintained by either party against the other unless the benefit of the statute as a defense is waived or lost by failure to plead it or otherwise. In other words where an action is brought upon an oral contract that should have been in writing the court is bound to enforce it unless the defense of the statute is specially pleaded. 27 C. J. 337.

While the statute of frauds is equally binding upon courts of equity and courts of law, its requirements will be relaxed in equity where to enforce the statute will make it an instrument of fraud. The statute can only be used as a shield, never as a sword. 47 App. D. C. 13.

X, administrator of Y's estate, contracts with Z to pay a debt owing by the estate out of X's own estate. This contract is unenforceable unless in writing and signed by X or his agent. A man cannot be held liable upon any special promise to answer for the debt, default or miscarriage of another person. This clause deals with guaranty and not with suretyship. The difference between guaranty and suretyship is this: In a guaranty the guarantor is not primarily but only secondarily liable for the debt, default or miscarriage of the principal debtor, whereas in suretyship the surety is primarily liable in the first instance along with the principal debtor. The promise of a guarantor is a promise to answer for the debt, default or miscarriage of another but the promise of a surety is his own promise and not one to answer for the debt, default or miscarriage of another. The pro- mise of a surety is not within the statute of frauds and does not have to be in writing but the promise of a guarantor is within the provisions of the statute of frauds and must be in writing to be enforceable. Y takes X into a first class liquor store in Washington and says to the dealer, "Deal with X and if he does not pay you, I will." Y is the person secondarily liable but X is the one primarily liable. This is therefore a case of guaranty and not suretyship. Y cannot be held liable on his guaranty because it was not made in writing. But if Y should say, "Let X have all the booze he wants; I will see you paid," then Y's promise is original as he incurred the obligation directly and merely pointed out X as the source of delivery. This is neither a case of guaranty or suretyship and Y may be liable on his oral promise. A goes surety for B on a loan made by the Potomac

-- 
- River Bank, not in writing but via telephone. A is liable because his promise is that of a surety which is that of original liability. In other words where a promise is original it need not be in writing but it must be in writing where it is collateral.

An indemnity is not within the statute and need not be in writing because it is an original promise and not collateral to another promise. An acceptance of a bill of exchange is not a collateral promise to pay another's debt and is therefore not within the statute. 2 Wheat. 385.

A owes B$100 and B owes C a like amount. The three parties agree that A shall pay the $100 directly to C and that C will release B. This three party agreement constitutes a novation whereby one debtor is substituted for another and is good orally because A's liability to C arises out of a new contract and is an original undertaking. Similarly where A owes B $10 and A is about to be sued by 

B, a promise by C to pay B the amount due if he will not sue A is good orally because of the new contract between C and B involving a promise by C to answer for his own debt and not for that of another. 

The clause requiring a writing in order to charge any person upon any agrec-. ment made in consideration of marriage is most usually applied to marriage settlements. It does not apply to mutual promises to marry as such promises are not covered by this part of the statute, although they do fall within the clause requiring an agreement to be in writing that is not to be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof. A typical example where an agreement made in consideration of marriage is involved is a case where X becomes engaged to marry Miss Y in reliance upon the promise of her father to give X or the couple $10,000, a home, or something else in consideration of the marriage. Such promise cannot be enforced unless in writing.

The clause requiring any contract or sale of lands, tenements or hereditaments or any interest in or concerning them to be in writing deals with contracts to sell land as well as with the actual sale of land, or any interest in or concerning them such as leases, mortgages, deeds of trust, and the like. Such interests embrace both legal and equitable interests. An agreement to cancel a written contract for the sale of land must be in writing because the equitable title had already passed. Dower, curtesy, rents, and assignment of leases must be in writing. 

A contract for an abstract of title need not be in writing because it is merely an arrangement preliminary to the acquisition of an interest and therefore not an interest itself. The interest in or concerning the land must be substantial and real. The Code provides that no estate of inheritance or for life or for a term longer than one year in any real property, corporeal or incorporeal, in the District or any declaration or limitation of uses in the same, for any of those estates, shall be created or take effect except by deed signed and sealed by the grantor, lessor, or declarant, or by will. By virtue of this section an oral lease up to and including one year is valid without a writing. S. 45106. Every estate in real property for more than one year attempted to be created in violation of the foregoing section will be deemed an estate by sufferance. S. 12 - 301. The 

Code defines estates by sufferance as: "All estates which by construction of the courts were estates from year to year at common law, as where a tenant goes into possession and pays ront without any agreement for a term, or where a tenant for years after the expiration of his term continues in possession and pays rent and the like, and all verbal hirings by the month or at any specified rate per month." 

S. 45 820. 

---

Every express trust in the District of Columbia must be in writing or by will. 

But implied trusts such as resulting and constructive trusts may arise as at common law by parol or orally. S. 12 303. Products of cultivation (Fructus industriales) such as corn, wheat, oats, soybeans, garlic, tomatoes, escarole and other annual products which are produced by man to be severed are not considered interests in land. They are merely chattels or choses in possession. Contracts in writing are not required as to those chattels under the 4th section but they may fall under the provisions of the 17th section as to the sale of goods. On the other hand products of nature (fructus naturales) are considered interests in land such as growing trees and growing timber. Contracts relating to the same must be in writing. But where trees have been cut and are lying on the ground, a contract for their sale involves a contract merely of chattels. X contracts with Y whereby Y is to cut growing trees on X's land, take them to the market and sell them.. This agreement need not be in writing. 

There are four types of contracts usually found concerning interests in land, (A) A contract merely to sell land. This must be in writing. (B) A contract to sever from the land and sell as where X contracts with Y to sever growing timber and to sell it to Y. This need not be in writing because at the time of the sale the severed timber has been changed into personalty. (C) A contract giving a vendee possession to cut timber on land would fall within the statute and be required to be in writing, because the vendee would thereby acquire an interest in the land viz., the possession. (D) A contract may give a license to enter and cut without giving possession in which case the licensee would have no interest in the land and the contract would be good orally.

Suppose that the line dividing Blackacre and Whiteacre is vague and uncertain. 

X owns Blackacre and Y owns whiteacre. The parties orally agree to make the boundary between their lands a definite line. This contract need not be in writing because no interest in land is transferred. The parties are merely ascertaining and defining the boundary. It cannot be said whether Y gets a part of X's land or whether X gets a part of Y's land. Cavanaugh v. Jackson, 91 Cal. 580. But assume that the dividing line is certain and X and Y agree orally to make a stream instead of the former line as the boundary. The stream winds about the former line. This oral contract is no good because not in writing for the reason that it transfers interests in land. The new contract would transfer some parts of X's land to Y and some parts of Y's land to X. 132 Cal. 282.

A private sale of real property at public auction is within the statute of frauds. The auctioneer or his clerk is the agent of the purchaser and is authorized by him to sign his name in the sales book which contains the terms of sale; this entry in the book is a memorandum in writing signed by the person to be charged therewith. 4 D.C. 121. Sheriff's or marshal's sales (execution sales) are within the statute and must be in writing. 39 U. S. 84. But a judicial sale at public auction is not within the statute and no writing is necessary. 27 C.J. 230. 

Dedication of public lands to the public are valid without writing. 

If a contract cannot possibly be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof, it comes within the statute of frauds. Where a contract can possibly be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof, it does not come within the statute regardless of the actual time that perchance may elapse in performing it. The fact that an agreement may not be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof is not enough to require it to be in writing so long as it is possible that it could be performed within that space. 

When we speak of a contract as being within the statute of frauds we merely mean it must be in writing; when we speak of a contract as not being within the statute of

- frauds we merely mean it is good orally. An oral contract whereby X agrees to maintain Y for the life of Y is not within the statute and is good orally even though the contract may take fifty years to be performed from the making thereof. 

This contract could be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof by the death of Y and the agreement would thereby have been completely performed. A verbal promise by X to Y not to enter into the wholesale drug business in Washington, D. C., for ten years is good because while the parties contemplate a performance covering a period more than one year from the making of the agreement nevertheless X may die within the space of one year and the contract would thereby be fully performed within the year. Doyle v. Dixon, 97 Mass. 206. These cases must be carefully distinguished from agreements for personal service. X employs Y for ten years as the general manager of his brokerage firm. This con- tract for services cannot possibly be performed within less than ten years and therefore must be in writing because performance is not possible within the space of one year from the making thereof. It is true that Y may die within a year which would terminate the contract, giving no rights to X. But unlike the cases where 

X agreed to maintain Y for life, and where X promised Y not to enter into a certain business for ten years, the death of the employee in an employment contract, while terminating the contract, does not fulfill its terms and consequently is not a considered "performance." In other words where a party to an employment contract dies, although the rights of the parties are terminated, yet there remains an unfinished symphony. The rule is clear that contracts for the services of an employee which cannot be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof must be in writing because the death of a party thereto leaves such contract unperformed. His death is not a performance, but merely an excuse for nonperformance.

On July 1, X employs Y for one year, the performance to commence on July 2. 

Does this contract have to be in writing to be good? While the authorities are in dispute, it has been held in England and some of the States that a contract made on December 6, 1901 to work for a year commencing on December 7, 1901, is not within the statute of frauds and is good orally since performance will end on the day exactly one year from the date of making the contract. The reason given for such decision is that the law disregards fractions of a day and therefore does not count the day on which the contract was made. Smith v. Gold Coast Explorers, (1903) 1 K. B. 285; acc., Dykema v. Story & Clark Piano Co., 220 Mich. 600. This view has been recognized by the Restatement of Contracts in S. 198, Illus. 6. By the weight of authority, however, other courts discard the old fiction that the law disregards fractions of a day and calculate the period exactly, holding that such contract is for a year and a day and must therefore be in writing because it is not to be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof. 27 C.J. 186. 

Therefore, where X employed Y for one year on July 1, the performance to commence on July 2, the weight of American authority holds that such a contract to be good must be in writing. The majority rule counts the first day. However, if any case performance is to begin later than the day following the formation of the contract and is to continue for a year thereafter, all jurisdictions agree that the contract is within the statute and must be in writing. On August 1, A offers to employ B as his cashier for one year if B "will report for work within the next six months." B reports for work on January 1, five months later. This contract for one year is good orally and is not within the statute because it is an agreement to be performed within the space of one year from the making thereof, A's offer called for an act and not a promise and looked forward to the making of a unilateral contract which could only be accepted by B's doing the act viz., reporting for work. A contract is made when it is accepted. This contract was made therefore when B reported for work on January 1, five months later, and accordingly was to be performed within the space

of one year.

It should be noted in this example that the date of performance and the date of making the agreement are identical. In Warner v. Texas & P. Ry. Co., 164 U. S. 418, it was held that where a railroad company orally agreed to place and maintain a switch opposite X's mill as long as he needed it, provided he would grade the ground and supply the ties, that the agreement was valid although not in writing. 

The statute of frauds has been held not to apply to quasi-contracts or executed contracts. It is the executory contract which falls within the statute. Thus where an oral contract has been completely executed on both sides, the statute has no application. 149 U. S. 481. But where a contract has not been completely executed on both sides and only one side has performed either in whole or in part then the contract falls within the requirements of the statute provided action thereon is instituted at law. 27 C. J. 180. In equity, however, a case involving part performance will often be taken out from within the statute of frauds, particularly in oral land contracts.

X and Y contract orally where it should have been in writing under the statute. 

X performs but Y refuses. X, who has partly performed, may recover from Y not on the contract but in quasi-contract in general assumpsit on the common counts on the theory of unjust enrichment. 17 Me. 296.

A and B enter into a contract in writing that is within the statute of frauds but the contract is signed only by A and not by B. B may hold A because he is the only party who signed the writing and consequently is the only party who can be charged therewith; A cannot hold B because as B did not sign the writing he consequently cannot be charged therewith. This illustrates the exception to the rule of the doctrine of mutuality of obligation making it possible for a valid contract to exist between two parties wherein one party may enforce it and the other cannot.

To create a memorandum which will satisfy the District Statute of Frauds, either under the "4th" or "17th" sections, the parties and the subject matter must appear and the writing must show a contract between the parties. SEALED INSTRUMENTS Prior 

Anciently a contract depended entirely on its form for validity. Prior to 

1285 (St. Westminster II) an agreement was not binding unless under seal. Unsealed promises were enforced only after 1285 as a result of the St. Westminster II creating actions on the case, two of which are general and special assumpsit. to 1285 when only sealed promises were enforced there was no need for the doctrine of consideration because only the formal requirement of the seal was necessary to make a promise binding. Through the influence of chancery the doctrine of consideration was adopted in order to have a basis for the support of unsealed promises in actions of general and special assumpsit. It should be distinctly remembered that the doctrine of consideration arose subsequent to the use of seals and was invented only as a ground for giving validity to promises not under seal. 

With this in' mind, evidently the so called rule that "a seal imports a consideration" is incorrect because when sealed promises were enforced no one ever heard of consideration. Thus a promise under seal today will be enforced in the District of Columbia not because a seal imports a consideration but because of the ancient importance attached to a seal giving a scaled promise validity morely because of the form in which it was made. The two modern methods today of creating an express contract are either to (1) give a promise under seal or (2) furnish a valuable consideration for the promise.

As a rule, a court of law will not go behind a sealed promise but equity will go behind a seal in proper cases in order to ascertain whether a promise is based on a substantial consideration in order to prevent a mere seal from being used as an instrument of fraud.

There are two kinds of seals, public and private. Public or official seals are those used by public officers to authenticate public documents. Private seals are those used by private parties in the execution of personal or private matters. 

In the District sealed instruments are absolutely necessary in order to convey any interest in real property or any leasehold interest for more than a year. There must be an impression to constitute a seal at common law but today a seal may be either of ink, of wax or a wafer. It may be in one's handwriting or scrawled or printed. Merely signing one's name on a dotted line at the end of which appears a printed seal constitutos a sealed instrument. The most usual form today of a seal is found in the expression "(L.S.)" or "(Seal)." Any mark whatsoever after one's signature will be a scal provided it was so intended. One's seal may serve for any number of persons signing an instrument where each one adopts it as his own but it is better to place a seal after each signature.

A "deed" in its broadest sense signifies any written instrument under seal and is synonymous with "specialty." In its narrow sense, however, a "deed" denotes a grant of an interest in real property. A deed in the sense of a grant of an interest in real estate is not necessarily a contract. Where it merely conveys title to real estate it is nothing more than a "grant" or a conveyance. But where the same deed to real estate contains covenants then such an instrument is both a grant and a contract. A covenant is merely any promise under seal. A deed to real estate to be effective must be signed, sealed and delivered. Delivery must be accepted. At common law a deed poll was one executed by one party and the parchment on which it was written had smooth edges. An indenture was a deed made by two parties on which the matter was copied for each party on the same parchment or paper and the copies were then cut apart with indented edges, thus the term "indenture." By fitting the two parts together the deed could be identified. Today we no longer cut deeds apart but all of them have smooth edges and sometimes smooth language the rein. A deed poll means merely a deed signed by one party and an indenture merely means a deed signed by two or more parties. 

In the District of Columbia promises not under seal are good for three years but promises under seal are good for twelve years under the statute of limitations. D. C. Code, S. 12-201. X gives Y two checks for $10 each drawn on the 

Riggs National Bank. The first check is not under seal but the second one was signed under seal. The first check is good for three years and the second one for twelve years. In the District, the statute of limitations on a demand instrument commences to run on the day following the delivery of the instrument. 

Delivery of an instrument may be made in escrow. An escrow is a scroll, writing, or deed delivered by the grantor into the hands of a third person to be held by the latter until the happening of a contingency or the performance of a condition and then by him delivered to the grantee. 

A sealed instrument takes effect as of the date of delivery regardless of the date of the instrument and until otherwise proved the date of the deed is prima facie presumed to be the date of delivery.

REALTY OF CONSENT 

Where there is no reality of consent there is no contract. 

There are five usual situations where there may be no reality of consent. (1) Where the consent of one of the parties is extorted through actual or threatened violence (duress). (2) Where there is practiced undue influence by one party on another so that there is no real consent. (3) Where the parties did not intend the same thing or where one or both while intending the same thing have formed untrue conclusions as to the subject matter of the agreement. This is termed "mistake." (4) Where one of the parties formed incorrect conclusions regarding the subject matter of the contract because the other party made certain statements or suppressed certain facts. This is termed "misrepresentation. 

This is termed "misrepresentation." (5) Where the incorrect and untrue conclusions of one party to an agreement result from the intentional misrepresentation or active concealment of facts by the other. This is termed "fraud". It is thus apparent that the five situations where there may be no genuine consent are (1) duress, (2) undue influence, (3) mistake, (4) misrepresentation and (5) fraud.

A contract may be avoided on the ground that a party was coerced into it by actual or threatened violence. This constitutes duress and renders a contract not void but voidable. Duress includes all cases of unlawful interference with. the person or property of another in order to force him to enter into a contract. 

It consists in the actual or threatened unlawful exercise of power actually possessed or believed to be possessed by one man over the person or property of another from which the latter has no other way of immediate relief except by entering into the contract. 95 U. S. 210. It is not duress to threaten to bring a civil action by X against Y if Y does not enter into a proposed contract nor is it duress to threaten to take possession of one's property by means of a judicial writ unless there is an attempt at the same time to abuse legal process, But threats of criminal prosecution made to compel another to enter into a contract is duress. 53 Kans. 

664. Where A is arrested on a bastardy charge and voluntarily marries the prosecutrix in order to escape punishment he cannot have his marriage annulled on the ground of duress. "shotgun marriage" constitutes duress and the marriage is voidable at the option of the victim. The duress must affect the promisor and not some third person, but a husband or wife may avoid a contract for duress on the other. 88 Wis. 188. A parent may avoid a contract for duress to his child and vice versa and the same holds true as to brothers and sisters. Bryant v. Peck, 154 Mass. 460.

Undue influence in the law of contract is the same as undue influence in the law of wills. Undue influence is merely the substituted will of another and not the will of the one speaking. Undue influence is a species of fraud which does not necessarily include the elements of deceit. It is an unconscionable use of the power arising out of the circumstances and conditions of the parties. Where a prima facie presumption of undue influence arises from the relative positions of the parties, the contract will not stand unless the one claiming the benefit of it can establish that it is fair, reasonable and just. This presumption may arise also from circumstances as well as from the relation of the contracting parties, as is evidenced by the rule that equity will not enforce a gratuitous promise even though it is under seal. In equity inadequacy of consideration is viewed as one element in raising the presumption of undue influence or fraud but does not in itself constitute proof of either. A presumption of undue influence will often be raised because of the relations of the parties such as relations between doctor and patient, attorney and client, broker and client, priest and penitent, husband and wife, parent and child, uncle and niece, etc. The presumption may be raised in other cases where it would be just and equitable to do so as where a person advanced in years deals with a youth or where a city slicker contracts with a country bumpkin. The law strives to guard persons against those who would exploit their ignorance, credulity, moral weakness, poverty, or other exposed situation, Even the hardboiled common law protects expectant heirs from extortionate rates of interest. In all such instances the dominant party will be required by equity to establish good faith and honesty. Undue influence gives one the right to avoid a contract similarly as in cases of fraud but with this difference: Affirmation after knowledge of the fraud is absolutely binding, but in case of undue influence a party will not be bound by his affirmation unless it is clear that the undue influence under which he labored was entirely removed at the time of affirmance, There are several instances of mistake. We will deal here only with mistake of intention and not mere mistake of expression. Mistake of expression pertains to the interpretation of contracts an example of which is where the parties are mutually agreed on the terms but they are by mistake so expressed in the contract as not to convey their meaning. In such cases the mistake may be corrected

Mistake of intention is where the parties did not intend the same thing or where one or both although intending the same thing form untrue conclusions as to the subject matter. 

Mistake as to the nature of the transaction arises in the execution of written instruments and is usually based on the misrepresentation or deceit on the part of a third party. An example is where X who is illiterate or blind or ignorant conveys his property to another by deed which is misread or misdescribed to him. Such an instrument is void where it is shown to be a different instrument from the one which he was led to believe it was. Although such cases come within the designation of mistake they really constitute a species of fraud. But where a man can read and does not read the instrument which he signs or if he is unable to read and signs without requesting that it be read to him, he will be estopped to deny that the contract is void if it is different from what he thought it was. 

116 Fed. 545. There is no premium in law upon foolhardiness, Mistake as to the subject matter of a contract occurs in three instances, viz., (1) mistake as to the existence of the subject matter; (2) mistake as to the identity of the subject matter and (3) mistake as to the nature and essential qualities of the subject matter: (1) If the contract is in regard to a thing which unknown to both parties does not exist at the time the contract is made the contract is void because the nonexistence of the subject matter goes to the very heart of the contract. (2) A mutual mistake as to the identity of the subject matter results in no contract. The famous "Peerless" case exemplifies this. A agreed to buy a cargo which was to arrive on the ship "Peerless" from Bombay, India.

It later developed that there were two ships of that name sailing from Bombay but at different dates. The vendor had one ship in mind and the vendee had the other. Held: no contract on the ground that the parties were not thinking of the same thing. (3) As a rule where the mistake occurs as to the nature and essential qualities of the subject matter it is immaterial. In other words the motives which induce the assent do not affect the validity of the reality of assent once given. X sold Y a little round white stone for 2 which he found while shucking an oyster. The little stone turned out to be a rare pearl worth $2,000. The contract was binding. 64 wis. 265. On the other hand where X makes a mistake in dealing with Y and Y knows of 's mistake, he has no right to take advantage of it. 

In other words, the law will not permit X to accept a promise made by Y when he knows Y intended to say something else which he failed to do by inadvertence or mistake. A merchant quotes a price of 6¢ to a regular customer on a nationally known package of cigarettes that regularly sells for 154. The customer knows that the merchant misquoted the price by mistake and tenders 6¢ in payment which is refused. No contract merely because the customer knew the merchant made a mistake in quoting the price. But where the customer does not know that the merchant has erred he can hold him to his promise.

A mistake as to the identity of the person with whom the contract is made may avoid it as where X makes a contract with Y under a belief that it is being made with Z and Y knows that X has mistaken his identity. Where a man intends to contract with one person, another cannot make himself a party to the contract by substituting himself. Here there is a contract only with X but not with the real owner because Y intended to deal only with X, the man at whom he was looking. But if X were merely representing an undisclosed principal then the exception to the rule applies that Y could hold either X or his undisclosed principal at his election and conversely Y would be liable either to X or X's undisclosed principal. A, falsely representing himself to be

As between A and C, who was deceived into believing A to be B, there is no contract because C intended to deal with B and not with A. Now suppose that A, having thereby obtained possession of the gems, sells them to K who buys them in good faith and for value. As between C and K, C loses and K prevails because A had title (which was voidable and not void) and could pass it on to an innocent purchaser for value. C and K are both innocent parties but C is less innocent because it was his credulity that enabled A to perpetrate his fraud on K. 

The rule is that as between two innocent parties (C and K) the one less innocent bears the loss.

X contracted with Y to erect an office building for Y for a consideration of $250,000. The contract is good although equity might consider the case on ground of hardship. Brown v. Levy, 69 S. W. 255. 

A mistake in inducement or motive which leads X into entering a contract is no ground for avoidance thereof. Where X leased a parcel of land under the mistaken idea that it contained oil he is bound by his lease even though there is no oil on the land. X mistakenly orders 48 cans of goat's milk from Macy's in New York whereas he actually intended to order only 8. While he was writing the order he was highly excited and worried because he had just received a letter that some of his relatives were going to visit him for a few weeks, all of which contributed to his inadvertence. After the acceptance of the order by Macy's, X has no ground for relief and is fully liable.

The doctrine of caveat emptor, although not so virile as it was in the 19th century, is still a rule of law properly applicable. The courts, however, will be quick to seize upon any species of fraud in order to prevent outright exploitation under whatever guise. A mere shrewd business transaction, usually termed "business acumen" by economists, is permissible. 

A unilateral or bilateral mistake of law is no ground for avoidance of a contract. In this connection it must be remembered that the law of a sister state or of another country or jurisdiction is a question of fact and not a question of law. 15 Me. 45. Generally where X informs Y (both laymen) as to the law on any certain point, it will be regarded as a matter of opinion upon which Y relies at his peril. 33 Ill. 237. In some cases, however, a court will set aside a contract or give other adequate relief where, although one party laid down the law to the other and the latter relied upon it, it can be established that it was laid down with intent to cheat, fool, deceive and defraud. In other words, a party does not rely on statements of law made to him at his peril where they were made with a design to deceive.

- A misrepresentation means an innocent misstatement or a nondisclosure of facts that should have been disclosed. Misrepresentation differs from fraud in that fraud consists of knowingly making false representations. Fraud gives rise to a tort action for deceit but an innocent misrepresentation merely gives rise to a breach of contract. A wilful or deliberate misrepresentation is a fraud. A representation differs from conditions and warranties. Conditions and warranties are representations constituting terms of the contract but representations, usually referred to as "mere representations," do not constitute terms of the contract and as a rule have no effect on a contract except in the case of contracts said to be involving "the most abundant good faith" (uberrimae fidei). 12 La. 134. Mere misrepresentation, as an exception to the rule, does affect certain contracts said to be uberrimae fidei, in which cases, because of their nature or peculiar circumstances, one party is obliged to rely on the other for his knowledge of the facts. 

The other is accordingly bound to the utmost good faith. These exceptional contracts are: (1) Contracts of marine, fire, and life insurance. (2) To a limited extent, contracts for the sale of land. (3) Contracts with promoters of a corporation for the purchase of shares. (4) Contracts between persons occupying a confidential relation as between principal and agent, guardian and ward, attorney and client, trustee and cestui que trust, etc. Where a misrepresentation has any effect at all, the effect is to render the contract voidable. Generally, & material misrepresentation is ground for granting or denying relief in equity, A representation is an affirmation by words or conduct of a matter of fact by way of inducement to the making of a contract.. 

As to marine insurance, the Supreme Court of the United States holds that the contract of insurance is one of mutual good faith and the principles governing it are those of enlightened moral policy. The underwriter must be presumed to act upon the belief that the party procuring insurance is not at the time in possession of any fact material to the risk which he does not disclose. Every fact which would influence the rejection or acceptance of the risk by the insurance company is material and must be disclosed. Any material concealment will avoid the policy even though resulting from mistake or accident. In fire insurance contracts, the description of the premises is a representation on the truth of which depends the validity of the contract, but it has been held that "not so high a degree of good faith and diligence is required in fire insurance as in marine insurance," The rule in marine insurance that the insured is bound, without being asked, to disclose every fact within his knowledge material to the risk, is not fully applied to fire insurance, Thus it has been held in 135 Mass. 505 that where an applicant for fire insurance fills out the inquiries submitted in writing and innocently fails to communicate or disclose facts about which he was not asked, the policy of insurance is not avoided, 71 N. Y. 508. As to life insurance, the true rule is luid down in Vosc v. Ins. Co., 6 Cush. 42, wherein the court said that an untrue allegation of a material fact will avoid the policy although such allegation or concealment be the result of accident, design or negligence. A representation material to the risk will avoid the policy whether made falsely or innocently; on the other hand, a false representation not material to the risk will not avoid the policy as where the insured lied about his age. Age is not material to the risk in life insurance and the policy may be enforced, with deductions allowed to the underwriter according to the true age. On the contrary, an innocent misrepresentation as to one's previous condition of health is material to the risk and will avoid a policy. Campbell v. Ins. Co., 98 Mass. 396; Phoenix v. Raddin, 120 U. S. 183.

- Although contracts for the sale of land are not strictly uberrimac fidei yet they come within the rule to a certain extent. In contracts of this nature a misdescription of the premises sold or the terms applicable though made without fraud will vitiate the agreement. 13 Pet. 26. But a purchaser of land is not bound to disclose facts within his knowledge which render the land worth much more than the price he offers. Also, although hidden defects in the title should be disclosed by the seller, yet if the seller has said or done nothing to throw the buyer off his guard or to conceal a visible defect, there is no fraudulent concealment on the part of the seller; the doctrine of let the buyer beware applies on the ground that the buyer had an opportunity of investigating for himself. 

As to contracts to purchase shares in corporations it has been held that those who issue a prospectus holding out to the public the great advantages which will accrue to persons who will take shares in a proposed undertaking, and inviting them to take shares on the faith of the representations therein contained, are bound to state everything with strict and scrupulous accuracy, and not only to abstain from stating as fact that which is not so, but to omit no one fact within their knowledge, the existence of which might in any degree affect the nature, extent, or quality of the privileges and advantages which the prospectus holds out as inducements to take the shares. Peek v. Gurney, L. R. 6 H. L. 403. A New York case held that the promoters of a corporation occupy before its organization a trust and confidential relationship towards those whom they seek to induce to invest in the enterprise. 122 N. Y. 349.

A representation to be a representation must be a representation of fact. 

Expression of mere opinion does not constitute a representation. Business "baloney" and commendatory expressions ordinarily used to entice parties to buy are not considered misrepresentations of fact but are deemed mere expressions of opinion or "business puff." Such expressions of opinion to induce a buyer to buy or a seller to sell are even permitted in the exceptional contracts just discussed. It should be remembered, however, that a misrepresentation of one's present state of mind is as much a misrepresentation of a present fact as a misrepresentation of the present state of one's liver. Furthermore a statement of opinion regarding facts peculiarly within the knowledge of the maker of the statement may amount to a representation that the maker of the statement knows facts justifying his opinion, 104 Mich. 625.

Although a representation of law ordinarily is harmless, yet a statement of fact simultaneously involving a conclusion of law is considered a statement of fact and may be actionable if misrepresented. 168 Mass. 333. Where X occupies a fiduciary relationship to Y or where X has greatly superior means of information, some cases hold that Y, as an exception to the rule, is entitled to rely on X's statements regarding the law in which case the representation of law is given the effect of a representation of fact. 35 Ala. 434. 

Where a representation does not merely affect the formation of the contract but is a part of the contract itself then such representation becomes a warranty or a condition. Where a warranty or a condition is false the contract is voidable at the option of the injured party or he may sue for breach of contract for damages, the measure of which is the difference between what he got and what he should have gotten at the time and place of performance. A mere representation is a statement or assertion made by one party to the other before or at the time of the contract of some matter or circumstances relating to it. Although a mere representation is ofttimes found in a written instrument yet, being a mere representation, it is not an integral part of the contract and the contract is not breached

in the event of the falsity of the representation. 

But if such "representation" is an integral or substantive part of the contract, going to the heart, root or essence thereof, then it is not a mere representation and amounts to a condition or warranty. This involves a question of law (construction) for the court to decide.

It is sufficient to avoid a contract if the misrepresentation is the material inducement thereof. 94 Ill. 475. It must be shown that the party to whom the representation is made was thereby induced to enter into the contract, that he was reasonably ignorant of the facts and reasonably and in good faith relied on the representation to his damage. In 154 Mass. 286, it was held that where a report of the concerns of a body corporate were filed with the Secretary of State as required by law, it did not amount to a representation upon which anyone was justified to act because it was made to the Commonwealth, and to no one else, by compulsion of statute. A representation must be acted upon within a reasonable time after it is made and in many cases it is required that the one relying must exercise ordinary care and diligence to discover for himself facts obvious to an ordinary and reasonable observer. This is particularly true where the means of knowledge is equally available to both sides. Where one fails to use such ordinary diligence he will be estopped to deny that he was misled. 13 Wall. 379.

Where X had deceived Y, X has no right to defend on the ground that Y was a fool or that he should not have trusted X, if Y innocently relied on X's misrepresentations to his damage. Where a misrepresentation is actionable the contract is voidable, furthermore, such misrepresentation is a defense to an action at law on the contract or it may serve as a foundation in chancery for rescission or reformation.

Fraud will vitiate any contract. Fraud in contracts is the same as fraud in torts as embodied in an action of deceit. To constitute fraud in law: (1) A false representation of a present fact must exist. (2) The falsity must be known. (3) A false representation recklessly made without regard to whether or not it is true or false is equivalent to making such statement knowingly with intent to deceive. (4) It must be made with intention that the statement should be acted upon by the one for whom it was intended. (5) And such party must have innocently relied thereon and have been misled thereby to his damage. 

A mere innocent nondisclosure amounts to no fraud. There must be present some active attempt to deceive either by a false statement, or by a representation, true as far as it goes, but in the company of such a suppression of facts as to make it convey a false notion. Where a party is under a duty to disclose and fails to disclose, it is fraud.

Where fraud is practiced in a contract, the injured party may sue in contract and waive the tort or he may sue in tort and waive the contract. If he sues in contract at law he sues for a breach thereof and may recover only money damages. 

If he sues in tort at law he must sue in an action of deceit and may recover only money damages. Thus the defrauded party in contract has a double right. On discovery of the fraud he may affirm the contract, retain the thing sold, and sue for damages on account of fraud; or he may decide to rescind the contract and defend any action brought against him to enforce it. In equity the victim is entitled to judicial avoidance of the transaction or, in a proper case, specific performance, cancellation, rescission or reformation.

LEGALITY OF THE SUBJECT MATTER 

Ordinarily the parties may contract in any way they choose with reference to the subject matter or object of the agreement. But this liberty or freedom of contract may be limited in three ways: (1) by the common law; (2) by statute; or (3) on grounds of public policy. Thus the object of an agreement may be illegal either by the written or unwritten law or forbidden on grounds of public policy. A contract for an illegal object, although possessing every requisite of a valid contract, will be considered null and void. On the other hand, where an agreement is performable in two ways one of which is illegal and the other legal, in the absence of proof to the contrary, the law will presume that the legal way was intended. 

A particular object may be legal in one jurisdiction and illegal in another; or, it may be illegal in one jurisdiction by common law and legal in another by statute. The United States and the states have myriads of statutes regulatory of infinite lines of business and other conduct particularly as to child labor, weights and measures, food, liquor, drugs and cosmetics, safety appliances, sanitation, slaughtering of animals, fair prices, unfair competition, wages and hours, labor problems, and the like. Although these regulatory statutes interfere with the right of liberty to contract they are invariably upheld, where reasonable and not otherwise unconstitutional, under the sovereign right of police power, In 114 Mass. 433, a merchant was denied recovery for meal which he sold by the bag in violation of a statute prescribing that it be sold by the bushel. A father cannot recover for the services of his minor child who was employed in violation of a child labor statute. 

13 R. I. 299. No recovery may be had for the price of liquor sold on Sunday in violation of a Sunday statute. 145 U. S. 421, Following New York, Congress enacted a law for the District of Columbia making it a misdemeanor to label or brand or sell or offer to sell within the District any meat as "kosher" which is not kosher. D.C. 

Code, S. 22-3404.

At common law and in the District of Columbia today, Sunday contracts are valid. 

District of Columbia v. Robinson, 30 App. D. C, 283. But Congress and the Commissioners of the District of Columbia, acting within the police powers, may provide otherwise, such as prohibiting sales of liquor on Sunday, amusements, etc. Siddons v. Gaming contracts such as wagers or bets are illegal and void and so is any agreement which has for its object the furtherance of a wager or bet. The agree- ment, however, must be so closely connected with the illegal object as to make the contractor a party to it. Where X lends money to Y, a gambler, knowing Y to be such and suspecting that he will use it for gambling, X may nevertheless recover because his mere knowledge that Y is a gambler or suspicion that he might use the money for gambling is not sufficient to make X a party to Y's illegal act. But where X lends money to Y for the express purpose of gambling the contract is void and X cannot recover, D. C. Code, S. 16-701, provides that contracts growing out of gaming transactions are void. But in S. 16-702, et seq., it is provided that under certain circumstances gaming losses may be recovered.

An example of this is found daily in transactions in the Chicago grain pit. 79 Ill. 351. It is immaterial where one of the parties contemplates an actual delivery but the other one does not.

Is is an old and settled principle of common law that contracts in unreasonable restraint of trade are illegal and void on the ground that they are contrary to public policy. Two gas companies who enjoyed equal and exclusive rights under a municipal franchise agreed to combine and apportion the city between them for the purpose of avoiding competition and raising prices. Such contract is null and void as being in promotion of monopolies and in restraint of trade. Chicago Gas Light 

Co. v. People's, etc., Co., 121 Ill. 530. On the other hand it has been held that stevedores, who owe no public duty, may legally apportion their business, although gas companies or other public utilities may not, 66 N. Y. 288. But where either private persons or public utilities are simply trying to obtain a monopoly and force prices, their contracts are void. The question is always whether the agreement, considering the nature of the business, imposes an unreasonable restraint upon trade.

Where the object is simply to secure freedom from competition and inflate prices, the contract will usually be declared void. 44 Fed. R. 721, The 

Sherman Anti-Trust law is an example where the federal government has acted in order to enforce the common law doctrine prohibiting contracts of monopolies and in restraint of trade. Violations of the Sherman law are made criminal, but the statute applies only to contracts of an interstate nature. 

Restrictive agreements of a certain character have long been recognized as valid. In 131 U. S. 88, it was held that the manufacturer of a patent medicine based on a secret formula may enforce a contract between him and an employee to keep the formula secret. Contracts not to engage in business will be upheld provided the restraint is a reasonable one. But such contracts unlimited in time or space are illegal as are those which impose unnecessary burdens. 

X was engaged in the manufacture of bed quilts. He sold his business to Y and promised not to engage again in the business. The contract was held illegal and void as being in unreasonable restraint of trade and as imposing an undue hardship upon X never again to follow his occupation. In Diamond - Match Co. v. Roeber, 106 N. Y. 473, an agreement not to manufacture matches except in Nevada was sustained on the ground that it was reasonable. The scope of the business must be considered. Where the business sold is purely local to a city or town or even a part of a large city, a promise not to engage in the same business within an area much larger than is necessary to protect the business is void. An agreement by a language teacher not to teach for one year in the state of Rhode Island was held illegal on the ground that it was broader than necessary to protect the interest involved. Champerty is a bargain made by a stranger with one of the parties to a suit or action by which such third person undertakes to carry on the litigation at his own cost and risk, in consideration of receiving, if he wins the suit, a part of the land or other subject matter sought to be recovered by the action. Champerty is the carrying on a suit in the name of another, but at one's own expense with the view of receiving as compensation a certain share of the avails of the suit. Where the agreement is not one to conduct litigation in the courts in the ordinary sense, but merely to recover a claim from the government, it cannot be "champertous," 

Wardman v. Leopold, 66 App. D. C. 111. Champerty differs from maintenance. In champerty the compensation to be given for the service rendered is a part of the matter in suit or some profit growing out of it, while in simple maintenance the question of compensation does not enter into the account. In maintenance the interfering party is in no way benefited by the success of the party aided but simply intermeddles officiously. Every champerty includes maintenance but not every maintenance includes champerty. Where X a stranger to Y promises to pay all expenses if Y will bring an action against Z, the agreement is illegal because it constitutes maintenance. But if X were interested in the suit or related to Y the contract would be valid. An attorney at law agrees with A to sue B, pay all the expenses of litigation and, if successful, receive as his compensation a certain percentage of the proceeds recovered from B, otherwise nothing. This constitutes champerty as well as maintenance. Merlaud v. Nat. Met. Bank, 65 App. D. C. 385 (1936). The contract is champer tous because the attorney, being a stranger to the litigation, is really purchasing an interest in the subject matter of the suit. 

The rule as to champerty has been generally relaxed in most jurisdictions and in the District of Columbia so that today it is perfectly legal for an attorney at law to handle litigation on a contingent basis provided the client pays the expenses of suit. If the client is without financial means, there is no objection to the attorney's advancing or lending the client the funds necessary so long as the attorney himself is not bearing such expense, Champerty and maintenance are both common law misdemeanors. As to the effect of illegality, the law will not generally interfere to aid either party to an illegal contract, but will leave them to stew in their own juice and enjoy the predicament which their own illegality brought about, regardless of

justice or injustice between them. 

To this rule there are some exceptions. One exception is where the contract is divisible. If it be possible to separate the illegal part from the legal part the courts will often permit recovery on the legal portion. Suppose X contracts to pay Y $2,000 for his stock in trade and also $500 for an agreement by Y never again to reengage in any form of business. Y's promise in the second part of the contract never to reengage in business is void because it is unreasonable. But the first part of the contract is valid and can be enforced because its value is fixed by the agreement of the parties at $2,000, a definite figure. Even where the consideration is entire as where X agrees to pay a lump sum of $2,000 for Y's goods and Y promises never to reengage in business, yet if X is willing to pay the entire consideration, the $2,000, for the stock of goods he is entitled to insist that Y carry out the legal part of the contract and deliver the goods. One who promises to do an illegal and a legal act in consideration of a lump sum cannot recover for the legal act unless it can be separated from the illegal act. X was employed as a waiter in a billiard hall conducted by Y. The conduct of the billiard hall was a legal enterprise but in addition to this Y was engaged in the illegal sale of whiskey on his premises. X's contract provided that he was to perform services for Y in both the billiard (legal) and whiskey (illegal) business. The amount which he was to receive for the legal act was not separated from the amount he was to receive for the illegal act. The court held that X could recover nothing at all. 51 N. H. 402. Similarly, where X agreed to pay Y $500 if 

Y would construct a concrete walk across X's land and also secure the dismissal of an indictment for rape against X, it was held that the promise to secure the dismissal of the indictment was highly immoral as tending to defeat the administration of the criminal law and, since the consideration was not apportioned, X could not recover against Y for not constructing the concrete walk; if Y had constructed the walk he could not, under the circumstances have recovered against X. 78 N. C. 328. 

Thus where parties enter into a contract involving legal and illegal acts and the consideration is not apportioned to these acts, the nonperformance of the legal act will not give rise to a right of action where the illegal acts are of a highly immoral character.

Very often a statute imposes a penalty for doing a certain act but does not declare the doing of such an act void. It then becomes necessary to ascertain the intent of such statute. It may be a mere "revenue provision" which in effect says to the parties that they may do the act upon paying the price named in the statute. 

On the contrary the provision may be intended as a punishment for the act which is also regarded as illegal. As a rule the test is said to be that (1) where the statute imposes a penalty for an act or omission, this is prima facie evidence of intention to prohibit; (2) if the object of the penalty is the protection of the public, it amounts to a prohibition; (3) but if the object is solely for revenue purposes, the act or omission is not prohibited. Thus a statute may render an agreement illegal by express prohibition or by imposing a penalty without an express prohibition. Where a statute expressly provides that a violation thereof shall constitute a misdemeanor, a contract in violation of it is illegal although the statute does not expressly prohibit the contract or declare it void. It has been held that the owner of a building who is guilty of a misdemeanor in failing to equip it with fire escapes as required by statute cannot maintain an action for the rent thereof. 116 Minn. 299. The law in this regard does not make any distinction as to statutory prohibitions between acts which are mala in se and mala prohibita. 

Some cases hold that whenever a statute imposes a penalty for an act or omission, it impliedly prohibits it, but the weight of authority holds that the imposition of a penalty is only prima facie evidence of an intention to prohibit. 

The intention of the legislature always governs. It is always important to determine whether the object of the penalty is protection to the public and not

merely for revenue.

If the penalty is designed to further the interests of public policy it amounts to a prohibition, but if it is designed purely for the sake of revenue a contract in violation thereof is not necessarily prohibited. Where the penalty is recurrent upon every breach of the statute the inference is that the penalty amounts to a prohibition. 106 Ky. 472. The absence of a penalty or the failure of the penal clause in a particular instance will not prevent the court from giving effect to an express prohibition. 

The purpose of the parties in entering into a contract is immaterial where it is clear that if the agreement is carried out it will violate the law. Accordingly where the object of a contract is unlawful the agreement is void regardless of the intention of the parties. 197 U. S. 244.

X was endeavoring to make a composition with his creditors; all had agreed to accept 104 on the dollar and release their claims, except Y. Y refused to enter into the composition unless he was given an additional compensation which X paid him. X recovered the money back from Y on the ground that Y exerted undue pressure on him under the circumstances. Miss A entered into a bargain with a marriage broker whereby she paid him $1500 in return for his promise to find her a first class husband. He did obtain a husband for her whom she later had to divorce on the ground that he was just "no good." Subsequently she successfully brought an action to recover back the fee paid to the broker on the ground that contracts of such character are prima facie induced by undue influence. 124 N. Y. 156.

X turned over money and property to Y in order to cheat X's creditors. The transaction had not been consummated. A makes a bet with B just before a federal election as to who will be elected president of the United States. The money is put into the hands of C, a stakeholder. At any time before the money is paid over by C to the winner, either party, on notifying the stakeholder that he has revoked the transaction, may recover back his deposit; if C does not pay it over after such notice, he is liable. This is on the theory that recovery tends to discourage illegality. These cases bring out the rule that in some instances there is a locus penitentiae or a place to repent,

and if the party does not let this opportunity slip away, the law will forgive and aid him.

A contract valid in one state is valid everywhere else unless in the state wherein enforcement is sought it is contrary to public policy. 21 Fed. 299. 

CONTRACTS FOR THE BENEFIT OF A THIRD PARTY 

Generally a contract cannot confer any rights on one who is not a party to it because of lack of privity. 27 Yale L. R. 1008. The rule is subject to apparent exceptions. (1) Where the contract is such as to constitute the promisor a trustee for the benefit of the third person, the latter may sue in equity on the theory of a trust. (2) where money or other property has come into the promisor's hands by virtue of a contract for the use of a third person, the law creates a contractual relation between him and the third person upon which the latter may maintain an action on the theory of a quasi contract. (3) Apart from the foregoing, in most of the states an exception is allowed in case of a contract made for the benefit of a third person permitting the third person to sue at law thereon in his own name. This may best be illustrated by the English doctrine, the Massachusetts doctrine, the New York doctrine and the Supreme Court of the United States doctrine. 

15 Harv. L.R. 767.

ENGLISH DOCTRINE. Under the English doctrine the third party for whose benefit a contract is made cannot maintain an action thereon in his own name. The reasons are that the third party gave no consideration for the promise, was not a party to the contract, and no privity exists between such third party and either of the parties to the contract. Under the English rule the third party beneficiary of a contract acquires no rights ex contractu even in equity, but if the contract is so framed as to make one of the parties trustee for a third person for whose benefit it is made, then the English rule permits such third person to sue in equity as a beneficiary merely by virtue of the trust. In England there must be some declaration of trust by one of the contracting parties in favor of the third person in order to permit the third person to enforce such trust in equity. The English doctrine also allows a third party to sue in a quasi contractual situation. A more contract between two parties that one of them shall pay money to a third does not constitute that third person a cestui que trust. 25 Ch. Div. 104.

MASSACHUSETTS DOCTRINE. Massachusetts recognizes the English doctrine and accordingly a third party for whose benefit a contract is made cannot sue on the contract because he is a stranger to the consideration. 157 Mass. 410. Under the 

Massachusetts doctrine, however, a third party will be permitted to sue where in reality the contract was so framed as to make the promisor a trustee for such third person, in which event the latter may enforce the trust in equity. 95 U. S. 576.

In addition to this there is another apparent exception in cases where, under a contract in which a promise is made for the benefit of a third person, assets come to the promisor's hands or under his control which in equity belong to the third person. In other words Massachusetts will permit the third party to sue in those cases in which the defendant has in his hands money which in equity and good conscience belongs to the third person. An example would be where one person receives from another money or property as a fund from which certain creditors of the depositor are to be paid and promises, either expressly or impliedly, to pay such creditors. 107 Mass. 37. England would not permit the third party to sue in such case. 

The theory of recovery, however, is not based on rights arising out of the express contract between the promisor and the promisee, but arises in quasi contract out of a promise implied in law from the promisor to the third person. 103 Mass. 556.

Massachusetts also recognizes quasi contractual rights of a third party. 

NEW YORK DOCTRINE. Under the New York doctrine it is held that a third party for whose benefit a contract has been made may recover thereon in his own name provided the situation involves the debtor-creditor, and not the sole beneficiary, type case. Lawrence v. Fox, 20 N. Y. 268. In that case A lent B a sum of money in consideration of which B promised to pay the same amount to C at a later time. C was a creditor of A and A was the debtor of C. C brought suit against B and in that celebrated case it was held that C could recover against B, although C was a stranger to the consideration, was not a party to the contract, and there was no privity between him and B. Various theories have been brought forth to support this radical departure from the common law by the courts of New York. The soundest one is based on the reasoning that the third person should have the right to sue in his own name because he is the sole person injured by a breach of the contract. It should be observed that Lawrence v. Fox involves the debtor-creditor type case which merely means that the parties were related to each other as creditors and debtors. Where the third party is a mere sole beneficiary he cannot maintain an action under the New York doctrine because such doctrine is limited to the debtor-creditor situation. 

Thus A and B contract with each other that each will pay C the sum of $100 when C passes the state bar cxaminations. C in this case would be a sole beneficiary and could not maintain an action under Lawrence v. Fox. By the weight of authority it is held that, under the New York rule, there must be something more than a merc incidental bone fit to the third person. The contract must have been entered into directly for his benefit and he must have some legal or equitable interest in its performance. X owns Whiteacre which he mortgages to A. Later X conveys to Y. 

Y does not assume the mortgage, i.e., he does not promise X to pay A. Y conveys to 

Z who does assume the mortgage, i.e., Z promises Y to pay A. A cannot recover on 

Z's promise to Y because this is not a debtor-creditor type case since Y was not indebted to A. Vrooman v. Turner, 69 N. Y. 280. This case holds that where the grantor (Y) is not personally bound to pay the mortgage upon the granted premises to the mortgagor (A), as where he (Y) has purchased subject to the mortgage but without assuming it, his grantee (Z) does not, by assuming the mortgage and thus promising his grantor (Y) to pay the mortgagee (A), become personally liable to the mortgage (A). It must appear that Y was under an obligation to pay A; in other words, there must be some privity between the one to whom the promise was made (Y) and the person to be benefited (A). There was no such privity between Y and A. 

Of course, A may proceed against the property itself under the mortgage. tract between Y and Z only incidently benefited A. Now suppose that A mortgages land to C and then conveys the property to B receiving as part of the consideration a promise by B to pay the mortgage debt to C. C may recover against B because the contract was made directly for C's benefit and there was privity between the one to whom the promise was made (A) and the person to be benefited (C). This situation is the same as in Lawrence v. Fox.

Grand Lodge, 98 U. S. 123. This line of cases does not recognize the rule in Lawrence v. Fox and accordingly will not permit the third party to recover in a debtor-creditor type case. A person is a sole or donee beneficiary if the purpose of the promisee in obtaining the promise of the promisor is to make a gift to the beneficiary or to confer a right upon him against the promisor to some performance neither due (nor supposed) or asserted to be due from the promisee to the beneficiary. Thus, A would like to see Y have a new car. A contracts with X for a consideration whereby X promises A to deliver a new car to Y. Y is a sole beneficiary. On the other hand a person is a creditor beneficiary, under the New York rule, if no intention to make a gift appears from the terms of the promise and performance of the promise will satisfy an actual (or supposed) or asserted duty of the promisce to the beneficiary. An incidental beneficiary is neither a donce beneficiary nor a creditor beneficiary but would merely receive an incidental benefit if the contract between the parties were carria out. 

An incidental beneficiary can never recover.

At one time it was held in England, Massachusetts and a few other jurisdictions that where a third person for whose benefit a contract was made is "closely related by blood" to the promisee, he can recover. Dutton v. Poole, 2 Levinz 210 (1677).

But England has repudiated this "close relationship" doctrine and so has the majority of the state courts. Tweddle v. Atkinson, 1 B. & S. 393 (1861); 5 L.R.A. 43.

Most states permit the third party to recover in "public contract" cases. Such cases are deemed of the creditor beneficiary type (Lawrence v. Fox) since the city or state (promisee) to which the promise of the promisor runs is under a legal duty or obligation to the third party in certain instances for the discharge of which the third party has given the city or state a valuable consideration in the form of taxes. A promisor under a public contract to render service to members of the public is under a legal obligation to compensate members of the public for injurious consequences caused thereby. Thus, where a water company contracted with the city not to charge more than an agreed rate to the residents thereof, X, one of the residents, successfully enjoined the company from raising water rates in violation of the contract between the company and the city. It must appear, however, in such cases that it was the intention of the contracting parties to impose a duty on the company as to each resident of the city (183 N. Furthermore, where one promises the city to render services, the nonperformance of which will subject the city to damage actions by those injured by such nonperformance, the injured third party may sue. X. 75 N. Y. 231.

It is often held that the promisee may release the promisor from his obligation provided it is done before the third party has accepted or acted upon the promise, but not afterwards. 40 N. Y. 432. But where the promisor has a good defense against the promisee, it will be good against the third party. X agrees with Y to pay Z. If X's promise was induced by Y's fraud, Y can prove this in an action by Z. The majority rule is, however, that after the right of the third party once arises, it cannot be destroyed without his consent, whether he has changed his position or not being immaterial. 122 111. 91.

The third party gets an immediate right whether he knows of the contract for his benefit or not. 248 Ill. 568. The third party need not be identified when the

contract is made provided he can be identified at the time the promisor breaks his promise. 183 N. Y. 330.

A third party is presumed to have accepted & promise made for his benefit on the ground that everyone will accept one, but within a reasonable time after learning there of he may disclaim and renounce the benefit unless such action on his part be a fraud on his creditors. Such disclaimer will be retroactive as from the beginning of the contract.

As to contracts under seal it has been held at common law that the third party cannot sue on the ground that the doctrine is limited only to simple contracts. 

But this rule has been modified by statutes and decisions thereunder which permit the third party to sue on the sealed contract. Where he is allowed to sue on sealed instruments a question usually arises as to whether the defendant is limited to the defense of an action of covenant or to that of assumpsit. Under the new federal rules of civil procedure, the defendant may plead any defense whatever, equitable as well as legal.

In the District of Columbia and in most jurisdictions, it is required that all actions shall be brought in the name of the "real party in interest." In the 

District it is so provided by the federal rules of civil procedure. Rule 17(a). 

This common provision in the so-called "code" states seems properly to have little bearing on the question whether the third party is the "real party in interest." 

This depends on a question of substantive law and most courts hold that, in spite of the code provision, the third party cannot recover merely by reason of such procedural requirement, but that his right to recover depends on whether or not in fact a contract was made for his express benefit. A contract made for the benefit of a third party must be carefully distinguished from a novation. In a contract made for the benefit of a third party there are only two parties to the contract and the third party is legally a stranger to the consideration. In a novation, however, all parties are parties to the contract and there is privity of contract between the three of them. The aim of a novation is to substitute another right for an existing obligation. In a contract made for the benefit of a third party, while the aim might be to substitute another right for an existing obligation, nevertheless the third party does not give his assent by being a party to the contract. A owes B and B owes C. All three agree that B shall be released of his obligation to C and that C will accept A as his debtor in place of B. This is a novation. But where A and B merely contract that B would pay A's debt to C and C was not a party to the contract, the result is merely a contract for the benefit of a third party.

ASSIGNMENT OF CONTRACTS 

Although a contract ordinarily binds only the parties to it, yet these parties may by their own conduct or by operation of law be replaced by other parties who in turn assume their rights and duties. The operation by which such a change eventuates is termed an assignment of the contract. 

Only rights may be assigned; liabilities may not. A person cannot assign debts or liabilities. Where X owes Y $100 for medical services, X cannot assign his liability to another without the consent of Y; but if Y consents, a new contract results, but no assignment. A person contracts with regard to the credit and character of a particular person and if this person could substitute another in his

---

stead to make good his liability then there would be no safety or security in contracts. X leased a delivery truck to Y for five years at a yearly rental. X agreed to paint the truck every year and keep it in good repair. Z was a partner of X, but Y contracted with X alone. Three years later X retired from business. 

Y was notified that thereafter Z would be answerable for the painting and the repair of the truck and would receive the payments due. It was held that Y rightfully refused to deal with Z and could return the truck on the ground that X had no right to assign his liability under the contract to Z, even though he was his partner sinc the contract was not made with the partnership but with X individually. 

There is an exception to the rule that liabilities cannot be assigned where the performance (liability) requires no special knowledge or skill and where it does not appear that the one owing the obligation has been selected with reference to any personal qualifications. Thus, where A contracts to paint X's home, X cannot complain if A procures the work to be done by K, an equally competent person. 

A, however, would be liable for the poor work of K on the ground that while he could delegate his duties to K, A nevertheless remains liable for a result, viz., to give 

X a workmanlike performance. Similarly, where A contracts to cut Y's grass, Y cannot complain if A substitutes K for himself to perform the obligation; but A would remain liable to Y for the faithful performance of his obligation. 

It is generally recognized that X may assign to Y the right of X to receive payment from Z on the ground that it is immaterial to Z whom he pays so long as he is thereby discharged.

At common law rights arising out of a contract were not assignable so as to enable the assignee to sue in his own name. The old rule was thus expressed that "a chose in action is nonassignable." The exception at common law existed, however, as to negotiable instruments by virtue of the law merchant. But assignment of rights in contract gave the assignee merely equitable rights recognizable in chancery; subsequently, however, the common law permitted an assignee of a contract right to sue in the name of his assignor. 

Equity courts permit the assignment of rights in contract whether those rights are legal or equitable. If the rights are equitable, the assignee may sue in equity in his own name; but if they are legal, equity will enable the assignee to sue in his own name only if he cannot do so at law. Equity will give no relief to an assignee where the latter may proceed at law unless the remedy at law is not plain, complete and adequate. To enforce an assignment in equity there must be a consideration given by the assignee for the benefit received. In addition, the one who is liable must be given notice of the assignment before it can take effect. Where X assigns his right against C to B and C pays X before notice of the assignment is delivered to him, C is discharged from liability. But if C pays X after B has given him notice, then C is still liable to B and will thus have to pay a second time. 

Of course, as between the assignor, X, and the assignee, B, the assignment may always be enforced. Wade v. Security Sav. & Com. Bank, 69 App. D. C. 226.

There is a distinction between a novation and an assignment. Strictly, the only mode by which contractual rights may be really transferable at law is not by an assignment but by means of a substituted agreement termed a novation. If A owes 

B $500 and B owes C $500, it may be agreed between all three that A shall pay C instead of paying B, so that B thereby terminates his legal relations with both parties. In such instance the consideration for A's promise is the discharge of B; the consideration for B's discharge of A is the extinguishment of his debt to C; the consideration for C's promise is the substitution of A's liability for that of B. This is a novation. 7 N. H. 397. To effect such a change of relations, there

must be ascertained sums due from A to B and from B to C, and it is further necessary that there shall be a definite agreement between the parties, because it is the promise of each which is the consideration for the promise of the others. It would be insufficient for A to merely say to C, "I will pay you instead of B" and to later inform B of the arrangement and obtain his assent. Nor would it be enough for B to authorize A in writing to pay to C and for A to acknowledge the paper in writing. In neither of these instances would there be such an agreement between all three persons as to constitute a novation. There would be no consideration for 

A's promise to pay C so as to support an action by C against A. An assignment is really a form of novation. The particular difference is that in a strict novation the change in relationship is solely by a voluntary act of the parties whereas in an assignment it may be either by the voluntary act of the parties or by operation of law without the voluntary consent of the parties. 

An assignee takes an assignment subject to all defenses and equities that the debtor may have against the assignor. Where X assigns to B his right against C, if X has no right against C then the latter may defend on that ground in a suit against him by B. The rule is the same in law and in equity that no person may acquire from another something that the other does not have. Therefore, where X assigned his rights against C to B, if X had no rights against C then B, the assignee, gets only what X had, which is nothing. An assignee stands in the shoes of his assignor and gets no more and no less than what his assignor had at the time of the assignment.

As a rule anything which directly or indirectly involves a right of property may be assigned. There is no doubt that mere money demands are assignable. An interesting situation arises in the case where X agrees to sell an article to B who agrees to pay there for on a day certain. The problem is whether or not X, who has the the undoubted right to assign his right of payment, may also assign his duty or liability to perform. The general rule is that he cannot. Where one endeavors to make a purported assignment of a liability he nevertheless remains as fully liable as before to the one to whom his duty is owing. One cannot escape a duty or liability by merely assigning it to another. 

Personal service contracts cannot be assigned. But one who has made a contract to render personal services may assign his right to receive pay for them after they have been rendered by him. Personal service contracts are not assignable because they do not survive the death of either party. Although the death of a party to most contracts passes all rights of action for breach of contract and all other attendant liabilities to his personal representatives, contracts of personal service are the exception to the rule. In personal service contracts, the death of one of the parties terminates all liability. Thus, where X was employed by Y as a law clerk for one year and Y died within the year, Y's estate is not liable for breach of contract. On the other hand, if X contracted with Y whereby Y was to build a house for X and Y died before the completion of the contract then Y's estate would be liable for the nonperformance. A fortiori, where a contract for personal service involves personal skill there can be no assignment nor delegation of the duty to perform. On the other hand, an obligation to perform merely ministerial duties involving no phase of the personal equation may be delegated but the delegator is not released from his primary duty to perform. In There is no particular form prescribed by which to execute an assignment. the District of Columbia, however, the Code provides, in Section 28 - 2501, that no assignment is valid unless in writing. Nevertheless, it has been held that where the parties intended an assignment but failed to put it in writing, equity has jurisdiction in a proper case to decree an equitable assignment in order to prevent

injustice. Ordinarily words of assignment consist of "sell, assign and transfer," or "sell, assign, transfer and set over." The word "assign" is usually sufficient. 

There are two classes of cases involving the question of assignment of future earnings. (1) Where there is an existing contract of employment it is generally held that future earnings as to that employment are assignable. 58 Vt. 553. Thus 

C is working for A by the week. C may assign to B his last week's salary. 

He may also assign to D his next week's salary under his contract of employment provided the contract extends to such next week. (2) But if A discharges C this week or if 

C terminates his employment with A and begins work with another employer, then the assignment of next week's earnings is not valid as to any earnings due from the new company. In such event a new assignment would necessarily have to be executed after the contract was made with the new employer because this new contract was not an existing contract of employment at the time of the original assignment. The rule is that one cannot assign future earnings not based on an existing contract. Such earnings are deemed a mere expectancy. A mere expectancy or a hope of gain in the future is not assignable on the ground that you can only assign what you have and you cannot assign what you do not have. Where an assignment read, "Shad Roe hereby assigns his wages due and to fall due from any and all present and future employments," it was held void. 61 N. H. 641.

The parties may agree by the terms of their contract that it shall not be assignable or that it may be assignable with the written or oral consent of one of the parties. Such a provision is usually found in leases in general usage in the 

District wherein it is provided that the tenant may not assign without the written consent of the landlord. If the agreement merely prohibits an assignment, then, it has been held by the District Court of Appeals, the tenant has the right to sublet, and vice-versa. To protect himself, the landlord should call for an agreement whereby the tenant agrees not to sublet or to assign. 

By the great weight of authority assignments of salaries by public officers, as well as of pensions, are inoperative. D. C. Code, S. 28 2503. 

Generally, a tort right is non-assignable. Thus a right of action for a purely personal tort such as libel, slander, seduction, assault and battery, false imprisonment, negligence and the like is not assignable. A right of action for breach of promise is not assignable on the ground that while suit is framed in terms ex contractu yet in substance it is in the nature of a claim ex delicto. It is the rule at common law and in the District, regardless of the Code, that a right of action for trespass to property is assignable. The general rule is that all rights of action in tort for injury to one's property, real or personal, or estate, are assignable. 6 Wall. 548. It has been said that the test of assignability as to a tort right depends on whether such right of action arising from the tort will, upon the death of the person injured, survive to his personal representatives. If it will, it is assignable; otherwise, it is not. 197 Fed. 780. 

By the common law, rights of entry reserved for breach of condition subsequent are inheritable but not assignable either before or after condition broken. 97 U.S. 

693. A right of entry is not assignable before condition broken because the grantor, having granted away his entire interest in the land, has left only a possibility of reverter, which is no more an interest or estate in the land than the possibility of its reconveyance is to him; the reason why it is not assignable after condition broken is that such an assignment of a mere right of action is in the nature of a tort and is regarded by the law as promoting maintenance and therefore against public policy.

A right of entry reserved in the grant of an easement upon condition constitute: an exception to the rule and is assignable by a conveyance of the servient tenement. 

The general rule now is that any estate or interest in real property may be assigned whether the estate is legal or equitable, vested or contingent. It is provided in D. C. Code, S. 45 - 815, that expectant estates shall be descendible, devisable and alienable in the same manner as estates in possession. This applies to personal property as to real property. Under this section it would appear that a right of entry is freely assignable in the District today. 

The practice of purchasing from prospective heirs, devisees, or legatees interests in property which have no present existence, either actual or potential, but which such persons expect to acquire by descent or by will is known as "catching bargains with heirs." 6 Cyc. 702. At common law the validity of an assignment of a mere possibility or right which has no present existence, either actual or potential, is void at law. Therefore, the assignment by a prospective heir or devisee of his expectancy of acquiring an estate by descent or by will is no good. 42 Ala. 

255. In the United States, however, the general rule is that an expectant heir may in the lifetime of his ancestor sell, assign, or release his expectant interest in the estate of the ancestor whatever it may turn out to be on his death and equity will enforce the contract upon a showing that it was made in good faith and for a valuable consideration. 201 Fed. 682; D. C. Code, S. 48 - 815. 

The law covering assignments is found in D. C. Code, T. 28, ch. 25. S. 282501 provides that a judgment or money decree may be assigned in writing and upon the assignment thereof being filed in the clerk's office the assignee may maintain an action or sue out a scire facias or execution on said judgment in his own name as the original plaintiff might have done. S. 28 2502 provides that any bond or obligation under seal for the payment of money may be assigned under the name and seal of the obligee therein named and the assignee may sue thereon in his own name. 

It should be noted that every assignment in the District must be in writing, but an assignment of a bond or obligation under seal for the payment of money must, in addition, be under seal. S. 28 - 2503 enacts that all nonnegotiable written agreements for the payment of money, including nonnegotiable bills of exchange and promissory notes, or for the delivery of personal property, all open accounts, debts and demands of a liquidated character may be assigned in writing but that claims against the United States or the salaries of public officers are not assignable. 

Where the assignee has his assignment in the form required by the statute he may sue thereon in his own name. S. 28 2504 provides that in case of a general assignment which shall include choses in action, it is not necessary to execute a separate assignment of each chose in action, but the assignee by virtue of the general assignment may sue on the several choses in action in his own name. Claims against the District of Columbia are not assignable, neither may the District be garnisheed. 

Chewning v. District of Columbia, 69 W. L. R. 541 (1941). 

A partial assignment is not recognized at law in the absence of consent on the ground that it would be a burden on the debtor to determine the relative rights of the substituted parties. Sincell v. Davis, 24 App. D. C. 218. Thus if A owes B $100, B may not assign to C a claim against A for $10, to D for $80 and the balance to E. But the law will recognize such partial assignments if A consents. 142 Mass. 366. On the other hand, equity may recognize a partial assignment where notice has been served.

It is important to understand the law as to priority of assignments. Where 

A assigns to B his claim against C and subsequently assigns the identical claim to Y, the question is whether B's rights are superior or inferior to those of Y. The

Supreme Court of the United States has held, followed by England and a number of states, that the assignee who first served notice on the debtor has the prior right. 

Therefore if Y served notice first, ho would prevail over B. "Assignment" and "negotiation" are entirely different. 

A negotiable instrument is assignable, negotiable or transferable, but a nonnegotiable one is merely assignable. Negotiation means a transfer in the form and manner prescribed by the law merchant or the N.I.L. By transferring a negotiable instrument the transferor may give more rights than he actually possesses to the transferee, but an assignor can never give more rights than he possesses to his assignee. 

An assignment will not bind the debtor unless the latter has notice thereof. 

But a transfer of a negotiable instrument binds the maker or drawer or other parties liable thereon regardless of notice of the transfer. An assignee is subject to all the defenses that may be raised against his assignor, but a transferee of a negotiable instrument is not subject to all the defenses (where he is a holder in due course) that could have been raised against his transferor.

It is generally held that prior to verdict or judgment a cause of action for death by wrongful act is not assignable because the beneficiary's interest is a mere expectancy and not a debt. 229 Fed. 765.

An assignment may arise by operation of law as well as by act of the parties. 

Thus, rights arising from contract are transfered or assigned by operation of law to others in the case of interests in real property which run with the land and when by the death or bankruptcy of a person his representatives acquire his rights and liabilities for limited purposes. Accordingly, agreements or covenants in a lease which "touch and concern the thing demised" as to repair and the like pass to the assignee of the lessee whether or not expressed to have been made with the lessee and assigns. 31 Minn. 75. Upon the death of a person there passes to his personal representative all his personal estate, all choses in action affecting it, and all liabilities chargeable thereon. Covenants affecting the freehold pass by operation of law to the devisee of the land or the heir. A breach of contract which involves purely a personal loss such as a breach of promise to marry, or personal service and the like gives no right of action to the personal representative. The rights of a trustee in bankruptcy or of an executor or administrator furnish the leading example: of an assignment by operation of law. 

CONTRACTS THAT ARE JOINT, JOINT AND SEVERAL, AND SEVERAL 

It is not essential that there be merely one party on each side of a contract. 

There may be more than one promisor and/or more than one promisee in any contract. (1) A may contract with B; (2) A may contract with B and C, or more persons; (3)

A and X (or more) may make an agreement with B alone; (4) A and X (or more) may contract with B and C (or more). The question in such instances is to solve the respective rights and duties of A, B. C and X. In the first instance where there is one promisor and one promisee we are not involved in the inquiry because there is only one party on each side. The second situation, however, has one promisor and two promisees; the third situation has two promisors and one promisee; and the fourth involves two promisors and two promisees.

The characteristics of joint promisees, therefore, must be determined as at common law. In order to avoid confusion in identity of the terms "promisee" and "promisor," it is to be remembered that for purposes of legal analysis it is impossible to distinguish between the two until an action is brought; once action is brought, the party designated the "promisee" is always the plaintiff and the one designated the "promisor" is always the defendant, who is being sued on his promise. A and B are joint promisees and C is the promisor.

Where a contract is in such form that the promisor can execute his part of the contract by doing either one of two acts, the contract is in the alternative. 

Ordinarily the one who has promised to do the act (promisor) has a right to decide or elect which act he will perform. X gave Y his nonnegotiable note for $1,000. 

The note provided that it might be paid in good leather of a certain description and at stipulated prices. This is clearly a contract in the alternative to either pay the $1,000 or the specified leather at certain valuations. Held, X (promisor) had a right to elect which he would do at or before the note fell due and that his failure to make such an election gave Y the right to elect as Y might wish. Accordingly, Y elected to sue for the money and recovered. 7 Ala. 775. On the other hand, the rule that the promisor has the right to elect is not applied where the circumstances indicate that the parties intended differently. A purchased merchandise of B, the latter telling A that he could pay in either six or nine months. After six months, A having failed to pay, B brought action. Held, A was not liable at this time because his failure to pay in six months was an election to adopt the alternative of nine months. When the alternative acts are to be done at different times, a failure to do the first act in order of time will be considered an election to do the second. 

Where one of the alternative performances becomes impossible then the promisor is nevertheless duty bound to the other. X owed money to Y on a note. The safe was destroyed by fire through inevitable accident. Y was held liable for its value under the alternative promise. 

If the safe had been morely delivered to Y for security, he would not have been liable in this case because he was not guilty of negligence. 117 Mass. 10. 

THE INTERPRETATION AND CONSTRUCTION OF CONTRACTS 

The rule in the famous case of Swift v. Tyson, 16 Pet. 1, 10 L.Ed. 865 (1842) that the federal courts are not bound by the decisions of the local courts on questions of general commercial law has been definitely overruled by the now more famous case of Erie Railroad v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 82 L.Ed. 518, 114 A.L.R. 

1487 (1938). Federal courts are now bound by the local statutes and local decisions of both the superior and inferior courts of the state in which the case is tried. 

The rule is now consequently well settled that in matters of general commercial law, a federal court is bound to follow local state law. 

After a contract has been proven according to the rules in evidence, a court may be called upon to interpret and construe it. On the other hand where there is no vagueness, doubt or ambiguity, and the terms are clear, precise and certain, then there is no need for interpretation and construction. But where it is necessary to interpret and construe an agreement, there are certain fundamental rules to aid

the court in determining the intention of the parties. It is basic in the law of contracts that the intention of the parties governs. This does not mean, however, that the court will execute the psychological intentions of the respective individual parties but rather that it will ascertain their combined intent evidenced by the contract itself. Reed v. Ins. Co., 95 U. S. 23.

There are three cardinal rules of construction. (1) Ordinary words are to be understood in their plain, ordinary, and literal meaning and technical words are to be technically construed in their technical sense. The application of this rule may lead to results uncontemplated by either or both parties. The rule is subject, also, to the admissibility of evidence of usage. (2) The court should interpret and construe a contract so as to best give effect to the intention of the parties. 

The sole purpose of interpretation and construction is to ascertain the intention of the parties, otherwise the court would be wrongfully making a contract for the parties other than the one they intended. (3) The intention of the parties should be ascertained from the entire instrument or transaction. There are five leading subsidiary rules to the ones just enumerated. (a) Obvious errors in grammar and writing will be corrected by the court. This implies also that the punctuation of a document, though it may assist the court in interpreting and construing it, will not control or change a meaning which is plain and unambiguous as derived from a consideration of the entire document under the circumstances. (b) A court will construe a contract so as to render it reasonable rather than unreasonable where it may possibly do so without overriding the apparent intention of the parties as gathered from the whole writing. (c) The meaning of general words will be narrowed by particular and specific descriptions of the subject matter to which they apply. 

Where there is a conflict between written and printed language or words, the written material controls. Sturm v. Boker, 150 U. S. 312. (d) That meaning of a word or language will be adopted which will make the instrument valid. Where a contract is susceptible of two constructions, one of which will render it absurd or unlawful and another which will render it reasonable or lawful, the court should adopt that construction which will render the agreement reasonable or lawful as the case may be. United States v. Central Pac. R. R. Co., 118 U. S. 235. (e) A court will construe the words and language of a contract most strongly against the party who employed them. The reason for the rule is that one is responsible for the ambiguity of his own expressions.

LIQUIDATED DAMAGES AND PENALTIES. Where there is any doubt in the mind of the court whether a provision amounts to liquidated damages or a penalty, it is the duty of the court, if it can reasonably do so, to construe the provision to be one calling for liquidated damages and not a forfeiture or penalty. In ascertaining this intention of the parties, the court will not be governed by the name given to the sum fixed by the parties in the contract. Sheffield v. Paul T. Stone, Inc., 68 App. D. C. 378, 66 W. L. R. 489 (1938). Thus a provision in a contract calling for "liquidated damages" has been construed to mean a penalty and was held unenforceable. On the contrary where the parties provided for a "penalty" and in reality it was merely a provision for liquidated damages, it was held enforceable based on the true intention of the parties. It is a well settled and uncontradicted rule that the law looks with disfavor upon private penalties and forfeitures and will endeavor to construe stipulations for liquidated damages as penalties or forfeitures when the amount on the face of the contract is greatly disproportionate to the probable loss. In Dean v. Nelson, the Supreme Court of the United States held that the test as to whether a provision calls for a penalty or liquidated damages is not the intention of the parties, but the magnitude of the sum compared with the subject matter. 10 Wall. 158.

It is only where the agreement is ambiguous that room is left for speculation as to the real intent of the parties. 54 App. D. C. 261. When the question arises by which law of several possible jurisdictions contract rights are to be determined, the rule is that the law of the lex fori governs merely as to the remedy, pleading, practice, and other adjective law, but whatever goes to the substance of the obligation and concerns the rights of the parties as growing out of the contract itself is governed by the lex loci contractus, unless intended otherwise. Willard v. Wood, 15 D. C. 538; 29 App. D. C. 538. The general principle is that a contract is to be governed by the law with a view to which it was made; this is a question of intention to be deduced, when not expressly declared, from the terms, character, place, and purposes of the transaction. Fisk Rubber Co. v. Muller, 42 App. D. C. 49.

The con- struction of a contract does not depend upon what either party thought but upon what they agreed. 84 U. S. 19. In construing a written contract, the whole instrument must be taken together in order to arrive at the intention of the parties. 82 

U. S. 94. A court is not justified in construing an instrument where only brief extracts therefrom are submitted to it. Fishback V. Reeves, 6 App. D. C. 76. Where a contract can by its terms be lawfully performed, it will be treated as legal even though performed in an illegal manner. 9 App. D. C. 71; 63 App. D. C. 150. Where writings are executed simultaneously and relate to the same transaction or subject matter, they must be construed together. 33 App. D. C. 142. Where the meaning of a deed of trust, executed to secure a bond, is doubtful, the bond is admissible to explain the deed. 18 D. C. 320. In construing a contract, the court has the right to consider the subject matter, the situation of the parties and surrounding circumstances. 63 App. D. C. 175. In construing an ambiguous contract, the practical interpretation of it by the parties themselves is entitled to great, if not controlling, influence. Consaul v. Cummings, 24 App. D. C. 36. Where recitals containe in a contract are ambiguous and the operative part is not ambiguous, the operative part must prevail. Where two agreements between the same parties related to the same subject matter and one of the agreements incorporated three letters between the parties, the five writings must be read together and in relation to each other in order to determine the rights of the parties. Crowell v. Gould, 68 App. D. C. 

297. Where a new contract is made in regard to the same matter and has the same scope as an earlier contract, and the terms of the two contracts are inconsistent, either in whole or in substantial part, so that they cannot subsist together, the new contract abrogates the prior one in toto. Wardman v. Wash. Loan and Trust Co., 67 App. D. C. 184. 

PERFORMANCE OF THE CONTRACT 

CONDITIONS. A promise may be (1) absolute or (2) conditional. X may promise to perform his part absolutely under any and all circumstances or he may promise to perform it conditionally. It is important, therefore, to ascertain the difference between independent promises and dependent- promises. An absolute promise is an independent promise and a conditional promise is a dependent promise. 

An absolute or independent promise is one entirely unconditional upon the performance by the other party. Example: X agrees to pay Y $5,000 on January 10th and Y in consideration thereof agrees to deliver an elephant to X on January 20th.

X failed to pay on January 10th whereupon Y sued for the money on January 12th. X pleads in defense that he has not received the elephant. The defense is not good because X's promise is independent of Y's promise. Using the test of the intention of the parties, it is clear that X was to perform on the 10th and Y on the 20th and X's promise was therefore not dependent in any way upon performance by Y. A dependent promise is one conditional upon performance by the other party. Example: X and 

Y agreed to sell and buy a baby carriage for $200. It will be presumed in the absenc

of evidence to the contrary that X intended to give the carriage only when he received the money and that Y intended to pay the money only when he received the article. This means that unless otherwise intended the law will construe promises as dependent rather than independent. Using this rule, the transaction between X and Y will be presumed a cash transaction. X could recover on Y's promise only by offering to perform and vice versa. 

A promise is not necessarily a condition nor does the same legal consequence result from a failure to perform a promise as in the case of the nonperformance of a condition. X promises to paint Y's home and Y promises to pay X when the work is done. If X does not fulfill his promise, he is liable to Y for damages. But if X does perform, he ordinarily must perform fully before he may recover from Y on Y's promise. Furthermore if X does not perform at all, he can never recover anything from Y on his promise. In other words Y's promise is dependent and subject to a condition, viz., the prior performance by X; the promise of X, on the other hand, is not subject to a condition. "Whether covenants be or be not independent of each other must depend on the good sense of the case, and on the order in which the several things are to be done." Morton v. Lamb, 7 T. R. 125. Hence the order in which the things are to be done is an accurate test for determining whether promises are absolute or not. When it appears that one of two covenant or promises is to be performed at an earlier date than the other, the rule is simple and uniform that the covenant or promise that is to be per formed first is independent and absolute, while the one that is to be performed last is dependent, the performance of the former being a condition precedent to the performance of the latter. 

A condition is a fact or event which must precede some change in the legal relations of two or more parties. A condition is a fact or event upon which, according to the intention of the parties, the contract depends. The fact or event must be uncertain but it is enough if that uncertainty exists in the minds of the contracting parties. Generally, the fact or event must be future as well as uncertain but a past or present event may be a condition if it is unknown or uncertain in the minds of the parties. This is based on the same line of reasoning which upholds the validity of a compromise based on an unfounded but honestly believed claim.

It is frequently difficult to determine whether a condition prescribed by the parties is to precede the existence of the contract or whether the contract comes into existence, subject to being defeated by the happening of the fact or event. This implies the important difference between conditions relating to the very existence of a contract and those relating merely to the performance of a contract already in existence. The happening of an event may be necessary, therefore, before there can be any contract. Example: X cannot recover on Y's parol promise to pay him $100 unless there bo a consideration for the promise. This is a condition imposed by law. The condition in such instance, however, may be prescribed by the parties as where X promises Y to pay him $100 if Y will promise to walk up to the top of the Washington Monument. In such case X is not bound by his promise unless Y gives his promisc. When Y gives his promise a contract comes into existence. An example where the condition does not role to to the very existance of a contract is where X employs Y so long as Y continues to reside in the District of Columbia; this results in a contract but subject to termination if Y should ever violate the condition subsequent. 

Conditions may be (1) express, (2) implied in law, and (3) implied in fact. 

As to their nature and effect, conditions may also be classified as (1) conditions precedent, (2) conditions concurrent, (3) conditions subsequent, and (4) conditions independent. An express condition is one expressly stated or written by the parties

in explicit terms. Words of condition are not necessary to create an express condition so long as the parties evidence such an intention. Thus, where X agrees to repair Y's violin and Y agrees to pay X on condition that the work is fully completed by July 15, the condition on which the payment by Y depends is an express condition by a fair construction of the contract evidencing the intention of the parties. On the other hand, a condition implied in fact is one that is not expressed in the contract. A condition will be implied in fact where such a condition, though unexpressed, is necessary to the performance of that which is expressed in order to carry out what the parties have agreed upon. The ground for implying a condition in fact is based upon the reasoning that such must have been intended by the parties. Where Y promises to perform work under the direction of an architect, there is a condition implied in fact that an architect will be appointed; and until such architect is appointed, performance by Y is excused. 

For all practical purposes, it is generally held that conditions implied in fact are treated the same as express conditions. The thought running through express conditions and conditions implied in fact is the same as that in express contracts and contracts implied in fact; one is just as good as the other. A condition implied in law is based on the same theory as a quasi contract which is a contract implied in law. 

Hence a condition implied in law is one which is not an express condition or a condition implied in fact, but a condition that as a matter of fact neither party intended. Unlike other conditions, conditions implied in law are supplicd by the courts on grounds of public policy in order to prevent injustice. Conditions implied in law (also known as constructive conditions) are not implied from the facts of the case but are imposed by the law arbitrarily as external rules of law. These conditions are based on equitable doctrines and the theory underlying them was invented by Lord Mansfield, one of the greatest English judges. Scholars report that the doctrine of conditions implied in law was first applied in the famous case of Kingston v. Preston, 2 Doug. (Eng.) 689 (1773), wherein D promised to sell his business to P. The latter promised to pay the price and to furnish good security. 

Without furnishing such security, P sued D for breach of the contract to sell. 

Lord Mansfield held in that case for D on the ground that P's promise to give security was a condition precedent, implied by the law, to D's obligation to sell. 

Express conditions and conditions implied in fact are true conditions and apply both to unilateral and bilateral contracts; conditions implied by law or constructive conditions apply only to bilateral contracts. 

Express conditions and those implied in fact demand strict performance but a condition implied in law may be satisfied by substantial performance; only a material breach of a condition implied in law will excuse performance by the other party. Where there is doubt as to whether a condition is implied in law or is implied in fact, the doubt should be resolved in favor of the condition implied in law on the ground that such a condition is more just. 

A condition precedent is an event or act which must be performed by one party or exist before any duty arises on the other party to perform. Where X agrees to repair Y's watch, for which Y is not to pay until X completes his performance, X must complete his performance before he has any right to payment from Y under the contract. Y is not liable on his promise until the condition precedent to his liability, viz., performance by X, is completed. X's promise is absolute and independent whereas Y's promise is dependent on and subject to a condition precedent.

Concurrent conditions and mutually dependent conditions are the same. The act which must precede liability on one side is a tender of performance on the other. If a druggist agrees to sell 

X a bottle of perfume and X agrees to pay $25 there for, the druggist must tender the

The view. may also be taken that conditions concurrent are only a species of conditions precedent. Applying this view to the case just cited, payment or tender of payment by X is a condition precedent to the liability of the druggist and, conversely, delivery or tender of delivery of the perfume by the druggist is a condition precedent to the liability of X. 14 Yale

Where X and Y made mutual promises that X shall pay $100 to Y and that Y shall pay $100 to Z, it was held that such a situation does not imply conditions concurrent, or mutually dependent conditions, because it is not necessarily evident that the performances were to be simultaneous and between the parties. 

A condition subsequent is where an existing obligation to perform is to be determined or ended by some event or act. In every condition subsequent situation, liability has already been incurred, but may be terminated or defeated by the performance of the condition subsequent. A condition subsequent will, upon its happening, relieve the promisor from liability under the contract. An example is where 

X, in a bond, promises to pay Y $5,000 and the bond provides that if X faithfully performs his promise then the bond is to be null and void. In such a contract liability falls upon X immediately to pay the specified sum to Y, but upon X's performance his liability is terminated. A mortgage or a deed of trust is another leading example of a condition subsequent. The condition subsequent is known as a defeasance clause and upon the happening of the condition liability terminates. 

In the law of contracts it will be implied wherever possible that conditions are concurrent as between conditions concurrent and conditions precedent; as between conditions precedent and conditions subsequent, however, conditions precedent will be implied. 13 C. J. 564 et seq. In the law of fire insurance policies, however as between conditions precedent and conditions subsequent, the doubt will be resolved in favor of conditions subsequent. A condition subsequent may be tested to see whether it is in reality a condition subsequent. The way to do this is to try to put it in the form of a condition precedent or a condition concurrent. If this can be accomplished, and the intention of the parties can also be fulfilled, then the condition is not a genuine condition subsequent. 

Miss X contracts with Y in a bilateral contract that she will marry Y provided passes the District of Columbia Bar Examinations before October 1, 1944. This case involves a condition precedent to the liability of X. She is under no obligation to marry Y unless the condition happens within the time specified. It will be noticed that in every condition precedent situation there is no liability when

the contract is made, but will arise only on the happening of the condition. X contracts with Y to work for Y as his private secretary so long as Y should remain married. This promise is on a condition subsequent. Observe also that in a condition subsequent situation there is liability immediately upon the making of the agreement, which liability, however, is subject to be defeated upon the happening of the event. X brings a dachshund into Y's law office and tells Y he can have the brute for $15. Y says "I'll take him." This case involves concurrent conditions and the law will imply concurrent performances. In such a situation, unlike the other two cases, it should be noticed that the performance of one is a condition precedent to the performance of the other and that both performances must eventuate then and there simultaneously. X sells Y a tight-lipped monkey, payment to be made six months after delivery.

A representation is an affirm ation by words or conduct of a matter of fact by way of inducement to the making' of a contract. A mere statement or expression of opinion or statement of intention will not amount to a representation, the falsity of which will avoid a contract. 

A representation must be a representation of fact. Commendatory expressions such as men usually employ in order to induce others to enter into a bargain are not regarded as representations of fact. A statement by an auctioneer that land which he offered for sale was "very fertile and improvable," whereas, in fact, it was partly abandoned as useless, was held to be "a mere flourishing description by an auctioneer," and not such a representation as would avoid the sale. Dimmock v. 

Hallett, L. R. 2 Ch. 21 and 27. But on the sale of a hotel it was held that the contract was avoided by a false statement that the present lessee was "a most desirable tenant," when in fact he was most undesirable. Smith v. Property Co., 28 Ch. Div. 7. Representations which do not form a part of the contract are either immaterial or in the nature of conditions going to the existence of the contract. 

The representation may be made either before or at the time of the making of the contract. Such representations that do not form a part of the contract consequently do not enter into the question of the performance of conditions in existing contracts. Representations which are embodied in the contract may be merely representations or conditions as well. If they are conditions they may be conditions as to either something that is material or immaterial. A material representation is usually hold to amount to a warranty. As a rule, a warranty is a contract collateral to a legal transaction by which one of the parties undertakes or promises that certain facts are or shall be as he represents them. Breach of warranty gives rise to a claim for damages in contract. Even the untruth alone of the warranty is enough to give the injured party a cause of action. On the other hand, where an action is based on a representation the plaintiff must prove that the representation was made fraudulently or recklessly without caring whether the facts were true or false. The word "warranty" is used variously in the law. Where it is employed in the law of real property, it does not have the sense of a condition. As used in a sale of chattels, in some jurisdictions it is called a collateral statement or representation to the contract of a sale having reference to the title, character, or quality of the things sold and by which the warrant or promises that certain facts are or shall be as he represented them to be. This kind of a warranty is not a condition. In other jurisdictions, the collateral promise idea is rejected and such a material promise is deemed a condition. In these courts where the condition is broken the buyer may repudiate or rescind the agreement. The definition of an express warranty is found in the Uniform Sales Act of the District of Columbia, D. C. Code, S. 28 - 1112: "Any affirmation of fact or any promise by the seller relating to the goods is an express warranty if the natural tendency of such affirmation or promise is to induce the buyer to purchase the goods, and if the buyer purchases the goods relying thereon. No affirmation of the value of the goods, nor any statement purporting to be a statement of the seller's opinion only shall

Ham not

be construed as a warranty." Conditions are either statements or promises which form the basis of a contract. 

Whether a representation or promise in a policy of insurance or a charter party is also a warranty depends on whether the parties have expressly, or by an unexpressed intention, collected from the whole instrument, made the validity of, or liability under, the contract dependent on the truth or the performance of such promise or representation. In other words whether a representation or promise is also a warranty depends on the importance attached to such representation or promise by the parties themselves. There is no absolute test even though the word "warrant" is used. 188 Ill. 133. The rule is well established that policies of insurance are always construed in favor of the insured. Consequently, where there is doubt, statements made by the applicant for insurance will be considered merely as representations and not warranties. 

A representation has been distinguished from conditions and warranties in the celebrated English case of Behn v. Burness, 3 Best & S. 751, by the court as follows: "Properly speaking, a representation is a statement or assertion, made by one party to the other, before or at the time of the contract, of some matter or circumstance relating to it. Though it is sometimes contained in the written instrument, it is not an integral part of the contract, and consequently the contract is not broken, though the representation proves to be untruc; nor (with the exception of the case of policies of insurance -- at all events, marine policies, which stand on a peculiar anomalous footing) is such untruth any cause of action, nor has it any efficacy whatever, unless the representation was made fraudulently, either by reason of its being made with a knowledge of its untruth, or by reason of its being made dishonestly, with a reckless ignorance whether it was true or untrue.....Though representations are not usually contained in the written instrument of contract, yet they sometimes are. But it is plain that their insertion therein cannot alter their nature. A question, however, may arise whether a descriptive statement in the written instrument is a mere representation, or whether it is a substantive part of the contract. This is a question of construction which the court, and not the jury, must determine. If the court should come to the conclusion that such a statement by one party was intended to be a substantive part of his contract, and not a mere representation, the often-discussed question may, of course, be raised, whether this part of the contract is a condition precedent, or only an independent agreement, a breach of which will not justify a repudiation of the contract, but will only be a cause of action for a compensation in damages. 

In the construction of charter parties, this question has often been raised with reference to stipulations that some future thing shall be done or shall happen, and has given rise to many nice distinctions. Thus, a statement that a vessel is to sail, or be ready to receive a cargo, on or before a given day, has been held to be a condition, while a stipulation that she shall sail with all convenient speed, or within a reasonable time, has been held to be only an agreement. But with respect to statements in a contract descriptive of the subject-matter of it, or of some material incident thereof, the true doctrine, established by principle as well as authority, appears to be, generally speaking, that, if such descriptive statement was intended to be a substantive part of the contract, it is to be regarded as a warranty; that is to say, a condition on the failure or nonperformance of which the other party may, if he is so minded, repudiate the contract in toto, and so be relieved from performing his part of it, provided it has not been partially executed in his favor. If, indeed, he has received the whole or any substantial part of the consideration for the promise on his part, the warranty loses the character of a condition, or, to speak perhaps more properly, ceases to be available as a condition, and becomes a warranty in the narrower sense of the word, viz., a stipulation by way of agreement, for the breach of which a compensation must be

sought in damages." The import of this accurate statement of the law is this: 

Where a mere nonfraudulent representation is untrue, no action will lie; where a warranty is broken, the injured party may sue only for damages but he is given no right to repudiate; where a condition is broken, the injured party, if he is so minded, may repudiate the contract; or, if he chooses, he may waive the breach as a breach of condition, in which case the condition sinks to the level of a warranty and he can only sue for damages sustained, but the right to rescind for condition broken is gone. This is often called a "warranty ex post facto." 

PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS. Where X promises to pay Y when X is able, the courts will place a reasonable interpretation on such promise. Upon a showing that X once becomes able to pay, the condition vanishes and his promise becomes absolute. 

X's subsequent inability to pay will not restore the condition. Work v. Beach, 13 N.Y. S. 678. Where a debtor is clearly insolvent, or if a compulsory payment would deprive him of practically all means of subsistence, then the debtor is not to be considered as being able to pay. 100 N. Y. 27. The mere fact that the debtor has enough property to satisfy the particular debt is not in itself evidence of an ability to pay. On the other hand, a promise to pay when able does not imply an ability to pay without embarrassment, or even without seriously damaging the debtor's business.

Very often building contracts contain an express proviso requiring that the contractor is to receive the agreed sum only upon the approval by an architect of the completed job. The cases in point are in discord but ordinarily the judgment of the third party architect is binding on the contracting parties and the express condition must be literally complied with, except in cases of fraud, collusion or other extraordinary circumstances. Bush v. Jones, 144 Fed. 942. Where there is fraudulent collusion between a party to the contract and the architect, every jurisdiction will excuse the nonperformance of the express condition. By the great weight of authority in this country, the fraud of the architect or his refusal to exercise an honest judgment, even if done noncollusively, excuses a failure to produce the certificate. By the New York rule the court apparently may disregard such express condition precedent and substitute its judgment or that of the jury for that of the architect if the defects for which the architect refuses to certify scem arbitrary or trivial. 88 N. Y. 648.

X agrees to supply Y with coal in such quantities as X may "consider fit and advisable." This has been held a promise to furnish nothing. X may cease furnishing any time he sees fit. The rule is that where the promise is made merely upon the will of the promisor, a mere voluntary obligation arises but not a contractual one.

The execution of a contract may be dependent upon the personal satisfaction of one of the parties thereto. X may agree to paint an oil portrait of Y to Y's personal satisfaction. The rule is in such a situation that X cannot recover until 

Y is actually satisfied. It is immaterial that Y is unusually discriminating since 

X agreed to run such risk. The fact that persons other than Y are satisfied with the portrait is disregarded. Brown v. Foster, 113 Mass. 136, Gibson v. Carnage, 39 Mich. 49. Other cases draw finer lines. Thus, it has been held otherwise that where X agrees to perform to the personal satisfaction of Y, the latter must act reasonably and will not be upheld in his judgment where it is arbitrary or capricious These casos declare that Y's judgment is subject to review by the court or jury as to whether or not he has acted in good faith in rejecting the tender of the completed work. This view is recommended as the one more just and equitable in order not to leave the door open to fraud. On the other hand, where X contracted to perform to Y's "absolute satisfaction" (or language of similar import), it has been

held that Y may even reject the performance capriciously, arbitrarily, or otherwise on the ground that X agreed absolutely to run the risk of Y's capriciousness. 

Similar cases arise where X employs Y for so long as Y's services are satisfactory. There is no doubt but in such contracts of hire the employee may be justly discharged for any reason or no reason. He was hired merely at the will of the employer. On the other hand, suppose X employs Y for a period of three years with the right to discharge Y if X considers him incompetent or his services unsatisfactory. In such case X may discharge Y for cause without making himself liable, but Y may recover upon a showing that X acted in bad faith and that his dissatisfaction was feigned. 

A condition implied in fact is governed for all practical purposes by the same general principles controlling express conditions. X rented property from Y, who promised to keep it in good repair. X was denied a recovery against Y for Y's failure to repair on the ground that he had not first notified him that the property was in need of repair. Y was not in a position to know of such defects and accordingly it was held that his performance was conditional upon notice by X. 

This illustrates the rule in contracts that where performance by Y is conditioned upon the happening of an uncertain event, knowledge of which is peculiarly within the breast of X, it is a condition precedent to Y's liability to perform that X (in possession of such knowledge) notify Y that the event has happened. 7 Gray 425.

On the other hand, if X agrees with Y to pay Y $500 when Z leaves Washington for London, and both have an equal opportunity to know of Z's departure, then Y may recover from X without notice. There is nothing to prevent the parties from stipulating expressly that a notice or demand is essential in order to impose a liability to perform on one party or the other. In such event the express condition must be performed, but it need not be accomplished in writing unless the agreement demands it. 34 Maine 379.

The law will imply conditions in a contract, on grounds of public policy, where the parties fail to provide a condition in any certain case which fair dealing and good business require. Accordingly there is an implied condition in law that in every bilateral contract the promises on one side are presumed to be the equivalent for all the promises on the other side. Where nothing is said in a bilateral contract about performances the law implies mutual and concurrent performances. 

Where a day is named for payment in a contract for the sale of any property and the agreement is silent as to the time of delivery of the deed or the chattel, the latter is presumed in law to be deliverable when the money is payable. 14 

Yale L. J. 424.

BREACH OF CONDITION. There are two kinds of breaches of contract, viz., (1) 

a breach in limine and (2) a breach after a part performance of the agreement. 

A breach in limine (at the outset) is a breach of condition before any performance has taken place. Such a breach will discharge the contract provided it is not merely a formal or trifling breach. This is based on the condition implied in law that each party shall be in a position to perform literally. Consequently a breach in limine by the plaintiff gives the defendant a perfect defense. X agrees to sell 

Yellowacre to Y with the timber growing there on. Before the day specified for the transfer of title, X cuts down a number of the trees and Y refused to accept the land. X sues Y alleging that his breach is immaterial. This is a breach in limine and is regarded quite differently from a breach after part performance. X cannot recover, A breach at the beginning of a contract and before there is any performance need not go to the essence of the contract in order to give the other party a right to rescind. A slight breach at that time will be fatal. It is understood,

407

however, that even in the case of a breach in limine, it will not be fatal unless it has some material bearing. 

On the other hand, to constitute a defense for nonperformance, a breach after part performance must be substantial on the ground that the law will not imply a breach. There must be something more than a mere breach to convey the idea that the party is not going to keep his promise and that he is not merely failing to perform one part of his agreement. In case of a breach in limine, a less breach will justify nonperformance by the other party than if the breach occurs after part performance. In order to justify nonperformance, a breach after part performance must run to the essence, root, or substance of the contract and defeat the main scope of the agreement. Kauffman v. Raeder, 108 Fed. 171. The temporary illness of a servant which does not go to the essence or root of the contract will not prevent him from enforcing the contract of employment, but if his illness makes it necessary for the employer to engage a substitute who cannot, however, be hired except for the full period of service of the ill servant, then the illness goes to the essence of the contract and the employer is discharged from further liability. 

In other words, a breach after part performance is fatal only when it goes to the heart or essence of the contract. Boone v. Eyre, 1 H. Bl. 273, Note. In a contract for personal service for a year, at a fixed sum for the entire term, if the employee should irretrievably break his agreement after ten months' service, he would not be entitled to recover anything for his past services. Eldridge v. 

Rowe, 7 Ill. 91. Some states, however, permit a recovery in such case on the doctrine of quasi contract, the employer being required to pay the employee the value of the services rendered on a quantum meruit. This is justified on the ground that otherwise the employer would be unjustly enriched at the expense of the employec. Duncan v. Baker, 21 Kans. 99.

The rule as to time in contracts is a rule of reason. Where the agreement fixes no time for performance, there is a condition implied in law allowing a reasonable time for performance. What constitutes a reasonable time depends upon the nature and circumstances of the particular case. The question of what constitutes a reasonable time is a question of law for the court to decide when it depends upon construction but it is a question of fact for the jury to decide when it depends on facts outside the contract or on matters of fact within the contract. 

Time is not always of the essence of a contract. Where the parties fix a time for performance and agree that time is to be of the essence then performance at that time must be literally and absolutely made. In cases where time is not made of the essence, it may or may not be of the essence, depending upon the intention of the parties to be ascertained from the four corners of their agreement. Time was held to be of the essence where a contract was made to "complete the work within three years, or the note to be void." Accordingly the failure to complete the work within such three years relieved the other party of his obligation. In the absence of an express provision, the court may construe time as of the essence when such time is a material object of the contract and is intended to be so by the intention of the parties. A provision in a contract making time of the essence may be waived by the express or implied conduct of the parties but unless there is such a waiver, performance after the specified time amounts to nothing. Underwood v. Wolf, 131 Ill. 425. The parties may agree that time shall be of the essence not only as to the day but as to the minute and the hour. Shinn v. Roberts, 20 N. J. L. 435. Thus, X may agree to purchase and pay cash for Y's land provided 

Y presents a deed there to to X at X's place of business prior to 12 o'clock noon on July 5, 1942, otherwise the deal is off. Unless Y complies literally, X is not bound. Generally, in mercantile contracts time is of the essence as to the delivery of property but not as to payment of money, unless the payment of money is a condition precedent to delivery or was expressly made of the essence. Where X ordered

Norrington v. Wright, 115 U. S. 188. But suppose that the corporation delivered the 20,000 tons of stecl to X on the 10th, as promised, and X was to pay therefor on the 9th. In the absence of an express provision making time of the essence, would be held that the time of payment would not be of the essence and if X made or tendered payment according to the tenor of his promise within a reasonable time after which it was duc, the corporation could not rescind but would have to perform. 

The reason behind the rule that time is never of the essence where money is to be paid, unless otherwise specified, is that the damages for delay in payment may be accurately ascertained by allowing interest at the legal rate to the party injured on the principal sum for the period covering the delay. This principle of justice is very frequently applied in the District of Columbia in landlord and tenant proceedings. Where X, a lessee, is ordered to vacate the leased premises, by judgment of the court, because of non-payment of rent as provided for by the lease, X may nevertheless retain possession provided he tenders the amount of the rent due plus interest and costs to the United States marshal who has come to put him out on the street. In such case the marshal has no right to execute his writ of restitution but is bound to accept the amount due and leave the tenant in possession. On the other hand, where a landlord promises possession to a tenant on a specified date, the law will imply that time is of the essence and a tender of possession 24 hours later would constitute a breach of condition entitling the lessee to rescind. An agreement to be performed "when convenient" or "as soon as possible" will be construed as requiring performance within a reasonable time. Ordinarily the rule is, as to computation of time, that the date on which the contract is executed shall be excluded but that the day of performance shall be included. Thus, X and Y contract on December 23 that Y shall supply X with two cases of Pommery champagne, brut, 1928, "within six days after date." Excluding the date of execution, but including the date of performance, Y must perform not later than during the day of December 29; in fact, Y is entitled to the entire day of the 29th within which to keep his promise. Shelton v. Gillet, 79 Mich. 173.

Where a contract calls for delivery by instalments and no time for payment therein is specified, there is a condition implied by law that payment shall be made upon each delivery on the ground that where the parties fail to provide for themselves otherwise, the law will imply concurrent conditions. 

This assumes, of course, that the agreement provides for a method of ascertaining the amount due for each instalment. On the other hand, where a lump sum payment is called for, without evidence of apportionment among the various instalments, and no time for payment is fixed, then the law implies a condition that payment in full and delivery of the last instalment would be concurrent. In such case all of the preceding instalments would be independent of payment, except the last instalment. Suppose

X contracts with Y whereby X agrees by means of a conditional sale to seal Y an automobile. Y is to pay the purchase price of $1,000 in 10 equal instalments at specified times. X has the right to recover against Y as to any instalment or instalments after they become due. Y, however, cannot obtain title to the car until he has paid his last instalment which is a condition precedent to his deriving title from the vendor. Accordingly, X may wait until all the instalments, except the last, become due and recover the amount owing at that time from Y, without tendering title to Y. But if X permits all the instalments to become due, including the last, then he can recover nothing from Y unless he makes tender of title. In other words, Y's promises to pay all the instalments, excepting the last, are independent of performance by X; but Y's promise to pay the last instalment is concurrent with performance by X.

A contract to be performed in instalments is termed a "divisible" contract. 

This is to be contrasted with a situation involving a series of independent contracts, each of which is a complete contract in itself. In order to determine whether an agreement constitutes one entire contract to be performed divisibly by instalments or whether it constitutes a series or succession of independent contracts, one following upon the other, resort must be had to the ascertainment of the intention of the parties. Where there is a series of contracts a breach of any one of the independent contracts is confined in its effect absolutely to that particular agreement and gives no rights to the injured party as to any of the other independent agreements in the series. But as to one entire contract, performable divisibly, where there is a breach in any one instalment that goes to the root, heart, or essence of the agreement, the great weight of American authority is that the injured party may rescind the entire contract and refuse to proceed further. Landvoigt v. Paul, 27 App. D. C. 423. The rule to be followed in the 

District of Columbia is the one laid down by the Supreme Court of the United States in Norrington v. Wright, 115 U. S. 188. In that case the contract was for the sale of "5,000 tons of iron rails, for shipment from European port or ports, at the rate of 1,000 tons per month, beginning February, 1880, but whole contract to be shipped before August, 1880, at $45 per ton, ex ship Philadelphia, settlement cash on presentation of bills," etc. Held, the vendor was bound to ship 1,000 tons each month, and since only 400 tons were shipped in February, and 885 tons in March, the buyer, although he had paid for the February shipment in ignorance of the defective shipments in that month and in March, had the right to rescind the whole contract for the defective delivery in respect to the first instalment. The decision was based on the ground that in contracts between merchants time is of the essence as to delivery and that the shipment at the time promised in the contract was a condition precedent, on the failure of which the vendee had the option of rescinding the whole contract. It was further pointed out that the contract was a single one for the sale of 5,000 tons of iron rails and the arrangement for payment and shipment only stipulated the manner in which it should be exccuted. 

A divisible contract is one entire contract where the payment and consideration are apportioned so that part payment may be secured after part delivery, but the agreement is still one single contract merely relating to a series of transactions or instalments and not to a series of successive independent contracts. 

X contracts with the Double cross Publishing Company to buy a series of 125 

volumes entitled "How to Pass the District of Columbia Bar Examinations without 

Knowing any Law." He obligated himself to pay for each volume upon delivery. This agreement constitutes one entire contract and not a series of contracts. After receiving, reading, and paying for the first two volumes, X notified the company that he rescinded the contract on the ground of fraud, but did not offer to return the said books. Held, X had a right to rescind but not in this case because he did not return or offer to return the two volumes which he had received and paid for. Barrie v. Earle, 143 Mass. 1.

X contracts with the Bovine Dairy Products Corporation of Washington, D. C., whereby the company is to furnish X a quart of unsterilized goat's milk every Saturday morning until further notice. This arrangement constitutes a series of independent contracts, each one made and performed each Saturday upon delivery, at which time, unless otherwise specified, payment would be due. 

X contracts to convey Blackacre to Y on November 2. Should he fail to do so at that time, a right of action would accrue in Y against him immediately. Suppose, however, that X two months before his said date for performance had notified Y that he was not going to convey Blackacre to him on November 2 as he had previously promised. This premature default by renunciation of the contract on the part of X

- is termed an anticipatory breach. There is an implied condition in law that parties to a wholly executory contract have a right to the maintenance of the contractual relationship up to the time of performance as well as a right to performance when performance is due. Consequently where X renounces his contractual obligations before the time when they are due, Y is excused from performing his part of the contract and by virtue of the doctrine of anticipatory breach may sue at once for damages without waiting for the time set for X's performance. It has been held in the District of Columbia that as soon as one person has disabled himself from performing his contract with another, by renouncing his liability before the time for performance, the injured party's right of action accrues, and he need not wait to ascertain whether his rights may not ultimately be secured to him. Payne v. Pomeroy, 21 D. C. 243. (1892). On the other hand, if the injured party elects to disregard the renunciation he may do so and bring his action after the date of performance has passed, in which event the previous renunciation before the time set for performance will not be deemed an anticipatory breach. Roehm v. Horst, 178 U. S. 1; Landvoigt v. Paul, 27 App. D. C. 423. An anticipatory breach becomes a wrongful act only if the promisee elects to treat it as such, but not otherwise. Sheffield v. Paul 1. Stone, Inc., 68 App. D. C. 378 (1938). In order for the doctrine of anticipatory breach to apply, the renunciation must cover the entire performance to which the contract obligates the promisor. A partial renunciation is insufficient to confer an immediate right of action on the promisee. Thus, it has been held that where the contract involved an entire lease, the anticipatory breach of one covenant did not entitle the lessee to sue at once. Johnstone v. Milling, 16 Q. B. D. 460. Furthermore a mere expression of an intention not to perform is not enough; there must be a categorical renunciation. Dingley v. Oler, 117 U. S. 490. X offers Y $100 if he will walk up to the top of the Washington Monument within the next three days. Y replies that he has no intention of ever walking up to the top of the Washington Monument, whereupon X sues Y for anticipatory breach. X has no right of action against Y whatever. This situation created a unilateral contract as X's offer called merely for an act and not for a promise. IMPOSSIBILITY OF PERFORMANCE. Impossibility of performance may exist various ly. (1) It may exist unknown to one or both of the parties at the time the contract is made; (2) it may arise after the making of the contract; or (3) it may be apparent on the face of the contract. In (1) impossibility of performance results because of the nonexistence of the subject matter. In such instance there is no contract because where the subject matter is nonexistent there is nothing as to which the parties could have contracted. In (2) the situation concerns impossibility of performance arising only after the agreement has been entered into. It is this class of cases with which we are most concerned. In (3) where impossibility is apparent on the face of the contract, no contract results because it is apparently physically impossible of performance and consequently preposterous. An example where impossibility of performance is apparent on the face of the contract is where A and B enter into an agreement whereby A obligates himself to pay B $50,000 and B obligates himself to make a journey to the planet Mars and back to Washington, D. C., again on a certain date. Obviously such an agreement is absurd and no contract would result. Suppose X and Y contract whereby X promises to buy Y's horse for $10 and Y contracts to deliver title to the horse. It later develops that at the time of the contract and unknown to both parties the horse had been dead about two days from the effects of poliomyelitis. No contract results on the ground that there is impossibility of performance resulting from the nonexistence of the subject matter.

Where impossibility of performance arises after the making of the agreement, two situations occur: (1) where the impossibility is known to one party only and (2) where it is known to both parties. Where it is known to both parties that there is impossibility of performance, no contract results because both parties neither intend nor expect performance. On the contrary, where the thing to be performed is physically possible but for some other reason is impossible and only the promisor is aware of this fact, he will nevertheless be bound by the agreement. X contracted to pay Y $5,000 "when I collect the money on the bond on which suit is pending." X knew that there was no such bond but Y did not know of that fact. Y was allowed a recovery against X on the ground that he acted in good faith and in ignorance of the impossibility by X to collect on a nonexistent bond which X knew was nonexistent. This situation where the impossibility is known to one party only very generally constitutes a fraud upon the other party and gives rise not only to a breach of contract but to a tort action for deceit, wherein the innocent party has the right to waive the contract and sue in tort. Another familiar example where impossibility of performance is known to one party but not to the other occurs where X, a married man, promises to marry Y, who has no knowledge of X's marital status. 

In spite of the fact that X could not possibly have legally married Y under the circumstances, Y may recover damages against X for his breach of promise to marry. 

In such instance, it is commonly held that although a suit for breach of promise to marry is in form ex contractu, in reality it is in substance an action ex delicto

- based on fraud. Wild v. Harris, 7 C. B. 999. On the other hand, where the impossibility of performance is known to the promisee (plaintiff) instead of to the promisor (defendant), the plaintiff would have no right to expect the promise to be carried out and the defendant would not be bound. 

The mere fact that performance turns out to be very difficult to accomplish or becomes burdensome is no ground for discharging the agreemtn. The rule is, therefore, that impossibility which arises subsequent to the making of a contract does not excuse the promisor on the ground that where one promises to do an act in futuro, he impliedly warrants that he runs the risks incident to its performance. 

Where X, an independent contractor, contracts to build an office building, he assumes the risk of increased prices in materials and labor. An insurance company, under the terms of the policy, rather than pay the insurance, elected to rebuild a structure which had been partially destroyed by incendiary fire. Thereafter the city declared the remaining portion dangerous and ordered the entire building razed. 

This necessarily increased the cost of rebuilding but did not constitute an act impossible of performance. Held, the insurance company had to rebuild at whatever expense. Brown v. Royal Insurance Company, 1 E. & E. 853. The rule is that where the law creates a duty or charge and the party is disabled from performing it without any default in himself, the law will excuse him; but where the party by his contract creates a duty or charge upon himself, he is bound to make it good, notwithstanding any accident by inevitable necessity, because he might have provided against it by his contract. Paradine v. Jane, Aleyn 26. Every promisor has the right to make the performance of his promise conditioned upon its continued possibility and if he does not do so the law will not assist him because of his lack of foresight. But in a case where the promisor makes the performance of his promise conditional upon its continued possibility, then the risk falls on the promisee if performance is impossible. Whipple v. Refining Co., 64 Misc. 363 (N. Y.). A manufacturer is not excused from performance where his plant breaks down, although without his fault, thus preventing him from manufacturing the goods called for in the contract. Where X promised to build a schoolhouse for a New Jersey town and the building collapsed when partially completed because of hidden defects in the foundation soil, it was held that this gave X no defense to an action for breach of contract. X took the risk of the nature of the foundation soil as incidental to his contract. School Trustees v. Bennett, 27 N. J. L. 513.

The harsh doctrine that impossibility of performance constitutes no defense has some exceptions. Where subsequent circumstances arise that are so extraordinary that the risk of them ought not to be cast on the promisor (defendant), the courts will very often so construe a contract as to relieve the defendant. The rule is that where the event is of such a character that it cannot reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made, they will not be held bound by general words, which, although large enough to include, were not used with reference to the possibility of the particular contingency which afterwards happens. Chicago, Mil. & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Hoyt, 149 U. S. 1. The four general exceptions usually recognized are: (1) the act of the promisee (plaintiff) ; (2) a change in the law; (3) destruction of the subject matter; and (4) incapacity for personal service. Unless provided for otherwise these four situations constitute well recognized exceptions to the rule that impossibility arising subsequent to the making of the contract is no excuse. These exceptions are considered conditions implied by the law because they really constitute equitable defenses that arise from the extraordinary circumstances and nature of the particular case. 

An act of God as a rule does not excuse performance of a contract, except in cases wherein personal service or the continued existence of a person is contracted for or intended by the agreement. For example, the death of X, who contracted to

It will be considered that X contracted absolutely and not conditionally, as he had a right to do. He bound himself to furnish the grapes at all events. 

Miss K contracted with a Broadway producer to act in a play for a specified period. 

The contract provided that she would be entitled to her salary in case she was unable to continue her services resulting from disability caused by an act of God, but not otherwise. She was prevented from acting for several months because of the fact that subsequent to the making of the contract she became enciente and within a reasonable time thereafter gave birth to a five-pound baby. The producer refused to pay her salary for the time during which she was thus incapacitated on the ground that she was not incapacitated by an "act of God." The actress contended that she was incapacitated by an act of God and should therefore receive her salary for the time she was out. It should be held that she is not entitled to recover on the ground that becoming enciente is not an act of God but an act of man. (There is a sharp distinction between an "act of God" and an "inevitable accident.")

Where the performance of a promise is made impossible by the act of the promisee (plaintiff) either during the course of performance or before the time for performance arrives, the promisor (defendant) is discharged from his obligation. 

He may even sue immediately for breach as though the promisee was guilty of an anticipatory breach. Where the performance, however, is made impossible by the act of the promisor (defendant), the impossibility of performance creates no defense. 

A promises to marry B within two years, but instead marries C within one year. may sue immediately. X agrees to sell real estate to Y on November 6, but on October 10 conveys the property to Z. Y is discharged from his obligation under the contract and may sue X at once. King v. Kersey, 2 Ind. 402.

The Doublecross Publishing Company engaged Blackstone to write for publication a law book on the subject of fraud. Before the manuscript was completed, the publisher abandoned the publication. The author was held entitled to damages and excused from further performance on his part. The abandonment of the publication discharged the agreement. This case illustrates the rule that impossibility created by the promisor during the course of performance excuses the promisee. Planche v. 

Colburn, 8 Bing. 14. Where an Englishman, who was employed as a stoker on a German warship, was informed when war was declared between Germany and Russia that a performance of his contract would bring him under certain penalties as to foreign enlistments, it was held that he was entitled to leave the battleship and sue for his wages, because the act of the German government had made his contract legally impossible. O'Neil v. Armstrong, Mitchell & Co., 2 Q. B. 418. (1885).

DISCHARGE 

By discharge, we must understand not merely the right to bring an action upon the contract because the other party has not fulfilled its terms, but the right to consider oneself exonerated from any further performance under the contract, viz., the right to treat the legal relations arising from the contract as having come to an end, and given place to a new obligation, a right of action. 

Where X fails to keep his promise he thereby commits a breach of the contract. Where there is a breach of a contract, the innocent party acquires a right of action for damages. But not every breach will discharge the innocent party from performing his part of the bargain. Thus, a contract may be broken entirely or partially, and the breach may operate as a discharge or it may not. Furthermore, the innocent party is under no obligation to insist upon a discharge of the contract, but may elect to ignore the breach, treat the contract as still subsisting, and merely reserve the right to bring an action for damages. (1) There are three general modes in which a contract may be discharged: by agreement; (2) by performance; and (3) by operation of law. Not only may a contract be discharged, but the right of action accruing from a breach of contract may also be discharged.

DISCHARGE BY AGREEMENT. 

It is perfectly proper for the parties to a contract to agree that the contract be discharged and that it shall no longer be obligatory on them. Such an agreement may merely alter some of the terms of the old contract or it may rescind the contract in toto. The new contract will supersede the old contract insofar as inconsistencies are encountered. Again, where the new contract entirely supersedes the old, then the rights of the parties will be determined entirely by the new agreement. On the other hand, the new agreement may be nothing more than a rescission of the old contract resulting in a mutual release of the parties completely. It is a fundamental principle that in order to discharge an old contract by a new contract the new contract must be a valid contract; otherwise, there is no discharge and the old contract lives on. 

Where a contract is wholly executory, it may discharged by the mutual promises of the parties, each releasing the other. This discharge by agreement is in itself a valid bilateral contract, the promise of one being a consideration for the promise of the other. But where a contract has been executed only on one side, an agreement of discharge requires a new consideration. Example: X contracts to paint Y's barn for $75. X paints the barn, thus fully performing his part. 

Y fails to pay X the $75, thus not performing his part. X tells Y that he is discharged from his obligation to pay him the $75 under the contract. X's attempt to thereby discharge Y is null and void and Y nevertheless is liable on the ground that he gave no consideration for X's promise of discharge. Davidson v. Burke, 143 Ill. 139. This rule applies also to promissory notes. The promise to surrender or cancel an obligation on a promissory note must be supported by a consideration. 

The District of Columbia Negotiable Instruments Law, however, provides that such an instrument may be discharged, inter alia, by the intentional cancelation thereof by the holder or by an absolute and unconditional renunciation which must be in writing, unless the instrument is delivered up to the person primarily liable thereon. D. C. Code, Ss. 28-801; 28-804.

The parties may agree to discharge a contract by a release. Where one mutually releases the other, a discharge will result on the ground that the mutual promises constitute a new binding bilateral contract operating to release and discharge both parties from further obligation, Where X and Y have contracted and

X merely promises to release Y, the release is ineffective because unsupported by a consideration. But if X promises to release Y by a writing under seal, then the release is effective and Y is discharged on the ground that where there is a promise under seal there is no necessity for a consideration to make it binding. 

The parties may agree not only for a substitution of terms, but also for a substitution of parties. This is called a novation. Where a contract is discharged by a novation it means merely that the new agreement provides for a new party in the substituted contract. In its simple form a novation may involve a change in the debtor or a change in the creditor. If A owes B a sum of money and there is substituted for this obligation a new obligation of C to B, there is a novation by the introduction of a new debtor (C). If there is substituted for the original obligation an obligation of A to C, there is a novation by the introduction of a new creditor (C). In order that a novation shall be valid, it is necessary that the original obligation shall be enforceable, otherwise the surrender of it will not constitute a sufficient consideration for the new contract. 

It is absolutely essential in order to constitute a novation that all of the parties to the contract be parties to the new agreement resulting in the novation. 

Thus, A owes B $100 and B owcs C $100. To constitute a valid novation whereby 

B will be released from his obligation to pay C, and A will be substituted in his stead, all the parties, A, B, and C, must be parties to the new contract, otherwise no novation. Where A and B merely agree between themselves that A should be substituted for B as to B's debt to C, and that B should be thereby released from his obligation to C, a novation would not result. B would still be liable to C. 

C, however, could recover against A on the theory of a contract made for the benefit of a third party in those jurisdictions recognizing the debtor-creditor type situation. A novation is really a new contract and unless the substituted party is a party to such new contract, there is no novation. It

It was the rule anciently at common law that a contract had to be discharged in the same form, or in as high a form, as the manner in which it was made. Therefore, an executory contract under seal could not be discharged by a parol agreement, whether verbal or a simple writing. McCreery v. Day, 119 N. Y. 1. This harsh rule has been greatly qualified today in practically every jurisdiction. is generally held that the parties may be parol extend the time agreed upon for the performance of a sealed contract and also may waive any of its terms in such undignified manner. Moses v. Loomis, 156 Ill. 392. It is practically the universal rule now that a sealed agreement may be modified or rescinded by an executed parol contract. Green v. Wells, 2 Cal. 584. The courts hold in such cases that where the parties have executed their parol agreemont it would be unconscionable not to respect it. Robinson v. Bullock, 66 Ala. 548. Accordingly, it is the law in the District of Columbia that an instrument under seal may be modified by a subsequent parol contract. Ches. & 0. Canal Co. v. Ray, 101 U. S. 522. In the 

District of Columbia a sealed contract may be rescinded by parol. Whore the consideration mentioned in a deed is "one dollar," the true consideration may be shown by parol. Hitz v. Nat. Met. Bank, 111 U. S. 722. The fact that a written contract provides that no modification shall be made except in writing, is immaterial. The reason given for such rule is that the very provision itself may be changed verbally, if both parties agree to it. Ford v. United States, 17 Ct. Cl. 60 (U. S.). 

Where a contract must be in writing, however, in order to satisfy the statute of frauds, then the new agreement must similarly be in writing for the same reason, particularly in cases where a new contract has been substituted for an old one. 

Abell v. Munson, 18 Mich. 306.

The parties may provide in the contract itself for methods of discharge, as where the terms recite that the agreement may be discharged upon the happening or nonhappening of some event.

DISCHARGE BY PERFORMANCE. 

A contract is discharged where it is fully performed (executed) by both parties. But there is no discharge where it is partially executed, viz., performed by one side only. In such cases it is usual to refer to a contract as being performed or executed by one side or the other (partial performance), but this does not amount to a discharge of the contract itself; it is merely a discharge of one of the parties from his own particular performance. 

The contract still exists.

The parties have a right to agree that a negotiable instrument given in payment of a claim or in satisfaction of a breach shall constitute a discharge of liability. In such event the instrument alone may be sucd on. It is always a question of intent as to whether or not such an instrument was accepted as absolute payment. In the absence of proof to the contrary, it is implied in law that the instrument is taken merely as a conditional payment and that no discharge will result unless and until the instrument is paid. If the instrument be not paid, then an action may be maintained on the contract, but if the instrument is paid, then there is an accord and satisfaction and the contract itself is thereby discharged. Where X pays an obligation by check, this is not deemed payment, but merely conditional payment. If the check is not paid, then there is no payment. 

But if the check is paid, then there is payment and the drawer is discharged from liability on his promise.

A mere tender of payment or of performance of an act may operate to discharge the person making tender from further obligation under the agreement. A tender is defined as an attempt to perform. It applies not only to the performance of a promise to do something, but also to the performance of a promise to pay something. 

Where X, in a contract for the sale of ladies' hats, met all of the requirements, and Y, the buyer, nevertheless refused to accept the merchandise, X will be discharged from his obligation under the contract upon his tendering the goods to Y. 

If Y should sue, X may pload tender as a defense, provided he pleads it specially. 

On the other hand, where performance consists of the payment of money, the contract will not be discharged by a tender by the debtor. Thus, where X by the terms of an agreement is obligated to pay Y $1,000, X must locate his creditor (Y) and pay him the money when it falls due. If Y declines to accept the tendered payment, X must nevertheless keep himself ever ready and willing to pay. If Y should sue him, X is entitled to plead tender but he must also plead his readiness to pay. 

In such event, Y may nevertheless recover the $1,000, but he will lose his costs of suit and also forfeit his right to interest from the date of tender. Werner v. Tuch, 127 N. Y. 217. In order to constitute a good performance, a tender must strictly comply with the provisions of the agreement. The exact sum must be offered and it must be offered in legal tender as defined by federal statutes. Legal tender is defined by an act of Congress. U. S. Code, T. 31, ch. 9, q.v. 

No foreign gold or silver coins can be a legal tender in payment of debts in the 

United States. 

In order to discharge an agreement at common law, the performance had to be strictly in accordance with the contract. Thus, where X contracted to sell 800 acres of timberland to Y and it developed that the tract contained only 797 acres, X could not recover against Y at all because X failed to strictly perform. 

Gradually, however, the common law courts, influenced by equitable doctrines, adopted the rule of substantial performance which is universally recognized today. 

Accordingly, if X brought an action against Y for specific performance to take and pay for the land, equity in its sound discretion could compel Y to specifically perform, deducting the reasonable value of the 3 acres. This is based on the theory of law that Y is getting substantially what he bargained for and he must take it, with a reasonable deduction for the deficiency. The equitable doctrine

of substantial performance is applied to suits at law involving almost every situation. The result of the application of the doctrine is that substantial performance (or substantial compliance) is deemed performance. Philip Hiss Co. v. Pitcairn, 107 Fed. 425.

DISCHARGE BY OPERATION OF LAW. There are six usual ways by which a contract may be discharged by operation of law: (1) by merger; (2) by alteration or loss of a written instrument; (3) by failure of consideration; (4) by a breach going to the essence; (5) by repudiation; (6) by marriage (at common law, but not under the D. C. Code). 

(1) X sues Y for a breach of contract and recovers & judgment. The contract is merged in the judgment. X accordingly no longer has any rights against Y based on the contract but may pursue his rights only by enforcing the judgment. The judgment absorbed the contract. X sues Y for assault and battery (or any other tort). X recovers judgment. As in the case of actions ex contractu, X's tort right is absorbed by and merged in the judgment. X and Y enter into a parol contract ("parol" means either verbal or by simple writing not under seal). Subsequently they substitute for the parol agreement a contract under seal executed by both. The parol contract is merged in the sealed instrument and thereby discharged. Clifton v. Jackson Iron Co., 74 Mich. 183. In the cases above mentioned where X recovered judgments against Y for breach of contract and in tort, the judgments in both instances discharged X's previous rights of action. Merger is therefore defined as the acceptance of a higher security in place of one of lower pedigree thus discharging by operation of law the inferior security. One simple contract will not extinguish nor merge another simple contract. Wylly v. Collins, 9 Ga. 223. The rule is that a second security accepted in addition to one similar in character does not affect the validity of the first one, unless the first one is thereby discharged. The reason given is that the second security is merely a substituted agreement. As no discharge was thereby effected the taking of the second security in addition to the first one cannot amount to a merger because a merger requires that there be an acceptance of a higher security in place of one of a lower degree the result of which is to swallow up, absorb, discharge and extinguish the prior right of action. In order to constitute a merger, also, it is essential that the subject matter of the two securities and the parties be identical. Shelby v. Chicago R. R. Co., 143 Ill. 385. Where the parties merely reduce an oral or verbal contract to a simple writing not under seal, there is no merger. They have merely substituted one kind of a parol agreement for another of the same degree. (2) It is generally held that a material alteration of any written instrument by one of the parties discharges the instrument by operation of law. X changes the sum Y owes him in a written contract from $300 to $800. This is a material alteration and operates to discharge the contract. A mere alteration is defined as any act done upon an instrument by which its legal meaning, language, or consequences is changed, altered or impaired. A mere alteration will not discharge a contract, but a material alteration will do so. If the alteration has no tendency to mislead any person or does not materially affect the rights of the contracting parties in any way, it is not a material alteration and consequently will not operate as a discharge. Only a material alteration will operate as & discharge. It is for the courts to determine what constitutes a material alteration. It is the general rule that any change in the words or form of a written instrument, even though made by one of the parties in interest, is not a material alteration where the legal effect and identity of the writing is unaltered, unaffected and unimpaired. The alteration must in some way materially affect the rights and duties of the parties. This doctrine has often been applied, even in

the criminal law, particularly in cases of forgery. Where X altered Y's check or negotiable promissory note and thus raised it from $300 to $800, merely by changing the numerals, but leaving the writton description of the sum untouched, it was held that X was not guilty of forgery on the ground that the alteration was immaterial. This is based on the rule of law that where there is a conflict between a sum expressed in numerals and also in writing, the writing governs. 

Accordingly under this view such an immaterial alteration should not operate to discharge the instrument. There is a difference between an "alteration" and a "spoliation." 2 Cyc. 151. An alteration is made by a party to the agreement or by a stranger with the consent of a party. A spoliation, however, in contemplation of law, is a material alteration made by a stranger to the instrument without the consent of either party. Alteration operates as a discharge. A spoliation will not operate as a discharge, but the instrument will be enforced in its original condition provided its original terms are ascertainable from the instrument itself. Camp v. Shaw, 52 Ill. App. 241. Where X makes an alteration by consent of the other party, it is considered a new agreement and not an alteration. Where an instrument was altered or mutilated or spoliated by accident or mistake, there is no discharge, even though it happened by act of one of the parties. 

Where a written instrument is lost, the contract is not discharged. The loser is still entitled to his rights created by the instrument. A written instrument is merely a chose in possession evidentiary of a chose in action. The property right is the intangible chose in action and the tangible chose in possession is merely proof of it. In the case of negotiable instruments, however, the holder loses his rights where he loses the instrument, unless he posts a bond or an undertaking to indemnify the party liable against other possible claims. As a rule, the law will permit the holder of a lost instrument to recover by giving secondary evidence of the contents of the instrument and posting sufficient security to protect the party liable from subsequent claims. McGregory v. McGregory, 107 Mass. 543. At common law the contents of a lost instrument could be established only in a court of equity. This is no longer true in the District of Columbia, By an act of Congress, it is now provided that no suit at law founded upon a lost instrument shall be dismissed on the ground that the suit should have been brought in equity, but a similar bond or undertaking to that required in equity shall be given as a condition precedent to judgment. D. C. Code, S. 13-204. (3) Based on equitable doctrines, failure of consideration operates to discharge a contract. This doctrine, originating in equity, is recognized by the common law courts in the United States and illustrates the modern trend of jurisprudence in this country toward the merging of legal substantive law into the substantive law of equity. Where X contracted to sell Y a General Electric refrigerator of 7 cubic feet capacity, but instead delivers an Eskimo refrigerator of 2 cubic feet capacity, there is a failure of consideration entitling Y to return the said icebox and be discharged from his obligation under the agreement. right to rescind is grounded upon failure of consideration. There is a condition implied in law attaching to every contract that a man shall get what he bargained for. This is often referred to as a condition subsequent implied by the law itself, regardless of the intention of the parties. Withers v. Greene, 9 How. 

(4) Where a breach goes to the essence or to the heart of a bilateral contract, the innocent party is thereby discharged by operation of law. A breach that does not go to the essence of a contract does not operate as a discharge. 

In such a situation, the contract still subsists, but the injured party may retrieve his loss by an action for breach of contract in assumpsit. Where a breach does not go to the essence of the agreement, the situation may very well result in

a case where the doctrine of substantial performance (substantial compliance) prevents even an action for breach of contract on the ground that performance is considered complete. The other party, however, may deduct for the deficiency. 

Where a breach of a bilateral contract goes to the essence of the contract, it does not necessarily operate as a discharge unless the other party elects to treat it as such. If he does not so elect, he may treat it merely as a breach and not as a discharge and seek to recover damages on that ground. (5) An absolute and unqualified refusal to perform entitles the innocent party to treat such refusal, at his election, as a discharge. Where there is a repudiation there may always be a discharge, whether it goes to the essence of the contract or not. (6) By the ancient common law the marriage of X and Y worked a discharge by operation of law of their mutual obligations, except where performance of the agreement was intended to eventuate after coverture. Cage v. Acton, 1 Ld. Raym. 

515. Marriage in the District of Columbia today does not generally operate as a discharge under the statutes in force. Notes v. Snyder, 55 App. D. C. 233. Therefore, where X and Y marry in the District of Columbia, their premarital contractual obligations remain intact; but as to premarital torts to the person, the rule is otherwise. Spector v. Weisman, 59 App. D. C. 280; D. C. Code, T. 30 ch. Z.

DISCHARGING THE RIGHT OF ACTION. The right of action may be discharged (1) 

by the provisions of the agreement; (2) by the voluntary act of the parties; (3) by an accord and satisfaction; (4) by arbitration and award; (5) by operation of law, such as by a discharge in bankruptcy and by the statute of limitations. 

X and Y make a contract. If X breaks his promise, a right of action accrues in favor of Y. Y's right of action may be discharged either by the provisions of the contract itself, by operation of law, or by the act of the parties themselves. 

A discharge of the right of action by terms of the contract is often found in insurance policies where it is provided that unless claim is filed within a specified time after the occurrence of a loss, the right of action is thereby barred. Examples where the right of action may be discharged by an act of the parties occurs in cases of waiver, release, gift, accord and satisfaction, award and arbitration. 

An accord and satisfaction is a new contract the consequence of which discharges the right of action possessed by one of the parties to a former contract. Accord and satisfaction may be either by parol or under seal. An accord alone will not operate as a discharge unless there follows the element of satisfaction. Kromer v. Heim, 75 N. Y. 574. Where X owes Y $500 and Y contracts to release X upon the delivery of a sow, Y is nevertheless entitled to suc X for the amount due up to the time that Y has actually accepted the sow. Even where X promises to deliver a sow in exchange for Y's promise to give a release, the new contract of accord and satisfaction is no defense to the original cause of action until the new contract is completely executed. It has been held, however, that an accord without satisfaction may amount to a valid defense where the parties have further contracted that the mere making of the new contract of accord shall per se supplant the old contract. Morehouse v. Second Nat. Bank, 98 N. Y. 503.

There is a distinction between an accord and a compromise. An accord and satisfaction is an agreement between two persons, one of whom has a right of action against the other, that the latter should do or give, and the former accept, something in satisfaction of the right of action different from, and usually less than, what might be legally enforced. When the accord is executed, it is called "satisfaction," resulting in an accord and satisfaction. A compromise, on the other hand, is an arrangement arrived at, either in court or out of court, for

settling a dispute upon what appears to the parties to be equitable terms, having regard to the uncertainty regarding the facts, or both the law and the facts. 

Rau v. U. S., 260 Fed. 131. A composition should also be distinguished from an accord. An accord properly denotes an arrangement between a debtor and a single creditor for a discharge of the obligation by part payment or on different terms. 

On the other hand, a composition designates an arrangement between a debtor and the whole body of his creitors (or at least a considerable proportion of them) for the liquidation of their claims by the dividend offered. A composition in bankruptcy is an arrangement between a bankrupt and his creditors whereby the amount he can be expected to pay is liquidated, and he is allowed to retain his assets, upon condition of his making the payments agreed upon. Myers v. International Trust Co., 273 U. S. 380.

The courts at common law did not favor the submission of controversies to arbitration and award on the ground that arbitrators were poaching upon the jurisdiction of the courts. Scott v. Avery, 5 H. L. C. 811. The tendency of the law today, however, is to encourage the submission of controversies to arbitration and award. The practice and procedure to be followed where a controversy is submitted to arbitration and award is outlined in D. C. Code, T. 16, ch. 17; S. 11-804; S. 11-520.

At common law there were no statutes of limitations and consequently the lapse of time did not affect contractual rights. The statutes of limitations in the District of Columbia do not affect the inherent character of an agreement, but merely give the defendant a right to plead the statute as a defense to a cause of action. The defense of the statute of limitations must be specially pleaded, otherwise it is waived and the plaintiff may have judgment. When pleaded the statute merely discharges the right of action, but not the contract itself.
