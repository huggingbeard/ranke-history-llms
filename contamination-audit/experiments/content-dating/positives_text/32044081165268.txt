DEMOCRACY IN GREAT BRITAIN AND FRANCE SINCE THE WORLD WAR I. Political Democracy & Economic Liberalism 

ASCISM may regard itself as the last bulwark against Com- Fmunism and may organize anti-Communist alliances. But in one respect at least Fascism and Communism have much in common. Both repudiate political democracy, Fascism openly and defiantly, Communism in fact if not in theory. The countries of western Europe and the United States, however, continue to cling to the nineteenth century belief in political democracy. In the past few years as the Fascist challenge has become ever more menacing, an increasing number of champions of the democratic form of government have made earnest and passionate confessions of their faith. In reading some of 

President Roosevelt's speeches, and the pamphlets of Hamilton 

Fish Armstrong, Thomas Mann and Dorothy Thompson, one could almost imagine one was reading Walt Whitman, Mazzini or John Stuart Mill.

Let us summarize here very briefly the basic tenets of the creed of political democracy. It must be recognized at the outset that the older concepts of liberalism have become fused with those of democracy. From liberalism springs the belief that every man has certain rights which no government can justly deny him. Freedom of speech, of the press, of association, religious toleration and the right to an impartial trial — such are some of those individual liberties which are absolutely essential to the democratic conception of government. From these individual liberties springs the corollary that no majority, however large, may deprive the minority of the right to voice its opposition so long as that opposition does not directly incite to violence. From democracy come the convictions that every man and woman has a right to participate in government

2 

Democracy in Great Britain through the exercise of the franchise, that popularly elected legislatures should make the laws, that the executive should have strictly limited powers and should be responsible either to the people directly or to the legislature. Such are the beliefs to which the average Englishman, Frenchman and American would heartily subscribe, and to which the average German, Italian and Russian in this mid-twentieth century would take vigorous exception.

Such are, then, the basic assumptions underlying British and French political developments since the World War. There are also certain economic assumptions characteristic of the modern democracies. No commonly accepted label can be given them, but perhaps "economic liberalism" is as good as any other, if by that term one does not mean the extreme laissez-faire philosophy of the early nineteenth century. These economic assumptions of economic liberalism are sharply enough in contrast with the beliefs accepted in Communist Russia, but the differences between the economic philosophy of the Fascist and democratic countries are those of degree and method more than of kind. 

In Fascism and Communism alike, of course, the state is supreme in economic life. The economic liberal, on the contrary, believes that as much private initiative as possible should be left to the individual and he rebels against the conception of totalitarianism in economic affairs. He also maintains that considerable economic power should be left to voluntary associations such as employers' associations, trade unions and consumers' societies, all of which organizations in Fascist and Communist countries are hardly more than agencies of the government without any independence of their own. It is in this belief that the individual should be allowed a large measure of economic initiative that the economic liberal differs most sharply from 

1, the Fascist or Communist.

It must not be supposed, however, that the economic liberal of the twentieth century opposes all government intervention and regulation. Certainly he accepts, without important qualification, the duty of the government to impose certain mini-

mum standards in health and sanitation. He is in favor of factory legislation and social insurance. He believes in a certain measure of government regulation of banking, monopolies and public utilities. He may even accept the direct operation of certain economic activities by governmental agencies. 

The average economic liberal still believes, however, in private property, capitalism, the profit motive and an automatic price structure. He may be willing to agree that certain economic activities, because of their public character, should be operated by the government, but he thinks that the average agricultural, industrial and commercial enterprise should be run as private property under the capitalist system. And he is decidedly distrustful of price regulation by the government. The contrast here of economic liberalism with Communism is of course very sharp but much less so with Fascism which also accepts private property, capitalism and profits. Fascism, however, has resorted to an ever tightening state control over private capitalism, a control far greater than that exercised under the New Deals in 

England, France and the United States.

To sum up, Communism has repudiated the whole system of private capitalism. Fascism operates in a capitalistic framework, but the constant encroachments of the state have left an ever narrower scope for private initiative. The economic liberal still accepts the capitalist system of free enterprise, private property, and an automatic price structure. (He still believes in private initiative and the right of the individual to associate himself freely with others to bring about economic ends. But he recognizes that the public interest requires government intervention. For him the problem is to find the proper balance between private initiative and government control, but he is convinced that there must be a balance. 

This pamphlet will attempt to describe the major political and economic developments since the World War in Great Britain and France, the two most important countries of Europe where political democracy and economic liberalism are still the prevalent beliefs among the great majority of the people. II. Great Britain 

REAT BRITAIN had no devastated areas after the World War, G but she did have ruined industries and consequently depressed areas. For many reasons her markets, especially for her staple exports—coal, iron and steel products, machinery and cotton textiles-have been greatly reduced in size. The use of substitute sources of power-oil and electricity generated by water power-have helped to ruin the British coal industry which has also suffered from antiquated methods of mining, from too large a number of unprofitable mines in operation, and from the English system of mining royalties by which large rental fees have to be paid to landlords. The popularity of rayon has hurt the cotton industry, as has the competition from Japan and the establishment of cotton factories in India. The English iron, steel, machine, and ship building industries have all had to meet intensified competition from such rivals as the United States, France, and Germany. There can be little doubt that these older British industries were operated in too small units, that their machinery was often antiquated, and that the English tradition of individualism prevented the consolidation requisite for more effective marketing.

The market for English goods was also narrowed at first by the inability of Germany and Russia to make large purchases from England. Moreover England found herself faced with rising tariff rates, notably in the United States and in the succession states of the old Austrian Empire, while France engaged in such devices to protect her market as the import quota. In 

1925 the English currency was restored to its pre-war value in gold at a time when the currency of England's chief European

rivals, Germany, France, and Italy, was badly depreciated. This step definitely put her at a disadvantage when competing with such rivals for world trade. 

As a result of these declining markets whole areas, the socalled "special areas", such as South Wales, southern Scotland, parts of the Midlands, and Newcastle with its environs, have suffered almost continuous depression since the World War. And of course the existence of such areas has meant a constant problem of unemployment on a large scale. From 1921 to the end of the decade the unemployment figure never fell below one million and after 1929 the number greatly increased. Efforts to reduce unemployment by settlement in the colonies were not strikingly successful. Eventually a large number were absorbed in the new industries which were springing up and booming in the south of England, but such shifts in population do not occur rapidly and they leave nasty scars behind them. 

To meet the problem of unemployment the government embarked on an ambitious and costly plan of unemployment insurance. The scheme had been inaugurated experimentally as a part of the National Insurance Act of 1911. In 1920 it was extended so as to include most manual workers. The time was an unfortunate one, for large scale unemployment started shortly thereafter and made the scheme unsound from an actuarial point of view. The plan had originally provided that relief could be given only for fifteen weeks in each year. This part of the plan broke down and the recipient was soon receiving what amounted to relief rather than insurance and was popularly known as a "dole". The cost of the scheme to the government grew as the total of unemployment increased and furnished the cabinets of the 'twenties with a perennial financial problem. It would be impossible in such a survey as this to trace in detail the numerous changes in the unemployment insurance laws. 

When the Conservatives were in power they emphasized the cost of the scheme and the way in which many workers preferred to accept the dole, with its allowances for dependents, rather than to seek work. Naturally the Conservatives tended

Democracy in Great Britain to tighten up the scheme in order to reduce costs. On the other hand the Labor Party and their supporters in the trade unions emphasized the need for relief, the inability of the unemployed to find work, and the sufferings and humiliations to which the workers were subjected by the devices of the Conservatives to reduce unemployment benefits. When Labor was in power, therefore, the system was administered with greater consideration for the laborer. At any rate, whatever the defects or merits of the system, the unemployed were cared for and their purchasing power was maintained. This latter point is important, for the continued purchasing power of labor prevented a catastrophic decline in the demand for consumers' goods and hence in production.

Unemployment insurance was expensive. Hardly less so were the government contributions towards old age pensions, which had become partially contributory in 1926, and the health insurance scheme. Also very expensive was the government housing program which provided more than a million dwellings between 1921 and 1935, the majority of them built by local government authorities with national government subsidies and the remainder constructed by private builders with generous government aid.

Partly in order to pay the bill for unemployment insurance, other social insurance benefits, and housing improvements, English taxes soared in the 'twenties. Probably no country was so heavily taxed as was Great Britain with an income tax that since the war has fluctuated between twenty and twenty-five per cent, and with very low exemptions. Inheritance taxes or death duties also were so high that many estates were forced on the market. The high burden of taxation undoubtedly contributed in no small measure to raising production costs in England and thus made it still more difficult for her to compete in the world market.

The political framework within which solutions have been sought for Britain's perplexing economic problems has changed but little since the World War. Great Britain is still a country

governed by King and Parliament under the cabinet system. 

The monarchy has become more popular than ever before. It is doubtful whether any English monarch was ever so much beloved as George V during the latter years of his reign. His respectability, his devotion to duty, his complete acceptance of his position, all tended to make him an ideal constitutional monarch.

In 1936 George V died and was succeeded by his son who, as Prince of Wales, had long been a popular and colorful figure and who took the title of Edward VIII. In less than a year he had abdicated and his brother had become king as George VI. The crisis over Edward's abdication was concerned with his desire to marry an American who had been twice divorced. The Prime 

Minister, Stanley Baldwin, refused to sanction the marriage and Edward preferred to abandon the throne rather than to give up the woman he loved. The episode was important chiefly because it showed that the King was a public figure even to the extent that his private life was subject to parliamentary control. 

Moreover, since the King is the most important bond between the mother country and the dominions, it is essential that he should do nothing in his private life which might loosen that bond. It is not surprising that the Anglican Church with its rigid views on divorce should have played an important role in the opposition to Edward's proposed marriage. At the time it was thought that the affair had rocked the throne, but George VI has apparently already gained the respect and love of his people and bids fair to be a monarch who will carry on worthily the tradition to which his father contributed so significantly. 

In 1914 Great Britain had a liberal franchise but not even complete manhood suffrage. English women had carried on a spectacular campaign for the suffrage before the war but had not attained their goal. In 1918 there was passed the Representation of the People Act which at last conferred complete manhood suffrage on all over twenty-one. Also, perhaps partly as a reward for their loyalty during the war, the suffragettes were given part of their demands. Women over thirty who occupied

Democracy in Great Britain property worth five pounds a year or whose husbands occupied such property might vote for members of Parliament. A further clause of the act reduced plural voting drastically, so that no one might vote in more than two constituencies. The Act of 1918 added about eight million voters to the electorate. Ten years later in 1928 the work was completed in the so-called "Flapper" Suffrage Bill, when the vote was extended to all women over twenty-one on the same basis as men. The process by which 

England thus gradually moved towards universal suffrage is typical of the English tendency to move slowly. It had taken nearly a century and the passage of five great reform bills to achieve a goal which some countries have tried to attain over night.

Probably the most important changes in English political conditions since the war have taken place in the field of party history. The two great political parties of the nineteenth century had been the Conservatives and Liberals, the parties of Disraeli and Gladstone respectively. In 1914 the Liberals were in power under Herbert Asquith, but in 1916 the Asquith ministry fell and David Lloyd George, the Liberal firebrand from

Wales who had made a reputation as a social reformer, formed a coalition government which lasted until 1922. By his action 

Lloyd George contributed notably towards the winning of the war but he also brought about the weakening of the Liberal Party, for the faction under Asquith's leadership never completely forgave him. In 1922 the Conservatives, who had always been the most numerous group in the coalition, withdrew and Lloyd George resigned. Since that time no Liberal has been prime minister and the party has declined until it is hardly more than a handful. Moreover it has been prey to severe factional quarrels.

As the Liberal Party declined, its place was taken by the rising Labor Party which had been formed in 1900 and had elected forty-two members to Parliament in 1910. After the war its growth was rapid, and in 1924 it first took office. Behind the 

Labor Party and providing most of its early voting strength

was the trade union movement. As the Liberal Party declined many middle class Liberals switched their votes to Labor candidates. Its program was moderately socialistic, but it always refused to countenance Communism of the Russian variety. 

Prominent among the planks in the Labor platform were demands for a capital levy and for the nationalization of mines and railways. But it was determined to seek its goals only by strictly constitutional methods and it decried violence. 

The leadership of the party had devolved upon the Scotchman, J. Ramsay MacDonald, son of a laborer, who educated himself, married the daughter of a wealthy university professor and thus acquired some means and social standing. As a pacifist 

MacDonald had served a prison sentence during the war. A man of great personal charm, MacDonald was something of a poet and he possessed a magnificent voice and considerable eloquence. Unfortunately for the Labor Party, he came to be happier in his later years in talking with George V than with the horny handed labor leaders upon whom his power rested. 

MacDonald's subordinates were drawn on the one hand from the intellectuals—such men as Philip Snowden and Sidney

Webb-and on the other hand from trade union leaders such as Arthur Henderson and J. H. Thomas. The intellectuals have always tended to dominate the party, a point which has caused considerable jealousy among the more numerous trade unionists. 

From 1922 to 1931 the Conservative and Labor Parties were alternately in power. The Conservatives had large majorities behind them when in office, but the two Labor cabinets (1924, 1929-1931) never had an absolute majority and were dependent upon the good will of the Liberals. Hence they could never achieve much in the direction of socialism. The Conservative leader was Stanley Baldwin, a wealthy and well connected business man, something of an intellectual himself, a plain, unpretentious man who possessed an unusual amount of tact and patience and who earned the affection even of his opponents. 

But Mr. Baldwin was not a great statesman, if by that term is meant a man who accomplishes great reforms. The years from

Democracy in Great Britain 

1922 to 1931 were remarkable for the paucity of constructive measures of domestic legislation. The Laborites were unable to carry through reforms, the Conservatives unwilling. 

Probably the most important and certainly the most dramatic incident in British internal history during the middle 'twenties was the General Strike of 1926. The origin of the strike was concerned with the situation in the depressed coal industry where the owners and operators refused to accept nationalization and the miners refused to accept wage cuts. After the Conservative Baldwin government had temporized by giving large subsidies to maintain wages and by appointing commissions of inquiry whose recommendations were disregarded, affairs came to a crisis. The Trades Union Congress voted to support the miners, and some three million workers went on strike. Although the heavy industries were involved, no attempt was made to call out the workers in the public utilities, food or public health services and thus the strike should perhaps more correctly be called a "sympathetic" than a "general" strike. Moreover no violence was displayed on either side. The strike was badly managed by the trade union leaders who called it off after nine days without gaining any of their objectives. The coal strike continued for several months more, but it also eventuated in a defeat for the miners with resultant lower wages and longer hours.

The general strike not only resulted in a first class defeat for the trade unions but it also aroused public opinion over the danger of such wide spread labor troubles. Consequently the 

Conservative Baldwin ministry used the occasion to pass in 

1927 the Trades Unions and Trades Disputes Act which made general and sympathetic strikes illegal, which made picketing very difficult, and which prevented trade union collections for the Labor Party unless the individual trade unionist expressly authorized such collections. 

The most important crisis in post-war British political history occurred in 1931. The second Labor cabinet under MacDonald had been in office for a little more than two years. The depths of the depression had been reached. The foreign trade balance was

unfavorable, unemployment stood at its highest point since the World War, the payments on the "dole" were steadily increasing, there was a large deficit in the budget, and the general unsettled outlook was leading to a drain upon the English gold reserve. 

In the summer of 1931 a commission of inquiry recommended slight increases in taxation and drastic cuts in the social service benefits. MacDonald and his Chancellor of the Exchequer, Philip Snowden, advised the acceptance of the commission's recommendations, but the rank and file of the Labor members of Parliament felt that the working classes would thus be called upon to make sacrifices much greater than the capitalists. Upon this difference the cabinet split and MacDonald resigned. On the next day he formed a new "national Coalition", composed predominantly of Conservatives, a small number of Liberals, and a few Labor leaders. MacDonald and his followers were not unnaturally regarded as traitors to their party and were promptly expelled.

Later in the autumn of 1931 new elections were held which resulted in an overwhelming victory for the National Government and a correspondingly stunning defeat for Labor. The 

Labor Party saw its representation in Parliament reduced to 52 as compared with 289 in 1929. Indeed the election constituted one of the most astounding electoral overturns in parliamentary history. MacDonald must therefore bear the chief responsibility for the eclipse, at least temporarily, of the party which he had done so much to bring to power. That his motives were patriotic can hardly be doubted. Unfortunately there can also be little doubt that he had long since grown away from the rank and file of his party. The "treason" of Ramsay MacDonald and his followers to the Labor Party has served to embitter relations between capital and labor and has helped to intensify the already existing class consciousness of the British laboring classes to a somewhat alarming degree. 

In the following years some National Laborites and National Liberals broke away from the Coalition. As a result the government was only "National" in name and became more and more

Democracy in Great Britain frankly Conservative. In 1935 MacDonald retired as Prime 

Minister in favor of Stanley Baldwin. In the same year another election gave the National Government another tremendous majority, although the Laborities secured about 150 seats in the House of Commons. In 1937 Baldwin resigned, went to the 

House of Lords and was succeeded as Prime Minister by Neville Chamberlain, a son of Joseph Chamberlain. He had served the 

National Government as Chancellor of the Exchequer and had long been regarded as presumptive heir to the Conservative leadership. Chamberlain is not a brilliant statesman, but he is regarded by the governing class as a "safe" man and is truly representative of the Conservative business men and squires. 

The National Government inaugurated a series of far-reaching changes in policy which have sometimes been referred to as the British "New Deal". It should be noted, however, that the 

British New Deal had but a single purpose, that of recovery from depression. It was not interested in reform, as were the 

American and French New Deals. The statesmen who carried through the British New Deal were Conservatives, not radicals or socialists. But it should also be noted that England had already made most of the reforms which engrossed so much of the attention of American and French statesmen. Social security legislation had been adopted twenty years before, factory legislation nearly a century previously. Trade union activities had been accepted and protected by a series of enactments dating back to Gladstone and Disraeli. Banking reforms dated from the nineteenth century. The British statesmen were thus faced by a less complex problem than were the New Dealers in the United States or the statesmen of the Popular Front in France. 

Although Great Britain had long since abandoned laissezfaire in so far as it related to labor problems, she was still as late as 1931 more faithful to the other aspects of laissez-faire than any other great nation. She was the last bulwark of free trade and a strong bulwark of orthodox monetary theories. And then within two or three years she carried through a series of revolutionary economic changes which marked the end of the

laissez-faire epoch. Back of these changes of course stood the abandonment of laissez-faire by Britain's rivals and the perplexing problems which had faced her continuously since the war and which had become intensified during the depression. The main problem, as the statesmen of the National Government saw it, was to bring about economic recovery which implied a balanced budget, a less unfavorable trade balance, the regaining of some of her lost markets, a revivified industry and agriculture.

England had been the first nation to adopt the gold standard (1821). Gradually throughout the nineteenth century most of the great economic powers followed her example. Like the others, Great Britain suspended the gold standard during the World War, but restored it in 1925 at the old pre-war rate. As has been noted above, such a restoration of the pre-war pound meant that it was over valued in terms of depreciated currencies in rival countries. The British investor was thus favored at the expense of the British manufacturer and exporter, for an over valued pound meant high costs of production and a handicap in competing with countries like Germany and France whose currencies had been depreciated. By 1931 the situation was aggravated by the fact that three-fifths of the world's gold supply was concentrated in the United States and France.

One of the first acts of the National Government in 1931 was the suspension of the gold standard and the depreciation of the pound from $4.86 to a position somewhere between $3.00 and $4.00. In the next year a large Exchange Equalization Fund was established to prevent violent fluctuations which might result from speculative operations. The Fund was also expected to maintain the value of the pound so as to prevent rapid price changes at home. When the pound threatened to become too high in terms of rival currencies the Equalization Fund sold pounds. When pounds were selling too cheaply on international exchanges the Fund purchased them. Thus the British pound had become a "managed currency". The devaluation of the pound gave a spur to British exports and thus helped to revive

Democracy in Great Britain industry. It was followed, moreover, by a successful conversion of the public debt from 5 to 32 per cent, an operation which greatly relieved the budget.

An even more revolutionary change in British policy occurred in 1932 when a protective tariff was adopted. Great Britain had been the first country to adopt free trade in the nineteenth century. Beginning about 1850 her tariff had been for revenue only and had been confined to a few articles chiefly of a luxury character not produced in England. Late in the nineteenth century the industrial rivalry of Germany and the United States, the catastrophic decline of British agriculture, and the desire to cement the Empire more closely together by trade ties had created a movement for a return to protective tariffs coupled with imperial preferences. This movement which had been led by Joseph Chamberlain had suffered decisive defeat at the polls in 1906. After the World War the protective movement grew rapidly. An increasing number of economists advocated a protective tariff. Certain war-time restrictions upon imports were continued after the war. The Lloyd George coalition in 1921 passed the Safeguarding of Industries Act which gave protection to a limited number of so-called "key" industries. In 1923 

Baldwin had brought the issue up again but the electorate returned to Parliament an anti-protectionist majority of Liberals and Laborites. All through the decade of the 'twenties the agitation continued. Why should England try to maintain free trade in a world where the United States imposed the Smoot-Hawley tariff and France used the import quota? Even many who believed theoretically in free trade were converted to the desirability of a tariff for retaliatory purposes. 

Early in 1932 the National Government, backed by its tremendous majority, passed a general tariff bill of ten per cent. 

It was dramatically fitting that the bill should have been introduced by Neville Chamberlain, son of the Joseph Chamberlain who had been the first great advocate of a return to protection. 

Later the general rates were raised and an Imports Advisory Board created which could raise rates for particular imports.

The imposition of a general protective tariff at last made it possible for Great Britain to give imperial preference to the dominions. In the summer of 1932 a great Imperial Conference met at Ottawa. Some ardent imperialists had hoped for substantial imperial free trade, but the dominions were loath to reduce tariffs upon their manufactured goods while England found it difficult to give large preferences in raw materials and foodstuffs because of her close relations with the Scandinavian countries and with Argentina. Eventually a series of bilateral treaties were negotiated which did act as a spur to inter-imperial trade. 

Because of the bargaining power which it gave her, the general tariff also permitted Great Britain to make a large number of trade treaties, notably with the Scandinavian countries and Argentina, which were so important as sources for English food supply and as markets for English manufactures. The most recent and most important of these treaties has been that with the United States in 1938 which brought about mutual reductions and promised mutual advantages. The treaty was especially difficult to negotiate because both Great Britain and the United States have such close trade and political relations with Canada, whose interests had to be consulted at every turn. 

In 1846 when the Corn Laws were repealed the British government withdrew all protection from British agriculture. The dominant manufacturers were more interested in cheap food for labor, a slogan which naturally appealed to the working classes. 

English agriculture had remained fairly prosperous, however, until about 1870 when the combination, in such countries as the United States, Russia, and Argentina, of virgin soils, large scale agricultural production and cheap ocean transportation had deprived the English farmer of his home market. From that time 

Great Britain purchased an ever larger proportion of her food supply abroad. Since the World War more interest has been shown in the problems of the British farmer. The danger of being starved out by submarine warfare convinced many Britishers that an attempt should be made to produce a larger proportion of their own food. Not that any sane Englishman dreamed

Democracy in Great Britain of agricultural self-sufficiency! Some kind of government aid must be given, however, if the English farmer was to compete with American and Canadian grain, Argentine meat, Irish and

Danish bacon and dairy products. The problem was complicated moreover by the important and almost traditional trade relations which had grown up around Argentinian meat and Danish bacon.

The government had started to protect British agriculture before 1931, but the attempts made after that date were more ambitious and wider in scope. The story is too complex to be told in detail in such a survey as this, but in general it can be said that the schemes adopted marked a complete repudiation of laissez-faire and that they constitute the most important experiments in government regulation yet attempted in Britain. 

Experts differ in their evaluation of the various schemes but certainly they have not been entirely successful. The government hesitated to levy tariffs on foodstuffs directly because of the opposition of public opinion. But they guaranteed wheat growers a price for their product way above the world price for wheat. They gave a direct and substantial subsidy to the beet sugar producer, and thus large areas have been converted to the growing of sugar beets. This industry has grown up entirely since the World War and owes its existence to government subsidies. Elaborate marketing schemes have been introducd for various branches of agricultural production — milk, hops, pigs and pig products. In some cases protection is given by the use of import quotas to foreign producers, thus guaranteeing the domestic producer a certain proportion of the home market. In some cases wholesale and retail prices have been determined by a Marketing Board for the product. In some cases the Marketing Board has controlled the whole domestic output and has established production quotas for the individual producer. 

There remains an evaluation of the British New Deal. Authorities differ as to the success of the agricultural schemes. 

But there can be no doubt that unemployment dropped rapidly,

that the budget was balanced as early as 1933, that imports decreased and exports increased, thus correcting somewhat the balance of trade. By 1935 or 1936 Great Britain seemed far more prosperous than any of her great rivals and British statesmen pointed proudly to the success of their "New Deal". There can be no doubt that the abandonment of the gold standard spurred British production and export, and that the heroic measures taken to balance the budget restored confidence in the government. Other factors in the situation are more controversial. The critics of the National Government would say that the return to prosperity was merely the upward swing of the business cycle. It should also be kept in mind that the tremendous rearmament program of the British government has played no small part in absorbing the unemployed and in keeping heavy industry going. In 1938 and 1939, however, the precipitous rise in armament costs has thrown the British budgets seriously out of balance. Not only have the already heavy taxes risen alarmingly, but the government has been forced to meet the deficits by loans.

A discussion of British problems since the World War would be incomplete without some reference to imperial relations. In no country of the world do the problems of empire play such an important role as in Great Britain. Imperial affairs may be divided into two parts, those having to do with the self-governing dominions, and those relating to regions of the empire which do not have self-government.

The post-war period has been marked by an increasing degree of independence for the dominions until they have become today hardly more than independent nations bound in a loose alliance with Great Britain. They were admitted into the 

League of Nations as separate members. This diplomatic independence was increased when Canada sent a minister to the United States. In 1926 at an imperial conference the famous 

Balfour Report was adopted which defined the dominions as "autonomous communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another . . . though

Democracy in Great Britain united by a common allegiance to the crown." In 1931 the report was legalized in the Statute of Westminster, which completed the edifice of legal autonomy and freed the dominions from any further legislative dependence on the British Parliament. The position of the King thus became even more important as almost the sole symbol of unity in the so-called "British Commonwealth of Nations". Sentimental ties remain strong, however, imperial conferences are held frequently to keep the members of the Commonwealth in close contact with each other, and trade relations are important. Moreover the British navy continues to furnish a very considerable degree of protection to the dominions.

Another dominion was created in 1922 when the Irish Free 

State was established. The Irish Home Rule Bill of 1912-14 

had never been put into operation and open rebellion broke out in Ireland in 1916. From that date until 1922 a bitter undeclared war was fought in Ireland between the British Government and the Sinn Fein Party. Finally in 1922 the southern counties of Ireland, constituting about two-thirds of the island, were organized as the Irish Free State and given dominion status. The remaining northern third, the old province of Ulster, was also given a parliament but not dominion status. The establishment of the Free State split the Sinn Fein Party and a second and even more tragic war raged for several years more. 

At last the Republicans under De Valera laid down their arms and reconstruction began. 

Eventually in 1932 De Valera became the President of the Free State with a policy which aimed to bring about complete Irish independence and the unity of the entire island. His regime has been marked by a bitter trade war with England, the abolition of the oath of allegiance to the King, and in 1937 by the establishment of the Irish Republic with a new constitution. In 1938 De Valera succeeded in negotiating a treaty with the British government by which the latter recognized the independence of the Irish Republic and removed the British fleet from Irish ports. The only outstanding problem was the separa-

tion of Ulster from the rest of Ireland, but the recent outrages by Irish extremists would seem to indicate that that problem may become acute in the near future.

Among those parts of the Empire which had not yet attained dominion status in 1914, Great Britain has encountered the most serious problems in the rising national movements among the Arabs of Egypt and Palestine, and among the peoples of India. Violence and terrorism have been common accompaniments of these nationalist movements and the British government has been forced to grant extensive concessions. Egypt was granted formal independence in 1922, but England has retained considerable control in the Sudan and at Suez. In 1936 a treaty regularized the position of Egypt as an "independent" state except in foreign affairs and in matters of defence. 

In 1917 the British government in the Balfour Declaration promised a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. 

Eventually Palestine was awarded to Great Britain as a mandate and the government began to redeem its promises. A large 

Jewish immigration followed and remarkable material progress resulted. But the Arab majority has been steadily opposed to the British policy and since 1936 the country has been the scene of repeated outrages by the Arabs against Jews and British officials. The Arabs have resented the steady purchase of increasing amounts of the small total of agricultural land. Moreover the Arabs feel that they cannot compete successfully with the Jews who have been plentifully supplied with capital from abroad. Most important of all, Arab nationalism feels that the whole country is theirs and that it is too small to be partitioned. 

Slowly and reluctantly the British government has resorted to severe repressive measures which have not thus far been remarkably successful even in maintaining order. Conciliation has been tried, partition suggested, repression attempted. All have thus far failed and no settlement of this particularly thorny problem is yet in sight.

III. France HE POLITICAL framework of the French Republic has not changed any more than that of the British monarchy since the World War. The President who "neither reigns nor rules" continues to act as titular head, but he is hardly more. That the 

French people were determined that he should not become a real executive seemed to be proved in 1924 when President Millerand was forced to resign. He had interfered in party politics, had forced one cabinet to resign and had even participated actively in the electoral campaign of 1924. Since his opponents were victorious it was but natural that their leaders should refuse to take office until he should resign.

The legislature still consists of the Senate and Chamber of Deputies. The power of the Senate, and its conservative character, was shown when in 1937 and again in 1938 it brought about the fall of the ministries of Léon Blum by refusing to grant him powers already voted by the Chamber of Deputies. 

Moreover it is the Senate which has consistently blocked every effort to adopt woman suffrage in France. 

French ministries have been just as unstable since the World War as before that event. The fundamental cause for the shortlived character of French cabinets is undoubtedly the multitude of parties in France, their lack of party discipline, and their tendency to split into personal factions. All ministries under such conditions must be coalition ministries and coalitions are naturally unstable. A further cause for instability has been the inability of the French premier to test public opinion during a crisis by dissolving the Chamber and calling for new elections./ 

Instead under French practice a Chamber of Deputies lasts out its four years regardless of the change in popular sentiment. An attempt by Premier Doumergue in 1934 to make a change in the constitution to enable the premier to dissolve the Chamber without the consent of the Senate was defeated. 

It must be recognized, however, that French ministries change far more frequently that do French policies. A wit said truly in the period shortly after the war, when the Lloyd George coalition

Democracy in France was still in office in England and when French ministries seemed particularly unstable, that the French changed their ministers but kept the same policies, while the English kept their ministers but changed their policies. Moreover a change of ministry in 

France does not necessitate a change in all of its members but only of two or three. Considerable stability is also maintained through the permanent bureaucracy. 

In such a brief survey as this no attempt can be made to trace French political history since the war. French parties have tended to fall into two main blocs, those of the Right which are conservative, and those of the Left which are radical. The Right is composed of many small groups without very much cohesion. 

The ablest statesman of the Right has been Raymond Poincaré, a corporation lawyer of great ability, a rigid nationalist, and a financial genius. The Left is made up chiefly of three fairly large and reasonably well disciplined parties. Since the Left has had a majority in the Chamber during most of the period since 1924 it is desirable to know something of these three main parties. 

The most conservative of the Left parties, the one nearest to the Center, is the Radical Socialist Party. This group, which is really not socialist at all, depends for its support upon the lower bourgeoisie and the peasantry. It is the party primarily of the black-coated, black-hatted and white-collared clerk, shopkeeper and small professional man. It is staunchly democratic and republican and is willing to support advanced social reform measures but it is not socialist. Among the most important leaders of the Radical Socialists have been Edouard Herriot and 

Edouard Daladier. Herriot gained a reputation as Mayor of

Lyons where he instituted many reforms, notably in public housing, and as a literary man. Several times premier, he is now 

President of the Chamber of Deputies. Daladier, the present premier (April, 1939), was formerly a school teacher and has shown himself a shrewd politician.

The second party of the Left is the Socialist Party, a Marxian party but moderate in its aims and methods. In the post-war period it has been closely allied with the powerful trade union

organization, the General Confederation of Labor (C. G. T.). 

It is more doctrinaire in its socialism than the British Labor Party but aims to achieve socialism gradually and through peaceful parliamentary methods. Its doctrinaire position was shown by its refusal until 1936 to make coalitions with bourgeois parties or to assume any government responsibility. This inflexible position inevitably made it necessary for the Radical Socialists to seek allies farther to the Right and helps to explain why most cabinets from 1924 to 1936 were more conservative than the composition of the Chamber would lead one to expect.

It is probable that the economic framework of France since the World War has changed to a greater degree than has the political framework. Nevertheless France remains predominantly a land of small peasant proprietors and of small scale highly skilled industries. The post-war period has witnessed, however, the growth of a considerable number of large scale mass production industries, so that it might almost be said that France has become industrialized. The extent of industrialism is still much less than in Germany, Great Britain, or the United states, but much greater than in France in 1914. 

At the end of the World War northern France was devastated, towns destroyed, mines flooded, industrial establishments in ruin. It must be remembered, moreover, that this was the very region which had produced before the war most of the coal, and a large portion of the iron, steel, textiles, and chemicals in France. This region had to be rebuilt and it was done fairly

Democracy in France quickly. Indeed restoration was substantially complete by the middle 'twenties. In the process of reconstruction the newest type of machinery and plant was installed and the efficiency of the new industries was high. By the Treaty of Versailles France had gained valuable economic resources, the richest iron ore beds in Europe, large potash deposits, the rich coal mines of the Saar (to be exploited at least temporarily by France), and many cotton, woolen and rayon factories. 

The process of reconstruction was directly aided by the French government which hoped to recover its advances through reparations from Germany. Vast amounts were spent and thus was inaugurated the problem of government finance which has proved perhaps the most difficult problem with which French statesmen in the post-war period have grappled, largely in vain. 

The reconstruction was financed partly by loans and partly by the printing of paper money. The expenses of the government constantly increased and the budget got badly out of balance. 

Moreover the currency declined in value until in 1926 it had sunk to about one-tenth of its pre-war value. The situation was made worse by the fact that reparations payments were much smaller than had been anticipated. From 1919 to 1926, then, government expenditures became constantly larger, the budget became more and more unbalanced, and the franc more and more depreciated. But there were compensations. The inflation of the currency contributed largely to the industrial boom. 

Foreign buyers found that their money went further in France than in England where the pound was at par, and consequently bought goods in France. Exports thus increased and the tourist trade boomed.

Finally the condition of government finance became so bad that the government could no longer float loans advantageously and there was danger that the inflation might become as serious as in Germany, where the mark had reached fantastic depths of depreciation. In 1926 Poincaré became premier and carried through a series of financial reforms which gained for him the title of "savior of the franc". Government expenditures were

sharply reduced and very heavy taxes were imposed, the budget was balanced and the value of the franc slowly rose until it was stabilized in 1928 at about four cents. The creditor class and the holders of government bonds had been forced to take severe losses but they had not been wiped out as in Germany. Poincaré remained premier until 1929 and during the three years in which he held office the national finances of France were sound. 

It was also during the Poincaré ministry that a remarkable bill for social insurance was enacted, although the system did not go into operation until 1930, after Poincaré's retirement.

Employers and employees each contributed three per cent of the worker's wage to which fund the state made additional grants. 

In return for the one payment the worker was given insurance against sickness, invalidity, maternity, old age, and death. This measure gave to France one of the most enlightened and comprehensive systems of social insurance in the world. 2. 

In 1929 Poincaré retired because of ill health and in the same year the world depression began. It affected France much more slowly than it did her neighbors but she has found it far more difficult to attain recovery. Until 1931 the depression was hardly noticable in France. Unemployment was not a serious problem. 

Although industry slowed up, agriculture, which had formerly employed much transient immigrant labor, took up much of the slack.

The years from 1931 to 1936 were very difficult ones in France. The successive unstable ministries were loath to devalue the franc a second time and to resort to inflation. The holders of government securities were a powerful group and naturally opposed further reductions in their income. The ministries attempted to cut down government expenditures but in vain. 

The budget could not be balanced, for returns from taxes decreased much more rapidly than did government expenses. After the pound had been devalued in 1931 and the dollar in 1933 the franc was probably over valued. The result was a shrinkage in exports and a trade balance alarmingly out of balance. Such scandals gave the extreme conservatives the opportunity to claim that the republic was rotten to the core. Fascism sprang up and soon claimed large bodies of adherents who favored a more authoritarian type of government. In February, 1934, serious rioting broke out in Paris and the police fired on the people. In the succeeding months armed clashes between Fascists and Communists became increasingly common, the same symptom that had appeared in Italy and Germany before the triumph of Fascism in those countries. To many it seemed that democracy was doomed in France. 

To meet the rising Fascist menace the parties of the Left joined together in the spring of 1936 to form the Popular Front. 

Wide divergencies between Communists, Socialists and Radical Socialists were temporarily forgotten in the common fear of Fascism. The elections of that year returned a large majority for the Popular Front parties, of which the Socialists elected the largest number of deputies. As a result Léon Blum departed from the traditional Socialist policy of refusal to enter coalitions, and became premier of a coalition cabinet. Although the 

Communists did not join the government they gave it their support.

Perhaps the most dramatic reform carried through by the Blum ministry was the reorganization of the Bank of France. 

This institution had long been in the hands of a small financial and industrial oligarchy, popularly known as the “two hundred families". It had a monopoly of the issue of notes and served as the bank of deposit for government funds. The reorganization provided for the appointment of the governor and two vice governors by the state and for an advisory council of twenty-six, chosen in as representative a manner as possible from the great economic interests of the country, including even the trade unions and cooperative societies.

A further step towards socialization was taken in the decision to nationalize the armament industries and as a beginning certain prominent armament factories were taken over by the government. To revive foreign trade and the tourist industry the Blum ministry also devalued the franc. A stabilization fund similar to that in Great Britain was established to protect the franc from speculative attacks and arrangements were made

The Blum government lasted for just over a year and was followed by other ministries dominated by the Popular Front. 

An important item in the French New Deal was passed in 1937 by one of these later Popular Front ministries when the various railway lines were united into one great company in which both private and public capital participated, but over which the government exercised final control. 

For several reasons the Popular Front ministries failed to to solve the financial problem. In the first place, the radical nature of their reforms had frightened capital. Business men maintained that the rise in wages contributed to higher costs in production which in turn retarded industrial recovery. They also complained that the forty hour week, which must be distributed over five days of eight hours each, made it impossible for them to run their plants long enough to make a profit. This combination of high wages and short hours constituted the chief grievance of capital and the chief slogan of labor. As time went on the issue began to take on symbolic significance so that rational compromise became difficult. In the second place, the budget remained unbalanced while rearmament costs mounted steadily. 

The constant fear that the Popular Front would embark on elaborate schemes of public works also made capital “jittery”.
