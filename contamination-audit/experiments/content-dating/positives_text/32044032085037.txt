LEGAL FOUNDATIONS OF HOUSING IN NEW YORK: THE CONSTITUTION AND THE OCCUPANCY TAX PLAN* MAXWELL H. TRETTER 

A PLAN for the construction of low-rent houses in the City of New York with the proceeds of city taxes and as a part of the city's regular budget has been initiated. It is the first undertaking of that character by any municipality in the country. The plan originally contemplated "appropriating funds in the Expense Budget of the City to take care of interest charges on and amortization of capital investment for the construction of low-rent houses. The bonds would be issued by the Housing Authority, and the appropriations made by the City would cover the difference between the actual investment, cost of operation, and the rental obtainable under the plan." To effectuate this plan, commonly known as the 

Mayor's plan, the 1938 session of the legislature amended the 

Municipal Housing Authorities Law to enable New York City to levy a special tax, the proceeds of which are to be turned over to the New York City Housing Authority for interest payments on bonds to be issued by the Authority.2

This method of low-cost housing financing will undoubtedly have a great bearing upon the entire housing movement in this country. The nature and functions of the public housing authority will not escape the impact of this development. But to employ the doctrine of "separateness" other than as a term of art and the attempt to apply it literally to all affairs of a housing authority would be error. A plan whereby the city would make payments to the authority to meet interest charges on the authority's bonds may be deemed a further development along these lines. An entity, however, which has been created with independent debt incurring power may nevertheless operate as an agency of the city without destroying such power. 15 The prevailing conception, therefore, that public authorities generally are separate and distinct from their local government needs reexamination.

In the past, it might be said that whenever an authority was created to construct and operate an improvement, the underlying assumption of the enabling legislation was that the authority would be self-liquidating. Indeed, the authority idea was a logical development from the "special fund" theory by which revenue bonds issued for the construction of self-liquidating improvements and payable solely from the revenue of such improvements were exempt from municipal and state debt limitations. On this basis, present and proposed methods of financing by housing authorities indicate a striking departure from prevailing ideas on the subject. 

It is true that occasionally the state or a local government may make a contribution to a public authority for initial capital, to pay part of the cost of construction or to defray early administrative expenses. The Public Works Administration, too, makes capital grants to some public authorities which result in a saving in construction cost for the particular improvements. In general, however, it was and still is the practice for authorities to issue their own bonds as a method of obtaining necessary capital and to repay the indebtedness so incurred completely out of revenues derived from the operation of their projects. The amendment to the Housing Authorities Law effectuating this idea provides: "Bonds issued (by an authority) for a non-federal project in a city of more than one million inhabitants may include terms providing that the amount necessary to pay the interest thereon until maturity shall be paid to the authority by such city without reimbursement or liability of the authority to such city therefor. Such tax shall be imposed upon any individual, copartnership or corporation occupying premises in any such city as an owner, tenant or concessionaire for any gainful purpose. Such tax shall be in an amount of not less than one dollar nor more than six dollars per year for each separate premises so occupied, the rate of tax to depend upon the size of such premises or upon such other reasonable standard as may be fixed in such local law. Revenues resulting from the imposition of taxes authorized by this act shall be paid into the treasury of any such city and shall not be credited or deposited in the general fund of any such city, but shall be deposited in a separate bank account or accounts and shall be available and used solely and exclusively for the purposes aforesaid. As in many other state constitutions, the basic law of New York prohibits a city from giving any money or property, or loaning its money or credit to or in aid of any corporation; nor is a city allowed to incur any indebtedness except for a "city purpose." 

While it is true that the banning of aid to any corporation has been held to include aid by one public body to another,25 it will be found, upon closer examination, that the prohibition is enforced only when the loan or payment does not result in any direct or general benefit to the contributing community. Where, however, the loans and payments by a city result in a general benefit to its inhabitants, there is no violation of the constitutional ban; and this is true though the contributions may also benefit an individual or a private corporation.26 It would be redundant to argue the point that payments by the city, though made to the authority, induce slum clearance and construction of houses for the low-income groups. The resulting improvement to the health, safety and welfare of the city fulfills a major "city purpose". Since the legislature could have conferred the power to undertake housing projects directly upon the city, there can be no doubt that payments to the authority for that purpose expressly authorized by statute are entirely proper. What effect will the occupancy tax plan have upon the character of the obligations of the authority? Since the city approves the authority's project and its bonds, and also undertakes to pay the interest on such bonds, some might contend that for all practical purposes, the city has assumed the obligations themselves; or that since housing authorities may no longer be said to be wholly self-liquidating, the use of "special fund" cases as a valid precedent for the independent status of the authority's obligations is no longer sound.

There still remains the question whether the obligation to pay interest is, within the meaning of the constitution, a debt of the city to the extent of the full amount of commuted interest. The answer to that question becomes clearer upon a further analysis of the nature of the city's obligation. Is the city's obligation to pay confined solely to the occupancy tax; or is the occupancy tax simply a primary source of payment through a taxing power additionally conferred upon the city without prohibition upon the city to resort to its general taxing power in order to meet its obligation? 

It would seem that under one reasonable construction of the statute the city is not confined solely to the occupancy tax. The operation of the interest payment provision authorized by the law to be included in the terms of the bonds, stating the city's obligations, does not expressly depend upon the occupancy tax. The act empowers the city to broaden its tax base in the manner provided "in addition to any and all other taxes which such city has the power to impose." It seems, therefore, that the city may undertake to make its payments to the authority from any other available tax source in addition to the occupancy tax.82 Upon these deductions, the city's undertaking to make payments to the authority might be held to be a general, as distinguished from a special or limited, obligation and, therefore, a debt within the meaning of the constitution to the extent of the full amount of the commuted interest.

It must also be considered that the action of the authority in clearing a city slum and providing decent shelter for low-income groups is a special benefit conferred upon the city as a whole. 

Improvements by the housing authority will bring a general increase in property values. Elimination of slum areas will result in budgetary savings to the city for various municipal services. Payments by the city to the housing authority, therefore, are a quid pro quo.38

The third basic element in the prevailing conception of a public authority, namely, that the authority has no taxing power and has no recourse to taxes of special assessments also may undergo change. It is true that the housing authority has no direct power of taxation. The arrangements contemplated in the Mayor's plan, however, will enable the authority to compel the city to levy the tax or taxes provided for under the plan.37 The urgent problem of the slums in our larger cities will not be solved with the limited funds presently available from the federal government. If the campaign to provide decent housing and clean cities is to be accelerated towards major accomplishment, the solution offered by the occupancy tax or other forms of special taxes may become a vital factor in housing authority finance.
