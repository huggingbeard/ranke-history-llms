INTRODUCTION 

How the Survey Came About 

In December, 1945, the Sherborn School Committee received a communication from the Holliston Committee on School Housing requesting a meeting with the Sherborn Committee regarding an educational survey in 

Sherborn and some of the neighboring towns. This meeting was held in 

January, 1946, following which another meeting was held in Ashland, attended by members of the school committees of Ashland, Hopkinton, Holliston, and Sherborn. As a result of these meetings and others, the 

Sherborn School Committee (Mr. Taylor, 

Miss Devitt, and Mr. Dowse) authorized a survey of the entire school system in Sherborn.

To represent the School Committee in the preliminary work of arranging the survey the following individuals were appointed: Mr. Lesley C. Paul and Mr. Arthur Hammarlund as lay members; Mr. Owen B. Kiernan and Mr. 

Richard Cochran of the school staff; 

and Mr. Charles W. Taylor, Chairman of the School Committee. This committee had several meetings with similar committees from the four towns mentioned and the work of the survey was finally started in January of 1947. The survey was under the guidance of the Center of Research and Field Studies in Edu cational Administration of the Harvard 

Graduate School of Education, Dr. 

Alfred D. Simpson, Director; Mr. David 

S. McLean, Field Director; and Mr. 

Thomas C. King, Assistant Field Director, assisted by several consultants from the Harvard Graduate School of Education. The survey in Sherborn has been of the participatory type; i.e., citizens of the town conducting the survey, rather than of the expert type; i.e., experts from outside conducting it. 

Meetings of the committees were held Monday evenings from February through June, and we feel that as you read the reports of the various committees it will be obvious that much time and effort has been spent on the survey. 

Let us emphasize at this point that the primary purpose of the survey was not and is not to try to "sell" any preconceived educational plan to the town. But as any wise householder or business man makes a periodic check of his affairs, so this survey attempts to do the same with Sherborn's schools. 

We feel that if the survey has done nothing else it has given many of our townspeople their first real inside view of our schools.

We are sending out this report to all the families of Sherborn so that they too may deepen their understanding about the schools.

The cost of the survey is borne by the Warren Benevolent Fund and, except for incidental stationery, postage, etc., has cost the town nothing. 

We deeply appreciate the efforts of all who participated in any way in the survey and extend our thanks to them.

Report of the Plan and Scope Committee Introduction 

When the time came for the Plan and Scope Committee to decide what it should do about the reports of the various subcommittees, two courses presented themselves. The first was to digest, refurbish, and in part to edit the various reports into a complete whole. 

The second course was to print in full detail each committee's report. This latter mechanism seemed the more satisfactory as it would provide the reader of this survey report with the exact facts and their interpretation as each subcommittee found them. This method was adopted. 

Then, in order to integrate the whole of the several special view points, the Plan and Scope Committee decided to write a report which would do just that. This overall report should indicate, then, the real accomplishments of the survey.

To our mechanism of integration we added the study of each report in full committee. In several of the reports we found omissions and possible correc tions. The Plan and Scope Committee did not make changes in the subcommittees' reports. We asked the subcommittees to alter their reports if they saw fit. If they felt satisfied with the report as it stood, that was their right. It is for this reason that the attentive reader may find a number of striking differences of opinion presented among the reports of the various subcommittees. We have tried to weigh these differences and come out with a course of action which will satisfy the majority of the sentiments expressed.

Before we get into the findings of the Plan and Scope Committee it should be pointed out that this report is written from two points of view, namely, the long range and the short range. There appeared to us to be certain things that could be done immediately. Others will take time. How we do the immediate ones depends to some extent on how we plan to solve our problems from the long range point of view. We shall try to show those items which we consider immediately necessary and those which we think should be approached gradually. 

Our Findings 

It is always much easier to show what is wrong with an organization than it is to point out what is obviously good. Social institutions, developed as they are by man, are constantly in need of re-evaluation, improvement, and alteration to increase their value and functioning in the society which they serve. It is perfectly possible to be quite satisfied with a system as it is and yet be able to see room for improvement. We do not wish to imply by this statement that we feel completely satisfied with out schools as they are. We do wish to point out that the survey has revealed to many of us that they are far better than we thought they were when we began the study. It should be borne in mind, therefore, in reading this report that, in spite of the large number of alterations that have been suggested, a sentiment of castigation is not active in our minds.

We hope it will be made clear without any further belaboring of the point, that our objective is that of trying to show wherein we could do better, where our system is below the general average, and by what mechanisms we can bring our whole system up above the national average at little or no increase in our costs.

Every committee but one has recommended that we improve our arrangements for physical education. 

It was found that no facilities were available for a year-round physical education program. In short, we must provide our system with a gymnasium. 

Every committee but one stated flatly or implied definitely that our teaching staff was too small. In some instances this finding came from the feeling that our students needed an expanded curriculum in the sense that we are failing to take care of those students who are not going to college. In others it derived from

- a study of how the work load was distributed. Since each committee attacked the problem from a different point of view this general finding of excessive load on our teachers should bear much weight.

These two general agreements in all the committees point toward a revision in our school plant, and in our educational program. To make such a revision requires money.

Turning now to the report of the 

Committee on Support and Financial 

Management, we discover some rather startling findings. It will be well to emphasize them here by listing them. 

1. That we have (on the basis of our assessed valuation) a far higher than average financial ability to support our schools. 

2. That we apportion a slightly higher than average amount of our taxes to the schools. 

3. Our per pupil cost is very nearly the highest in the state. 

4. That from the financial point of view we are not getting our money's worth.

As can be judged from the four findings just listed, this committee voiced the one exception to the two general agreements cited above. 

Bearing in mind what the Finance

Committee has said about our financial management, the School Plant Committee's report should be studied. 

They found first of all that the buildings we have are, by national criteria, "substandard" and "very poor" for the grade school and the high school respectively. At this point then we can say that the recommendations definitely call for a new building program. 

Before we go further on this problem let us review recommendations made in more particular fashion. All the committees studying the subject felt that we should have more guidance for the pupils in our schools. Some felt that a special teacher should be hired to do this work; another felt that our teachers should all be encouraged to study the problem. 

The Committee on Personnel suggested that we improve working conditions in general for our teachers. They felt the teachers were underpaid and were not working in pleasant surroundings. They felt, too, that the teachers should have greater weight in deciding school procedure and policy.

The committees on Educational Policy and Special Services concurred in the se findings at those points where the scope of the three committees overlapped.

Several committees suggested the addition of a kindergarten to our pro gram. The Committee on Educational 

Policy felt strongly that the teacherpupil ratio in the grade school should be reduced.

The Special Services Committee rec ommended an increase, as did other committees, in the extracurricular and health activities of the school system.

It appears to us that the attainment of almost all these recommendations requires, as a first step, the building of extra plant facilities. 

At almost every point, implementing recommendations is made impossible by the fact that we have not the space in our school plant to put the recommendations into effect.

Let us take up the plant problem first. It is obvious from the Finance Committee's report that Sherborn can afford (as judged by other towns) to build up gradually what the school needs. Yet we have an obsolete plant. What can we do about it?

Two courses of action are possible. 

One, we can erect a new building, costing in the neighborhood of $200,000 depending on the facilities necessary to meet the educational requirements of our system from the kindergarten through the 12th grade.

But here we must face the fact that if we do this we are by no means acting sensibly from the financial and educational points of view. Even with only 40 students in our high school, it was the consensus of opinion that:

- 1. Our teachers were overloaded. 

2. That our students were not enjoying a modern standard of educational opportunity. 

If, then, we should build a new high school, we would have to hire more teachers in order to have our investment pay. Thus our operating cost would rise. If we were sure that our high school population would grow, this would be satisfactory and our action could be justified. But a study of our population trend by the plant committee indicates only a small, essentially negligible, increase. On top of that, educators agree that any high school containing less than 300 students can hardly be considered to be functioning with a high degree of effectiveness and efficiency. It will take a long time for us to grow to the point where we have a 300 student enrollment in our high school. 

What then would be the most efficient method? Another plan, a longterm program of the following nature, appeared to us to be more reasonable: 

1. By placing the 7th and 8th grades in the high school, and by improving the science laboratory we can temporize until the long-term program is complete. 

2. We can erect a small combination gymnasium and assembly hall annex to the grade school. This annex would house only the grade school in the future, that is, it would serve 150-175 pupils at the most. 

3. We can try to make a long-term but time-limited arrangement with a neighboring town to set up a regional school system for our high school. Before we begin the building of our gymnasium, we should be certain that we can form a regional school system. It takes at least two towns to do this. We may find it impossible. 

4. We can check through with other town authorities to determine if the schools' building program can not be coupled with other town needs, such as that for an adequate auditorium for town meetings and functions. 

5. Since we are planning to build our gymnasium only for the grade school, we will not have to provide for an athletic field, etc.

This should reduce the cost of this program and it is suggested that the plan be limited in this fashion and that grandiose notions be excluded.

This program will certainly cost far less than the first method of approach. 

Let us see if it will give us what we want.

If we form a regional school with another town, we will lose certain aspects of our community life. We will no longer have a Sherborn High

School graduation ceremony; we will not enjoy the Sherborn High School class plays, dances, and athletic events. In this sense the community will suffer because these events are of importance to the town. 

While we as adults may lose them, we must realize that the students will not. They will have their school functions as before; in fact, they will have larger and possibly better ones. Certainly the parents of students will only be able to note more of a change of name rather than nature in these events. It is possible that the community will find almost as much, if not quite all, the interest in the regional schools extracurricular activities that it did in those of the Sherborn School.

Furthermore, it should be recognized that our present program is decidedly limited. In athletics, for example, we have neither a football nor a basketball team. Using athletics as an example, it is possible to argue that, because members of our community will be engaged in these activities, then, in this field at least, even greater interest will be shown by the community members in student activities. 

That the children will stand to gain by this procedure seems unarguable from the educational point of view. Because of the larger student

- In population, practically all of the recommendations concerning our high school can be readily fulfilled. view of the fact that, for the same return, it will cost the community less, this arrangement should satisfy the taxpayers.

It should be pointed out, however, that the gains available in a regional school are not possible solely by effecting a junction between two or more towns. The towns must be suitable in size and composition of population. 

The school committee of Sherborn must be able to have a strong voice in directing the activities of the regional school. Surveys of the regional school should be frequent. The contract between towns should be limited so that Sherborn would not be bound in perpetuity. These latter considerations make the final school set-up as much ours as the neighboring towns'. We are helping them as much as they are helping us.

If we can not form a regional high school then it seems to be best to close our high school and send our pupils out on a tuition basis. 

If we did not send them out on a tuition basis, it would be necessary to spend within the next few years an absolute minimum of $200,000 to permit us to have a good school. The upkeep of such a plant would be at least double our present cost. This is an unpalatable fact to swallow but seems to be unavoidable. Within the next three years, it is distinctly possible that the attending enrollment of the high school will drop to 20 pupils. can ease this picture by putting the 

7th and 8th grades into the high school building.

The fact that we have large 5th and 6th grades at present is no proof that these will later increase our high school enrollment. Our present 7th and 8th grades were once twice their present size. For good or bad reasons, students seem to leave our school in larger than normal numbers beginning at the 7th grade.

The cause of this exodus is of importance only in finding out how we can improve our high school (see re port of Committee on Educational Pro gram). For future planning it remains true that we must assume that a similar decrease in number of students will occur. With this assumption it appears to the members of our committee that no amount of "sprucing up" of the high school situation is going to help matters.

By present day standards, no matter how well it operates, a 50 to 75 student high school is not an economical unit. It is like running a small water pump in a house with a 20 horsepower motor. It works, and one is rarely out of water, but it costs an excessive amount per gallon. What is more, the water tastes no better than if it had been pumped by an efficient rig.

Thus either way we look at it-closure of our present high school seems to be a goal toward which we should drive. We sincerely hope that a regional school can be set up in which Sherborn can have a good measure of control over the education of its high school students. 

We do not think that, from either the educational or financial point of view, a new high school building in 

Sherborn is justified.

Once we get over the hurdle of this fact, things look far brighter.

It will be easy to provide a small gymnasium for our grade school. If the trustees of Sawin Academy will permit the use of the building by the grade school no expansion of our facilities beyond the gymnasium will be required. Space requirements will be met for some time to come by this action.

A number of the specific recommendations made by committees should be followed through whether we adopt our long-range plan or not. 

We emphasize again the need for physical education.

The easement of the teachers' load, especially in the grade school and an achievement of the general sentiment expressed in the Personnel Committee's report should be promulgated immediately. If this were to be done and our teachers' lot improved, much would be gained.

- We should get a kindergarten operating as soon as space becomes available. We have the need for it and our bus system can be arranged to provide transportation. To date space limitations have prevented the establishment of such a kindergarten.

As long as we maintain our high school we should try to improve the guidance of our pupils. The precise course will be dependent upon our general plan for the future, but some improvement here seems greatly needed.

Our health program, especially the dental aspect, calls for immediate improvement.

Depending in large part again on our general plan for exact method, we should try to meet all the recommen dations of the Educational Program 

Committee.

Summary 

Since this committee can only recommend to the School Committee, there seemed to be little point in its going into precise detail as to procedure in meeting its recommendations. As pointed out in the various committee reports, the School Committee is the final arbiter. (To insure a continuity of program within the School Committee several groups have recommended that the Committee consist of five members.) 

This committee wishes to go on record as feeling that,, within the limitations of the present system, the schools of Sherborn are operating well. It should be noted again that most of our recommendations have to do with the manner of running the system. We found no failures in the personnel and their operations within the present set-up. In fact, all members of the committee were impressed by the personnel and their achievements. As we went further into the daily routine of operation we became more and more impressed by their achievements in a difficult situation. 

Teaching is not an easy job. Evaluation of teaching and its product is difficult for laymen and professional alike. Certain things, however, like school plant, cost per pupil, teacherpupil ratio, and simplicity of administration are things with which under other names such as hidden cost, red tape, and average day's work output, we grapple every day. Some of us are administrators in business; others are in business for themselves. Once we saw the connection between our daily tasks and these items in the teacher's work, our understanding increased materially.

Having studied, argued, sought counsel and finally committed ourselves to this report after eighteen weeks of effort, we sincerely hope that the school committee will try to follow through on our suggestions.

History of the Sherborn Schools 

We read that on June 4, 1694, Edward West was chosen schoolmaster for 

Sherborn by the town. 

Thus twenty years after its incorporation, the town established a public school system. No doubt before this time the settlers were giving all their attention to clearing land and building, and needed the help of their children. The cost of the schools at this time was paid for by those who sent their children to the school. 

On April 16, 1679, four years after the incorporation of the town, it was provided that fifty acres in the land annexed from Natick should be set out where the commissioners of the colonies, Major Gookin and Mr. Eliot and the Indian Rulers, chose and should be appropriated forever to the use of a free school for teaching to the English and Indian children the 

English tongue and other sciences. 

Up to 1709 the school had been kept only a short time each year, but at that time it was voted in town meeting to have school kept for three months at three parts of the town, the 

Plain, South Sherborn and West Sherborn, probably one month in each place. From this time on, the town assumed the cost of the schools, though they were still kept at private houses. In December, 1719, schools were added in two other parts of the town.

The building of the first public school house was voted in 1727, and was to be a twenty by eighteen foot structure south of the meeting house. 

A committee was appointed to sell the 50 acres of school´land to raise the money to build and furnish the school. 

Until 1761, the schools were kept during the winter and taught by a man. 

In this year, 4 pounds were granted "for the support of a woman schoolteacher to teach small children. 11 The schools had taught chiefly "reading, writing, and cyphering" but the General Court had prescribed that higher branches of learning should also be taught. In 1767, a tax of nine pounds was assessed on polls and. estates to pay a fine for not having a Grammar (secondary) School. 

School district bounds were established in 1828, and in this year also the first school committee of the town was chosen, consisting of seven members. They made their first report in Town Meeting in 1839. The first printed report, in conformity with the law which required that a report be read in Town Meeting or printed and distributed to the inhabitants, was 8 pages in length and was issued in 1852. There were now seven schools in districts numbered from one to seven.

At this time men teachers taught in the winter, when the larger boys attended school, and women teachers in the summer term. The total number of pupils for the summer was 192 and for the winter, 247. Since many pupils were absent from school during the berrying season, it was decided that all schools should close during August.

In 1858, a new two-story school building was built in District No. 1, the central district, with one room on each floor.

A few years later the district numbers were replaced by the names: 11 11 "Center, "Plain, " "West, " "South 

Sherborn, "North," "Southwest," and "The Farm. 11 In the fall of 1894, the district schools were closed and the pupils transported to the Central District

School. The grades were divided into three groups, the Primary, in the lower room of the Center School, the Intermediate in the upper room, and the Grammar in the High School building which had been erected in 1874. 

At the Town Meeting in 1855, it was voted to establish a High School for four months of the year and to take $250 from the school grant of $1200 for its support. The School

Committee declined to do this, as the entire grant was needed for the district schools. The next year, the

- committee asked for $300 for a High

School, but the town did not grant it. 

Although efforts to establish a High School at public expense had failed, the desire found realization a few years later as the result of a gift of $5,000 to the town from the estate of 

Thomas Dowse.

By the conditions of the gift, the income from this fund, which at that time amounted to $400, must be expended annually for the High School.

There being no suitable place to hold the High School, the town voted to postpone its opening. In the meantime, the $5,000 principle was expended in fitting up a schoolroom in the Town Hall. Thus the town bound itself to the payment, for the support of schools, of the income of the Dowse School Fund in addition to the regular school appropriation. The Dowse High 

School opened with a membership of sixty pupils and a session of eleven weeks. 

In 1869, a bequest of $42,324.16 was received from the estate of Martha Sawin to establish "a select school in the center of the Town." A site for the school was bought from the Russell estate and constituted a natural park in the center of the village. 

The building was dedicated September 10, 1874. The school, Sawin Academy and Dowse High School, opened 

September 16th with sixty-four pupils, seven of whom were from out of town. 

Edward A. H. Allen was the first principal. The number of pupils varied from year to year, the lowest number being in 1904, when there were one senior, no juniors, four sophomores, and two freshmen. A business college had opened in Framingham and had drawn many students away from the High Sch- 

001. At that time the eighth and ninth grades were in the same rooms with the High School, but during the year, 1904, the ninth grade was abolished. 

Sherborn joined with Holliston and 

Medway in 1896 to form a superintendency union. Since then, that union has been dissolved and Sherborn has joined with Wayland and Sudbury.

The present Center School was built in 1909 and the eight elementary grades were moved into the four rooms, with two grades in each classroom.

Many present Sherborn residents will recall some of the changes and improvements that have been made in 

Sherborn schools in more recent years.

Improvements have included the organi zation of a Parent-Teacher Associa tion which has served and benefitted the schools; the use of special supervisors for the program in art, music and penmanship; the appointment of a school physician and a school nurse; and the provision of a limited school cafeteria.

Though handicapped in some respects by the size of its schools, Sherborn has not hesitated to spend money for the improvement of educational opportunities for its boys and girls.

SECTION III Report of the Committee on Educational Program 

The educational program committee has concerned itself with taking inventory of the present courses offered in the Sherborn schools; appraising them by what it considered present day needs; and trying to agree upon recommendations for their improvement.

Let it be known from the start that opinions reached were based on the reading of books on education, group discussion, information supplied by teacher members of the committee, and consultants from the Harvard Graduate 

School of Education. At the end of our study most of the lay members of the committee felt that much more direct observation would be necessary before a group of amateurs, however interested, could be sure that their opinions were valid. When all the findings of our Monday night studies had been made, it must still be admitted that there is little point of contact between a group of townspeople who discuss academic questions hypothetically, and the realities of educational procedure. 

Elementary School 

We found in the first and second grades that the children were being taught the fundamental skills in such a way as to increase their means of communication, at the same time making use of the social experience of their age. That is, their reading and arithmetic books had a controlled vocabulary and application which would fit in with their present knowledge of their surroundings. Music and art are part of the daily program and there are weekly visits from the supervisors in both of these subjects. A supervisor directs the teaching of penmanship by monthly visitations to each classroom. 

The curriculum in the first and second grades, based on Scott, Foresman texts, is listed below:

The first grade prints up to Christmas, and writes after that. There are seventeen students in the first grade and ten in the second grade.

From grades 3-8 pupils are taught the tool subjects of reading, writing and arithmetic, plus language, history, geography, science, health, and music and art. But in grades 7-8, music and art have now become only weekly subjects under the instruction of the special supervisors. 

Once in two weeks the 5th and 6th grades have an hour long talk on nature study by a lecturer from the 

Audobon Society. That interest in this subject goes beyond school hours may be attested by the fact that the fifth and sixth graders borrow more books on birds from the town library than any other non-fiction classification except books on useful arts. 

There is a visual education program of films once a week. As often as possible these films are related to class work. 

All classes starting with the 5th grade go to the town library for one half hour once a week. The 5th grade receives instruction on the use of the library and the card catalogue. 

All classrooms have their own libraries of supplementary reading. For the most part these books, like the textbooks used, are recent editions and liberally supplied.

The 4-H clubs play an important

- part in the extra curricular activities of the fifth and sixth grades. The boys and girls meet in separate groups and their activities cover such things as goat, poultry, and rabbit raising; gardening, farm labor, home arts, and canning. It was noted, however, that at the seventh and eighth grade ages, interest in 4-H clubs wanes.

Recommendations for Elementary Schools Generally speaking the committee felt that the elementary school education was satisfactory for pupil needs and community needs, but that there were two serious omissions in the present program. 

We have no kindergarten.

The gen- eral, though not unanimous, opinion of this committee was that the kindergarten serves to adjust the pre-school child to his surroundings, and to accelerate a healthy group behavior. 

Therefore, the committee concludes, the benefits ought to be available to all children by means of a publicly supported kindergarten which is an integral part of the elementary school system. The suggestion was then made that the kindergarten teacher, whose class would go home at noon, could devote the last two periods of the day to remedial reading or other subject problems. At one time, for a period of two years, the school did enjoy the services of a special teacher who devoted all his time to special instruction of pupils who failed to keep up with the majority. The teachers considered this kind of service of great value.

Now, although our study is concerned with the educational program, and we do not intend to encroach on the interests of other committees, we were constantly reminded in our findings, and in the voluntary statements of our student committee members, that the physical education program in both schools is sadly lacking. What physical education there is consists of informal games at recess; some folk dancing at the weekly music period; and whatever calisthenics busy teachers might find time for between classes. The committee recognizes that the poor building facilities present a problem. Nevertheless, we hope there will be a swift and thoroughgoing reform in this aspect of modern education in Sherborn. 

We recommend placing the seventh and eighth grades in the high school building where we feel they will be more at home socially; and where the science equipment which they now have to borrow and transport for homeroom use, will be more easily procurable. 

In connection with these grades, it might be interesting to note that the present class has five students in each grade whereas when this same group made up the third and fourth grades, there were twenty of them. A reading of a special report, in

Appendix I, is germane at this point. 

This report was made and written by one member of the committee who interviewed the parents of fifteen students who attend schools outside of Sherborn. 

Most of the committee felt that two grades in one room under one teacher was not ideal but by no means a threat to solid education. One member dissented from this opinion and regretted especially grades one and two being combined. It was pointed out to that member that actually this is a problem of the number of pupils a teacher can handle, and not a problem of grading.

And that a single grade of twenty-seven pupils might present as many gradations of intelligence and readiness as the present combined first and second grades. The dissenting member remained unconvinced. The information was supplied that a few years ago when there was a large incoming group of children in the school, individual teachers were provided for grades one and two. Most of the committee believed a similar situation in the future would likewise be cared for.

11 In time we came to consider the set up of a regional school which might serve several adjoining towns and thus make available through size those advantages which only a large enough school can have.

Advantages

Report on Interviews with Parents of Sherborn Children Attending Schools Out of Town 

In asking questions of the parents as to the reasons why they do not send their children to the Sherborn schools we tried to be unbiased in the tone of our queries. Without impartiality it would be quite easy to elicit replies which would favor our own personal opinions. Likewise, in making an interpretation of these replies we endeavored to be unprejudiced. One can easily and unintentionally pervert the findings.

We did not communicate with the parents of all of the children known to be going to school elsewhere. The parents of fifteen children were consulted. It was hardly necessary to ask for comments because they were given freely and were quite revealing. 

The first question concerned the quality of instruction, both as to the teachers as a group and as to the teaching material available. The next question was about the courses of instruction. Are there any courses in themselves inadequate and are there a sufficient number to provide a comprehensive educational program? The question of athletics, physical education, competitive play and facilities--was the third. The question on extracurricular activities was intended to include dramatics, music, school publications, debating, etc. The fifth question concerned the size of the school. This did not relate to the size of the physical properties but rather it was interested in classroom competition, specialized teaching, and the extent of shop work instruction. 

For the sixth question we asked about the nature of the social contacts, which specifically meant the variety of friendships and all that it means in breadth of understanding, and the school social functions.

The answers were varied in respect to the particular points brought up within the scope of the question, and they were too numerous to tabulate completely. However, a tabulation is presented below on the basis of the negative and affirmative answers as to whether or not the situation represented by the question had a bearing on the parents' decision. In other words, the negative replies indicate, in general, that improvement should be made; and the affirmative replies indicate that the situation is satisfactory. 

As one would expect, the comments covered the gamut of all the ideas anyone could possibly have. On the brighter side, several families think that the Sherborn schools have improved within the past ten years. One family stated that Sherborn offers more in science courses, civics and government studies than some of the so-called "better" schools, a fact which is believed to be to the advantage of the non-college preparatory course. 

Four of the families gave particular emphasis to the condition in the Sherborn schools in which a combination of two grades, in the elementary school, are under a single teacher and, in the high school, several courses are taught by the same teacher. 

The parents consider this a definite draw-back, in comparison with a single teacher for a single elementary grade and a single teacher for each high school subject.

It is appropriate to mention here that without exception the families consulted are vitally concerned over educational problems. They regard the school survey as a step in the right direction, and accordingly, they were glad to cooperate. The questions asked were thoughtfully answered. Obviously each family sent its children to other schools for reasons sufficiently convincing to justify to themselves the added expenditure of money and effort.

Report of the Committee on Citizenship, Leisure Time, Home and Family Life, and Adult Education 

"Boys and girls need to be instructed so that they can discriminate between the enjoyments that enrich and enlarge their lives and those which degrade and dissipate." (John Dewey)

Recognition of this fact by forward thinking communities has brought about expansion of school programs to include art, music, hobbies, clubs and year-round sports activities.

The aims of this committee, therefore, have been twofold; (1) to review

Sherborn's present school program with respect to leisure time education; (2) to suggest how pleasurable and worthwhile activities might be included in Sherborn's school program in order that our students can benefit from wiser use of leisure time now and in later life. Music

A Common Language of the World 

The pleasure of music is to be had for the listening. No technical knowledge or introductory study is required in order to get excitement and satisfaction from our really good music. Although a visiting music instructor conducts group singing in all classes at various intervals, insufficient time is devoted to passive enjoyment or appreciation of music. Sherborn's accessibility to Boston and her ranking musical organizations offers an opportunity to our pupils that should not be slighted. Other than group singing, no time is set aside for active participation in music study. Recommendations 

1. That a course in music appreciation become a part of the school curricula--both elementary and high. a. That a record playing machine be made available for regular use by the schools. That recordings of classical music be purchased or borrowed to facilitate developing an appreciation of good music by all students. That music memory contests be conducted to accelerate student interest. 

2. That more children be encouraged to attend concerts by leading 

Boston musical organizations. Students denied instrumental instruction in school may, through this passive activity, be encouraged to participate in music in an active way. 

3. That instrumental instruction for individuals or groups be made available as an extra-curricula activity. 

Arts and Crafts

Many satisfying and pleasurable leisure hours can be devoted to art. 

It is a means of expression in which everyone can participate in some form whether it be photography, handcraft, tool-working, block printing or designing of stage sets. To help students find opportunity for active and passive participation in some kind of art should be an aim of our extracurricular activities as well as of our classroom projects. A visiting instructor conducts art classes in the High School and Elementary grades, and an annual art exhibit is held. 

Projects include design and painting of posters to announce civic and student activities and timely events, and some metal work is done. Recommendations 

1. That more active participation in art be encouraged in order that its value as an avocation may be recognized by Sherborn pupils. Inadequate facilities for school dramatics provide an excellent reason for encouragement of the study of stage set designing. Student art work, particularly that done in the home, should be exhibited in class and discussed. An art group formed for sketching or for taking photographs of local historical or scenic interest could be formed as an extra-curricular activity. The results might be displayed and discussed in class. 

2. That passive interest in art be

- encouraged by arranging tours to 

Boston Art Museums and outstanding exhibits. With some effort, and little cost, Sherborn students could be given an opportunity to observe many of the world's best known works of art. 

3. That reproductions of favorite works of art be acquired for all classrooms. Student reports on these reproductions and on the artists would be a means of creating further appreciation of new acquisitions. 

4. That more emphasis be placed on crafts such as leather tooling, metal and woodworking and that a shop be set up with necessary equipment for this purpose. Sports It is more important to enjoy participation in sports activity than to excel in them. So that more students may enjoy the healthful and characterbuilding benefits of sports, it should be the aim of Sherborn's school program to encourage all-year-round guidance in minor sports and physical development activities. With proper facilities to permit an expanded program, the average student in a small school is in a better position to participate--hence benefit--than is a student from a school affording keener competition. 

Organized and supervised sports now encouraged in Sherborn schools are softball for the girls and baseball for boys. Recommendations 

1. That locker room and shower facilities be provided for boys and for girls of Sherborn schools. 

2. That faculty supervision be provided for a year-round physical development program for boys and girls, for elementary and High School. 

3. That tennis, basketball, and volley ball teams for boys and girls be formed and supervised. 

4. That the school program be revised to include ample time for a steppedup athletic schedule and that bus trips be changed to take care of this. 

5. That proceeds from visiting bathers at Farm Pond might be made available for securing a part-time supervisor at the Sherborn playground during the summer months. 

Hobby Clubs in High School 

Recognizing that learning becomes easier as interest increases, schools of today are allocating portions of the curriculum to include hobby clubs and activities which have been elected by students. A High School paper is written and mimeographed by the Sherborn students and distributed at regular intervals. School dances are conducted at various intervals during the year. 

Senior students finance their annual trip by sales and other projects. Recommendations 

1. That High School teachers be encouraged to initiate and supervise not less than one active and worthwhile leisure-time activity. 

2. That the High School paper be published more frequently to serve as an outlet and proving ground for creative writing and drawing ability, as well as for reporting school news.

Summary of Recommendations 

1. That a music appreciation course be initiated. 

2. That a dramatics program be included. 

3. That an auditorium be provided for public speaking, assemblies, etc. 

4. That a gymnasium be provided for an active physical education program. 

5. That an activities room be provided for the printing of the school paper and for craft work. 

6. That future teaching personnel be selected for their ability to contribute to an adequate leisuretime program. 

7. That summertime supervision be provided for playground activities.

Education for Citizenship 

A good citizen is one who not only has the respect of his fellow men, but who in turn has respect for the rights of others. Citizenship should, therefore, become as much a part of us as good habits, and only through early training can this be accomplished. We cannot expect our very young to view citizenship in an adult manner, but we can begin, at this time, to instill the thoughts of good citizenship which will carry them through adulthood.

When very young, they can be taught to share, to get along with other people, and to have regard for other people's property as well as for their own. 

They should learn in older childhood that our government has made laws by which we must abide in order to get along with ourselves as a community and in the world as a whole. 

We as a committee find that a great deal has been accomplished in the elementary grades, in actual practice and knowledge, by the following activities:

Class parties 

Drives 

Responsibility and care of school grounds 

Fire drills

Conservation of plants and school property 

Study of safety practices and fire prevention.

We do feel that more could be accomplished in the upper elementary grades and that emphasis should be given to a comprehensive guidance program. 

In the High School citizenship is confined mostly to textbooks, class organizations, and planning by the students of their own social activities. Government is studied under a course in Democracy, but there is too little opportunity for the actual practice of this topic. We realize that the High School is, in itself, rather small, yet a student council could and should become an active part of the school, together with discussion groups, forums, debating, and assembly programs. 

Therefore we recommend:

2. An Assembly Hall for Elementary grades, to enable them to carry out their program of patriotic and civic activities. 

3. An auditorium for. the High School for open forums, debating and assembly programs.

Report of the Committee on Special Educational Services 

This committee made a study of the following educational services: guidance, cafeteria, health and physical education, school library. The committee has made recommendations on the basis of what it considers a minimum program for the boys and girls of Sherborn.

Guidance A Concept of Guidance 

Guidance is an organized service designed to give systematic aid to pupils in making adjustments to various types of problems which they must meet--educational, vocational, health, moral, social, civic and personal. It should endeavor to: 

1. Help the pupil to know himself as an individual and as a member of society. 

2. Enable him to correct certain of his shortcomings that interfere with progress. 

3. Know about vocations and professions so that he may intelligently choose and prepare, in whole or in part, for a life career. 

4. Assist him in the constant discovery and development of abiding creative and recreational interests.

Such objectives should be achieved through: 

1. A better understanding between the school, the home, and the community. 

2. A closer coordination between the schools within a system. 

3. Adequate and specific data on the individual pupil secured at or prior to his entrance to the sch- 001. 

4. A system of cumulative records and reports. 

5. A comprehensive and effective system of counseling and guidance. 

6. Definite provisions for articulating the work of the school with whatever activity the individual engages in after he leaves it.

Guidance in the Sherborn Schools 

In the Sherborn schools we find that all teachers take an active part in the guidance program, and request the aid of parents whenever further information is needed to solve a particular problem. Usually no other agencies are employed.

The guidance program is not organized.

It is carried out by persons with varying degrees of preparation and experience, and is mainly concerned with developing self direction in pupils. 

The teachers feel that they exert considerable influence in the various phases of guidance. They possess much information regarding the background and special problems of each pupil because of the small enrollment in the schools. All records necessary for the work of guidance are kept up to date and are accessible to all concerned. Teachers keep in constant touch with the pupils' personal and social development, and discuss with them their scholastic progress at frequent intervals. 

Records of test results are kept up to date, including mental, educational development, and achievement tests. A pupil's overall test record is carefully considered in any matter of guidance. There is no admission test. No formal personality rating or case studies are kept but teachers have many opportunities to discuss and compare their views in these matters. There are no exploratory courses, classes for the study of occupations, nor any planned informative talks with guidance value. 

The library cooperates in offering guidance materials for pupils. 

Informal interviews with parents are frequent, although there are no home visits, progress reports, or planned follow-up on graduates.

- 

Because of the small enrollment in both schools it is felt that articulation between the work of the two groups is excellent. Study difficulties are regularly noted, and means for improving them are employed. Much material is furnished to those interested in post-secondary schools, e.g., a plentiful supply of catalogues, scholarship announcements and occupational briefs. There is no provision for placement.

Informal talks with pupils often help to guide their leisure activities and social and civic relationships, although problems of a more personal nature are seldom discussed. 

The greatest value of the guidance program for in-school relationships seems to be the understanding which it cultivates among pupils and between pupils and teachers. Each pupil feels that he may approach his teachers at any time concerning his scholastic record, problems, or future plans. Its effectiveness in promoting better post school and out of school relationships lies chiefly in its vocational aspects. 

The greatest advantage of the guidance program would seem to be its informality--the fact that the teachers do not have to refer to written records when dealing with any individual and that all teachers are at least partially aware of the background, abilities, limitations, and problems of every pupil in the system.

The program could be improved by the addition of some kind of placement service, a possible series of informative talks by experts on vocational guidance, as well as through home visits by the teachers.

The committee, during the discussion of this subject agreed that guidance could be carried on in an informal manner due to the small enrollment, however the committee recommends that complete written records should be kept because personnel often change and reports on pupils who have been out of school five or more years are often requested. It was also realized that with the introduction of a more detailed record card more clerical work would be required. Recommendations 

1. That there be a teacher trained in guidance techniques to supervise guidance for both schools.

He should institute a comprehensive testing program, see that guidance records are kept, counsel with pupils, teachers, and parents on all student problems, help teachers develop the modern concept of guidance, conduct student groups in the study of occupations and establish liaison with educational and business establishments. 

2. That a part time clerical assistant be provided--part of her duties being to keep complete guidance records. 

3. That a new record card be adopted supplying more complete information. 

4. That all teachers be encouraged to take courses in guidance. 

5. That use be made, when necessary, of guidance clinics in the nearby universities.

School Library 

The library should be a center of the educational life of the school, not merely a collection of books. It should provide the reading and reference facilities necessary to make the educational program effective. Its books and other resources should, therefore, be chosen in the light of the specific aims and purposes of the school. Many pupils do not have access to good books and periodicals in their homes and therefore lack the background which acquaintance with such material would supply. By teaching pupils how and where to find 11brary material, how to select them, and how to use them effectively, the library should provide pupils with valuable means not only of extending their knowledge and understanding, but also of developing desirable leisure habits. The library and its facilities not only should be readily and easily accessible, but also should be so attractively equipped that aesthetic tastes will be developed.

- Adequate provisions for a school library should include: 

1. A trained librarian. 

2. Books and periodicals to supply the needs for reference, research, and cultural and inspirational reading. 

3. Provision for keeping all material fully catalogued and well organized. 

4. A budget which provides adequately for the maintenance and improvement of the library. 

5. Encouragement of the pupils in the development of habits of reading and enjoying books and periodicals of good quality and real value.

Library Facilities in Sherborn 

Since the Sherborn school system cannot maintain a complete school library of its own chiefly because of the small enrollment, and because the faculty has no one available to serve as librarian, only a small library is now kept in the high school for research work and outside reading. There are three sets of encyclopedias and several books recommended by the various departments as necessary for the preparation of school work.

In the grade school rooms are several books for young people useful as collateral reading and character building. These books are selected, in the main, by the school superintendent.

For the faculty there is an excellent nucleus of a professional library. 

In trying to stimulate the desire for reading, the schools and the trus tees of the Sherborn Public Library have set up a system whereby the scholars are allowed to go to the library on school time, supervised by a teacher. The librarian, who is extremely helpful with young people, listens to their needs and in the short time available finds them what they ask for. Visitation to the library is made on Mondays at 1:30 by the pupils of 5th and 6th grades; and at 2:00 by those of the 7th and 8th grades. high school period is at 2:00 on Wednesday.

The The trustees of the public library have shown themselves willing to hear any suggestions that can improve the coordination between school and library. They have in the past, on request, sent fifty books or more to the school to be exchanged at regular intervals.

That practice has been discontinued for the present, but can be renewed on request.

The library is less than a quarter of a mile from the schools and contains thousands of books and periodicals and employs a particularly understanding librarian with considerable library training. A story hour is held at intervals for small children and there is a special Children's Room. Book reviews, open to the public, have been conducted under the auspices of the trustees. Recommendations. 1. 

That the use of the town library by the school children be extended and encouraged. 

2. That the school committee earmark a sum of money annually for the improvement and enlargement of the school library. 

3. That additions to the professional library shelf be made periodically. 

4. That all school library books be catalogued and a system of supervision be set up either by the use of students or as one of the duties of a clerical assistant. 

5. That more periodicals be added to the school library. 

6. That more teachers take advantage of the cooperative policy of the town library willingness to purchase books recommended by teachers for use in their courses.

Physical Education A Concept of Physical Education 

Since the curriculum comprises all the experience which pupils have while under the direction of the school, there can be no rigid dividing

- line, educationally, between the usual classroom activities and those activities sometimes called "extra-curricular activities" which commonly permit more freedom and are more largely initiated and directed by the pupils themselves. There is need for pupil participation and expression in experiences which are more nearly out-ofschool and daily-life experiences in an environment favorable to their growth and, in general character, so similar to life outside the classroom that a maximum carry over may be expected. Under competent guidance pupils should share responsibilities for the selection, organization, and evaluation of such activities and of their probable outcomes. In all such activities the development of leadership ability in pupils should be an objective. Opportunities for exercising leadership should therefore be abundantly provided.

Physical Education in the Sherborn Schools 

In our schools there definitely is not a diversity of sports and games. 

There is no special physical education instructor, but what physical activities there are come under the direction of the high school principal for baseball and the various teachers supervise and organize the rest. There is not much attention given to intraschool athletics. There is softball and some ping pong, but winter is definitely the low point.

There is no emphasis given to those games which have the greatest carryover value for the individual such as golf and tennis, and this is unfortunate. There are provisions made for activities involving team play; soft ball, and an organized baseball team in the high school which plays high school teams of other towns. This program encourages good sportsmanship on the part of all spectators under the control of the school. The girls in high school have organized a soft ball team and enjoy it very much, but there is no coach. The provisions for voluntary, organized physical activities for either boys or girls are poor. 

There is no gymnastic work at all and, other than the activities already mentioned, there is included only a little basketball for the girls. However, both boys and girls are very enthusiastic about the activities available. Although the physical education program does seem to lack certain things which might be beneficial, it should be taken into consideration that this is a rural community, and in order for boys and girls to get to and from any activities in the town, they have to do a great deal of walking or bicycling in good fresh country air. Also, although swimming is not a part of the school program, the town is very fortunate in having a fine pond with excellent facilities for supervised swimming and instruction and the young people of the town certainly take advantage of this facility. Recommendations 

1. That arrangements be made for a part-time physical education instructor. This arrangement should provide for both boys and girls athletics. 

2. That a study be made of the possibility of forming a league of small high schools such as Sudbury, Stowe, and others for playing sixman football. 

3. That facilities be made available immediately for the playing of basketball, hiring a hall in a nearby town if necessary. 

4. That the bus schedule be arranged so that pupils can participate in sports. 

5. That a definite physical education program be started for the girls-such as archery, volleyball, and tennis. 

6. That eventually a gymnasium be built. 

7. That regular physical education classes be held for all pupils. 

8. That the present intra-mural sports be encouraged and extended. 

9. That an athletic association be formed.

Health Education 

The health of the individual child in school and at home has a direct relationship to the protection of the general public health. The health of a child while attending school is directly related to his school efficiency and attitudes. Too much stress cannot be placed upon the wholesome development of the physical, intellectual, and emotional aspects of the child's life. It is important that a child begin his school career with efficient sight, sound hearing, good teeth, and general good health. With such a beginning his educational growth will be, at least in part, assured.

Health Education in the Sherborn 

Schools Our Sherborn schools do have a physical record of each child before school entrance and continuous records are kept of the physical history, conditions, and treatment of each child.

In cases of such conditions as underweight, nervousness, sickly appearance, frequent illness, loss of weight or failure to gain, poor posture, fatigue, and backwardness in school work, reports are made to the parents and it is up to the parents to have the family physician care for the child. Recommendations are made by the school nurse or doctor, so that the parents are kept informed. A report of the examination and the physician's recommendations are made available to all teachers coming in contact with the child when the interests of the child demand it. 

Health inspection is made every morning by the teachers in the first four grades, but this is not done consistently beyond that grade. The teacher is supposed to be keen and alert in detecting early signs of departure from normal physical health. If there is any question, the school nurse is notified.

There is no provision made for special attention to those children whose social and emotional problems require treatment which is beyond the scope and training of the regular school staff. 

No real provision is made for reference to the proper health authorities of all boys and girls who need medical attention. Notices of defects discovered in physical and dental examinations are sent to parents and are followed wherever necessary by the nurse's home visits. The teachers, principal and superintendent have participated in a program of curriculum construction in health education.

Health instruction opportunities are definitely provided and recognized in each school. The lower grades have excellent health books, well illustrated, and in these grades a definite point is made of health instruction to lay a good foundation. In the upper classes health instruction is taken care of in the chemistry course and in biology, and such instruction is supplementary to everything that is done in the school. 

Also, Junior Red Cross classes have been arranged in home nursing and nutrition. The movie projector is used as a supplement to the instruction in foods and nutrition. Health instruction is directly based on the needs, interests and abilities of the children. In the primary grades it is centered around daily living experience; i.e., good habits in teeth and general cleansing daily. It is not a required area of instruction in the high school, but as has been pointed out, both chemistry and biology courses incorporate a great deal of health instruction. 

The principal is responsible for coordinating the health education activities in science, home economics, social studies and other related areas of instruction. However, there is not adequate instruction and supervision in physical activities. No provisions have been made for prog rams of sex education. The school does use suitable available material on temperance education and there are lectures on safety. The daily program gives opportunity for recreation and social activities. There are extra-curriculum activities and assemblies and this year a dancing class was arranged

- which was a real advantage for many of the young people of the town. Community recreation facilities and leadership are available, to supplement that of the school, in the form of 

4-H clubs, Campfire Girls, Boy Scouts, and Farm Pond with its well-supervised swimming. 

The health education program does not provide for mental health as well as physical health. There is no mental hygiene staff and no program. The school does not provide for the study of personality difficulties through an adequately trained mental hygiene staff.

It is felt that the school does not provide enough of a desirable outlet for feelings in play, nor in music. It is felt that there are desirable outlets for feelings in social activities and that there is a good healthy social outlook. There is a fine arts program and the dramatics work is good. There are first aid outfits in each building and the teachers are responsible in cases where first aid is needed, and of course, the school nurse can always be called upon.

It is not felt that the buildings are equipped adequately for heating and ventilating demands; the systems are too antiquated. 

Opportunity is not furnished for in-service training of teachers in health education. Everyone who comes in contact with the pupils in any way, from the men who drive the school busses to the workers in the lunchroom, as well as the teaching staff, must have chest X-rays. No further health qualifications are required. The school nurse has a yearly fund of $75.00 provided by the Salvation Army for hardship cases in need of dental work or glasses, and of course, the authorities have recourse to the Red Cross.

The school personnel cooperates with social agencies in discovering and helping needy cases. Last year the Department of Public Health sent out a Dental Hygienist who did a dental check-up. She conducted an experiment in the use of fluorine in allaying the development of cavities. Recommendations 1. That there be a definite annual dental program initiated in the school. 

2. That there be a definite procedure set up for making available expert advice on emotional problems. 

3. That there be a program started for corrective work in posture-primarily by a physical education instructor. 

4. That there be a closer correlation of health education with all school activities. 

5. That a program for in-service training of teachers in health education be started. Cafeteria

The Cafeteria in Sherborn 

In a rural community such as ours it is impossible for students to go home at lunch time. However, the need for warm food at meal times is vital to the growing child.

This need was recognized by our 

Parent-Teacher Association and, in the spring of 1944, a committee sol1cited contributions for a fund to start school lunches. This fund was used to obtain equipment and make the necessary alterations, including plumbing, to convert a school storeroom into a kitchen. The work of cooking and serving was handled by volunteer workers under a paid supervisor or manager. 

Members of the P.T.A., both mothers and those who had no children in our schools, gave freely of their time to this project.

The P.T.A. ladies worked on this venture even during the summer months, when townspeople donated fruits and vegetables from their gardens.

Volunteer workers met at different homes to can this food for use the following winter. These contributions helped in a large degree in bringing down the cost per meal.

- As the operation became more efficient less volunteer work was needed in the lunchroom. Then in September, 1946, a further step forward was made. 

The manager and her assistant were both reimbursed and no volunteer workers were required.

The Federal Government pays a subsidy of 74 per meal provided that the proper dietary balance is followed in meals. "Surplus" foods are also available from this source, free, except for the cost of handling. In return the government issues lists of "abundant" foods and recipients of aid are pledged to use such abundant items as much as possible. This helps maintain fair price levels for farm produce.

There is also available a Federal fund for needed equipment. During the past year two new electric stoves were installed in our kitchen and a large electric refrigerator is now on order.

These are to be paid for from Federal subsidy funds. 

Meals furnished are wholesome, appetizing, and thoroughly enjoyed by all. It is possible to purchase soup at 54, a main dish at 124, and dessert at 5%. A complete meal, well rounded, costs the student only 224. Approximately 75 to 90 pupils buy the main dish daily and 50 also purchase soup and dessert. Children may also buy fruit or a snack at recess. The main dish is a daily special which is not repeated too often and the children look forward to lunchtime.

The facilities for cooking and serving are very good. Federal supervisors, at intervals, check location, equipment, and operation of the school lunch unit. They have been very frank in their praise of the food served and the manner in which it is handled. 

The chief problem is that no space is available to use as a lunch room. The elementary school children eat their meal in their homerooms.

The high school students, however, must eat in corridors and cloakrooms of the lower school. Such a set up is not conducive to proper relaxation from school routine. 

On January 1st of this year the 

School Committee voted to assume supervision of the school lunch pro gram. The duties of the P.T.A. 

ended on that date but their interest in its operation continues high. 

A splendid project was started and enthusiastically continued by the 

P.T.A., and its members are to be commended for this excellent accomplishment. Recommendations 

1. That a pleasant, comfortable eating place, large enough to seat all pupils be provided at once. 

2. That an electric dish washer be procured for the kitchen.

Report of the Committee on Personnel 

It is a well recognized fact that the personnel in a school is the most important factor to be considered in striving for good education. Sherborn has been fortunate in having some fine and loyal teachers but the committee feels that improvements in teaching conditions are needed to insure keeping good teachers and reducing turnover. 

We recognize the manifold problems to be faced in selecting and holding good personnel in these times, and we make our suggestions with full understanding of the difficulties which face the school committee and the superintendent.

Selection and Appointment of Teachers 

The superintendent, through the cooperation of larger neighboring towns, is able to use their files as a source of supply. He also visits teachers' colleges and contacts teachers' agencies. When possible, teachers are observed teaching. 

The school committee may interview the candidates which the superintendent recommends but, as a rule, accepts his recommendation. Which is to say that almost complete responsibility for the final hiring of teachers is taken by the superintendent. this is as it should be. 

We feel 

Physical examination is not required of prospective teachers although X-rays are required and are also taken of the lungs of all personnel once every three years.

The turnover in the school has averaged forty per cent in the past ten years and it is to combat this toohigh figure that we make many of our suggestions.

Our inability to compete with neighboring towns' salary scales has been somewhat lessened this year by the recent increase voted the teachers. However, this will not solve the problem for long. The trend is constantly moving toward a higher scale of salaries for the teaching profession and if Sherborn hopes to give her children good education, she must keep up with the trend. Further on in our report we will make some concrete recommendations with regard to salary policies. 

Qualification of Teachers 

The qualification of teachers is established to include only those teachers having a degree from an accredited academic or teachers' college. No requirement is mentioned calling for training in the teaching profession of those who have their degrees from an academic college. 

The committee recommends that if a degree is from an academic college, the teacher should also have not less than fifteen hours of professional courses among which practice teaching should be included. Especially considering the financial handicap under which we find ourselves in hiring teachers, we feel that in judging personality, pride in and loyalty to the teaching profession should be used in gauging the adequacy of the applicant as well as degrees held. 

Any appreciable amount of experience is not, and in a small town cannot be, a requisite in the appointment of teachers.

In-Service Training 

The policy of the school committee is to give additional increments to teachers taking six units of professional study every five years.

The teachers are allowed great latitude in choosing the courses they may wish to take. Up to the present date, no program of workshops has been in existence.

This group feels that it might be wise for the superintendent to be consulted in the choosing of courses for additional training. 

He might very probably feel that there were particular courses that would most benefit different teachers.

We recommend that a program of workshops be inaugurated to study problems thought by the staff and the administration to be important or of current interest. These could meet as often as necessary to do the job and would be recognized as

- professional study. Experts in education might be secured to aid these workshops.

Job Analysis 

The hours of work at the Sherborn school are not longer than they are in most schools. Due to the fact that there are so few extra-curricular activities, the teachers are not called upon to engage in much but their own particular classes.

The variety of subjects taught by each teacher is considered to be excessive. However, the size of the school (particularly the High School) is such that under the present set-up, little can be done to correct the situation. 

The committee does feel, though, that for a teacher to have to teach four subjects or perhaps even more is an undue load. It means that specialization is impossible and that the work load is a trying one.

Although a small teacher-pupil ratio can be of great benefit in many cases, it is unfortunately true that in cases of personality clashes, the influence of the teacher can be as strong for the bad as for the good.

The choosing of a profession or vocation is today an important part of education. The committee recommends that more time be allowed some member of the high school staff to work with the young people on this problem. The giving of tests, the making of contacts with the labor market and the time for interviewing students would all be in the province of a guidance teacher.

Conditions of Employment 

There are really no adequate provisions made for rest rooms for the teaching staff in the Sherborn school. 

The committee feels that in an effort to make the conditions of employment as attractive as possible, more adequate and attractive rest rooms should be provided for the personnel. Also, we suggest that an effort should be made to arrange the schedule so that a teacher will be allowed some few minutes (if not more) of free time each day. In handling the multiple problems that confront a teacher daily, a "breather" is a real need.

An interesting and beneficial policy could be established by allowing the teachers each to have at least two or three days during the school year to visit other schools. A fresh outlook can be gained thereby and new ideas encountered. Because of lack of time, this committee did not go into the subject of exchange of teachers or sabbatical leaves but suggests that perhaps at a later date these problems could be studied with benefit to the schools. The personnel in Sherborn are allowed ten days sick leave each year.

Under normal conditions, this should certainly prove to be sufficient. We wonder, however, if this might not be allowed to be cumulative up to thirty days to allow for the seldom-encountered but financially disabling long illness or emergency.

Administration 

As far as this committee can gather, no very definite participation in the making of policy on the part of the teachers is encouraged. Because having a voice in the making of policy is always an incentive toward doing one's best and searching for improvement, we urge that teachers be encouraged to help in the formation of policy. Particularly do we recommend that in view of the fact that the superintendent is here only one day a week, the principal be, at least part of the time, invited to sit in with the school committee members at their meetings. The present method of having to have needs or problems go from teacher to principal to superintendent to school committee seems needlessly roundabout and inefficient. And it would stand to reason that the principal is better acquainted with some of the situations that commonly arise and call for discussion at the school committee meetings.

In considering the supervision that is available, it becomes immediately apparent that there is not enough. The superintendent has so little time to devote to Sherborn that he must necessarily spend much of it on administrative work instead of consulting with the staff in a supervisory role. Nor

- has he sufficient time to observe the teachers in classes and help them by advice and suggestion. 

The duties of the superintendent are so numerous as inevitably to be burdensome for one man. He must supervise the mapping out of the curriculum, oversee the planning of the school budget, act as intermediary between the school and its committee and take care of the appointing of all teachers. 

Considering this very demanding schedule and the very small amount of time that our superintendent has for

Sherborn, we of the personnel committee would like to see an assistant appointed to take over the supervision of the elementary school. 

The principal must be in charge of both the personnel and the pupils of the school, must keep most of the records other than the daily individual ones kept by the teachers themselves, arrange for tests to be given and teach classes himself. We strongly feel that a principal should teach so as to keep the teacher's and not purely the administrator's viewpoint. But at the same time, if such problems as discipline, guidance, administration, etc., are to be well taken care of, an effort should be made to see that the principal's teaching load is not too heavy. 

We heartily approve of the policy of having a man teacher for the grades which he teaches (seventh and eighth). 

But we think that either he should have more time for his duties as assistant principal or that the principal himself should be able to spend more time overseeing the elementary school. 

The school committee now consists of three members. We suggest that there might be more continuity of thought and policy if this were enlarged to five.

Non-Instructional Employees 

The janitor takes care of both the high school and the elementary school buildings and the grounds surrounding them. He does what this committee considers an amazing job in keeping them both in good condition. We would suggest, however, that the school committee consider hiring an assistant to help him. 

Especially during the winter months the load is more than one man should have to carry.

As the school nurse is in Sherborn two mornings a week and on call through the week, (a schedule which we found compared favorably with other small towns) it has been suggested that one teacher in the elementary school and one in the high school be required to take a first aid course.

This would mean that there would always be someone at the school well qualified to take care of any accidents that might occur. It has been mentioned that the Plan and Scope Committee consider the possibility of the town Health Department's taking over the cost of paying the school nurse.

Because one of the major potential sources of waste in public school management is in the assignment of work to highly trained and paid personnel which could as well be performed by people with less training, we suggest that it might be wise for the principal to have some part-time clerical help.

Additional Topics 

In most communities a kindergarten is a part of the school that has for many years been taken for granted as a necessity. In Sherborn there still seems to be doubt in the minds of some as to the need for one. The possibility of establishing one should still, however, be studied. 

As we all know almost too well, one of the biggest problems in running a small school is to keep down the cost per pupil while still giving the best possible education. Fully cognizant of the fact that it would increase the budget, we strongly recommend provision being made for the students to have physical education. The lack of it is one of the most general criticisms one hears, to say nothing of the fact that the state law requires it. Another need that we discussed was that of at least a part-time remedial instructor to help those children with academic problems.

- 

Community Relationships 

One of the urgent problems involving personnel is the need for much closer cooperation between the parents and teachers. Many parents have only a slight notion of what is being done in the schools. And apparent lack of interest on the part of the parents is bound to be a contributing factor in teacher morale. Many ways of improving this situation have been suggested. 

Education Week could and should be given more emphasis. Simple plays should be presented to which the parents are invited, more exhibitions might be held, debates given, musical programs presented, etc. In short, any and every means should be used to bring the parent closer to the school. 

In order better to prepare our students for civic responsibilities, we feel that Student Government should be established and made a working force in the school. The training derived from work of this sort is invaluable. 

One plan that has been used elsewhere and that Sherborn might find to have merit in the bringing together of parent and teacher is this. Two afternoons (after school hours) following the issuance of report cards might be set aside as definite times when the teachers and parents can discuss the problems of the child. Another suggestion has been made to start groups which could meet with the teachers on a room basis. A class mother might be appointed and meetings arranged for the parents of children in one room. In this way, more specialized subjects and problems can be comprehensively discussed than at P.T.A. meetings where the subject matter must necessarily be more general. This is not to say that we recommend lessening the role that the Parent Teacher Association should play. In fact, we hope that many of the issues brought forth in this survey will be studied further by that organization. in Our teachers take an active part in community activities and are, fact, among our most civic-minded members of the community. 

Salary Policy

Although paying higher salaries will not necessarily guarantee the best teachers, offering the lowest rates will certainly mean having a smaller and perhaps poorer selection of applicants from whom to choose.

The state median for teachers' salaries has recently been computed at $2540 per year. Because of increased costs of living, this is comparable to not much more than $1800 in 1941. 

This is felt to be entirely too low to attract the type of person who fits the needs of present-day education.

Sherborn's median salary is $2300. 

This immediately shows where we stand in a highly competitive field today.

We might try to solve this problem by raising salaries so that Sherborn's median will at least meet that of the state. Or we might consider adopting a new and more rewarding salary schedule.

The committee understands the everpresent problem of fiscal ability but this is a question that must face every town that has as small a teacherpupil ratio as we have in our High 

School. 

Suggested Salary Schedule 

We are submitting to the town a tentative salary schedule. Although it is not high as such schedules go, it was as far as we felt we could go. 

In fact, with such a small enrollment in the high school, we hesitate to advocate increasing the cost per pupil further. This is not a problem for the Personnel Committee to settle, but it is one which must be seriously considered by the town in the effort to evaluate the wisdom of maintaining a small high school.

Our proposed salary schedule is of the same type as our present schedule, namely a single salary preparational type schedule, which is in line with present trends all over the country. 

It recognizes the fact that, other conditions being equal, the better educated teacher is the better teacher--and we wish to have the best teachers obtainable for our children. 

The purpose of a salary schedule is to attract and retain good teachers. 

The following proposed schedule will

Report of the Committee on Determination and Execution of Educational Policy 

The committee interpreted the scope of its investigation to be essentially the mode of operation of the school administrative system. Its recommendations have been made in this sense. 

That is, we have not raised the question--"Is the system as set up doing a good job or a poor one?" Instead we have asked--"Is the system as operating generally considered a good one?"

This difference in attitude is at first glance not very striking. But it makes a large difference in the interpretation of the situation under study. 

To furnish us an example of what we mean by this distinction let us take the physical plant of the High School. 

The present mode of operation calls for the use of the Sawin Academy by the School Committee. But the build ing is maintained by what amounts to a private organization. The School 

Committee has no control over the building except for the furniture. The arrangement is basically that which exists between landlord and tenant.

The tenant may not like certain phases of the structure but if the landlord is unwilling to alter the premises, that is just too bad. In this instance, unless the School Committee wishes to construct a new building, it must use the building as is. 

As we state below, we came to the conclusion that, from the administrative point of view, this situation is not at all favorable toward efficient administration. We are not disputing the fact that the system has worked well in the past. It has. But the fact is inescapable that for efficient administration one body only, and that the School Committee, should be in charge of and responsible for the school plant.

We wish to emphasize this distinction in our approach to the administrative setup because of the nature of our recommendations. It may be felt by many that our present arrangement is satisfactory. We agree with this attitude to a large extent. It is satisfactory in the quality of product. 

But is is not returning to us in training of our children the amount that it could if it were administered more efficiently. Here again we must define our words. By efficiently here we do not mean the manner in which it is done by the personnel operating at present. We do mean that the personnel now present, or any future personnel, could operate more efficiently if our recommendations are carried through.

We consider this point of maximum operating efficiency all important. 

The teacher shortage, the future increments in salaries, and the future high costs of building maintenance all demand that the machinery of educationviz., buildings, personnel, and mode of operation--be the best possible calibre. Any setup which does not provide for maximum utilization of the two components (building and personnel) means that the taxpayer is wasting his money. He may not know it. He may think he is getting off cheaper with the less efficient system. But he pays for it and more in later providing for his children special training which they could have gotten from a modern school system. He pays, too, when he finds his children competing outside the school for further education with better trained children from better schools, schools which his town could have had and supported had he expressed approval of those projects. 

Sherborn's schools, we felt, have been well supported in the past by the community. The portion of the town budget devoted to the schools is significant. The problem now, as we see it, is the efficient management of these monies.

At the start of our study we as a group had no specific idea of what constituted an efficient administrative system. We did agree that the professional portion of the system should be given all the freedom available within the financial resources of the community.

We began our study with an exam-

- ination of the role of the school committee. We found that under Massachusetts law the school committee has almost unlimited powers. It is immune except through the ballot box, from town control. It is able to state categorically what should be done and how in every phase of school activity. "Within reason, it can spend as much or as little of the town's money as it wishes. It need take no advice from any professional member of the staff in its appointments or decisions. 11 We felt this to be wrong. Most sch-. ool committees are composed of people whose only contact with education has been their own and their children's schooling. Their viewpoints have been formulated least often by study of educational problems, but more often by prejudice and word-of-mouth information. 

Many of our abuses of the educational system, much of the hiring of incompetent teachers, the loss of teacher morale and the entry of plain politics into the school plant has had its origin in these vast powers of the School Committee. Sherborn has escaped this aspect to a large extent. 

The committee felt that the school committee's function should be advisory as to policy, and liaison between the town and the teaching staff as to the financial phase. The actual administration of educational policy, mode of teaching and staff should be the superintendent's and principal's job.

These divisions should be formulated by state law.

The committee's relation to the town should also be improved. We felt that a 3-man board was too small. Because of this number a complete reversal in policy can be effected by the change of one member. In one sense this may--. from the Town's point of view--be a good thing. It permits the Town through election to regulate the board's activities. It could mean, however, tremendous vacillation in policy. This would be detrimental to the better operation of the staff.

In our opinion a five-man board-each member serving for five years-would tend to provide a more continuous policy. (This was tried in Sherborn for a time and then given up, apparently because of the lack of people interested in serving on the committee.)

The Superintendent 

The superintendent of a school system, as we see him, occupies the position of chief executive. He is the agent of the school committee. Thru him the school committee makes its decisions concerning the teaching staff. It is his job to carry out the committee's policies.

Under the system in which we operate we have a superintendent, in effect, for one day a week. The other towns of the Union--Wayland and Sudbury--receive the rest of his time. 

While there is nothing we can do about it under this system, we feel that we should have more supervisory time. We are actually receiving more of his time than is due us on a salary basis.

For this reason we looked into the possibility of having a superintendentprincipal. Some of the committee felt that this was a more desirable arrangement than the present one. On this question there seems to be a great deal of disagreement. Most professional people seem to feel that our present arrangement is preferable. The state certainly does, since we are reimbursed in part for the superintendent's salary. We would not be if we had a superintendent-principal arrangement.

We agreed, however, that our present system should lay more administrative duties and responsibility on our principal. It should also relieve him of all but a minor teaching load. We recommend in this connection that the school be divided into a kindergarten 

6/6 setup with an administrator for each group (2 principals or a superintendent-principal and principal) responsible for the curriculum planning and teaching procedures.

Recommendations made in relation to teacher activity: 

1. Up to and through grade six an interview with a parent of each of the pupils should become a routine requirement for the teacher.

- 

2. In-service teacher training should be encouraged and recognized even more than at present. Any special service rendered the school by a teacher should be similarly recognized. 

3. In the opinion of this committee each elementary school teacher should teach no more than one grade, irrespective of the number of pupils. No more than 20 pupils should be in one grade up through the 6th grade. (This is the consensus of opinion of the several school superintendents interviewed by the committee. This recommendation has been further made by the 

National Education Association's committee on the study of rural education.)

We have given the recommendations concerning the teachers prior to this explanation for the sake of clarity and ease of discussion. We found nothing to improve in the relationship between teachers, principal, and superintendent.

There is a large area for improvement, however, in the relations between the teachers and the town. Until recently there were no incentives set up by which the teachers would be stimulated to improve their professional rating. In view of the higher costs of living we feel that the base rates should be increased.

We were particularly emphatic about the number of grades per teacher because we found that the school is not training the number of students that are available. The average entry class is fifteen students. A check-up reveals that a number of students go out of the town for kindergarten through second grade. The reason for this is that many parents and educators feel that the kindergarten and first two grades are the most important. (These are the grades in which a child learns to read and write. Without these fundamentals he is handicapped.) 

The reader may ask, "what difference does four to five students per year per grade going out of town make to us?" It means simply that--whereas now it is possible to state that there are not enough students to justify one teacher per grade; if those students came to our grade school we would have too many for the two grade per teacher arrangement.

When twenty students per teacher is considered the optimum, then the difference between ten, fifteen, and twenty becomes very important. means here that we have, from our point of view of the efficiency of our system, a tremendous lack because (1) we are not educating a number of our children and (2) that we are missing in the students which we are educating the benefits accruing from the generally accepted more desirable system of one teacher per grade.

We now come to the point of the desirability of attracting to our school the students that are not attending it. Many may feel that if they come costs will go up. That is true. But they will not go up much over what they are now.

In return for making our school attractive to these students we will find that the school is fulfilling its duty to the best of its ability. 

We are convinced that it is not doing so now because of this failure in the administrative setup. And worst of all, those children who are attending our grade school are not obtaining from it all they could if the classes were larger. Thus both groups of students, those going to Sherborn grade school and those going out of town, can be considered to be losing available benefits. Here

This same situation applies more critically to the High School. we have three and "275's" teachers. 

Not four, as is generally assumed, because our principal must devote part of his time to administration. 

This means simply that while the school's published curriculum is good, that its implementation cannot be at peak efficiency. Courses must be given in alternate years. Teachers must teach in more than one field. It is obvious that such a situation means that the process of educating the students is not running efficiently. The teachers are not able to give all they have to their students. The students are not able to take all the teachers can give. Thus here again--

- purely from an administrative point of view--we as taxpayers are paying for more than we get.

Report of the Committee on Support and Financial Management 

It is important to emphasize at the beginning that our schools, good or bad, are a direct reflection on us and mirror to the fullest extent either the lack of funds or the adequacy of funds which are earmarked for educational purposes and the efficiency with which they are spent.

The committee analyzed the complete financial status of the town in relation to assessed valuation, tax rate, private sources of support, and state and federal aid for schools. These factors were all taken into consideration in weighing Sherborn's present expenditures for schools and its ability to pay increased costs, if necessary. 

Assessed Valuation 

It was found that the valuation of the town for tax purposes for the past six years has changed very little-the valuation being $2,109,250 in 1941, and $2,179,901 in 1946.

Tax Rate 

The tax rate, due to unchanged assessed valuations has in the past directly reflected town expenditures, having gone from $27.20 in 1941 to $24.00 in 1945 and up to $36.40 in 1946. 

On present valuations, the tax rate increases approximately 45¢ for each $1,000 of taxes levied.

State Aid 

Payments to the town by the state have been under 10% of the total school budget for any year since 1941, the earliest year under review. Further reference to state aid is made later in this report.

Federal Aid 

No funds are at present being received from the federal government for education, although funds are available to those schools which offer special courses in such subjects as agriculture and industrial arts. 

Private Sources 

Through the will of Martha Sawin the town has the use of the Sawin 

Academy and the Sawin Fund. The money invested from this fund produced an income of $609.91 for the year ended December 31, 1946. income is used for repairs to the academy building at the discretion of the trustees.

Summary 

In general, support can only be increased by additional revenue from one of the above sources. It appears that there will be no increase from either private sources or federal aid in the near future. The national trend is toward increased state aid and a number of states are now contributing 50% or more of the costs for local schools. This trend is reflected in Massachusetts by several bills which are now before the State Legislature. The passage of one of these would result in about $12,000 aid to Sherborn or nearly 40% of the present school budget. This bill also includes an additional item of $60.00 per pupil for those towns which close high schools of less than 100 pupils and send their students to an approved high school of larger size.

At present, there appear to be only two sure means of increasing school revenue; namely, revaluation of assessed property to increase the tax base, or increase in the tax rate on the present assessed valuation.

Preface Report of the Committee on School Plant 

The understanding of the problems encountered in school planning for Sherborn will involve some of the history of our schools. The physical organization of the school system, prior to 1873, was typical of the decentralized, rural type. Lack of modern transportation required a larger number of schools, each serving fewer pupils.

The present school plant, as the subject of this study, begins with the construction of Sawin Academy in 1873. This endowed Academy, offering college preparatory education to any who so aspired, was one of the benefits enjoyed by small New England towns, when the Industrial Revolution in this country joined the need for better education with the private monies available. Originally privately supported and administered, Sawin Academy was brought under the authority of the town during the first decade of this century. At present the five trustees are elected by the town and function in the maintenance of the fund and the building, while the school affairs are under the supervision of the School Committee.

The consolidation of the district schools was completed about 1890 and modernized in 1909 with the construction of the elementary school building. 

Our school plant has changed but little since this last construction and this study will attempt to show a realistic estimation of the relationship between our needs and our present equipment. 

The problem is to determine what we have; what is considered standard educational school housing; and in what ways we may equate these considerations.

Report 

The investigation of the school plant divides into two areas of study: 

1. Population trends and their effect on the school plant, now and in the future. 

2. The study of the physical plant. 

Population Trends and School Membership 

School planning for the future requires a consideration of the size of the school plant needed to accommodate future membership in the school, and an adequate program to meet the changing educational needs of American children. Any estimate of the probable size of future membership in the schools involves a study of population trends in the community and those factors which may influence that trend. 

Sherborn Population Trend 

Estimating the probable population of Sherborn ten years from now is largely guess work, due to the uncertainty of conditions outside Sherborn, which could influence immigration into the town and which are numerically incalculable.

It is possible, by accepted techniques which have been fairly reliable in other estimates, to make a tentative forecast on the basis of past population figures of what the population would be in the event conditions remained unchanged. This assumption cannot be made, but it is valuable to compute such estimates in order to obtain some tentative numerical working basis, subject to modification by other factors.

Factors Unfavorable to Population In crease Through Immigration The character of Sherborn as a community is probably the dominant factor affecting the rate and type of population increase through immigration. 

In the old days, before the date for this study, Sherborn was more than now a self-contained, small rural town, chiefly an agricultural center with an 

Inn, railroad station, several schools, some small businesses, a bit of manufacturing, a reformatory, and a history. With the growth of larger Framingham, however, from a rural center to a large town and the growth of other nearby towns as manufacturing centers, Sherborn was bypassed by 80- }. called "progress. 11 In the last 20 years it has made its own individual progress. It has developed into its present character of a pleasant country residential community, a large part of whose residents commute to business, profession or trade within a radius including Boston on the one hand and Worcester on the other. It retains also its rural aspect with its dairy, poultry and truck farms; though many former farms which have changed hands are now converted into residential farms, either not farmed at all, divided into residence and leased or sold land, farmed as an avocation, or partially farmed as aid to subsistence on incomes earned elsewhere.

The sentiment of the townspeople tends toward preserving the present character of Sherborn as a residential country community, undeveloped in either a suburban or urban sense. The older families devoted to the traditions of Sherborn, together with the newer element which has immigrated here, attracted by the quality of the place as it is, do not wish and probably would not purposely promote any measures which might lead to a large influx of population not easily assimilable into the spirit of the town. 

What may continue to happen is that those inhabitants, who find themselves dissatisfied with this kind of place will move out, to be gradually replaced by those attracted by its present qualities.

The zoning laws reflect the town sentiment against business or industrial developments from within or without which might tend to change extensively the residential character of the town.

A building code was adopted after the new General Motors Plant involved 

Sherborn's periphery. Together with the zoning laws, this tends to prevent any large real estate investment in shoddy housing on crowded lots, although of course there could be a considerable population increase more gradually through private land purchase and building.

- The absence of town water, because of the town's refusal, to date, to vote or appropriate money for it, is a reflection in part of the town sentiment against developments which might "attract people to Sherborn" and in part of the unwillingness of residents in outlying areas to be taxed for a benefit they themselves do not need and which would affect only a small part of the town. In any event, at present, anyone wishing to build in Sherborn is confronted immediately with the often unpredictable expense of drilling a well.

The limited educational facilities of the Sherborn schools have, in the past, been a deterrent to prospective settlers with children to educate. This limitation is not to be construed, however, in view of the town's participation in the present survey, as a result of the town's sentiment against development.

No plans for real estate development in the sense of housing, to be facilitated by the town, have been submitted by the Planning Board. No town property has been designated for such development or is likely to be. 

Factors Favorable to Population In crease Through Immigration On the other hand there are positive factors without the town which could operate against Sherborn's gradual growth trend as observed and theoretically predicted. 

Decentralization from metropolitan areas, a recognized nationwide trend, operates in this vicinity as the spread from metropolitan Boston. The type of decentralization affecting Sherborn is considered to be that from Boston suburbs rather than from the metropolis itself, as Sherborn is located in the next peripheral zone beyond Wellesley which has doubled its population in the last fifteen years. Whether or not, and by what time, Sherborn can expect to be significantly affected by the trend away from Boston is a matter of conjecture.

Two factors would indicate a conservative estimate: 

1. Sherborn's situation off the main railway and communication routes. 

2. The outward pressure created by population growth in the Boston area is not too great, the population being relatively stable.

Probable industrial developments in areas beyond Sherborn would tend to affect Sherborn's growth more significantly than the trend away from 

Boston. General industrial expansion and development away from large cities is presently exemplified in this vicinity by the erection of the new 

General Motors Plant. It is probable that new or expanded plants in towns around Sherborn would positively influence immigration here, modified by factors noted in the preceding sections.

Possible private real estate development, as a result of the above industrial development, seems the factor most likely to increase the growth trend as theoretically predicted. How probable would be a capital investment in such a development, (served possibly by a large central well in lieu of town water), when, and from what source it could come, is all a matter only of conjecture. 

There are no definitely announced plans of this sort. Without capital investment, however, residents holding more land than personally needed could sell off lots of one acre or more to individuals for building (when the building situation becomes alleviated) within the code. The possible extent of population increase through such a mechanism could be theoretically calculated by use of a property map. As none is presently available, no such calculation is offered here. The possibility of town water being eventually available should be noted also as a potentially favorable factor affecting immigration.

Sherborn High School, according to the theoretical prediction we would still have in 1956-57 a high school membership of under 100 pupils. According to authoritative educational opinion 300-500 is the minimum number of students for an American High School providing an adequately broad educational program at a financial figure justified by educational value received by the students. 

Sherborn's per pupil cost for 194546 was $307, the median for the state being $190 and the highest being $440 (see financial report). (The cost per pupil for 1946-47 is expected to be over $400.) Sherborn is financially able to pay the highest per pupil cost in the state, but the educational program is limited both by the plant and by the size of the enrollment; a high school of such a small membership can not make the most valuable educational use of a broader program, with attendant personnel expansion and subsequently increased financial outlay. 

Conclusion from membership figures:

- 

Maintenance of the present. high school, with its present or predicted membership, is both educationally and financially unjustified by potential educational value received.

1. The first two parts of the general question, 1.e., what we have and what we should have, are combined in a rating sheet of a type which has enjoyed considerable success in previous investigations of school plants. The rating sheet and scoring method, devised by Mr. 

Ralph McLeary of Concord, is a list of the essential items which should appear in a modern school plant, and includes the following as main topical headings: 

Topics

Possible Score 120 170 225 315 170 1000 (perfect score)

Further sub-divisions operate so as to reduce each main topic to its elements, through sub-topics and items. 

The relative importance of each element of an item to the whole of the school plant is indicated by the numerical scores which each item may achieve if considered perfect. A brief statement given with the element presents a standard with which to judge the individual element under scrutiny.

Rating is done by comparing each element with the standards, and subjecting the element to a numerical penalty. 

The sum of the individual penalties subtracted from the total possible item score results in a quantitative estimation of the relative worth of the items. These item scores are totalled for each sub-topic and topic, and the sum of all main topics is the score given to the entire plant. (In 

Table VII is a list of reasons given to explain some of the more important penalties.)

Methods of Evaluation of Facts, by Profile Charts 

In order to record the facts effectively, the main topical scores and some of the sub-topical scores are entered as points on a graph. Since several distinct sets of characteristics are combined to show the entire picture in outline, this graph becomes a profile chart of the school plant, 

(see Table VI). Areas in which the plant rates poorly or well are seen instantly and in their proper relationship to each other.

3. Conclusions as to expanded program requirements. a. The present building cannot accommodate an expanded program, since it is utilized to the limits of its safety and efficiency.

Further reduction of grounds by additional buildings is undesirable for both schools.
