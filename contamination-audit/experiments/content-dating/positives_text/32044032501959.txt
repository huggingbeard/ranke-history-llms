Unnecessary consumption of time and energy, delay in disposition of disputes, and enormous expenditures of money are among the vices resulting from the circumstances described, but the principal vice is that such conditions create confusion, magnify uncertainty, multiply the possibilities of error, and otherwise tend to make less certain and less accurate the judicial determination of disputed issues. The latter vices, if permitted to continue to exist, might threaten the judicial process itself in respect to complex controversies.

It should be commented that in spite of the difficulties unreasonable delay in the final disposition of these cases by the trial courts does not appear; that is, undue delay between the filing of complaints and the entries of judgments. It is true that the elapsed time in these cases averages considerably more than the time for ordinary cases; for example, the median time for antitrust cases in 1946-48 was about 22-23 months, whereas the median for all civil cases was about 9 months. But, in view of the complexity of these cases and the extent of the necessary preparation and preliminary proceedings, a median time of less than two years to judgment is not considered undue or subject to criticism.

III COMMENTS INTRODUCTORY TO SUGGESTIONS 

'Your Committee does not recommend legislation or rules. It believes that the maximum benefit will be achieved by an authoritative statement of the importance of the problem in the administration of justice and a description of remedial methods and measures thought by experienced judges to be effective.

This report is confined to cases of potentially abnormal length of hearing or volume of record. Many of the suggestions and comments which it contains are inappropriate in ordinary actions.

This report relates to unnecessary volume, expense and delay. Many cases involving modern commerce, economics, finance and industry are inherently complicated and necessarily involve considerable time and expense. Necessary features of such cases should not be curtailed.

This report deals with procedural methods only. 

It contains no suggestion or expression of opinion upon matters of legislative policy. 

Each case of the kind here considered is a peculiar problem; it is sui generis. No uniform pattern of procedure can be devised for them. While the report is generally applicable to all types of cases-civil, Government and private, and criminal -trial judges may find it desirable to make distinctions for the different types. Every trial judge

who approaches one of these trials should realize that he must formulate and apply a plan for the particular case and its peculiarities. These cases will not fit into a mold. However, the cases do have some common problems which yield to similar treatment. This report deals with such problems and the treatment of them. 

This report deals with judicial procedure only.

Procedure before the administrative agencies is the subject of a separate report.

IV GENERAL APPROACH TO THE PROBLEM 

The problem of unnecessary delay, volume and expense in these trials is much the same as is the problem of efficiency in any undertaking in which numbers of people and masses of material are involved. The answer lies in the avoidance of nonuseful action and material and in the effective organization of personnel and useful material prior to the beginning of the operation itself. So the efficient conduct of one of these cases depends in large part upon a skillful and complete pre-trial organization of the proposed evidence, the expected witnesses, and the trial personnel. Organization of that sort requires complete preparation on the part of all participants.

The person who must insure that a case of this nature is thoroughly prepared prior to the trial is the trial judge himself. Preparation and organization relate to many matters, which will be discussed in detail later in this report. The actual work must be done by counsel. But one of the tasks of the trial judge in a case of this sort is to make

certain, prior to setting the case for trial, that counsel are completely prepared and have efficiently organized their material. This is a task of inquiry and negotiation on the part of the judge and may require considerable patience and persistence. 

Without a firm attitude on the part of the judge no measure for minimum volume, expense and delay will be effective. Of course, firmness must be exercised equally toward both parties. Your Committee has heard vigorous protests in both directions; from the Department of Justice that firmness of limitation is exercised toward it but not toward private parties, and from private practitioners to the precise opposite effect. Neither protest should be well-founded.

It is not practical to proceed in these cases as in a lawsuit of ordinary complexity and bulk; that is, to let the parties exhaust the cross fire of pleading, to conduct open-court pre-trial hearings, or to let counsel try the case as they please. The potential range of issues, evidence and argument is so great, and the necessities of adversary representation so compelling, that the activities of counsel will result in records of fantastic size and complexity unless the trial judge exercises rigid control from the time the complaint is filed. Experiences related to and known by your Committee indicate that, generally speaking, when the trial judge imposes requirements upon the preparation of such a case for trial, he will receive the active cooperation of counsel for the parties.

The principal factors causing unnecessary delay, volume and expense in these cases are (1) vagueness of the issues, (2) proffers of masses of unnecessary evidence, and (3) lack of organization

of material and personnel. The principal available remedial means is the informal conference between judge and counsel prior to trial.

PARTICULARIZATION OF THE ISSUES 

In the cases with which this report is concerned, complaints and charges are usually couched in broad terms and are met with general denials or demands for strict proof for lack of information. Under the view as to the function of pleadings which has prevailed in the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, the complaint and answer cannot in most cases of the type now under discussion be relied upon as the means of framing the issues with sufficient definiteness. Nor, as a practical matter, will much gain in this respect be obtained by a motion under Rule 12(e) to make the complaint more definite; however broad and indefinite the allegations of the complaint may be, the complaint will generally meet the test of Rule 12(e) of being definite enough to permit the framing of a responsive pleading. At some time subsequent to the filing of the pleading, reduction of the generality of the complaint to specific disputed propositions must be made in the interest of efficient conduct of the trial and of proper disposition of the case. The more specific the issue for trial, the less complicated and time-consuming the trial can be and the more accurate the results. 

Whatever may be the objections and difficulties to the specification of issues in ordinary actions, the necessity for such specification in the cases with which this report is concerned is so great as to require that it be done no matter what the objection

or difficulty. Unless it is done, the hearing cannot be confined to its proper limits, counsel are at a loss as to their positions, and the judge is unable to relate the evidence to issues which are in dispute or to limit it to that which is relevant. 

Two obstacles are frequently met in efforts to specify issues. They are the reluctance of lawyers to be specific lest they thereby forego some advantage which might develop in the course of trial, and the failure of counsel to prepare the case thoroughly prior to trial, the lack of complete preparation causing them to be cautious in commitments. Neither of these conditions should prevent the court from insisting upon a specification of the issues which are in actual dispute and upon the elimination from the trial of all propositions not actually disputed. 

It is said that vague complaints in civil antitrust cases brought by the Government are unavoidable, because the inquisitorial power of the Department of Justice in civil matters is inadequate and the Department's only recourse is to file complaints containing indefinite allegations and thereafter to utilize the processes of the courts to discover the facts. It may well be that the powers of investigation of the Department are not sufficient for these tasks and that Congress should be asked to remedy that deficiency, or that the ample investigative powers of the Federal Trade Commission should be utilized. The Rules of Civil Procedure relating to discovery are, of course, to be given the widest meaning and effect, and the processes of discovery there provided are in the nature of an investigation. 

But those rules have limitations and were not intended to make the courts an investigatory adjunct to the Department of Justice. In the cases here

under consideration those processes must necessarily be held to the scope of the judge's program for the case, as developed by him in the conferences with counsel here under discussion. (See Part XIII hereinafter.) Investigations not related to issues presented for litigation in a pending court case, in civil matters, are no part of the judicial function, and the compulsory processes of the judicial system should not be made available for other than judicial purposes. If a plaintiff brings bona fide charges, it may discover such relevant evidence as the respondent possesses. It cannot pretend to bring charges in order to discover whether actual charges should be brought. Therefore, if, in the course of conferences prior to the trial of a civil action, a judge concludes that the Government, or the plaintiff, has no knowledge of charges of such specificity as to permit understandable and triable statement, trial should be postponed until the lack of particularization can be fully remedied, or the case should be dismissed.

Pleadings will not serve to particularize issues sufficiently in these cases, and motions for particulars will not serve that purpose. Such particularization must be achieved by informal conferences between judge and counsel well in advance of a possible trial date. The judge should insist that he and all parties know definitely what issues are to be tried, that they understand what the issues are, that the issues be stated simply and succinctly, and that the issues as stated be susceptible of trial and determination.

The issues as determined and framed in informal conference should be incorporated in an order of the court which should control the proceedings

thereafter, as Rule 16 provides. It sometimes happens that issues to be thus formally and finally fixed cannot adequately be framed prior to such processes of discovery as may be advisable or necessary. In cases of the sort here under consideration, informal conferences between the judge and counsel should nevertheless be held when the basic pleadings are in, and before discovery processes are begun. The issues indicated or known at that point should be framed and stated. They should serve as the basis for the bounds of permissible discovery. Thereafter, if it appears advisable or necessary, the issues which are to control the subsequent course of the trial may be reframed and stated in the pre-trial order contemplated by Rule 16.

These processes by informal conference require careful and intensive study of the pleadings as filed, frank discussion by judge and counsel, and a cooperative effort by all concerned. These are not too great requirements for the judge to make.

VI OTHER OBJECTIVES OF CONFERENCES PRIOR TO TRIAL 

The complete preparation and organization of a complicated case for trial involves many features. 

For these purposes the formal pre-trial hearing in open court, with counsel at counsel table and the judge on the bench, is not effective. Rule 16 provides for pre-trial "conference". Informal conferences between trial judge and counsel are best suited to the purposes covered by this report. These conferences may be prolonged and repeated. They should be continued until all possible advantages have been derived from them.

While this report uses the term "prior to trial" in describing the informal conferences here advocated, such conferences may be imperative at periods during the trial. Principally this necessity arises when the trial is divided into parts; as, for example, when the plan of trial calls for a recess after the plaintiff's case is presented and before the defendant begins his presentation, and also when issues in a case are tried seriatim separately. 

Among the features of preparation and organization for trial which can be dealt with in these conferences are:

Opening Statements 

Pre-trial opening statements, oral or written or both, by counsel tend toward crystallization of issues, complete preparation for trial, elimination of uncontested issues, elimination of irrelevant and immaterial matter, and a general shortening of the trial record, lessening of expense and conservation of time. Such statements or briefs may include both the positions of the parties as to facts and summary memoranda upon the law points involved. 

It is true that such opening statements in advance of a trial tend to eliminate the element of surprise from the proceeding. But the Civil Rules have largely eliminated surprise as a factor in ordinary litigation, and in any event a tactical advantage by surprise is not possible in the trial of cases of the type subject to this report. Many maneuvers are available to opposing counsel to insure against such a disadvantage. Claims of parties to the tactical advantage of surprise in the course of a trial should be rejected in these cases.

There is a difference of opinion among the mem-

bers of your Committee as to whether trial briefs in civil suits, when filed, should or should not be available to opposing counsel. On the one hand it is urged that the judge ought never to have information as to one side of a case which the opposing side does not have; this is a matter of common justice. On the other hand it is urged that such briefs are merely for the personal information of the judge and that counsel are more free and frank and less formal when the briefs are not for service on opponents. Consistent with an advisory ruling of the senior Judicial Conference, trial briefs in criminal cases should be exchanged by opposing counsel. 

When trial briefs prior to trial are required, they should be strictly limited as to length. In the United Shoe Machinery case, supra, a trial brief of 7,000 pages was filed.

Exchange of Exhibits 

Unless it is impossible for some compelling reason, the parties to these cases should be required to exchange prior to trial copies of proposed exhibits. 

The advantages of such exchange in preventing delay and volume of record in the trial are obvious. 

Before such a case is called for trial each side should be advised fully as to the material which his opponent proposes to offer.

Preparation of Documentary Material 

This subject is discussed in detail hereinafter under the heading "Bulk Documentary Evidence".

Suffice it at this point to say that the judge should require that prior to trial all proposed exhibits be authenticated and marked for identification purposes.

Stipulations 

Stipulations can frequently be reached upon matters not actually disputed and upon matters as to which initial disputes are quickly dissolved. Matters properly subject to stipulation are (1) the identification and authentication of documents, (2) undisputed facts or facts not subject to justifiable dispute, (3) undisputed conclusions upon pleaded issues or conclusions not subject to justifiable dispute. Such stipulations are a proper part of proceedings prior to trial.

Sometimes counsel desires to stipulate that undisputed material be placed in a record for "background" or "atmosphere", and in some cases this is desirable and helpful. But whenever it is permitted the practice should be sharply restricted to material which is indeed helpful and material to the consideration of the disputed issues. Otherwise vast quantities of unnecessary material find their way into the record. 

Advance Exchange of Testimony

It frequently happens in these cases that the testimony of technical or expert witnesses on unusually complicated matters is prepared in writing well in advance of the trial. This is known colloquially as "canned testimony". When it is feasible to supply opposing counsel with copies of such testimony in advance of the trial, considerable unnecessary delay and volume of record are avoided. This possibility should be explored in the course of the conferences prior to trial.

Conference of Expert Witnesses 

Where experts are to appear as witnesses on the opposing sides in a major matter, it may be helpful if an informal conference of such experts can be arranged prior to the trial. Such informal discussions by the experts and counsel, with or without the judge, may result in stipulations as to many items and a considerable narrowing of the area of dispute.

A Plan of Trial 

A detailed plan of trial tends to promote efficiency and to eliminate unnecessary delay, volume and expense. Such a plan is in contrast to an uncharted proceeding from day to day as circumstances may indicate and the trial tactics of counsel accomplish. 

Among the features which may be considered in designing in advance a plan of trial are the following: (a) A time schedule. This schedule may include the daily hour of convening, the times and hours of mid-day recessing, and the daily hour of adjournment; the weekly days of hearing; the dates for anticipated long recesses, if any.

These pre-ar- rangements permit counsel to make schedules as to witnesses and material and thus eliminate minor dilatory moves. (b) Identification of interests. Where a case involves several parties, it is of great value to identify their interests prior to the beginning of trial. Sometimes there are but two adversary interests, but frequently the parties nominally on one side in these cases actually have diverse interests, on some if not all issues. Sometimes this diversity of interests cannot be defined prior to trial, but where such pre-

trial definition is possible it is valuable in making other arrangements in the plan of trial. 

Arrangements (c) Mechanics of transcript. 

should be made in advance as to the time for delivery of the reporter's transcript (daily, weekly, or merely upon conclusion), the number of copies to be furnished and to whom, and the time and manner of making corrections to the transcript. In a long trial it is advisable to require that proposed corrections to the transcript be submitted within a stated time after the transcript is delivered. (d) Participation of counsel in the examination of witnesses, both direct and cross. If there is only one lawyer (meaning one senior, or leader) on each side in a case, no arrangement as to participation is necessary. But usually in these cases there are several parties represented by different lawyers, and frequently, even where there is but one party, several senior lawyers are at counsel table. Moreover, as we have indicated in (b) above, there are frequently different interests among parties nominally on the same side, and groups of parties may compose each such interest. In all such cases arrangements as to the participation of various lawyers in direct examination, and the rights of the various lawyers, on behalf of their respective clients, to cross-examine are important features of a plan of trial. Experienced lawyers in such a situation usually adopt among themselves some sort of informal organization or arrangement so that courtroom activities can proceed with order and dispatch. These arrangements frequently take the form of a designated one or few to act as manager or spokesman for the group in such matters or manner as they may designate. Of course, the

judge should not prescribe any such arrangement or designation, except in so far as it may be necessary for him to limit examination of witnesses or to require orderly and expeditious progress of the trial. 

However, he may always call to the attention of counsel the great desirability of some such plan. 

The chief objective of an advance arrangement as to the participation of counsel is a minimum of repetition in direct and in cross examination. (e) Order of procedure in chief. If more than two opposing parties, or groups of parties, participate, the order of procedure in presentation of evidence-in-chief should be arranged in advance; similarly, the order of rebuttal proof should be arranged in advance. (f) The manner and extent of making objections. Whether argument on objections (in addition to the statement of grounds authorized by the Rules of Procedure) should be permitted and, if so, whether at the option of the judge or at the option of counsel, and whether such argument should be reported on the record, should be arranged in advance. When more than two parties participate in a trial it is well to decide in advance the extent to which objections by one party are to be deemed to be on behalf of other parties. The time for making objections to documentary evidence, whether immediately upon the offer in evidence or reserved until after cross examination, should be arranged in advance. (g) Cross examination. The right to, order and time of cross examination should be arranged prior to trial. Where there are several parties nominally on the same side of a case, advance settlement of this feature of the proceeding is of special impor-

tance. Even where there are only two parties the time for cross examination (whether it must be immediately upon conclusion of direct examination, or may be reserved and, if so, to what point in the proceeding) had best be determined in advance. (h) The mechanics of handling exhibits. These mechanics include the number of copies to be furnished, the size of copies, the style of copies (whether printed, mimeographed, photostated, or reproduced by some other method), the time and system of numbering, and, if large numbers of exhibits are involved, an agreed method of keeping sets of exhibits (after presentation) by the judge and all counsel. A master list (not for admission in evidence) of proposed exhibits, prepared early in the case, is a helpful facility. (i) Indexing of the record. A current indexing of the record, a subject discussed later in this report, should be arranged. (j) The method which the judge will follow, after the trial, in the preparation of the findings of fact should be announced prior to the trial. The purpose of such announcement is to enable counsel to prepare during the trial for the performance of that part of the task which may be assigned to them. The preparation of findings is discussed hereinafter. (k) The appointment of a capable and experienced lawyer (who may or may not be the judge's law clerk) to sit in at the pre-trial and trial stages of a large case should provide invaluable help in the handling of documents, statistics, and other evidence and in the analysis of complex questions of fact and law. Authority might be requested from the Administrative Office to implement such a course.

Summary on Pre-Trial 

In sum, the purposes of conferences prior to trial are these: to insure that all participants in the forthcoming trial understand the issues to be tried; to insure that counsel for all parties are thoroughly prepared for the trial according to the requirements of the trial judge as to an efficient and economical presentation of evidence and argument; to insure that each party is as well informed as circumstances permit concerning what his adversary will proffer; to insure, so far as practicable, that unnecessary material will not be proffered, and if proffered not admitted, in the course of the trial; to insure that all admissible documentary material is physically prepared and arranged for orderly presentation; to insure that every practicable step is taken so that findings can be derived from the proof presented and conclusions drawn from the findings, as expeditiously as the inherent substantive nature of the case permits.

VII SEPARATION OF ISSUES 

These cases are sometimes divisible into parts and the trials of the several parts separable from each other. Thus issues may depend upon each other seriatim and so be triable separately in order. 

For example, if a case involves issues of both liability and damages, the liability issue can be tried first and separately. Much time, energy and money can frequently be saved by this procedure.

In other cases, where there are numbers of issues, it may be helpful to separate the presentation of evidence according to issues; that is, to require that all the evidence on both sides of a particular issue be presented before other evidence on other issues is presented. This method, where practical, permits the judge to concentrate his attention upon one issue at a time and greatly assists him in evaluating and weighing the opposing evidence upon each point. It is undoubtedly the most efficient way in which to proceed, where the case is of a nature to permit it. When it is to be followed, careful specification of the issues is imperative and careful organization of material for such procedure is necessary. Arrangements for such separate presentation according to issues must be made prior to trial and must be clearly understood well in advance of the trial.

Again, it is frequently possible to postpone the taking of evidence and hearing of argument upon the content and form of the decree until after the findings and decision upon the merits of the case have been made. Such postponement frequently saves much time, money, and volume of record, because after the findings and decision the decree can be settled without prolonged controversy, or by cooperative effort on the part of counsel. 

VIII PERIOD OF INQUIRY 

In antitrust litigation the Government frequently asserts as relevant and material facts concerning the operations of the defendants over long periods of years-twenty, thirty, and even fifty years being

mentioned to your Committee as instances. It is believed that such periods of inquiry should be defined and limited by the trial judge. It is clear that if the formation of a conspiracy is alleged the incidence of that formation may be proved no matter when it may have occurred. But overt acts or courses of action alleged to have been pursuant to the alleged conspiracy may well be irrelevant to any present remedial or punitive action, if such overt acts occurred at a remote period of time. Therefore, the trial judge is fully justified in limiting proof of action alleged to have been pursuant to an established conspiracy to a period of time reasonably relevant to present possibilities of effective judgment.

Consideration of this feature of the litigation is a function of conferences prior to trial. IX

BULK DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE 

A serious problem is presented by the growing practice of offering in evidence large numbers of documents. The problem arises in two forms. One is the offer of large quantities of documentary material alleged to be direct evidence. The other is the offer in evidence of large quantities of material alleged and shown to be the underlying or basic material for a conclusion of fact stated by a witness, usually an expert testifying to an opinion. The two forms of this problem are wholly different and we discuss them separately.

Bulk Direct Evidence 

The presentation of direct evidence in bulk is a cause of delay, excessive volume of record, great confusion, and extreme inefficiency. One, two, three, and even as many as five thousand exhibits have been presented at one time and offered for admission. In one case reported to your Committee a single exhibit consisting of six thousand patents was offered.

The mere physical bulk of such evidence is a difficulty. Long periods of time and much energy are consumed in reading or listening to these vast amounts of material. In one instance which came to our attention, the judge was told that it would take between one and two years for him to read the exhibits offered by one party. Even if the material is relevant, these difficulties exist. If irrelevant material is included, the trouble is aggravated. Confusion is compounded in keeping track of the subject-content of such masses. The expenses of reporting and printing are enormous. But those are not the most objectionable difficulties. Every fact or alleged fact admitted to a record on behalf of one party requires that the opposite party put in one or more contradictory or explanatory facts, if any there be. And so the material in the record grows in concentric circles. Moreover, and still more important, the trial judge must wander, or dig, through mazes and masses of printed data in order to find the pertinent bits from which he will deduce the determining facts. In addition to the labor thus involved, the possibilities of inaccuracy and error are greatly increased.

The trial judge has two opportunities to deal with this problem, one at the conferences prior to trial

1 and the other at the trial. The two should be coordinated. 

At the conferences prior to trial the judge should: (1) Announce that he will not admit to the record unassorted, unexpurgated, or unidentified documentary material. (2) Require all exhibits intended for proffer to be submitted to opposing counsel in time to permit adequate examination prior to presentation at the trial. (3) Require that when portions only of books or documents are admissible as relevant and material the offering party prepare and supply copies of those portions only, with accompanying due identification of source. The whole of the original books or documents should be made available to the judge and to opposing counsel for purposes of identification and cross examination, but admission to the record should be restricted to those portions of such documents as are relevant, material, etc., to the issues in the case at bar.

Physically the record should consist of the copies of the relevant portions only. (4) Require that counsel offering large numbers of documentary exhibits group the exhibits according to the issues to which they pertain and identify the groups with the respective pertinent issues. (5) Require that documents of similar content or effect be grouped and their similarity announced by the party proposing to offer them.

The purpose of this requirement is to permit the court to deal effectively with cumulative evidence. (6) Require that all documents be arranged and

marked in such manner as to facilitate efficient handling at the trial. (7) Require that opposing counsel confer in a cooperative effort to cull irrelevant material from proposed exhibits. Counsel should thereafter report to the judge the results of their efforts in this direction. (8) Announce the procedure which he will follow at the time of the presentation of the documents at the trial, the varieties of which procedure are hereinafter discussed. (9) Announce the rules which he will apply in admitting documents to, or excluding them from, the record at the trial.

At the trial, when large quantities of documentary material are offered in evidence, the judge should: (1) Refuse to admit unassorted, unexpurgated, or unidentified exhibits. (2) Require the party offering, or proposing to offer, quantities of documents to show that they are competent, relevant and material. This is a reversal of traditional procedure. The accepted method is to require that the opposing party submit an objection to incompetent, irrelevant or immaterial matter and then support the objection. 

That method requires that the opposing party be familiar with the proffered material and places upon him the duty of protecting the record against unnecessary encumbrances. That method also affords a party an opportunity to confuse and delay a proceeding by merely offering material which he himself has not analyzed or arranged.

It is believed, and recommended, that in these cases which involve great quantities of documentary evidence, the burden of protecting the court against unnecessary material should be placed upon him who makes the offer; he knows, or should be required to know, whether each bit of evidence is competent, relevant and material. He should be required to make such a showing as will enable the judge to evaluate the material.

It is not believed that the admission in evidence of exhibits subject to a later showing of relevancy and materiality is efficient procedure.

A preferable method is to identify exhibits, mark them, and delay the offer and admission until admissibility has been established. (3) Apply not only the rules of evidence as to competency and relevancy but also the rule as to materiality. When large quantities of documentary evidence are offered, the judge should limit admission to that which is actually necessary to his consideration of the issues presented by the case. Such evidence as is merely "possibly helpful", or which merely supplies "atmosphere" or "background", may be rigidly excluded. However desirable such evidence may be in the ordinary case, in cases where the ability, time and energy of the judge are to be taxed by the necessities of the case, that which is unnecessary should be strictly excluded from the record.

Courts have applied that concept, as reference to the cases collected by the authorities readily shows. The matter is within the discretion of the trial judge. When, as in the cases here under consideration, material offered is in great quantities, some of which is of importance to the de-

cision and some of which is of no such importance and so is unnecessary to it, the trial judge may properly reject the latter part. In any event, whether or not the trial judge is disposed to be rigid on the matter, it is important that he realize that this course is available to him. 

To hold evidence inadmissible because not necessary to a decision of the issues is somewhat of an expansion of the rule as to materiality, but it is not a deviation from the principle of that rule. Whether or not the rule be logically a part of the law of evidence (See Wigmore, Evidence § 2), it is an established rule in the conduct of trials. "Materiality" is defined by Bouvier as "The property of substantial importance or influence, especially as distinguished from formal requirement. Capability of properly influencing the result of the trial." To exclude that which is of no substantial importance in reaching a decision is well within established principles. Where the quantity of substantially important evidence is great, all proffered evidence not shown to be of that nature should be rejected. (4) Exclude all merely cumulative evidence. That such evidence is available can be summarily noted on the record.

The method by which the trial judge masters a mass of documentary material in order to derive from it his findings of the facts is a matter of personal preference. Several methods are available and are severally thought preferable by different experienced trial judges. Three such methods are mentioned here: (1) The judge may read such material in his own time and at his own convenience off the

bench. If he is to follow that method, it is of vital importance that he require of counsel fully adequate marking, arrangement, coordination and indexing of the material. Counsel should clearly indicate which portion of such material must be read by the judge, and why. A judge following this method of procedure should not permit himself to be saddled with work which counsel ought to perform, particularly the labor of arrangement, indexing, expurgation and summarization. (2) The judge may require that the relevant portions of exhibits be read to him in open court. 

This method, of course, consumes a greater amount of time in court than does the method above mentioned, but on the whole it tends to a lesser consumption of time, because reading in open court tends to promote excision of irrelevant portions; irrelevancies become so strikingly apparent when read aloud. Moreover, this method permits discussion of each bit of evidence as it is presented, enabling the judge to rule on admissibility with a minimum of unnecessary effort and also enabling him to seek and obtain the aid of counsel in evaluating each bit of evidence and coordinating it with the whole.

The problem as to whether, when this method is pursued, the opposing party should be permitted to comment upon evidence as the offeror presents and comments upon it, and at the same time to present other portions of the same document, requires a ruling by the trial judge. In the du- 

Pont case (Civil No. 1216, Del.) this was permitted to be done. (3) Sometimes documentary material offered in

quantities is of such nature that the judge may require the offeror to present a witness who will offer orally a summary of the content and meaning of the documents. In such instances, the documents themselves should be present and available, and the witness would, of course, be subject to cross examination. Rebuttal testimony of the same character would be permitted when this method is followed. A statement of the Supreme court in Burton v. Driggs, 20 Wall. 125, 22 L.Ed. 299 (1874), appears to be federal authority for this practice, and the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit discussed and approved it in United States v. Mortimer, 118 F.2d 

266, 269 (1941). It is also used in the Ninth Circuit and in the state courts in California. The method is valuable, for example, when accounts must be reconstructed or deductions drawn from them; expert accountants are used to summarize the books and records which are the best evidence, and only the summary is admitted to the record. 

When this method is followed, after the oral testimony concerning the documents has been completed, only those portions of the documents which are disputed, either as to authenticity or as to meaning, need be admitted to the record, the undisputed descriptive summary of the remainder being a sufficient basis for findings of the facts therein reflected. All purposes served by the rules requiring the originals of documentary evidence to be presented are served by the presentation and identification of the documents. 

Your Committee is of the view that whenever large quantities of documentary material permit this treatment by a witness testifying orally, it

should be adopted by the court. It saves time and effort on the part of the judge and, of greater importance, makes for greater accuracy in his consideration of the evidence and in his conclusions therefrom. 

Bulk Underlying Data

The second problem in respect to bulk documentary evidence, i.e., the treatment of data which is basic or underlying to a material fact in the case, can be solved by the refusal of the judge to admit documentary material which is not disputed and which has no value except as underlying data for other testimony or exhibits.

It frequently occurs that the best-evidence rules require in justice to the parties that original documents instead of mere alleged copies, books of original entry instead of mere compilations or computations therefrom, be presented and made available to opposing parties. But that requirement can be met fully by the presentation of such material and its marking for identification, without an actual offer of it in evidence. Counsel should be permitted to identify material needed for reference purposes or as foundation for other evidence, without encumbering the record with material which is unnecessary in the final analysis for findings of facts. The mere identification (and marking) of documents should be carefully treated as a separate and distinct action from the offer in evidence of such documents. The record should be deemed to contain only evidence offered and formally admitted in evidence.

It is believed that the procedures suggested hereinabove in respect to bulk documentary material

will tend to minimize the volume of a record, conserve the time of the court, make more certain the evidentiary meaning of a mass of documents, and simplify the formulation of findings of facts from such documents.

Voluminous Depositions 

In these cases unduly voluminous depositions are frequently taken. When they are received in the formal record upon the trial in the full text as taken, they unnecessarily increase the bulk of material which must be analyzed and arranged in order to derive the pertinent and valuable points from the testimony. When volumes of depositions are presented upon the trial, the judge may require that a succinct narrative summary of each be filed with it, and he may require the opposing party to point out alleged inaccuracies in such summaries; it should be clearly understood that, absent material inaccuracies, the judge will assume the accuracy and completeness of such summaries for all purposes of the case. The judge should be rigid in the requirement that summaries of depositions be not prolix or repetitious. This suggested method was followed in Templeton v. Brotherhood of Railway 

Trainmen (7 F.R.D. 116 (W. D. Mo. 1946)), and 

3,000 pages of depositions were condensed and summarized in a document of 112 pages. 

PROOF OF SCIENTIFIC AND SIMILAR FACTS 

The cases with which this report is concerned frequently involve findings of enormously complicated scientific and other facts of a very specialized

or technical nature. In many instances such facts can be stipulated, but in many instances they are disputed issues. The rights of parties frequently depend upon such facts.

We are not here referring to theories of conflicting natures but to factual data: facts which may be well known to specialists in the field but which are so technical as to be unusually difficult for laymen.

Proof of such facts usually consists of expert testimony. When experts differ in their conclusions it is usually because they differ in their qualifications, the basic material used, or the processes by which they fashion their conclusions from the material used. Resolution of such differences is not the usual task involved in evaluating conflicting testimony where the fact to be established is an event and the differences between the witnesses are differences in truth-telling or in opportunity for observation. 

The traditional procedure for proof of these facts is the presentation of experts as witnesses by the opposing parties, direct examination by questions by the lawyers, and cross examination by questions by the opposing lawyers. Of course many such facts are of the sort which can be proved and found by the court by those established processes of testimony and finding. But frequently the cases here being considered involve scientific or similar facts of such complexity and difficulty and such enormous amounts of technical data as to be insusceptible of accurate determination by a non-expert, except after long and careful study of underlying material and, even then, at considerable risk of inaccuracy. 

The traditional method of proof when applied to such issues of fact is cumbersome, unnecessarily time-consuming, and uncertain in its results.

Some phases of this problem have been discussed by Judge Yankwich in "On the Use of Experts in 

Patent Cases" (22 J. Pat. Off. Soc'y 639 (1940)) 

and by Professor Dession in "The Trial of Economic and Technological Issues of Fact" (58 Yale L.J. 

1019, 1242 (1949)).

One procedure for the treatment of this problem is the selection of expert witnesses by agreement of the parties or by the judge with agreement of the parties. This procedure may be advisable in respect to simple issues of scientific or similar facts but is not believed to be as effective as the one hereinafter suggested when the issues as to such facts are unusually difficult or complicated. The use of witnesses so selected could not preempt the right of a party to present contradictory evidence, and the evaluation of such testimony and the formulation of conclusions is necessarily left for the judge. The reasons given hereinafter for the use of experts as masters point to the advantages of that method. 

The advantages of informal conferences of proposed expert witnesses, prior to trial, have already been suggested earlier in this report. 

Your Committee submits as a procedure which may be helpful in the determination of disputed scientific or extremely technical facts of unusual complexity or difficulty, the submission of such questions, precisely stated and defined, to experts as special masters. An expert or a panel of experts in the field in which the disputed fact lies should be selected, either by agreement of the opposing parties or by the parties and the judge. The expert or experts thus selected should be designated as a special master or as special masters to act en banc. The disputed issue of fact should be submitted to him

(including a panel as "him") in writing. He should be empowered to hold hearings, to receive such evidence presented by the parties as he may deem relevant and material to the issue, to permit cross examination, and otherwise to control the hearing so as to achieve the maximum expedition consistent with fair opportunity for the parties. The master or masters should make a written report, including basic findings and a conclusion upon the issue presented. This report should be received by the court as a special master's report under the 

Rules of Procedure, subject to exceptions, argument and the other established features of that procedure.

The reasons for the designation of experts as masters in respect to these issues are that they (1) understand better than non-experts the terminology used by expert witnesses in discussing the subject, (2) can more accurately evaluate differences in the qualifications of expert witnesses, in the material used by such witnesses, and in the processes by which such witnesses fashion conclusions from the material used, and (3) can more accurately formulate and express basic findings and conclusions upon such issues. It is believed that the use of experts as masters will eliminate much unnecessary delay, volume of record, and expense which occur inevitably when laymen in the field (i.e., the lawyers) are attempting to prove a complex, technical scientific or similar fact to another layman in the field (i.e., the judge). This belief is supported by the known fact that when the judge is himself an expert in the field the hearings are shorter in time, less voluminous in record, and far more certain in accuracy of conclusion.

If a panel of experts who are not lawyers be used as special masters, some practical problems of procedure may arise; principally in respect to the admissibility of evidence. Several solutions are available. One is the appointment of one lawyer member of the panel. Another is the designation of a lawyer amicus curiae to advise the masters upon strictly legal procedural points. Another is the immediate reference of substantial disputes of this nature to the trial judge. These problems are not regarded as too serious, since exceptions would lie to erroneous rulings by the masters and substantial error in these respects can, therefore, be corrected. 

XI CURRENT INDEX OF THE RECORD 

Confusion and delay are caused in a long trial if no current index of the record is kept. They arise in part when a lawyer or the judge is unable to locate quickly in the record items of evidence, and they also occur when the task of preparing findings is attempted without a ready index. The same lack of an index produces inaccuracy in the findings unless time is taken to prepare an index after the hearing has concluded, a task which may consume much time.

Digests, summary or otherwise, of the evidence, made from time to time, are not recommended.

They are difficult to prepare and too often prove inaccurate.

A topical index of the evidence as it is admitted to the record, kept currently throughout the trial, is recommended.

A method which has proved satisfactory in keep-

ing such an index follows: A person qualified to make such an index (preferably a junior lawyer) was selected in advance of the hearing and a competent secretary employed for him. As the reporter's transcript was received-daily in this instance -the indexer entered as a heading on an index card the name of each witness and then noted as subheadings on the card the topics discussed by the witness and the page numbers of the discussion. 

Meantime the secretary to the indexer, working with another set of cards, entered as a heading on a card each topic entered by the indexer on his witness cards, and as subheads she entered the names of the witnesses and the numbers of the pages at which the topic was discussed by each. 

The same general method was followed with exhibits.

The results of this system were two sets of cards, one headed with the names of all witnesses (and the numbers of all exhibits) and bearing notations of each topic discussed; and the other set having as headings the topics discussed and bearing as notations the names of all witnesses who discussed the topics and the numbers of all exhibits relating thereto, with proper page references. The work being done accurately and currently, the judge and all counsel could refer instantly and accurately to the testimony of any witness on any subject, to all the testimony on any subject, and to all the testimony of any witness. The preparation of findings of fact began immediately upon the conclusion of the taking of testimony, and the findings were premised upon instant reference to all the evidence in the entire record upon each topic. 

The "topics" used in this system of indexing were

indicative words or phrases only; no attempt was made by the indexer to summarize or describe the evidence. The object was to enable the judge or counsel to locate evidence in the record, not to learn from the index what the evidence was. 

It is practicable for the official reporter to supply daily a reproduction of such an index, so that all counsel and the court may have copies.

Details of the above-described method of current indexing can be obtained from the Administrative 

Office of the Courts. 

XII PREPARATION OF FINDINGS 

Considerable delay frequently ensues between the conclusion of a trial and the preparation of findings of facts. The greatest cause of such delay is lack of an adequate index to the record, discussed hereinabove.

In some cases, where the material has been well arranged and well indexed, the judge is able to promulgate complete and final findings of the facts without undue delay. Your Committee believes that this is the best method where it is feasible. 

In other cases it is possible for the judge promptly to make findings of the major and controlling facts and leave the formal all-inclusive findings to a later and different preparation. Where the trial judge is able to make such findings, that procedure would seem to be satisfactory.

Most judges find it helpful to require that proposed findings be filed, either by both parties or by one party with exceptions by the other. Your

Committee suggests that the complaining party be

the initial movant in the latter event, because it is our view that in these cases the finding of the facts should precede the decision. Whenever proposed findings are submitted in this manner, full oral argument on the facts should be had thereafter.

It is not believed that an announcement of the decision in the case, without more, and thereafter a preparation of findings of fact by the successful party is a desirable practice. The facts as found from the evidence of record should be the premise upon which the determination of the issues is reached.

In any event it is suggested that either written briefs on the facts, prepared after the record is closed, or a full oral argument on the facts, are almost indispensable aids to the judge in this part of his task.

XIII MISCELLANEOUS COMMENT Reference to Masters 

Your Committee is of opinion that cases such as those here considered should not be referred in their entireties to masters.

Deposition and Discovery Proceedings 

In a case of the type here under consideration, where pre-trial is a necessary, although preliminary, part of the trial proceeding, deposition or discovery proceedings outside the planned scope of the judge's direction are likely to be at least surplusage if not quite in conflict with the judge's program. Your

Committee is, therefore, of the view that interparty discovery proceedings, under the Rules of Procedure, not arranged for in the judge's pre-trial program, are not helpful in the elimination of unnecessary delay, expense, or volume of record. 

Limitation of Time of Hearing 

Your Committee does not believe that a pre-trial limitation upon the hours or days to be devoted to the hearing or to the presentation of any party is a desirable practice. It may be desirable to limit the further time to be devoted to any phase of the case, after developments in the hearing have indicated the necessity of desirability of such limitation.

Assignment of Entire Case to One Judge 

All preliminary motions should be considered and disposed of by the judge who will hear and determine the cause.

The judge to whom a case such as those considered in this report is assigned, should be relieved of all other duties from the commencement of the trial until his judgment is pronounced. 

XIV CONCLUSIONS 

The conclusions of your Committee are: 

1. That unnecessary delay, volume of record, and expense in judicial proceedings constitute an obstruction to the administration of justice; that such delay, volume and expense occur in a sufficient number of cases to constitute a serious problem.

2. That prevention of these conditions depends upon a fixed determination on the part of judges to prevent them and a firm course of action to that end. 

3. That the most effective means of preventing unnecessary delay, volume of record, and expense are: (a) conferences between judge and counsel prior to trial, such conferences to be of a style and scope to meet the peculiar needs of the particular case; (b) the exclusion from the record of all unnecessary, as well as all irrelevant and incompetent, matter; and (c) a course of procedure which will minimize delay in the accurate disposition of the cause after completion of the trial.

Specific methods meeting with the approval of your Committee for these purposes are described hereinabove.
