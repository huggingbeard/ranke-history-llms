Preface THE FOREIGN SERVICE of the United States is a large body of people almost entirely surrounded by water-sometimes salt, sometimes Scotch and. The Department of State (white formal paper), the International Cooperation Administration (green folding paper), and the United States Information Agency (yellow copy paper) all belong to the foreign service family. 

Through the foreign aid program (ICA), a country may receive outright gifts of money, equipment and advice. Military missions help train local armies. Various agencies work with the people of foreign lands in health, education and general welfare projects. Diplomatic missions employ local citizens who learn our slang while trying to understand us. 

In the Far East, one of the largest investments of American money and manpower has been made in Indochina. Tucked below the sweeping coastline of China, the country was once a leading outpost of French colonialism. It contributed much to the French domestic economy and was an important source of raw materials.

Within the past three years, France has lost Indochina; today all three of the country-states (Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos) are-politically, at least-fully independent. 

France's default had beginnings in the gradual decline of her military power, culminating in the defeat at Dien Bien Phu a kind of French Pearl Harbor in reverse.

France was forced to the wall at Geneva in July of 1954. 

The Geneva Accords divided Vietnam at the 17th Parallel. The northern part was handed over to Communist-led Viet Minh forces while the southern sector was to remain free-a situation somewhat similar to that in Korea.

The United States began to step up aid to Indochina after Geneva. From then on help was concentrated in South Vietnam, most progressive and most heavily populated of the three states. 

With independence from colonialism came internal peace. 

And problems undreamed of by the architects of democracy. 

Such as the four hundred girls of the Hall of Jade. 

The Hall of Jade was a typical pleasure dome in the French sin circuit, complete with terraced courts, Muzak, and a far-ranging selection of companions. The four hundred girls were fullstatus citizens; they lived in no climate of shadowed legality. 

France supported-or was supported by-the infinite varieties of vice in Vietnam. For sin was one commodity free of duty and customs regulation.

But with the new independence and the exodus of the French from Indochina came a change of philosophy. The new leader, a 

Vietnamese, had his own ideas of pleasure. They differed radically from the ideas of his predecessors. 

An official proclamation soon outlawed gambling, opium and prostitution. Open establishments were to be closed. Violations would be followed up and prosecuted. The four hundred were on their own.

Some of Tigers in the House concerns well-meaning members of the American Foreign Service who decided to do something about the dispossessed ones by establishing a special school to teach such things as cooking, flower arrangement, conversation, and sewing. The girls were to learn a new “trade.” They would begin a new way of life. 

The author spent more than two years in Indochina with the United States Information Service, assigned to Saigon, Vietnam. 

In all sincerity, he has not attempted to ridicule, in any way, the Foreign Service of our government or American non-government organizations overseas.

The majority of overseas Americans are doing-and have been doing-an excellent job, working with great dedication and effectiveness. And with far too little thanks.

A Career at Last 

THE NOT-SO-GOOD THINGS about Marge, Harry Starret thought, were her using the word "tinkle” for her urinary compulsions and a glutted tangerine tint for her fingernails. She was foremost in a growing dovetail of hot-chicken-broth-bearing women who click-heeled to his apartment more frequently than his bronchitis allowed. 

Harry Gorham Starret maintained a high degree of mobility: i.e., he was unmarried, tall-hunched, with a low grey-hair count and a rather attractive basal metabolism. Life in Colorado's 

Denver meant cooling wine in the bathtub and sun-licked mornings sitting against park maples with copies of the Saturday Review and Greyhound Gil's Green Sheet.

He was unemployed. 

He was lazy but not offensive about it, dashing to employment offices with a casual furor that alarmed would-be employers. A fistful of months had been thrown to this and that, a looking around, a feeling out, away from the cozy rest of his home-grown home town and the what-are-you-doing-now handshakes.

He had driven away from the Ministry of Neighborliness one spring day in 1954, with a just-like-thatness which would have distressed the home folk had they noted the shift in his eye and the haughty swish of the old Ford, proudly released, at last, from 

Main Street patrol.

The 1937 gas-prone roadster was like her owner-flaking some at the pride lines but withal a creature of moods. If Harry Starret's Ford yearned to nudge an offensive blue Cadillac in the cover of twilight, she did so-not with showy nonchalance but in the quiet pursuit of a quirk. She nudged away and that was that. 

So it was with Harry Starret; he was a friend who knew the

vagrant moods of Joyce Hammerschmidt the Car. And now in the year of 1954, in Denver, Marge Kanner's tinkling and tangerine-ing were changing key. Starret sensed the velvet trap: marriage, and with it, work. 

One of the few factors remaining constant in his life had been work. He did not fear it. Indeed, he had accepted, with good grace, the pursuit of labor by friends and strangers alike. Now and then-perhaps to maintain a balanced objectivity-he undertook positions consistent with his philosophy: that work for work's sake was a kind of spiritual serfdom. 

In his years of casting about in labor's waters, he had tried and found wanting department-store selling, college athletic publicity, highway construction, agricultural journalism and auto-club public relations. 

His girls were medical technicians, secretaries and kindergarten teachers, not models, television assistants or fashion coordinators. If he did not aspire to the Austin-Healey class, it was because he disliked extending himself. He found the proper degree of dedication in the battered-MG and Pontiac group. 

Having reached the age of twenty-nine on his own time, Harry G. Starret kept busy with field trips to the two-dollar window at the Rocky Mountain Kennel Club, teaching Sunday school (it was a temptation to include Elementary Handicapping) to a mixed assortment of nine- and eleven-year-olds, and submitting pieces of fiction to hostile magazine editors. Winnings at the dog track and a part-time ploy as hospital cashier kept him comfortably solvent.

The political situation in Vietnam at the time of Lorna 

Disher's safari was precarious. Private guerrilla armies, operated by strange religious sects, roamed the country, exacting tribute from villagers and living on the land. Two such independent armies virtually held the areas outside Saigon and two or three principal cities. These were the armies of the Cao Cao and the Binh Dao.

Cao Cao's army was the more powerful and was larger, made up of four to five thousand men. Binh Dao's army numbered about three to four thousand at the most.

Both armies had been receiving support from their gambling and vice interests in Saigon and Cholon, the Chinese section of Saigon. With the national government's proclamation outlawing such activities, both armies stepped up operations and made ready to challenge the National Army itself if necessary. 

Cao Cao owned a monstrous temple near Lacgia, forty miles north of Saigon. There, in a building festooned with green and gold plaster snakes, two-headed peacocks and eyes set in triangles, the Pope of Cao Cao lived and held religious audiences with his followers.

It was a period of great activity and importance for the Pope.

Halfway around the world, Linus Rumble was having a wonderful time. Harry Starret, as his unofficial aide, was properly awed, if not a little confused. Here was his chief, whose only previous experience at command had been directions to

Saigon taxi drivers and orders to waiters, now in command of almost six thousand men. The entire force had been drawn up at 

Lacgia. Harry watched the soldiers make ready for the march north and the Rumble War, blinking his eyes in the dancing light; he had an idea it was all a giant dream and would soon go away.

But the lengthy conference that noon was real enough as Rumble explained the battle plan in greater detail and the corps commanders were given their orders. 

The Army was to split into three units, but the units would march northward in parallel columns. The First and Third Corps would halt near Hao Dihn, seventy miles north, there to assume, as Rumble said, "the eventual patterns of thrust.”

First Corps, on the westernmost flank, would remain north of Hao Dihn where the Sesan River narrowed. Second Corps would continue straight north, slipping past the enemy's Bantu Gha headquarters. Continuing on, they were to swing to the northeast, engaging in light raids to draw off some of the Binh 

Dao forces. 

Third Corps would cross the river north of Hao Dihn and travel up the western side to a point where the river again narrowed-approximately fifteen miles north of Bantu Gha. 

On the seventh morning marked from the present day at Lacgia, all forces were to converge on Bantu: First up from Hao 

Dinh, Second southwest from their diversionary raids and Third would cross the Sesan and drive directly south. Thus, with the 

Sesan River on the west, the Binh Dao would be hemmed in from the north, the south and the northeast. To the southeast lay Cam Rao-a vast swamp completely impassable for all practical purposes.

Timing and coordination were all-important. Six full days would allow for unforeseen difficulties and natural hazards. The groups would have time enough to move slowly and carefully. 

The Pope and Rumble stood together on a tower balcony which commanded a wide view of the Cao Cao encampment. "General Rumble, big walk start tomorrow morning."

"Right, Your Rightness. We've got a big job ahead but I think we're gonna come out on top." "It is written." "You said it, Pope." 

Deputy Director Pepper had his orders: Rumble was to be tracked down and brought in. The one question in Pepper's mind was how Washington expected him to organize a rescue party with Rumble chasing all over northern Vietnam. He could be up any one of a million trees, in any one of several thousand villages. Rumble would be lucky if he got back to Saigon on his own and willingly; to try to smoke him out was not only foolish but impossible-typical of Washington's concept of Asia. 

Pepper felt his father-in-law tried to be a good chief. But no man could operate a program from a Stateside desk. 

Pepper decided, therefore, on the easiest course: do nothing with a maximum of effort. Hold staff meetings, call a press conference, step up memo production, keep the lights burning at night. He'd try getting a message through to his field representatives but it was unlikely many were traveling near the border these days. There was no shortwave net up there and no established mail system. Linus dealt himself in and there was no way to call for a new shuffle. And it seemed to be Rumble's deck.

In north central Vietnam, General-elect Rumble carried on with the Third Corps, basing his estimate of the situation on his understanding of the Siege of Vicksburg. What General Amos 

Fletcher might have done in the middle of a tropical jungle could have been quite different from the tactics of Linus Rumble almost a century later. Rumble put his faith in what he deemed an almost parallel situation as to general terrain, disposition of forces and mobility. 

His errors, however, instead of cumulating, cancelled each other out. A mistake of fifteen miles in the distance between two corps marching north was compensated by heavy, unexpected

jungle growth along the route. Half a day's delay in the forward line of travel worked out when Rumble's estimates of distance (he was, unfortunately, in charge of such estimates) proved twenty-five per cent too high. 

Third Corps joined up with First Corps north of Hao Dinh the evening of the fourth day. The men were in good spirits and showed no effects of the almost continual hacking through walls of dense and stubborn jungle.

Linus Rumble was in fair condition. He had fifty-odd gnat bites, seven rain leeches in residence, a fungus condition behind the left ear, one bruised left elbow, prickly heat, superficial facial scratches, a bump on the forehead from a falling mango pod, swollen feet and a mild case of heartburn. Starret was merely bruised, tired and hungry.

As the rearmost element of Third Corps joined the waiting First, Rumble had a few sharp but fleeting doubts as to his ability to continue coping with jungle warfare, but decided survival was mainly mind over matter. Since his mind was in better condition than his matter, he had the advantage. 

First Corps had followed the northward run of the Sesan along the shoreline, experiencing little difficulty. The jungle usually kept its distance along that section; the river people had cleared much of the area. Consequently, First Corps was in better shape and fully rested after their day and a half of waiting. 

Commander of all the Cao Cao Army Phuong was in charge of Second Corps, presumably driving toward the enemy at Bantu Gha. Of all the five thousand, seven hundred men in the big adventure, Linus Rumble was in the worst physical predicament. 

At daybreak the morning of the fifth day, Third Corps moved out toward the river, leaving First Corps in bivouac to prepare for the attack on Bantu Gha.

The river crossing was made without incident. Rumble insisted on having Starret and himself accompany the lead boat across, perhaps remembering the painting of Washington on the

Delaware. He had to be content with sunken logs instead of ice floes and clouds of gnats in place of snowflakes.

After crossing, the troops made for a point about fifteen miles north where they would cross again at night to prepare for the morning drive southward when all Cao Cao forces were to converge on the Binh Dao camp. 

Rumble glanced at the bright new sky. "Well, Amos, looks like this is it. Wish you were here, Fletch.” "Did you say something, Linus?" Starret asked. "Just said, Harry, it looks like payday at the mines." "Well--" "We're gonna see something tomorrow.” "I agree there."

Reports on the Battle of Bantu Gha filtered into Saigon slowly. There was word of two white men, one fat and one thin, leading an army sweeping down from the north against the rebel Binh Dao. The raiders were said to be Cao Cao. Their purpose seemed to be complete extermination of the Binh Dao, leaving themselves exclusive tribute territory in all Vietnam. 

The frightened USCREAM field representative from Bantu Gha said the battle was a fierce one. The talking sticks could be heard all down the valley of the Sesan with "many killed from the mouths of the sticks."

Pepper brought the man directly to his home where he could question him privately. "Who are these white men you say are leading the Cao Cao?" "The leader is the man you know. The father of USCREAM.” "Ong Rumble?" "Yes, sir. The other is his assistant." "You have seen them?" "I have not. But friend has and other friends has. They speak true." "What has happened at your place? Does the Cao Cao win?" "The fighting is very much. Cao Cao push in from everywhere

and surround camp at Bantu Gha. No can get out. Cao Cao very strong. Very. Binh Dao unhappy. Very. Many dead both." "Are the white leaders alive?" "I think is okay. Say how to fight. Friend say Ong Fat One stay in back, look at paper, shout. Very much with paper and book." "Tan, I want you to go back to your post and try to find Ong

Rumble and Ong Starret. Say to them they are wanted in Saigon by big chief in Washington. I will give you a message. Either have friend take it to them or you go. I do not care how. But they are ordered to come back to Saigon. At once. You understand this?" "Yes, Ong Pepper. But if they are busy? If they are leading 

Cao Cao toward the victory?" "They are to come to Saigon." "No care how?" "No care how."

The field representative waited in the hallway while Pepper prepared his message. It was short and to the point: 

RUMBLE AND SANDERS FROM USCREAM PEPPER WASHINGTON SAYS COME HOME. ALL IS FORGIVEN. 

Six days later the travel-stained paper was thrust into the hand of General Rumble. The man who brought it out of the jungle popped back before Rumble could ask any questions. 

Didn't even wait for a tip, Rumble thought.

The campaign had been going well. The noose around Bantu 

Gha was drawing tighter and tighter. It seemed only a matter of time, Rumble felt. The enemy had no mobility, no escape. 

Perhaps, Rumble reflected, even Commander of all Cao Cao 

Armies can handle things by himself now. And you don't fool around with Washington. Pepper had gotten the word. He and 

Starret had best make arrangements to get back. There wasn't much time left on his tour. And he did have a few odds and ends to clean up.

“Kha,” he shouted, "find Ong Starret and bring him to me.” 

They would have to hurry.

Linus Rumble's nomination as Saint-in-Waiting of the Order of Cao Cao was made by the Pope in Lacgia shortly after Rumble's return to Saigon and two days before the Cao Cao Army crushed the Binh Dao at Bantu Gha. The surrender was unconditional; all arms and ammunition of the enemy were confiscated; all Binh Dao tribute territories given to Cao Cao. 

There would be no need to insist on repeal of the national government's proclamation. With all of Vietnam for tribute, who needed the opium, the girls and the gambling in Saigon? Many soldiers of the Binh Dao desired to come over to Cao Cao. They were Vietnamese first and soldiers second and they knew how to sow one field and reap another. All would unite against the common dog of North Vietnam, the Communist Vietminh. Everything would be good.

Rumble was distressed about his School of Jade; the closing was like a personal indictment. He had placed much faith in the idea and in his judgment of raw material for the school. But reports that most of the students seemed gainfully and properly employed made him happy. He visited government offices and saw for himself how his pupils were progressing. As to the pornographic films, he was satisfied to allow Pepper and his aides view them in their own way but on their own time. 

A wise Vietnamese observed: "When at last the temple is pulled down, the man at the rope turns his head to the mountain." 

Less than a month remained for the Rumbles in Saigon. Mrs. 

Rumble busied herself with the thousand details of packing and making travel plans while trying to gather together the threads of her husband's adventure. She had worried little about him in the many weeks of his exodus, knowing Linus was much like 

Mrs. Disher-able to talk with elephants, almost.

Marie Rumble would be happy to return to Washington and reassignment, though she felt a twinge of sadness at losing the

unhurried existence of Vietnam. There was much to learn from these people, the kind and quiet ones who wanted so to understand what had been happening to their world. 

But the service was like that. Your life is divided into twoyear compartments, she felt. Just when you begin to know what it was all about, off you go into the wild new wonder. 

The business about Linus becoming a Saint was, well, interesting, she thought. He was such a Boy Scout. Would they give him a medal, inscribed "Apprentice Saint"? Would he pay dues to the National Sainthood Council and receive the fraternal magazine, “Sainting"? Oh, what a wonderful country!

At USCREAM headquarters, the Director was clearing the desketeria and dictating final memorandums to Miss Estance. 

The Ambassador had been very understanding, Rumble thought. 

He said the National Government was not unhappy with developments in the north. There were good indications that the Cao 

Cao forces soon would cast their lot with the National Army. 

Rumble's replacement would arrive in a day. The Director felt a general information memorandum might make the job of adjustment easier and would give him a chance to point out deficiencies and good points in the general program. “Mr. Rumble, is it really true you're going to be a Saint in the 

Cao Cao religion?" Miss Estance asked. "Seems as though I'm nominated. Like at a national convention, Lavonne." "What happens now? When do you make the grade?” "Well, as I understand it, the Pope has put my name before his Imperial Board. The Board approves and then it goes to the sub-Popes of the Order. If they approve-and nobody but nobody bucks the Pope-the people accept. But if the candidate is unpopular or not too well-known, the Saint-To-Be donation boxes are not filled. The money needed to send the Saint candidate over the two hundred and eleven Islands of Bad Things is not on hand and the poor candidate goes under. The contributions

are used for statues and special tracts written to advertise all the fine things for the candidate." "Mr. Rumble, you're making some of that up!” "No, that's about how it works, Lavonne." "Well, I'll be!" "Likewise, my dear." 

Christopher Arnold Crampett, the new USCREAM director, was to arrive on the same aircraft the Rumbles would take on to Singapore.

The day was bright and clean-skied. A cheering breeze ruffled the cloak-dresses of the Vietnamese women and the blazing whites of the men.

The Pope of Cao Cao, Phuong, Commander of all Cao Cao 

Armies, Wilder's pal, Third General Do, various officials and

Starret were officially present as well as unofficial thousands of the Cao Cao faithful and assorted spectators. In his role as assistant hero, Starret wore a new white suit, white shoes and a blue silk bow tie, a gift from Wilman's current "cook," a lovely Chinese girl whose lone culinary achievement was shark's fin soup.

At noon exactly, the Pope conferred the degree of Saint-To- Be in the Holy Imperial Order of Cao Cao on Linus Theobald Rumble. Harry received a medal and the title of "Excellent Assistant of Saint-To-Be." It was a long and impressive ceremony. 

Mr. and Mrs. Crampett stepped from their plane as the ceremony closed and as the Imperial Cao Cao Marching and Playing Band struck up the "Cao Cao Imperial Song." This was followed by their version of "The Star Spangled Banner."

Mrs. Crampett was impressed. Perhaps, she thought, Charles is going to be more important here than I thought. 

Her husband was studying the bright-eyed airport hostess approaching the ramp. He thought it would be nice to have her as his secretary. Or whatever they call it out here, he mused.

"Hallo, sor! We are veree hoppy to greet you in Vietnam. 

We hoping you shall be veree hoppy." "This is a pleasure. And you speak English! May I present 

Mrs. Crampett? You can call me Charlie.” 

What a doll baby! "Ah, Chollee. My name Kim Son." "I hate to leave, Marie," Rumble said. "Look at 'em. They've been standing out here for us five or six hours." "I know, dear. I'll miss Vietnam." "Well, at least Starret and your old man are big shots in 

Cao Cao. How do you like the idea of having a genuine Saint around the house?" "I'll manage. As long as I don't have to burn incense." "There is one thing. I gotta die before I make the grade. 

May be worth more dead than alive." "Not at all, Linus. You've got to be alive before you're dead.

A lot of people keep one foot in both camps. Not you." "Hail and farewell, hey, Starry?" Orfitz said. "Rumble takes off a Saint. And our new chief seems a ripe choir boy." "Crampett looks startled," Starret said. "He's doing an imitation of you. The day you were dumped off at the house. If you'll excuse the observation of an old Asia hand, you were pretty green, son. But you growed up since, as we say." "Does anybody ever come back to the Far East, Wil? To Indochina?” "Assistant Hero, sir, you take a little and you leave a little.

Not many come back. Time and the little detail of making a living get in between you and the promise." "I know," Starret said. "You don't know yet. Wait'll you get back. Then you begin to realize something." "What's that, Wil?"

"How much of you really stays here."

At the edge of the crowd, Third General Do was speaking to a friend. "General is a great man." "Oh, yes, Third General. Everybody give much honor and praise. Also I hear people of his home village making him most high tail twister of the American Club." "What is this American Club?" "It is by name of The Lions." "The Americans do not twist the tails of Lions. It is only a way to talk of things." "But, Third General, is honor, too. But I know ours is a better honor. To be a Saint of the Cao Cao. He can be of the great. 

Like Ong Thomas Edison or Ong Victor Hugo. Now has to do only one thing." "That is to die." “Yes, he must die. Now is only Waiting-To-Be-Saint.” "When will our friend die?" Third General asked. "Who can tell but the Ultimate One?"

The airplane thundered down the far reach of the runway. 

Then it stepped slowly into the sky. "Yes, truly, who can tell," Third General said.

The plane became smaller and smaller in the blue of the day. 

And then it was gone.

Meet the Author THOMAS B. STREISSGUTH, Assistant for Public Re lations to Senator Edward J. Thye, was a newspaper publisher in his native Minnesota before most kids are delivering papers. At the age of nine he published Tommy's Tatler. 

During the war, he served in the Navy Amphibious Corps, saw action in the Philippines, Ulithi,

Eniwetok and Okinawa. Before assignment as Assistant Publications Officer for the U. S. Information Service in Indochina (1955-57), he was on the staff of the New Ulm Daily Journal in Minnesota, director of the news bureau at Pennsylvania's Geneva College, public relations director for the Tampa Motor Club in Florida, and a literary consultant in Denver.
