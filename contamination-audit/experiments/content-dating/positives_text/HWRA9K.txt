OM MEWHERE in the Bible it tells about somebody who was "born to trouble as the sparks fly upward." Well, I'm beginning to think that that's the way I was born to murderthe last two words being a preposition and a noun, and not an infinitive verb. Althea Raeburn says that it's only what is to be expected of a female who deliberately tempts Fate by writing bloodthirsty detective stories; and perhaps she's right. Anyway, I've decided that the man who said lightning never strikes twice in the same place, simply didn't know what he was talking about. Not only did it do exactly that last summer, when Althea's innocently planned weekend party for our writers' club turned out to be a murder-fest, but it seems now actually to be making a habit of it. 11

It began this time with Helen Blake's invitation to the members of the Quill Club to spend 

Hallowe'en with her. Helen and her husband had been members of our little writers' group in its early days, but Bill's transfer to Philadelphia a year or so before had taken them away from us. 

Since then, most of us had kept in touch with them by intermittent correspondence.

The invitation itself was innocuous enough. 

The glaring red ink-evidently meant to simulate blood-in which it had been written mocked the formality of its phrasing; the effect being wholly characteristic of Helen: "Mrs. William Blake requests the pleasure of Miss

Walk, for the week-half beginning October thirtyfirst. Materializations and other psychic phenomena guaranteed.

R.S.V.P.D.Q."

And you all must have noticed, from association with her, to which of these two classifications Miss Hutton belonged." "That's what you call psychological proof of a crime, isn't it?" Althea inquired as he paused.

He nodded, and lit a fresh cigarette. (Instinct had warned him that it would be flirting with physical violence to smoke a pipe like his in 

Helen Blake's house.) "Yes," he answered as he flicked out the match, "and in my opinion, that's the only sort of

evidence that's really reliable. Circumstantial evidence may be misleading; even direct testimony may be mistaken or deliberately false.

But once all the factors have been taken into careful consideration, psychological evidence never lies. "Of course," he continued, "psychological evidence alone is not enough. It must be combined with proven motive and opportunity. 

And even then these three must be backed up by a certain amount of material evidence, and rightly so; since the average juryman is not a trained psychologist. But in this particular instance, at least one piece of material-or at least circumstantial-evidence was apparent even before the fact of the first murder had been established." "What was that?" I asked curiously. "It was Kate Hutton's lack of an alibi for the night Flagg was killed," he answered. "And I owe that to you, Peter, for you had it carefully written down in that time schedule of yours."

He brought out the sheet of paper containing the roughly penciled notes that I had given him two days before, and consulted them as he talked. "You say here," he went on, "that Miss Hutton left to see Gregory Nolan at about eightthirty, but that she didn't return until nearly

nine-fifty. That meant, according to her own story, that it had taken her almost an hour and twenty minutes to walk the two blocks to Nolan's house, be told by his butler that he had gone out, and to walk back here again. Now it was inconceivable that it could have taken her all that time, even if she had walked on her hands. The question was, what had she been doing in the rest of that interval? "That gave her opportunity; while the fact that she had lied about her movements that night added a suspicious circumstance unfavorable to the supposition of her innocence. However, I didn't give it much thought at the time, for I didn't know then who had been murdered. "But even after I did, I've got to admit that I still didn't consider her as more than a general possibility; because, while she had had the opportunity to commit the crime, she couldn't have disposed of the body in the way that it had been disposed of. That seemed to have been-and was, as we learned afterwards-Nolan's work. 

But I couldn't imagine Nolan and Kate Hutton working together; they were too antagonistic. 

So I shelved the possibility of her guilt temporarily, and began to look in other directions." "And that was when you thought of Carol 

Blandon?" I suggested.

If she were "Yes," he admitted, "it was. aware that her husband, John Blandon, had been actually Winston Flagg, or if she knew about that third will naming her as Flagg's heir, it would give her a motive for his murder. But she proved that she did not know when she consented so readily to my suggestion that she give up her court battle for the estate. People don't throw in their hand when they have inside knowledge that the cards are stacked in their favor. "That brought me around to Gregory Nolan. 

I didn't think he was the murderer; but I was convinced that he knew a great deal more than he had been telling. So I decided to see what reaction we could get from him when he learned that Mrs. Blandon was withdrawing her claim to the estate. Besides, it had already been arranged that Sergeant Boone should arrest him on suspicion, in the hope that that might induce him to talk."

He stopped to knock the ashes from his cigarette; then he continued: "You all know how those two devices worked out. But even before he came here I had begun to swing back to my original idea of Kate Hutton as the killer. Then the second murder threatened to throw me off again; for while I

could think of plenty of motives for her having killed her ex-husband, I couldn't think of any logical reason why she should have shot Nolan. 

Then you came forward with that business about the will, Peter, and the whole thing came to me in a flash.' "I seem to have the knack of solving mysteries without realizing it," I remarked. "I can't decide whether it's a sign of intelligence, or the opposite." 

He chuckled. "You realized what you were doing all right when you worked out that bit about the readingglass's having been used to touch off the shell in the pistol," he pointed out. "And without that, Kate Hutton would have gone scot free; for unless we were able to prove how the murder of Nolan had been committed, she could have argued lack of opportunity, and gotten away with it. So you see, it was pretty much your case from beginning to end."

There was a brief interval of silence when he had finished speaking; then Bill asked, "And now that it's all over, who does get the Flagg estate; Kate Hutton or Carol Blandon?" "Mrs. Blandon will receive it all," Trelawney told him. "That last will is perfectly valid in spite of its date; and now that we know for cer-

tain that Flagg is actually dead, it can be entered for probate whenever Templeton sees fit. But even if it weren't, she could still inherit as 

Flagg's second wife. Kate Hutton, on the other hand, forfeits any rights she may originally have had; since the law does not permit a known criminal to profit by his crime. Rather ironic, isn't it?" 

We all agreed that it was.

Suddenly Althea gave a little shudder. "You know," she said in that awed tone of voice she always uses when speaking about what she considers the supernatural, "I've just thought of something: You remember, Pete, how

Maude Tuttle told Kate Hutton's fortune that first night we were here, and how she told us afterwards that she had seen death in the cards? 

We both took for granted that it meant death for Kate Hutton; but it didn't. It-it was just the other way 'round!" "That reminds me!" Helen exclaimed. She sprang up abruptly and dashed from the room, returning a few seconds later with a letter in her hand. "This came late this afternoon," she began, drawing the enclosure from its envelope. "It's from Maude Tuttle. Listen to what she says.' 

She glanced hastily through the first part of

the letter until she came to the paragraph she was looking for; then she read aloud: "I have just heard on the news broadcast over the radio about the terrible things that have been happening around Ghosts Walk, and I feel that there is something I ought to tell you. It's all just a lot of superstitious nonsense, I know; but when I told that Miss Hutton's fortune a few nights ago, I saw in the cards that she was destined to become a murderess. No, it doesn't prove anything, I realize; but I can't get it out of my head, and keep feeling that you ought to know, anyway. Now laugh at me, all of you, if you want to; and you probably will.'"

But somehow, as Helen refolded the letter and put it back into its envelope, none of us felt particularly like laughing. "Well," Bill observed with what was evidently intended as an attempt to pass the situation off lightly, "there's a new type of evidence for you, Mr. Trelawney; not psychological, but just plain psychic." 

Trelawney smiled faintly. "So it would seem," he remarked. Then he turned to me. "By the way, Peter," he began, "it isn't vitally important any more, now that Kate Hutton has made a full confession, but where did you hide

that reading-glass? You never did get around to telling me this afternoon." "Oh, Heavens!" I exclaimed. "I'd forgotten all about the reading-glass." And then I giggled like an idiot. "What's so funny?" Althea demanded. "Pete, what did you do with it?" "I hid it," I answered, "in the one place where 

I felt sure she'd never think to look for it—in her own room. It's in the middle bureau drawer, wrapped up nice and snug in her best nightie."
