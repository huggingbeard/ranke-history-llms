A Plea for a Redistribution of the Tax Bur den in Georgia

By EDGAR H. JOHNSON P. O. BOX 396 EMORY UNIVERSITY, GA.

THO HOSE who have had experience with plowing mules know that sometimes a sore or galled spot will be made on that part of the shoulder where the pressure is unduly heavy. If the load needs to be lessened the plowman will adjust the harness or the plowstock so that the point of the plow will not go so deep into the ground. Perhaps the trouble is not due to a too heavy pull but to the fact that too much of the pressure bears on certain points of the mule's shoulder. 

In that case the plowman will try to adjust the harness so as to shift the pressure. 

It is mainly by taxation that the community carries the financial burden of government. That the harness of taxation is working certain sore spots on the body politic is quite clear. State and local taxation in Georgia is now bearing with special severity and hardship on the owners of farms, of residences, of automobiles, and of railroads.

That public expenditures, and consequently taxes also, have increased everywhere in recent years is generally known. In 1915 the per capita of revenue receipts for the state of Georgia was $2.30 while in 1930 the per capita of revenue receipts was $12.25. 

The amount for 1930 is more than five times as large as that of only fifteen years before.

There are thousands of farms in Georgia which do not yield a net rental large enough to pay taxes.¹ 

Much land lies idle. Should a farm house be destroyed by fire the landlord would often be unable or unwilling to rebuild. Taxes on dwellings are very high in the towns and cities. Many an owner of town property is unable from his rent to pay the interest on the mortgage, repairs, insurance and taxes. The landlord may in many cases truthfully declare that while he is serving the state and the tenant he is not serving himself. As to the taxes borne by auto owners, Professor M. H. Bryan of the University of Georgia says that "only those states which are rushing their road programs by bond issue are ahead of Georgia in expenditures per motor vehicle." In 1928 the average expenditure per motor car throughout the United States was $27.00, but in Georgia the average was $40.00. Florida is the only state in the union which has a higher tax than Georgia on the sale of gasoline.

In view of the heavy tax burdens, a decline in the price level and a decreasing ability on the part of the tax-payers to contribute toward the government, the demand for a lessening of governmental expenditures is proper and inevitable. It is well that commissions and individuals should study how to secure economy and increased efficiency in government.

If such an instance was quite rare it might show only the incompetence or the hard luck of this particular landlord. 

The truth is that many thousands of farmers fared no better or even worse. How does such taxation agree with the motto on Georgia's seal-Wisdom, Justice, Moderation? 2

However, while expenditure and taxation are closely related they are really distinct subjects. For example, the city of Atlanta recently effected savings in its electric light bill, but this saving was effected by electrical engineers and not by students of taxation. 

This little pamphlet treats of taxation and of the redistribution of the tax burden; it does not discuss the problem of reducing expendiure.2

Whatever reductions may be found necessary, it is to be hoped that there will be no decline in the efficiency of our school system. Wise parents who find themselves in financial straits will make strenuous endeavor to keep their children in school. Should not the legislature, which has in its keeping our system of public education, maintain the same attitude and see that there is no curtailment of the educational opportunities of our children? The real wealth of any state consists in the character, the industry, the abilities of its citizens more than in its natural resources. Difficult changes in the state's agriculture and industry probably lie ahead of us. We need an educated and a trained people to solve these problems. Massachusetts is a state whose natural resources are in some respects not so good as our own, and yet its per capita wealth is more than double that of Georgia. (Massachusetts, $3,144; Georgia, $1,377.) The economic position of this New England state cannot be explained without giving much credit to its system of public education and its institutions of higher learning.

The county official expressed sympathy for the tax payer but went on to explain: "You know we issued serial bonds several years ago to build a highway through the county. We have interest payments to meet and every year some of the bonds fall due and must be paid. You approved of the consolidated school in your district, but this required a bond issue also, and every year there must be a special tax to maintain the school. Yet it is clear that it will be very hurtful if the present high taxes on property are continued. What can be done about it? The writer believes that something can be done. He believes that the state should relieve the counties and town in good part, but not wholly, of the expense of maintaining the public schools. It seems reasonable that later on the state should assume a larger part of the expense of maintaining the county roads. If this were done the county expenses would be much reduced and the taxes on property correspondingly lowered. The problem is for the state to find the additional revenue and so make it possible to relieve property of the heavy taxes it now bears.

There is another pressing financial problem that should be considered at the same time. There remains still unpaid more than six million dollars of appropriation made by the legislature before the administration of the present Governor. In anticipation of the payment of those appropriations loans were made by colleges, county boards of education and other institutions, and these loans remain largely unpaid. The legislature which met in the summer of 

1931 authorized the sale of rent-notes of the State's railroad (the W. & A.), and this brought in nearly two million dollars. The legislature did not adequately provide for these past due appropriations. It even allowed the sales tax to lapse. This tax brought in almost one million dollars ($952,195) during 1931. 

The machinery for its collection was already in operation. The sales tax was a light one and it seems strange indeed that such a source of revenue should have been given up when the state was in such pressing need of money. The legislature did not regard that part of the State Democratic platform which declared in October, 1930, that the deficit of past due appropriations be paid as quickly as possible.

One method of solving the state's financial problem which some advocate is to maintain the present high taxes on the automobile licenses and on the sale of gasoline, to lessen considerably the expenditures for the building and maintenance of roads, and to use much of the revenue from the users of automobiles to meet the general expenses of the state. The deficit in appropriations could be met in this way, and if such a policy were continued it would permit a reduction in the taxes on property. But is it right and desirable that so large a part of the burden of supporting the state and local government should fall on the users of motor cars? The gas tax is not a luxury tax and it certainly is not paid in proportion to the ability of the tax payer. 

The washer-woman often uses an old car in carrying the clothes that she launders; the peddler and the grower of garden produce makes much use of motor cars and contributes heavily to the gas tax. The country doctor will sometimes go a hundred miles a day in visiting the sick and get only small compensation for his service while there are physicians with large incomes in the cities who do little practice outside the hospital and their offices, and who pay less gas tax than poorer country doctors. Those who find a solution of the state's financial problem in the gas tax are endeavoring to tax heavily what has become a necessity to many a poor man and to omit sales taxes on non-essentials and on luxuries. 

The platform of the State Democratic convention of 1930 contains this statement: "We oppose the diversion of any of the funds allocated to this department [the highway] to any other purpose and favor the exclusive use of the same in constructing and maintaining our state highway system.”

Cities are permitted to levy special assessments against real estate in order to meet all or part of the expenses of making roads and sidewalks which abut on the property thus assessed. The justification for this special assessment lies in the increased convenience and value which the improved sidewalks and roads bring to the real estate against which the levies are made. In essence the high license and gasoline taxes which the autoist pays are the same as the special assessments for road improvements. The city lot is made more valuable by paved streets and sidewalks in its vicinity. The convenience and utility for an automobile are increased by the improvement of roads which are miles away from the home of its owner.

The proposal to treat all the revenue of the state as a homogeneous fund which may be appropriated to the various enterprises of the state as the legislature and the governor may estimate their needs is as unfair as it would be to levy special assessments against the real estate owners of a certain street and then use the proceeds to maintain the city hospital. 

There is only one good reason for levying these high special taxes on automobile users, and that is that the revenue be spent in a way that will be of special benefit to them. It would be all right to put all the tax revenue into a common fund if taxes were levied on luxuries and non-essentials or in such a way as to lay an approximately equal burden on all citizens, but there can be no pretense of such equality when the sales tax on gasoline is as high as thirty per cent. Yet there are citizens of Georgia who favor putting this thirty per cent sales tax on gasoline into the general fund and who oppose any increase of sales taxes on luxuries and non-essentials. 

There are two main reasons for the movement to use for the general expenses of the state the income from the very high sales tax on gasoline. The first lies in the unpaid appropriations to such state 5

functions as education and the care of the insane. 

Why should the state build new roads, it is asked, when such past due appropriations are still unpaid? 

The other reason proceeds from the fact that many citizens believe or suspect that the large funds turned over to the highway department are not efficiently administered, and that the department is or has been "in politics." If these beliefs and suspicions are justified the proper course to pursue is to seek a change in the personnel of the highway department and not to saddle such a large part of the burden of general state expenses on the buyers of gasoline. 

If Georgia should put this revenue into the general treasury without allocation it would pursue a course not followed by a single one of the forty-eight states of the Union. Our state now allocates two-thirds of the gasoline revenue to the highway board, one-sixth to the county roads and one-sixth to the public schools. Thirty-four states of the Union allocate a larger proportion than two-thirds of the gasoline revenue to the highway boards; five other states have the same proportion as Georgia. These are the figures for 1930.

The writer believes that the state needs to increase its revenue in order, first, that it may increase now its appropriation to schools and later to county roads, thus allowing the local governments to reduce taxes on property; and second, in order that the state may pay off the unpaid appropriations of previous legislatures. The additional revenue should be found, if possible, in such a way that the necessary cost of living for the average citizen will be increased but little, if at all. In order to attain this the writer suggests the following: 

1. The income tax should be amended so as to reduce exemptions and to decrease the size of the brackets. At present a married man with no children is allowed an exemption of $3,500. In South 

Carolina the exemption would be $2,500, in North

Carolina and Virginia only $2,000. Under the new federal income tax the exemption is $2,500. While 

Georgia has a smaller per capita wealth than any of the three states named, it yet allows a higher exemption than any of them. A married man in

Georgia who has an income of $3,500 and no children dependent on him is clearly able to pay an income tax and should be made to do so. Georgia begins with a 1% rate, and for the first $5,000 of income above exemption does not raise the rate. North

Carolina begins with a rate of 2% and raises it to 3% after the first $2,000 of income above exemption. 

A married man with no dependent children and with no exemptions due to taxes, contributions, etc., would, if he had a net income of $4,000, pay in Georgia an income tax of $5.00. In South Carolina the tax would be $22.50; in North Carolina $40.00; and in Virginia $30.00. There is probably no one tax which is fairer than the income tax and Georgia should secure more revenue from this source.

Also, the administration of our state income tax should be improved. With the same exemptions as that of the Federal Income tax, Georgia in 1929 had 12.827 individual taxable returns while the federal government had 18,068 such returns. In the same year Georgia had 7,773 individual non-taxable returns while the federal government had 14,221.

2. Under our present estate tax Georgia allows an exemption of $100,000 on all estates. An estate of that amount thus pays no estate tax, although the per capita wealth of Georgia according to the estimate of the National Industrial Conference Board for 1930 was only $1,377 as compared with a national average of $2,677. It seems very clear that this exemption should be lowered and that Georgia should secure a greater revenue from the transfer of estates and inheritances. The exemption under the federal estate tax is now reduced to $50,000, yet the legislature of Georgia in 1931 continued its $100,000 exemption in the face of unpaid past due appropriations that ran into the millions. 

3. South Carolina has a tax of one cent on each five cent retail price of bottled drinks and of 76 cents per gallon on syrup used for fountain drinks. Its average income from this source during the past five years has been $1,099,000. If an estimate be made on the basis of comparative populations the income in Georgia would have been 1.8 million dollars per year. If Georgia had had this tax during the past four years it would have brought in more than enough money to wipe out our present state deficit. If the Georgia legislature were willing to adopt this law which works so well in South Carolina it would go far toward solving the state's financial problems. It would be a heavy tax but not as heavy as the present tax on gasoline, or as heavy in its burden as the property tax is on the owner of farms. The soft drinks are non-essentials. The tax should affect the laborers and farmers very little. 

4. South Carolina has a tax of one cent for each ten cents charged for admission to any place of amusement. The average income from this tax for the past five years is $276,000. In Georgia the average annual yield would have been a little more than $450,000. This is another tax on a non-essential. 

5. Last year there were 274,576 passenger cars which were registered and 45,736 trucks. The lowest fee for an automobile is $11.25. Thousands of cars in 

Georgia have a value of not more than $25.00. There are many thousasds whose value does not exceed $50.00. The minimum license fee is a very high charge for the privilege of operating a car ever so little. A farm tenant told me last year that he had not been able to use his car at all. If there had been no charge for the license he would have used the car but little.

If a uniform reduction of $10.25 were made from the present fees for autos and trucks the tag for the lightest weight car would be brought to $1.00.

This would cause a reduction of about $3,280,000 in the highways funds. It would grant that much reduction in the taxes now paid by the owners of cars, and in this time of depression some reduction is in order. It would still leave more than a million dollars per month for the highway board to use in constructing and maintaining roads, if one takes account of the federal grants toward highway construction.

The heavier cars do more hurt to the roads than the light cars and it is fair that their license tags

should cost more. Under the plan of a uniform reduction the difference in the cost of the license tags would remain just what they are now. 

6. Georgia levies a tax of 12% on life insurance premiums. Only seven other states (not counting 

Connecticut whose law is on a retaliatory basis) have a rate so low as this. The most common tax on premiums is 2%. Georgians of course pay the same insurance premiums as those of the same age in other states. It seems wise to increase the state tax from 12% to 2% on all insurance premiums. A number of states exceed this rate. Georgia's present tax on insurance premiums (life, fire, casualty, etc.) brings in a little more thas $900,000. A change from 

12% to 2% in the tax rate would increase this revenue by $300,000.

One difficulty in carrying out the above suggestion lies in the fact that there are a few cities in Georgia which have the privilege of levying a percentage tax on premiums. The tax in Columbus is larger than that levied by the state. If the suggested increase in the state tax life insurance premiums should be made, all cities should be prohibited from levying such a tax, and they should be limited in the tax to be levied on agencies and agents. The representatives coming from cities that enjoy this special privilege will probably oppose a measure that would reduce their revenue. On the other hand the same premiums on life insurance policies are charged throughout the state, and one city should have the same right to tax them as another. 

7. We should in accordance with act 8, section 3, of the State Constitution distinctly allocate the proceeds of the poll tax to the common schools. It would be well to provide that the taxes collected in each county should go to the common schools of that county. Locally elected officials will be more diligent in the collection of such a tax if the proceeds of the tax are locally expended. Such an allocation would probably result in the increase of revenue from the poll tax and of the number of citizens who vote. Both results are to be desired. 

8. The uniform tax on all kinds of property is unfair in theory and it has never proved capable of a fair administration. We should permit the use of different rates of taxation for different classes of property. The constitutional amendment to be voted on in November is a step in the right direction and it should be adopted. It would permit a low rate on intangible property. Later amendments should provide for other classes of property, e. g., forest lands. 

9. One of the amendments to be voted on in November provides for the repayment to the counties of about $25,000,000 spent for the building of roads. 

The money is to come from funds allocated to the Highway Department and the payments are to begin in 1936. The proposed amendment should be approved. It will permit the lessening of the local tax on property.

The writer believes that the state needs to increase its revenue in order, first, that it may increase now its appropriation to schools and later to county roads, thus allowing the local governments to reduce taxes on property; and second, in order that the state may pay off the unpaid appropriations of previous legislatures. The additional revenue should be found, if possible, in such a way that the necessary cost of living for the average citizen will be increased but little, if at all. In order to attain this the writer suggests the following: 

1. The income tax should be amended so as to reduce exemptions and to decrease the size of the brackets. At present a married man with no children is allowed an exemption of $3,500. In South 

Carolina the exemption would be $2,500, in North

Carolina and Virginia only $2,000. Under the new federal income tax the exemption is $2,500. While 

Georgia has a smaller per capita wealth than any of the three states named, it yet allows a higher exemption than any of them. A married man in

Georgia who has an income of $3,500 and no children dependent on him is clearly able to pay an income tax and should be made to do so. Georgia begins with a 1% rate, and for the first $5,000 of income above exemption does not raise the rate. North

Carolina begins with a rate of 2% and raises it to 3% after the first $2,000 of income above exemption. 

A married man with no dependent children and with no exemptions due to taxes, contributions, etc., would, if he had a net income of $4,000, pay in Georgia an income tax of $5.00. In South Carolina the tax would be $22.50; in North Carolina $40.00; and in Virginia $30.00. There is probably no one tax which is fairer than the income tax and Georgia should secure more revenue from this source.

Also, the administration of our state income tax should be improved. With the same exemptions as that of the Federal Income tax, Georgia in 1929 had 12,827 individual taxable returns while the federal government had 18,068 such returns. In the same year Georgia had 7,773 individual non-taxable returns while the federal government had 14,221.

2. Under our present estate tax Georgia allows an exemption of $100,000 on all estates. An estate of that amount thus pays no estate tax, although the per capita wealth of Georgia according to the estimate of the National Industrial Conference Board for 1930 was only $1,377 as compared with a national average of $2,677. It seems very clear that this exemption should be lowered and that Georgia should secure a greater revenue from the transfer of estates and inheritances. The exemption under the federal estate tax is now reduced to $50,000, yet the legislature of Georgia in 1931 continued its $100,000 exemption in the face of unpaid past due appropriations that ran into the millions. 

3. South Carolina has a tax of one cent on each five cent retail price of bottled drinks and of 76 cents per gallon on syrup used for fountain drinks. Its average income from this source during the past five years has been $1,099,000. If an estimate be made on the basis of comparative populations the income in Georgia would have been 1.8 million dollars per year. If Georgia had had this tax during the past four years it would have brought in more than enough money to wipe out our present state deficit. If the Georgia legislature were willing to adopt this law which works so well in South Carolina it would go far toward solving the state's financial problems. It would be a heavy tax but not as heavy as the present tax on gasoline, or as heavy in its burden as the property tax is on the owner of farms. The soft drinks are non-essentials. The tax should affect the laborers and farmers very little. 

4. South Carolina has a tax of one cent for each ten cents charged for admission to any place of amusement. The average income from this tax for the past five years is $276,000. In Georgia the average annual yield would have been a little more than $450,000. This is another tax on a non-essential. 

5. Last year there were 274,576 passenger cars which were registered and 45,736 trucks. The lowest fee for an automobile is $11.25. Thousands of cars in 

Georgia have a value of not more than $25.00. There are many thousasds whose value does not exceed $50.00. The minimum license fee is a very high charge for the privilege of operating a car ever so little. A farm tenant told me last year that he had not been able to use his car at all. If there had been no charge for the license he would have used the car but little.

If a uniform reduction of $10.25 were made from the present fees for autos and trucks the tag for the lightest weight car would be brought to $1.00.

This would cause a reduction of about $3,280,000 in the highways funds. It would grant that much reduction in the taxes now paid by the owners of cars, and in this time of depression some reduction is in order. It would still leave more than a million dollars per month for the highway board to use in constructing and maintaining roads, if one takes account of the federal grants toward highway construction.

The heavier cars do more hurt to the roads than the light cars and it is fair that their license tags

should cost more. Under the plan of a uniform reduction the difference in the cost of the license tags would remain just what they are now. 

6. Georgia levies a tax of 12% on life insurance premiums. Only seven other states (not counting 

Connecticut whose law is on a retaliatory basis) have a rate so low as this. The most common tax on premiums is 2%. Georgians of course pay the same insurance premiums as those of the same age in other states. It seems wise to increase the state tax from 12% to 2% on all insurance premiums. A number of states exceed this rate. Georgia's present tax on insurance premiums (life, fire, casualty, etc.) brings in a little more thas $900,000. A change from 

12% to 2% in the tax rate would increase this revenue by $300,000.

One difficulty in carrying out the above suggestion lies in the fact that there are a few cities in Georgia which have the privilege of levying a percentage tax on premiums. The tax in Columbus is larger than that levied by the state. If the suggested increase in the state tax life insurance premiums should be made, all cities should be prohibited from levying such a tax, and they should be limited in the tax to be levied on agencies and agents. The representatives coming from cities that enjoy this special privilege will probably oppose a measure that would reduce their revenue. On the other hand the same premiums on life insurance policies are charged throughout the state, and one city should have the same right to tax them as another. 

7. We should in accordance with act 8, section 3, of the State Constitution distinctly allocate the proceeds of the poll tax to the common schools. It would be well to provide that the taxes collected in each county should go to the common schools of that county. Locally elected officials will be more diligent in the collection of such a tax if the proceeds of the tax are locally expended. Such an allocation would probably result in the increase of revenue from the poll tax and of the number of citizens who vote. Both results are to be desired. 

8. The uniform tax on all kinds of property is unfair in theory and it has never proved capable of a fair administration. We should permit the use of different rates of taxation for different classes of property. The constitutional amendment to be voted on in November is a step in the right direction and it should be adopted. It would permit a low rate on intangible property. Later amendments should provide for other classes of property, e. g., forest lands. 

9. One of the amendments to be voted on in November provides for the repayment to the counties of about $25,000,000 spent for the building of roads. 

The money is to come from funds allocated to the Highway Department and the payments are to begin in 1936. The proposed amendment should be approved. It will permit the lessening of the local tax on property. 

September 8, 1932.
