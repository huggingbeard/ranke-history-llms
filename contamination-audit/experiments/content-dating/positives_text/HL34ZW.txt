The samples for Durham and other cities in the population class of 50,000 to 250,000 were small, and the averages for some classes in the accompanying tables are based on reports from very few families. 

The number of families in each class is shown in every table so that users can recognize the limitations of the averages in each case. This presentation of complete detail allows the analyse to regroup the data for his particular needs without loss of information and thus make maximum use of the data.

Families in Durham spent an average of $4,373 for current living expenses 1/ in 1961. In addition, they made gifts and contributions averaging $197, put $271 into various types of life insurance and retirement funds, and showed savings of $175, on the average, through a net increase in assets over liabilities. After payment of $687 in income and other personal taxes, they had an average money income of $4,982. (See table on p. 2.) These are the initial findings of the Survey of Consumer Expenditures conducted by the U.S. Department of Labor's Bureau of Labor Statistics among families and single consumers living in the Durham metropolitan area. 2/ 

About one-half of the average family's current consumption expenditures in 1961 went for the three basic categories of living expenses: food and beverages, 23.8 percent; shelter (including fuel, light, etc.), 18.4 percent; and clothing, 10.0 percent. The purchase and operation of automobiles, owned by 3 out of 5 families in Durham at the end of 1961, accounted for another 13.2 percent. Of the 7.9 percent of the family expenses applied to medical care, about one-fifth was for payment of premiums for hospitalization, surgical, and other forms of health insurance or health service plans, under which 3 out of 4 families were covered. 

Recreation represented only 3.7 percent of current consumption expenditures in 1961--a smaller share than might be expected. Recreation, as defined in this survey, does not include some expenditures customarily regarded as recreational. The survey definition, in fact, follows the practice of most families, who tend to include vacation and other recreational travel expenses in their accounts for automobile operation, other transportation, food, and lodging away from home. 

The remaining important categories of current living expenses accounted for the following shares: house furnishings and equipment, 5.0 percent; household operations, 6.7 percent; personal care, 3.2 percent; tobacco, 1.6 percent; and reading and education, 1.4 percent.

The total classified as going into personal insurance in 1961 was about equally divided between various types of life insurance and payments into social security and other public and private retirement funds.

Variation in Expenditures by Selected Family Characteristics 

The relative importance of food and shelter in family spending customarily declines as income rises and, in general, this was the pattern in Durham during 1961. However, the relationship did not follow a regular progression from lowest to highest income, probably because of the small number of families in some income classes (table 1). If the Durham families are combined into broader income classes, the expected relationship is more apparent. For example, among families with incomes below $3,000, food averaged 33 percent of total consumption expenditures; in the $3,000 to $7,500 range, 24 percent; and in the class $7,500 and over, 18 percent. The proportion spent for shelter, fuel, light, etc. declined similarly. Thus for food and shelter combined, the percentages averaged about 61, 42, and 33, respectively, for the three broad income classes. 

Although surveys have shown a decrease in the relative importance of clothing expenditures as incomes have risen over the past half century, they usually have shown that this category commands an increasing proportion of family spending at successively higher income levels in any given year. 

In Durham, clothing averaged about 7 percent of total expenditures for families with incomes below $3,000 and between 10 and 11 percent for families above that income level. Families in the lowest of the three broad income groups therefore devoted about two-thirds of their expenditures for current consumption to food, shelter, and clothing, while for the highest income group, about two-fifths went for these three basic categories of consumption spending. 

The relative importance of automobile purchase and operation in these three broad income groups increased from 3 percent at the lower end of the income scale to 13 percent in the middle cange, and up to 16 percent for families with incomes of $7,500 or more. 

Position in the income distribution is related to a variety of family characteristics. 

About one-third of the families in Durham had incomes below $3,000 in 1961. As compared with families in the higher income classes, these families had fewer members and fewer full-time earners, their heads were older and had less education, and relatively more of them were nonwhite. 

About 15 percent of the families in the Durham sample were single consumers (i.e., oneperson families) who averaged 57 years of age (table 2). As a group, they were older than the heads of families of 2 or more persons, and their expenditures for current consumption averaged only $2,727, compared with an average of $4,650 for the larger families. The single consumers spent proportionately more for food away from home, shelter, and transportation other than automobile, than the larger families. Iwo-person families, who constituted about one-third of the sample, spent an average of $3,993 for current consumption. 

The remaining 52 percent of the Durham families consisted of three or more persons, and their average current living expenses were approximately $5,100 in 1961. The majority of these families were car owners, lived in rented housing, and had more full-time earners and children under 18 years of age than the smaller families.

The heads of the larger families with children of school age or younger were generally under 55 years of age (table 3). Income after taxes was highest for families whose heads were 75 years or over; however, the highest average expenditures were reported by the 25-34 age group which ranked second in income after taxes. 

Families in Durham whose heads were self-employed had higher average income and expenditures in 1961 than those in other occupations (table 4). Averages for this group must be used with caution, however; it was comparatively small (5.2 percent of the families in the sample), and the self-employed family heads may differ widely in training and business or professional experience. Ranking next in income and expenditures were families headed by salaried workers in professional or managerial occupations. This group comprised 10 percent of the Durham families, and their heads were among the youngest and most highly educated of all occupational groups. Almost one-fourth of the heads of all 

Durham families were either retired or had not worked in 1961 for some other reason. The latter group might include, for example, a family headed by a widow receiving survivor's benefits and remaining at home, or a family whose head was ill and unable to work. 

About 56 percent of the Durham families were renters all year, while 43 percent were homeowners (table 5). The remainder of the sample rented part of the year and lived the rest of the year in a home which they owned. The all-year renters spent $746, or 19.9 percent of their current expenditures on shelter, fuel, light, etc. The comparable outlay for homeowners was $888, but this represented only 16.9 percent of their total consumption expenditures. 1.047444

Definitions 

Family. The family, or consumer unit, refers (1) to a group of people usually living together who pooled their income and drew from a common fund for their major items of expense, or (2) to a person living alone or in a household with others but who was financially independent, i.e., his income and expenditures were not pooled. Never-married children, living with parents were always considered as members of the consumer unit. Information was recorded for the family as it existed in the survey year.

Family Size. 

The number of equivalent, full-year members, based on the total number of weeks during which both full-year and part-year members belonged to the family in the survey year, divided by 52 weeks.

Family Head. 

In husband-wife families, the husband was considered the head. In other types of families, the person recognized as the head by other family members was so designated. 

Education of Head. Years of school completed during or before the survey year, in elementary or high school, college, university, or professional school. Persons not reporting the number of years completed were included in the class, "8 years or less.' "1 Full-time Earners. A count of family members who were employed 48 weeks or more in the survey year, and for 35 hours or more per week in wage and salary occupations. The minimum hours requirement did not apply to self-employed workers in an unincorporated business or profession. 

Occupation of Family Head. The major occupation at which employed for the largest number of weeks in the survey year.

The classification was made in accordance with the 1960 Census of Population, Alphabetical Index of Occupations and Industries, except that the self-employed (including businessmen, professionals, and artisans) were separated from salaried managers, officials, and professional workers. Members of the Armed Forces, living off base, and therefore eligible for the survey, were classified separately, but the small number of farmers and farm workers living in urban places were not classified separately. The retired included heads who were wholly retired and some with nominal earnings which were less than their retirement income. 

Tenure. The tenure of the family at its principal place of residence during the entire survey year. Families who were owners part of the year and renters part of the year were classified as "other. Owner-occupants of cooperative apartments were classified as owners. Families who "1 received rent free were included with renters.

The value of two nonmoney items--food and housing received as pay--were counted as money income and as expenditures. 

Money Income after Taxes. Money income after deduction of personal taxes (Federal, State, and local income taxes, poll taxes, and personal property taxes). 

Other Money Receipts. Inheritances and occasional large gifts of money less taxes, legal fees, and other expenses required to obtain such receipts; and net receipts from the lump-sum settlement of fire and accident insurance policies. Gifts and inheritances in the form of real estate, securities, and other property were not included unless they had been sold during the survey year. 

Increases in Assets. Increases in cash holdirgs, business investments, and money owed to the family; purchase and improvement of dwellings and other real property; purchases of stocks and bonds; and increases in other assets. 

Decreases in Assets. Decreases in cash holdings, business investments, and money owed to the family; sale of owner-occupied dwellings, other real property, and personal property; sale or retirement of stocks and bonds; settlement or surrender of personal insurance policies; and decreases in other assets.

Decreases and Increases in Liabilities. Changes in the mortgage debt on owner-occupied dwellings and other real property; money owed to banks, insurance companies, etc.; money owed for rent, taxes, automobiles, housefurnishings and equipment, and other goods and services; and changes in other liabilities.

during the year.

Account Balancing Difference. The difference between reported total receipts and reported total disbursements. Total receipts consist of income after taxes, other money receipts, and moneys or credit received from decreasing assets or increasing liabilities. Total disbursements consist of expenditures for current consumption, personal insurance, gifts and contributions, and outlays of money that resulted in increasing assets or decreasing liabilities. 

Gifts and Contributions. Cash contributions to persons outside the family and to welfare, religious, educational, and other organizations; and the cost of goods and services purchased in the survey year and given to persons outside the family. 

Personal Insurance. Payments or deductions from pay for life, endowment, and annuity insurance; fraternal, union, and other mutual aid insurance; Social Security; and railroad, government, and other retirement plans. Employer's contributions are not included. 

Expenditures for Current Consumption (or Current Living Expenses). The cost of goods and services for family living (including financing charges and sales and excise taxes) bought during the survey year, whether or not payments were completed during the year. Purpose and Scope of the Survey 

The information in this report was collected by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) part of its nationwide Survey of Consumer Expenditures (CES) in urban places. The primary objective of this multipurpose survey was to obtain detailed expenditure data for use in revising the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The present tabulations, covering both the index and nonindex population in the survey area, are designed to provide data serving other important survey objectives, such as analysis of expenditure patterns for purposes of economic policy and marketing and academic research. The 

Survey was conducted in 2 years--in 1961, covering family expenditures and income in the calendar year 1960, and in 1962, providing data for 1961. 

A three-stage sample design was used to select a sample representing all families in the urban population.

The first stage was the selection of the cities to be surveyed. The sample of cities for the entire urban United States was developed by sampling Standard Metropolitan Statistical Areas (SMSA) and individual urban places in nonmetropolitan areas. 1/ A sample of 66 urban places was selected to represent all urban places in the 50 States; all of the 12 largest SMSA's were included in the sample. (The New York area was divided into two subareas.) A list of the cities in the sample and the sample size (i.e., the number of assignment addresses in the master sample) for each year is available upon request.

Since the consumer expenditure surveys were conducted in 2 years, it was necessary to divide the city sample into two balanced subsamples, each representative of the U. S. urban population. In the 12 largest SMSA's, data were collected from one-half the sample in each year. 

At the second stage, a sample of living quarter addresses was obtained in each city from the Comprehensive Housing Unit Survey (CHUS) conducted by BLS, or from listings recorded in the 1960 Census of Population and Housing. The BLS housing unit surveys, conducted in cities with populations of 50,000 or more in 1960 and in places with populations of 2,500 or more in 1961, were based on area block samples designed to represent all noninstitutional living quarters (including hotels and rooming houses) in the urban area. The 1960 sample for cities of less than 50,000 population was selected from Census listings of living quarter addresses, arranged by enumeration districts. 

In the final stage, the addresses for the Survey of Consumer Expenditures were selected as a subsample of the addresses for each city obtained from the CHUS or the Census. From both the BLS and Census arrays of addresses, an alternate sample was selected by picking the address following each address selected in the master sample. These alternate addresses were substituted if the "master" address could not be located or the unit was vacant, if no one was at home after at least two visits, or if the occupant refused or was unable to give the minimum information required for classifying the family. The procedures yielded a sample of approximately 12,000 living quarter addresses in 66 urban places. 

All members of consumer units residing at a sample address were eligible for inclusion in the survey except for the time in the survey year during which they were living in military camps, posts, or reservations (other than periods of 45 days or less in a reserve or National Guard unit); in institutions; in rural communities; 2/ abroad (except on vacation, etc.); or were members of another consumer unit. The tabulations in this report, however, cover only full-year consumer units, i.e., units with at least one member who was eligible for the entire survey year. If more than one consumer unit was living at an address, each unit was included in the sample. 

Collection and Processing of the Data All data were collected by personal interviews. Interviews for the 1960 and 1961 CES were conducted in the spring and summer of 1961 and 1962, respectively. The detailed questionnaires used by the BLS agents in interviewing the families were developed out of the long experience of the BLS in this type of survey and had been pretested in three cities--Cincinnati, Ohio, and Fairbanks and Anchorage, Alaska--in 1959. The reporting forms were designed with numerous probes and other aids to recall, and families were encouraged to refer to records wherever possible. 

Reported receipts and disbursements were summarized and reviewed in the field to determine the completeness, consistency, and balance of the family account. Families were reinterviewed when necessary to clarify ambiguous entries and complete the record. Prior to editing and coding the schedules for machine tabulation, the schedules were reviewed in the Washington office primarily to determine conformance of the entries to the survey concepts and methodology. This review was concentrated on sections of the schedule which had proved most difficult in previous survey experience, and on unusual situations which required specialized instructions. Completeness and reasonableness of the reported account, rather than the degree to which receipts and disbursements balanced, were primary criteria for determining the acceptability of a schedule. 

All data were edited, coded, and transferred to punch cards for tabulating with electronic data processing equipment. Numerous checks for internal consistency, reasonableness, and accuracy of the data were written into the programs for screening the machine listings of the data prior to tabulation.

Evaluation of the Data 

Data obtained from a sample survey as complex as the Survey of Consumer Expenditures are subject to many types of errors. These include sampling, recording, and processing errors, and errors due to the refusal or inability of some families to give the information requested. 

All data have been reviewed, edited, and screened to minimize processing errors. Chance variations due to sampling can be measured statistically and the BLS is planning to estimate the sampling error for selected items and groups of items. Families in the national sample furnishing usable schedules comprised approximately 75 percent of the 1960 sample, and 77 percent of the 1961 sample. Some of the nonrespondents supplied limited information on family characteristics which will be used to evaluate the nature of the sample losses due to nonresponse. Among the participating families, inaccurate reporting is a source of error despite continued research in schedule design and intensive training of the interviewers. Such inaccuracies result from memory errors, misunder. standing of a question or reluctance to answer it, and incorrect entries by the interviewer. 

Although the BLS has accumulated substantial knowledge about such reporting errors and will continue research in this field, these errors cannot be quantified satisfactorily. 

In using the CES data for analytical purposes and for comparison with related data from other sources, the basic orientation of the survey and the limitations this imposes on other uses of the results must be recognized. The survey was designed to obtain the most accurate information possible about family expenditures and spending patterns in detail, including the quantity and price of some purchases. Information on family income was needed for interpreting the expenditure pattern. To complete the account of the family's financial experience, information was requested on the net change in assets and liabilities. 

If a family's 12-month account were complete and error-free, receipts would equal disbursements. Such precision is rarely achieved. If a schedule met the test of the editing instructions with respect to internal completeness and consistency of expenditures with each other and with the family's reported manner of living, the record was used even though reported expenditures plus savings did not equal income. The amount of the "account balancing difference" was recorded on the schedule and the average amount per family in the sample is shown in the tabulations. Schedules were not automatically rejected from the tabulations because of high balancing differences. 

To summarize, the account balancing difference is a measure of net reporting discrepancy. 

From the nature of the survey, it is not possible to say how much of the difference arises from inaccuracy in reporting expenditures, income, or changes in the family's savings or debt position. 

The primary emphasis throughout the survey was on a complete and reasonable record of expenditures and, if these conditions appeared to be satisfied, the standards for completeness of the savings and income record were less rigorous. 

Guides to Interpreting Statistical Summaries 

The averages and percentages in the accompanying tables are based on all families included in each class, whether or not they reported receipts or disbursements for a particular item. Averages were calculated by dividing the aggregate amount of income, expenditures, or savings by the total number of families in the class. Since all averages for a class are based on a common divisor, they are additive. 3/

The 

In releasing data for individual cities in the same detail which will be shown in the regional and national tabulations, the procedure that was initiated in the Bureau's 1950 survey has been followed. Namely, averages are shown for all classes of families for which any observations are available, even though only one reporting family happened to fall in a class. The city tabulations are the integral parts of the electronic data processing program which prints in complete detail all information which is to be cumulated into the regional and United States totals. number of families included in each class is shown in every tabulation, and it is assumed that the analyst will recognize the limitations of the averages computed for small numbers of families. An advantage of this procedure is that the inclusion of all observations in the tabulations allows the analyst to regroup the data for his particular needs without loss of information and thus make maximum use of the data.

Particular care is required in using the averages for families at the extremes of the income scale. These averages are based on small numbers of families which may differ sharply in their spending patterns. For example, the lower income classes include predominantly elderly persons, but they may also include an occasional student (with high education expenses) or a family which reported losses from a business which they operated or from rental property. Occasional apparent inconsistencies in the averages of income before and after taxes for small groups of families may be explained by the fact that income received and tax payments and tax refunds are not reported within the same calendar year.

NOTE: Durham is but 1 of 40 cities surveyed for 1961. 

The results apply to the Durham metropolitan area only, and they may differ significantly from summary averages for the United States and for four geographic regions to be published later. The sample for Durham and other cities in the population class of 50,000 to 250,000 were small, and the averages for some classes in the accompanying tables are based on reports from very few families. The number of families in each class is shown in every table so that users can recognize the limitations of the averages in each case. This presentation of complete detail allows the analyst to regroup the data for his particular needs without loss of information and thus make maximum use of the data. 

The attached tables supplement the information contained in the advance report on consumer expenditures and income in 1961 for this city, and are based on the same sample of consumer units. For these tables, the consumer units are classified by the following additional family characteristics which are defined below 1/: education of family head; race; family type; and number of full-time earners.

Education of Family Head. Years of school completed during or before the survey year in an elementary or high school or a college, university, or professional school. 

Eight years or less corresponds to years or grades completed in an elementary school; 9-12 years, to the completion of 1 to 4 years of high school; 13-16 years, to the completion of 

1 to 4 years of college; and over 16 years, to postgraduate work after college. All per- sons in the latter class were recorded as having completed 17 years of school. Persons who did not give any information on the extent of their education were included with the class "8 years or less. 11 Race. The term "race" refers to the classification of families in three groups: white, Negro, and other. "Other" included Japanese, Chinese, Indians, and other nonwhite races except Negroes.

Family Type. Families were classified in seven types on the basis of the relationship of family members and the age of the children of the head of the family. Five types consisted of consumer units in which both the husband and wife were present. One of these was composed of a husband and wife only; husband and wife families with their own children (including adopted and stepchildren) but with no other persons in the family were classified in three types according to the age of the oldest child (under 6 years, 6-17 years, 18 years and over); and the fifth type, "other husband-wife" families, included those with or without their own children but with other persons in the family. A sixth type included families with children and only one parent (the head) present and no other persons in the family. The remaining type covered all other consumer units, including one-person families.

Number of Full-Time Earners. A count of family members who were employed 48 weeks or more in the survey year, and for 35 hours or more per week in wage and salary Occupations. Members employed in industries where customary full-time employment is less than 48 weeks or 35 hours per week (e.g., school systems or airlines) were counted as full-time earners. The minimum hours requirement did not apply to self-employed workers in an unincorporated business or profession. 1/ For definitions of other terms, see pp. 4-5 of BLS Report No. 237-69.
