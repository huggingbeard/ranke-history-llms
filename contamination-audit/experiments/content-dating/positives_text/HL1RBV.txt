CLASSIFICATION OF DRIVERS' LICENSES INTRODUCTION 

The concept of driver's license classification is simply a recognition that certain kinds of driving call for special skills, knowledge and other qualifications beyond what is necessary for performance of the basic driving task. In requiring some form of classified driver's license a state is recognizing that drivers should display the necessary qualifications before being licensed to perform these special driving tasks. While this concept is not new, it is one which has recently been refined and expanded, particularly in terms of redefining those driving tasks which involve special qualifications.

This Commentary will review the status of state laws pertaining to classification of drivers' licenses in the context of relevant provisions of the Uniform Vehicle Code. The basic concept of classification, including a history of the concept's development, will be discussed. The Commentary will review in detail the various defined classifications identified in the state statutes. Only statutory material will be included. No attempt has been made to review the various state regulations which may further define classifications and qualifications. State laws in effect as of January 1, 1976 will be covered.

THE CONCEPT OF CLASSIFICATION Historical Perspective 

The concept of driver's license classification is probably as old as the concept of driver licensing itself. As will be described below, both concepts were part of the original Uniform Vehicle Code published in 1926. Since that time the classification concept in the Code and in state laws has been extensively modified. It has involved at various times different combinations of the following three basic approaches:

Driver's Employment. The first approach involves the employment status of the driver. The employment factor has been utilized almost exclusively in conjunction with the operator-chauffeur distinction. A person who drives a motor vehicle as a principal purpose of his employment is a chauffeur, and is required to obtain a chauffeur's license. Drivers who are not required to have chauffeurs licenses are required to obtain operators' licenses. The chauffeur -R 1

is generally required to meet a higher minimum age standard, his license is issued for a shorter term, and he is more likely to be subject to examination upon renewal, as compared with the operator. Although not very sophisticated in terms of contemporary license classification systems, the operator-chauffeur distinction is clearly an example of license classification. The basic underlying premise is that the qualifications of a person who drives as an occupation should be more closely scrutinized than the qualifications of the nonprofessional driver. 

Vehicle Use. A second approach to license classification involves the use of the vehicle being driven. Under this approach a person who drives a vehicle which is used for specific purposes defined by law is required to have a special license (sometimes a chauffeur's license), or to meet special license qualifications.

The two primary examples of vehicle use which have been designated for such special licensing treatment are use to transport pupils to and from school and use to transport persons or property for compensation. In order to be licensed to drive these special-use vehicles, a person may be required to meet a higher minimum age standard, to present proof of prior driving experience, good character, competency and fitness, and to complete special courses and/or examinations. A few other kinds of vehicle use have also been designated for special licensing in several states. The vehicle use factor has also been used in some states to designate exceptions to license classifications based primarily on vehicle types. In some cases this approach has been used to designate classifications which require less stringent licensing standards, such as farm vehicles. 

Vehicle Type. A third approach, and the one which is probably most frequently associated with the concept of license classification, involves the type of vehicle which is being driven. A number of different criteria have been used to define classifications based upon vehicle type, including vehicle body style, size, length, width, weight, number of vehicles in combination, type of combination, number of axles or wheels, passenger capacity, and whether operating with cargo or empty. In order to drive some of these special vehicle types, a person may be required to meet higher minimum age standards, to present proof of prior experience driving that type vehicle or proof of ability to operate such vehicles, and to complete special examinations. - 2 -

Uniform Vehicle Code History. The original Uniform Vehicle 

Code utilized both the driver's employment factor, and the vehicle use factor in its relatively simple license classification system. 

The Code defined the term "chauffeur" to include any person employed for the principal purpose of operating a motor vehicle (the driver's employment factor) or any person who drives a motor vehicle while in use as a public or common carrier of persons or property (the vehicle use factor).1/ A driver meeting either of these criteria was required to obtain a chauffeur's license.2/ 

The minimum age limit for a chauffeur's license was 18 years, while it was 16 years for an operator's license.3/ The Code specified additional age restrictions based upon vehicle use factors. 

The minimum age to drive a school bus for the transportation of pupils to or from school was 18 years.4/ The minimum age to drive a motor vehicle while in use as a public passenger carrying vehicle was 21 years.5/ Chauffeurs' licenses could be issued for a shorter term than operators' licenses, and the basis for reexamination upon renewal differed. 6/ In other respects the qualifications for operators' and chauffeurs' licenses were generally the same. 

The 1934 edition of the Code included some significant changes in the license classification concept. The operator-chauffeur distinction was retained as a basis for license classification.7/ A new classification, the "special chauffeur's license" was introduced and required for the driver of a school bus or a vehicle used as a public or common carrier of persons or property. The minimum age limit for a special chauffeur's license was 21 years.9/ This re- 10/ placed the minimum age limits set out in the 1926 Code for drivers of school buses and public passenger carrying vehicles. Other qualifications for the special chauffeur's license included one year of driving experience and certification of good moral character by three responsible persons.

Prior to issuance of a special chauffeur's license the department had to be fully satisfied as to the applicants "competency and fitness.

Other licensing qualifications were generally the same for operators and chauffeurs, although as in 1926 the validity period and basis for reexamination on renewal differed. 12/ .. 11/

In 1938 the operator-chauffeur distinction was retained as a basis for classification. 13/

The concept of the "special chauffeur's license" was deleted and replaced by a concept of classified chauffeurs' licenses. All chauffeurs' licenses issued were required to bear an indication of the "class of license.". 14/ The department was required to appropriately examine each applicant, according to 15/ the class of license for which application was made.

The depart- ment was authorized to impose such rules and regulations for the use - 3 

---

of classified chauffeurs' licenses as it deemed necessary for the 16/ public safety and welfare. These provisions constituted authority to the department, with respect to chauffeurs' licenses but not operators' licenses, to designate particular classifications. These classifications could be based upon vehicle use factors or vehicle type factors or a combination thereof. 

Since a chauffeur's license was required for a person driving as a principal duty of his employment, or a person driving a motor vehicle used as a school bus or to transport either persons or property for compensation, the classified chauffeur's license concept probably applied to most of the drivers for whom classifications would be desirable, except those driving motorcycles. Still, it was possible for a person to drive any type of vehicle with an unclassified operator's license so long as he did not fall within the definition of a chauffeur.

Although the special chauffeurs' license concept was deleted, all the special qualifications for school bus drivers and drivers of motor vehicles used to transport persons or property for compensation were retained in the 1938 Code. 17/ 

Other licensing qualifications for operators and chauffeurs were generally the same under the 1938 Code although as in earlier editions chauffeurs' licenses were issued for a shorter term than operators' licenses. 18/ The classified chauffeur's license concept was retained in the Code without substantial amendment until 1968.19/ In 1968 the operator-chauffeur distinction was deleted from the Code and the classified chauffeur's license concept was expanded to cover all drivers' licenses. This amendment removed the driver's employment status factor from consideration in license classification. 

Under the amended provisions classifications are based upon vehicle type or vehicle use factors, or a combination thereof.

Classification by Vehicle Use 

As indicated above, the two primary classifications based upon vehicle use are motor vehicles used to transport pupils to and from school (school buses), and motor vehicles used to transport persons (or property) for compensation.

Apart from these two classifications, the vehicle use factor has very limited application. Two states require the drivers of vehicles used for particular purposes to be licensed as chauffeurs regardless of their employment status. Arizona requires the driver of a tow truck, when in use for moving or transporting wrecked, damaged, disabled or abandoned vehicles to have a chauffeur's license. Colorado requires the driver of any motor vehicle, when transporting explosives or inflammable material to have a chauffeur's license.

More commonly the vehicle use factor has been used to designate types of vehicle use for which a chauffeur's license is not required. Thus, for example, three states (Florida, North Carolina and Rhode Island) specifically provide that a chauffeur's license is not required to drive certain vehicles used as authorized emergency vehicles. Two states (Arizona and Oregon) specify that drivers of car pool vehicles are not chauffeurs. Two states (Florida and Texas) specify with regard to property hauling vehicles that a chauffeur's license is not required if the property being transported belongs to the owner or lessee of the vehicle. 

Five states (Iowa, Michigan, Montana, Oklahoma, and Wisconsin) exempt certain farm vehicle drivers from the chauffeur's license requirement. Wisconsin exempts funeral vehicles and light delivery vehicles operated by students also.

There is likewise little agreement in the basic approach to follow in delineating the classes. Most of the laws use some basic vehicle-type terms such as passenger car, truck and bus. Some of the terms are more limited such as house car, farm truck, straight truck, or farm labor truck. Some terms differentiate combinations such as tractor-trailer combination, tractor-semitrailer combination, truck-trailer combination, trucktractor-semitrailer combination and all lawful combinations. In addition to this vehicle-type terminology, most of the states add weight criteria, generally based upon the vehicle's registered gross weight limitation. A few states also use criteria based upon number of axles, vehicle width, vehicle length, or passenger capacity. 

Another good example of the different basic approach in delineating classifications is found in the diverse treatment of motorcycle drivers in these 14 states. All of these 14 states except Nebraska require special licensing for motorcycle operation. 

It is not clear what Nebraska requires for motorcycle operators. 

Seven of the states (California, Georgia, Hawaii, Kansas, Maryland, North Dakota and Wyoming) provide a separate license classification for motorcycles only. Hawaii has two appropriate classes, one for motor scooters, and one for such motor scooters and motorcycles. 

In these seven states (except Kansas) either a separate license with the motorcycle classification may be issued, or the motorcycle classification may be endorsed onto any other class of license. Kansas specifies that a person is eligible for the motorcycle class only if he first holds another license classification, so issuance of a separate motorcycle-only license is not allowed. In four other states (Louisiana, Maine, Minnesota and New York) motorcycle operation is authorized by endorsement upon a classified license only.

There is no separate class for motorcycles in these states, and just as in Kansas a person who wants to drive a motorcycle must first obtain a license for some other class of motor vehicle. 

In the other two states (Connecticut and New Hampshire) motorcycle operation is apparently authorized by issuance of a separate motorcycle license. The statutes in these states do not provide for endorsement of the motorcycle privilege onto another class of license. These laws do not require the motorcycle operator to obtain a license for another class of motor vehicle before being licensed to operate a motorcycle. If the person already has another license, however, they require the issuance of a second license, valid for motorcycles only, and this appears to conflict with the one license concept. 

Another example of basic disagreement among these 14 laws involves the relationship of the different classes. All of the states have a basic class which includes passenger cars and perhaps a few similar kinds of vehicles. This could be described as the lowest class, and it is certainly the most commonly held license classification. Most of the states then designate higher classes which are inclusive of the lower classes. Each higher class includes all of the lower classes (except motorcycles and, in a few cases, school buses). The highest class under this system authorizes the operation of any motor vehicle except those requiring special endorsement, like motorcycles. In these states, if you are licensed to drive a tractor-trailer combination (generally in the highest class), you are also licensed to drive a bus (generally in a lower classification). In a few states, however, the higher classes are not inclusive of all the lower classes. 

In these states (Hawaii and New York) you might be licensed to operate a tractor-trailer combination without being licensed to operate a bus also. The New York law provides that the bus classification may be endorsed upon another classification. Hawaii has several classes which constitute various combinations of lower classifications.

The significance of the above analysis should not be misconstrued. A person who is lawfully driving a particular kind or class of motor vehicle or combination of vehicles in his home state will not be operating illegally if he drives in some other state. THE ONE LICENSE CONCEPT 

The Uniform Vehicle Code specifies that no person may possess more than one valid license at any one time: 

No person shall receive a driver's license unless and until he surrenders to the department all valid licenses in his possession issued to him by this or any other jurisdiction. All surrendered licenses issued by another jurisdiction shall be returned thereto, together with information that the person is licensed in this State. No person shall be permitted to have more than one valid driver's license at any time.37/

This provision is primarily intended to insure that a driver will not hold valid licenses issued by two or more jurisdictions at the same time. A driver's license should be issued only by the jurisdiction in which the licensee is a resident. The provision is also intended to insure that a driver will not hold multiple valid licenses issued by the same jurisdiction. Each person has only a single privilege to drive. There is danger under a classified license system that each classification will be viewed as a separate driving privilege. The Code specifies clearly that there is only one privilege. No matter how many different types or classes of vehicles a licensee has been qualified to drive, all those classifications should be noted on a single license. This is generally referred to as the "one license concept.

' "I The one license concept is desirable in order to avoid the inconvenience, confusion and enforcement difficulties which a multiple license system could foster. A violator should not have the opportunity to apportion his points under a point system by his selective display of one of several licenses to an officer. - 37 - 

---

Also, when a license is suspended, revoked or restricted, all evidence of the former privilege should be removed from the licensee to prevent abuse. This is made easier under a one license system.

Other matters such as license issuanc renewal, name and address changes, and license photographs, are also complicated by multiple license systems. 3 Although the one license concept may be generally accepted in the driver licensing practice of most states, not many have adopted a provision specifically requiring the practice, and some states have laws which are contrary to the one license concept.

OBSERVATIONS 

1. It makes good sense to require a person to show that he is able and qualified to drive a particular kind of vehicle before allowing that person to drive that vehicle on a highway. Most state's licensing systems are now based on that premise. But a large number of states still issue operator's licenses and chauffeur's licenses, distinguishing drivers on the basis of employment or nonemployment, with little or no regard to the kind of vehicle being driven. A person with an operator's license may have experience driving, and may be qualified to drive, only passenger cars. Yet he is licensed to drive the largest bus, or the most complex combination of vehicles, so long as he does not do so as a principal purpose of his employment or in the transportation of persons or property for hire. A person with a chauffeur's license is authorized to drive buses and combinations of vehicles even as his employment and even when transporting persons or property for compensation, yet his sole driving experience may be driving a passenger car as a taxicab.

It is time to recognize that the driver's employment status bears no reasonable or necessary relationship to his ability to drive a particular kind of motor vehicle. In those states which maintain the operator-chauffeur distinction, it is time to replace this concept with a more modern and comprehensive system of license classification based primarily on vehicle type. 2. 

Some might understandably question the concept of special licensing for particular vehicle types, however, given the absence of agreement among state laws designating the vehicle types for which special licensing is necessary. In this Commentary we have addressed this question only in terms of the 14 states which have full license classification systems set out in their laws. The review of those laws shows very little agreement. A vehicle type which would require special licensing in some states, would require only a passenger car license in some other states. Rather clearly 

40 -

both groups of states are not correct in this classification. 

Either a vehicle does require special knowledge, experience and ability beyond what is required to drive a passenger car, or it does not. Agreement among the states ought to be possible. 

The problem may be in placing the classification system in the statutes. It is difficult to coordinate the activities of legislatures. Also once a classification system is enacted as statutory law, it is not easy to change. The Uniform Vehicle 

Code does not contain a classification system. Rather it authorizes the administrative promulgation of such a system. Regulations are considerably easier than laws to amend, and cooperation among the administrators of the states is much more likely than among legislatures. 3.

In determining appropriate classifications, more concern should be shown for the amount of special knowledge, experience and ability required to safely drive a particular vehicle. Perhaps less concern should be shown for convenience. It may be quite convenient, for example, that a licensee who has never driven a motor vehicle with a standard transmission is nevertheless licensed to drive such a vehicle. While it may be convenient to not distinguish transmission types for licensing purposes, it may be more significant to ask whether a standard transmission vehicle, as opposed to an automatic transmission vehicle, requires special knowledge, experience or ability for safe driving. Should a driver who has experience driving passenger cars only be allowed to tow his boat trailer without first displaying the necessary knowledge and ability to do so safely? What about driving a large rented truck to move his household goods, or a motorhome that may be as large as a bus, or a pick-up truck loaded with a large, heavy camper body? 

4. The vehicle use factor in license classification has generally been limited to defining classes for school buses and public passenger vehicles. For some vehicles the special knowledge, experience and ability which should be required relates more to the vehicle's use than to its type. Regardless of the type of vehicle being driven, if it is used as an authorized emergency vehicle the driver should be required to display the special knowledge and ability necessary for operation of an emergency vehicle. The same reasoning might apply to the drivers of vehicles used to transport explosives, inflammables, radioactive or other dangerous materials and to the drivers of tow trucks.
