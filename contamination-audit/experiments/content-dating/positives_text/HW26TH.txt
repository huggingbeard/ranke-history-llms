Danson Hall, near the Cotswold Hills, had been the home of the Weatherstones for generations past. The castle was covered almost entirely with ivy, surrounded by magnificent cedars and velvety lawns; built in the midst of an immense park.

The interior of Danson Hall is both luxurious and simple. The furniture of old mahogany and oak, covered with Utrech velvet, the fine library, the massive silver, gives one the impression of respectability. 

The home of the Weatherstones was always filled with the most congenial guests, the host being a sportsman, wondrously pleased to contemplate his magnificent thoroughbred steeds, each held in subjection by a groom, all prancing and dancing, chafing at the bit, ready at the first burst of freedom to be off with their riders, to follow the hounds released from their leash. 

The mounts as eager seemingly-as those who rode them to obtain the brush of the small animal of which they were in quest.

The lady of the mansion, a true English matron, was of a domestic turn, priding herself rather upon the epicurianism of her house parties, than upon the more frivolous features. While her husband, loving the companionship of younger folks, was always planning for their enjoyment. Therefore, when Harold and Muriel Hawthorne arrived from their home in 

Washington D. C., he welcomed them heartily.

The interior of 'Seamon Hall'-the ancestral home of Lord Scuttering-was both luxurious and simple.

The furniture of old mahogany and oak covered with

The stables at 'Seamon Hall' are luxuriously supplied with horses and the kennel is organized with a care which shows a thorough comprehension of the canine race. In the meadows confining the little park Jersey cows graze all day, and not far away is to be seen the thatched roof of some of the tenant cottages.

CHAPTER XXIV. 

Friendship! What a wonderful phenomena it is.

For a long period of time we walk in different pathsthen we cross suddenly toward the same point, and meet each other. Many circumstances bring us together with significant persistence. A photographic transmission of images and impressions takes place between our brains. Each of them puts a little of his or her substance into the soul of the other-enough to produce mutual vibrations-more or less profound of course.

So it was with Mrs. Benton's guests as they gathered around her hospitable board for the last time with two exceptions-who, by their actions-had ruffled and disturbed the tranquillity of the occasion.
