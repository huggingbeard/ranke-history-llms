COURTROOM DESIGN AND COURTHOUSE FACILITIES 

The Judicial Council recommends that all pertinent information relating to the proper design, construction and use of courtrooms and courthouses, including modern advances in the field, be collected and assembled in the office of the Executive Secretary and made available to judges or others contemplating changes or new construction in courtrooms and courthouses. 

The above recommendation is predicated on the report printed on the following pages. The report was prepared by a study committee of the Council and approved by the Council's Executive Committee. It was unanimously adopted by the full Council on 

June 14, 1957. -1-

REPORT TO JUDICIAL COUNCIL EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE 

by 

COMMITTEE ON COURTROOM DESIGN AND COURTHOUSE FACILITIES 

This committee was created to make a full study regarding standards of courthouses and courtrooms and make the results of such study available to counties remodeling or building new courtrooms or courthouses. 

In the area of courtroom design the committee was directed to study the following items: minimum standards for the convenience of the judge, jury, attorneys, parties, witnesses, spectators, reporters and bailiffs; arrangement of the witness chair, or a movable witness chair, in order that the court, jury and counsel may have a full-face view of the witness; a bench designed so that the court may view slides shown to the jury without walking through the courtroom; acoustics; placement of the court reporter so that he can hear jury responses as well as counsel and the witness; counsel tables equidistant from the jury; a proper size and shape for the bench and proper access to chambers; dignity and decor; ventilation and air conditioning; lighting; an efficient display for charts, drawings, visual exhibits, etc.; all other matters relating to courtroom design which occur to the committee. 

In the area of courthouse facilities the committee was directed to study the following items: a definition of what actually is the, function of the building called a courthouse; standards regarding the facilities which should be available for the judge, lawyers, court attaches, clerk, jurors, witnesses and the public by way of rooms, telephones, seating, etc.; adequate and convenient jury rooms and conference rooms, chambers with easy access to an adequate library and yet with privacy; a door for attorneys to enter chambers other than the one through which the judge passes; waiting rooms for witnesses; access to the jail without jeopardizing security; juvenile housing; furnishings for the courtroom, chambers and accessory rooms; all other matters relating to facilities which occur to the committee. 

The committee held two meetings during the year. One was held in Portland in conjunction with the meeting of the Circuit Judges in December. The other was held on February 22nd in Eugene. The committee, as a result of these two meetings, feels its work must be of a continuing nature. There are two phases to its work. One is concerned with the study of the subject. The other is the accumulation and assembling of information. The committee has concluded further that its work, if it is to be of aid to the Council, must be divided at least into three basic areas. a. b. C. 

Single Judge Counties. 

Counties having 2 to 4 judges. 

Counties having more than ↳ judges.

The necessity of dividing the work of the committee into these groupings became immediately apparent upon consideration of the different problems. The committee has construed the term "facilities" to include an analysis of the proper relationship of the judge's chambers, jury rooms, clerk's office, handling of prisoners, court reporters, secretary, etc. -2-

It developed that Lane County is planning for a new courthouse. As a part of the planning for this work one of the courtrooms in that county was set up on an experimental basis, and its equipment, including bench, jury box, counsel tables, witness box, etc., made mobile. More than a dozen different arrangements were experimented with over several months. As a part of the experiment, questionnaires were submitted to each jury member serving in the courtroom, each courtroom attache and the attorneys appearing in each case. A design of each experimental layout was maintained, and questionnaires for each layout were filed with the appropriate plan. 

The local bar association, at the request of the court, appointed a special committee to work with the judges and the architects in considering the basic problems both of the courtroom and related facilities. The material accumulated from the experiment was made available to the bar committee and to the architects. Recommendations were made by the bar committee and by the judges to the architects. 

A considerable quantity of information, including drawings and photographs were accumulated during the year. In connection with the planning of the Lane County 

Courthouse, one of the circuit judges made an extensive trip to recommended courthouses in various parts of the nation in order to see what was being attempted elsewhere. This information has been filed with the committee. 

Your committee believes an important part of its functions will be to accumulate information to be made available to judges in the state who may at any time be considering the desirability of rearranging or otherwise improving the facilities within their own courtrooms. To this end all information accumulated by it should ultimately be maintained in the office of the Executive Secretary of the Council so that any judge may quickly receive whatever information he may desire. 

Ultimately your committee believes that its study should include consideration of the handling and displaying of exhibits, design of witness and jury boxes, acoustical problems, etc. It is not, however, the thought of this committee that it should do more than accumulate information to be available for any judge at such time as he may wish to use it. Your committee, however, does feel that if some of the serious design mistakes found in many of Oregon's present courtrooms are to be avoided in future building, that whenever a new structure is to be built or drastically remodeled, that the judges affected should call upon the Executive Secretary of the Council for the information which has been accumulated.

JUVENILE CODE 

The Judicial Council recommends the thorough restudy, revision and recodification of the juvenile laws of Oregon in relation to their substance, procedure and philosophy. 

The above recommendation is predicated on the report printed on the following pages. The report was prepared by a study committee of the Council and approved by the Council's Executive Committee. It was unanimously adopted by the full 

Council on June 14, 1957.

REPORT TO THE JUDICIAL COUNCIL EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE 

by 

COMMITTEE ON JUVENILE CODE 

This committee was created to make a full study relative to a possible revision of the juvenile code, and specifically to study 20 questions proposed by various juvenile court judges, and to serve as a juvenile court judges' workshop. 

The committee met in Salem on April 5 shortly after its creation. All members were present except Judge Felton. The 20 questions proposed in the letter of appointment were considered at length. Generally, we were of the opinion that most of these questions merit study, and in some cases statutory changes and in others setting up of uniform procedures between the various courts handling juvenile matters in Oregon.

Specifically, we were all in agreement that the juvenile code, if our loosely organized statutes dealing with children in Oregon can be called a code, is badly in need of revision. It was our uniform idea that a research lawyer is needed to do this job of research in conjunction with continuing committee work. The discussion touched upon such things as giving general equity jurisdiction to juvenile and domestic relations courts, the question of what concurrent jurisdiction should be provided for, how the jurisdiction in courts handling juvenile matters should be defined as it relates to circuit and county courts, each of which in various areas handle juvenile matters, the transfer of cases from circuit courts to county courts where these courts of different level sit in juvenile matters in different counties, what additional checking system should be established on sufficiency of procedure on juvenile cases and adoption cases, a clear definition of what is necessary in the way of preliminary hearings in juvenile matters, the possibility of foster home selection for neglected, dependent and delinquent children by the juvenile courts through their own personnel rather than through welfare departments, and a number of other things.

As noted above, any one of these studies or possible revisions of statutes or procedure is going to take considerable study, and certainly an experienced person in the field of codification should be available. Senate Joint Resolution 24, passed by the current legislature, provides for an interim study in juvenile matters. 

This resolution carried with it an appropriation. We talked with the secretary of the Ways and Means Committee during the session, and it was concluded that your chairman and Judge Hammond should attend a subcommittee meeting of the Ways and Means Committee to talk with them about passage of the resolution and the uses to which any appropriation might be put. We were instructed by the subcommittee to make it clear that we had no authority to act for the Judicial Council, but due to the time element involved, we should advance as the ideas of ourselves as individuals the thought that an appropriation should be concentrated upon juvenile code revision. Accordingly, Judge Hammond and your chairman did attend the Ways and Means committee meeting. The resolution was then amended to provide for a subcommittee to study juvenile code revision. It was also learned during the meeting that there may be one or more persons available in the Legislative Counsel's office who have had experience in the revision of statutes. This report has been -5-

delayed until we could learn whether the legislature had taken favorable action upon this resolution. I am informed today that the resolution has passed both the 

House and Senate, but I do not know the full details concerning its provisions as finally enacted, but it seems rather clear that there will be available some kind of help to make the necessary study and preparation for an intelligent revision and codification of the juvenile laws in this state. The committee has some very definite ideas as to what the activities of the interim committee should be, insofar as they relate to juvenile matters.

PRE-SENTENCE INVESTIGATION REPORTS 

The Judicial Council recommends that ORS 137.110 be repealed.

The above recommendation is predicated on the report printed on the following pages. The report was prepared by a study committee of the Council and approved by the Council's Executive Committee. It was unanimously adopted by the full Council on 

June 14, 1957. -7-

REPORT TO JUDICIAL COUNCIL EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE 

by 

COMMITTEE ON PRE-SENTENCE INVESTIGATION REPORTS 

Your committee was directed to make a study and report on:

8. 

The effect of Opp. Atty. Gen. #3372, May 14, 1956. b. The necessity of a full legislative revision of the 

Oregon pre-sentence procedure, namely ORS 137.080 to 

137.110 and 137.530.

C. 

Recommendations for legislation implementing the decision of the committee. 

Sections 137.080 to 137.110 provide a pre-sentencing procedure for the trial court after a plea and finding of guilty, when the court is of the opinion that matters may properly be considered in aggravation or mitigation of punishment. Such matters may be heard summarily. ORS 137.110 is the crucial section. It and preceding sections were first enacted in 1864 and are seldom used today. The section is quoted below: "No affidavit or testimony or representation of any kind, verbal or written, can be offered to or received by the court in aggravation or mitigation of the punishment, except as provided in ORS 137.080 to 137.100."

The right of the court to order a pre-sentence investigation report from the Department of Parole and Probation is found in ORS 137.530, enacted in 1931 and in general use today. It is set forth below: "Probation officers, when directed by the court, shall fully investigate and report to the court in writing on the circumstances of the offense, criminal record, social history and present condition and environment of any defendant; and unless the court directs otherwise in individual cases, no defendant shall be placed on probation until the report of such investigation has been presented to and considered by the court. 

Whenever desirable, and facilities exist therefor, such investigation shall include physical and mental examinations of such defendants."

The reports furnished the court by the parole board attempt to give to the sentencing judge a social history of the defendant, a statement of the crime and its circumstances, and a list of the defendant's prior criminal record, including arrests. 

The material so furnished the court is not sworn to. The unsworn report is subject to criticism because of interest of persons' making the statements and because the matters in the prior criminal record may upon close investigation be found to pertain to someone else with a similar name.

Under the generally prevailing practice, no copy is given to the defendant or to his attorney and thus there is no opportunity to refute any material presented in the pre-sentence investigation report. The above-mentioned opinion of the Attorney -8-

General, however, in substance stated that ORS 137.110 and ORS 137.530 were in conflict and that the defendant must consent to the use of the report by the court and have an opportunity to examine it and make a statement as to any matters contained therein.

The practical effect of such a practice would be to largely render ineffective a pre-sentence investigation report by the parole board for the reason that much of the social history is gathered from friends and acquaintances of the defendant who would not speak frankly to the investigator compiling the report if their statements were to be released to the defendant. 

In view of the apparent conflict and in view of the excellent aid to sentencing gained by the confidential pre-sentence investigation report compiled under 

ORS 137.530, it is the recommendation of the committee that ORS 137.110 be repealed.

The committee feels that adequate safeguards are present in 137.080, and that the court could, pursuant to request of either party under ORS 137.080, provide for a summary hearing in its discretion; particularly with regard to any question of an alleged prior criminal record. 

Respectfully submitted, P. K. Hammond

COMMITTEE ON REDISTRICTING AND LAY JUDGES 

This committee was directed to study the dual problems of redistricting and lay judges in the light of Council policies established in 1956. Specifically, your committee was directed to formulate a plan of redistricting where necessary or advantageous to the administration of justice, integrating therein a plan to effect the rapid transfer of jurisdiction from lay to law-trained judges, particularly in those circuit courts which could readily absorb the additional jurisdiction with available manpower. 

The Legislature in the 1957 session was faced with so many bills involving redistricting or the transfer of jurisdiction from lay judges (County Court) to law-trained judges (Circuit and District Court), that it was felt the committee could accomplish no useful purpose until the conclusion of the session. 1 For example, the Legislature split the 2nd District into three separate districts. 

At the same time all jurisdiction except juvenile matters has been transferred from county to district court in Washington, Benton, Deschutes, Linn and Umatilla Counties.

Presumably such jurisdiction will be given to the district courts in Clatsop and Wasco Counties when they come into existence two and six years hence, respectively. 

In Lake and Coos Counties all jurisdiction has been transferred from county to circuit court.

In passing it should be mentioned that probate jurisdiction in Oregon is vested in three different courts; circuit, district and county, depending upon which county you are in, and juvenile jurisdiction is vested in circuit or county court. One 

Two other bills affecting the jurisdiction of county courts failed to pass. would have transferred all probate and guardianship jurisdiction to the circuit courts throughout the state and the other, a Judicial Council bill, would have transferred adoption jurisdiction from county to circuit. court.

The members of the committee feel that the committee should be continued in order to further the policies of the Council as expressed in the 1956 report, and to work with the newly created legislative interim committee, which has been specifically instructed to study the problem of redistricting.
