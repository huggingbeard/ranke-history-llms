CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

In the last 6-month period, the third semi-annual period in its history, the Small Business Administration expanded its program in discharging the three types of responsibilities which have been entrusted to it by Congress: 

1. Helping small business gain access to adequate capital and credit. 

2. Helping small business to obtain a fair share of Government procurement. 

3. Helping small business to obtain competent management, technical and production counseling.

The effectiveness of the Small Business Administration in rendering greater help to small business results from the groundwork in establishing a strong administrative foundation and building a staff, from the increasing knowledge among small businesses of the assistance that can be rendered by the Small Business Administration and from the cooperation that has been achieved with other Government agencies.

The major call upon our time and resources continues to be in the financial area. The total number of loan applications, business and disaster loans, increased to 2, 792, compared with 2, 345 in the preceding 6-month period. The total amount of loans approved, business and disaster loans, was at approximately the same level as during the preceding period--$31, 880, 746 as against $31, 933, 902.

A notable accomplishment has been the fact that the time for processing loan applications and the backlog has been materially reduced. The Agency's procedures are constantly being streamlined to cut down the time for processing loans, but, at the same time, protect the interests of the Government. 1

2 Maximum emphasis has been put on our financial counseling program, which enables an increasing number of firms to secure their financing from a private source. The lending facilities of the Small Business Administration are designed to supplement those of private lending sources, not compete with them. Frequently through our financial counseling, financing is made available by banks that originally declined to make loans to the firms.

It is a source of gratification that private banks participate in two-thirds of the loans made by the Agency. The proportion of participation loans on a deferred basis amounts to 50 percent of the total amount of loans made by the Small Business Administration, direct as well as participating. The likelihood is that a good share of the funds obligated to this type of loan may never be drawn from the Federal Treasury.

An analysis in Chapter III indicates that the Agency has had success in channeling its loan assistance to the smaller members of the small business community. More than 88 percent of all loans to manufacturers have gone to firms which employ fewer than 100 persons. Sixty-five and three-tenths percent by number and 32 percent of the amount of business loans approved have been under $50,000.

A highly important factor in obtaining defense contracts for small firms has been the Joint Determination Program of the 

Small Business Administration and the Department of Defense. 

Both the number and amount of Government contracts reserved for small business showed a substantial increase over the previous period. Furthermore, the percentage of joint determinations initiated by the Small Business Administration and later rejected by the Military Services declined sharply. From August 1, 1953, to January 31, 1955, the Small Business Administration and the 

Military Agencies agreed to 3, 414 joint determinations, totaling $436, 032, 838. As of the same date, 1, 894 procurements awarded resulted in 2, 791 contracts to small firms, totaling $188, 055, 552. 

Additional awards will be made as a result of the 3, 414 joint determinations already made.

Cooperation with the Military Services is constantly being improved. A significant step was the recent appointment by the 

Small Business Administration at the request of the Department of Defense of a liaison officer who will participate at the planning

level of the Defense Department in decisions made concerning small business activities.

There are other services provided through procurement programs of the Agency which do not lend themselves as readily to statistical treatment as the Joint Determination Program, but they are equally important. These include procurement counseling, referring prime contracts to small businesses for which the procurements are suitable and disseminating more widely information about procurement opportunities. There is a greater need for stimulating subcontracts as a result of reduced defense expenditures in many areas. This problem is under our study and an effort is being made to develop a more effective subcontracting assistance program through our liaison officer in the Pentagon.

For some small firms, the most serious problem is not one of obtaining financing or contracts; it is an urgent need to acquire greater management and technical skill. While most of industrial and production assistance is rendered daily to small firms through the "know-how" of our field staff, a substantial amount of management and technical assistance also is furnished through staff specialists in Washington. In addition, the Agency issues a wide range of publications in the management aid field. Through reprinting by numerous trade association organs and other media, these Small Business Administration publications gain wide coverage. A cooperative publishing program with the Department of Commerce has widened the effectiveness of these publications at a considerable savings. 

A program inaugurated during the last year which has created tremendous interest among small businesses has been our 

Products Assistance Program. The development and introduction of new and improved products and processes is essential to the continued expansion of our national economy and maintenance of our high standard of living. Large numbers of small business concerns are unable to conduct an effective or continuous research on new products and product development. Our Products Assistance Program is designed to help many small firms with product development problems; it has proven in a short period of time that it is potentially a valuable tool to the small firm in its efforts to remain in a favorable competitive position.

An educational program of great worth to operators of small firms was given a trial run during the spring of 1954 in cooperation with two educational institutions. The courses were so successful that the program is being extended rapidly by many educational

institutions throughout the country. These courses have been or are being offered in 39 educational institutions during the 1954-55 school year. By the end of this fiscal year, approximately 1, 800 businessmen will have completed such courses to increase their administrative know-how.

A full amplification of the accomplishments which have been summed up here and other programs not discussed are contained in this Report. However, it does not fully encompass all the activities, thinking and influence of this Agency. There are other Government agencies which, in the course of their work, must consider the impact of their policies on small business and which influence the economic climate in which small as well as large business must live. As the advocate of small business in the National Administration, the Small Business Administration has working arrangements with other Government agencies, such as the Department of Commerce, the Federal Trade Commission, the Securities and Exchange Commission, the General Accounting Office, the General Services Administration, the Atomic Energy 

Commission, the Department of Labor and the Department of 

Defense. Through these channels, the Small Business Adminis tration expresses its point of view on legislation and administrative policies affecting small business, and thus is able to enhance its contribution in protecting small business.

CHAPTER II ECONOMIC POSITION OF SMALL BUSINESS 

In his State of the Union Message, the President declared that "... the transition to a peace-time economy is largely behind us. 

The economic outlook is good." Subsequently, in his Economic Report the President stated: "The vigor of the recent recovery, which has already made up half of the preceding decline in industrial production, suggests that economic expansion will probably continue during coming months. It holds out the promise that we shall achieve a high and satisfactory level of employment and production within the current year, "I

The fact that "the economic outlook is good" is important to small business. In a healthy economic climate, the position of small business is strengthened. There are more opportunities for free entry into business and for the expression and growth of personal initiative and individual judgment. The likelihood of full and free competition is increased. 

The Small Business Administration's field offices report each month on the economic situation and the health of small business in their areas. These reports similarly indicate that we are in a period of economic expansion. At the same time, they point out continuing problems which face small business. Prominent among these problems are the reduction of subcontract opportunities for small firms where prime contracts have been curtailed, the high cost of carrying inventory, and the necessity for more modern equipment and other facilities if small firms are to keep up in the competitive race. The field office reports also discuss a wide range of other small business problems, such as lack of facilities by small firms to engage in product research; pressure on small firms due to increasing mergers; influx of imports which jeopardize domestic markets; the chronic problem of surplus labor in sections which are undergoing economic readjustment; and the difficulty experienced by small firms in tapping the equity capital market.

These field office reports indicate the wide boundaries of the small business problem. They also emphasize the fact that even in a

period of economic expansion and buoyant confidence as to the over-all economic outlook, the best solution of some small business problems may be found through Government measures supplementing private initiative.

A study of employment data indicates that, with the exception of firms which have three or fewer employees, the small firms' proportion of total paid employment in business is being reduced. Comparable employment data for the years 1945 through 1951 are available from the Office of Business Economics, Department of Commerce.

In comparing employment changes during this period, it is better to start with 1947 than either of the two preceding years, which were marked by a rapid increase in business population and reconversion from war to civilian production. By 1947 the business population and business employment were on a more normal basis. 

In the case of very small firms those with three or fewer employees there is an increase in the proportion of employees. 

This is understandable in view of the rush to establish new businesses during the several years after the war. 

At the other end of the spectrum, in the case of firms with 1,000 or more employees, there was a noticeable increase in the proportion of employees between 1947 and 1951. This increase was registered at the expense of firms in all the other brackets, that is, those with between 4 and 1,000 employees.

As will be seen in the table below, between 1947 and 1951, there was an increase of 11 percent in the number of businesses of all types and of 7 percent in the total number of paid employees. The totals for firms with three or fewer employees increased by 13 and 14 percent respectively. Firms with 4 to 500 employees increased by 6 percent in number and showed the same or a slightly greater percentage increase in paid employment. Because not very many firms with fewer than 500 employees grew enough to employ 500 or more, there was little change in either the number or employment of firms in the size category of 500 to 1, 000 employees. On the other hand, there was a 3 percent increase in the number and an 8 percent increase in the employment of those firms with 1,000 or more employees.

New Business Incorporations 

Figures on recent business incorporations are a significant index of business growth. They include not only new businesses, but also the conversion of existing businesses into corporations. In 1953 there were 102, 545 new incorporations compared with 92, 819 in the preceding year. This same trend continued into 1954. In 1954 each month witnessed an increase in the number of incorporations over the same month of the preceding year. The total for the year was 117, 164.

Financial Position of Small Business 

Charts and discussions which appeared in the two previous Semi-Annual Reports of the Small Business Administration underlined

the deterioration of the financial position of small, independent manufacturers compared with the larger manufacturing firms. However, this long decline in the net sales and earnings of small firms appears to have been stopped. There is evidence that the trend of the past few years has finally been halted.

This belief is based on an analysis of the Quarterly Financial Reports of the Federal Trade Commission and the Securities and Exchange Commission. These reports provide the most current data on the financial position of small business. 

Net Sales 

The quarterly index of net sales of corporations having assets under $1, 000, 000 declined more or less steadily from the final quarter of 1952, when it was 117, to the first quarter of 1954, when it was 94. From this point, the quarterly index rose and reached 101 during the third quarter. These indexes are based on the annual data published by the U. S. Treasury Department in "Statistics of Income" for the years 1947-1949. That is, 1947-1949 equals 100 (Chart 4).

The annual index of net sales of the larger corporations rose rapidly from 100 in 1949 to 120 in 1950 and 140 in 1951. Another sharp rise in large corporations' net sales occurred beginning with the last quarter of 1952 and the index rose to 155 in the second quarter of 1953. 

Thereafter, through the third quarter of 1954, the index declined to 141. This decline developed partly because of the reduction in defense expenditures and partly because of a change in the demand for certain lines of durable goods for civilian use, chiefly automobiles. 

Although data which would permit an analysis of net sales by asset size during the final quarter of 1954 are not available, it is known that total sales increased, and it seems reasonable to conclude that net sales have recently been rising for both small and large corporations.

Financial Factors Affecting Small Business 

A conspicuous feature of the economic climate has been the sluggishness in business loans by commercial banks during most of 1954. In December 1953 weekly reporting member banks of the Federal 

Reserve System reported a decrease of $139, 000, 000 in business loans outstanding compared with December 1952. A different story was recorded in 1954. In March, the figure was $535, 000, 000 below the 

December 1953 figure. In June, it was $1, 433, 000, 000 below the 

December 1953 figure and in September, $2,283,000,000 (Chart 6). 

The primary reason for the decline was the slow-up in business activity accompanied by a steady liquidation of inventory.. Compared with a year earlier, business loans at the end of September were down 

9 percent, while inventories had declined 5 percent in the same period.

A very close relationship had prevailed between business loans and business inventories after World War II. However, after the 

Korean conflict, business loans shot ahead of inventories because, as the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland states, they included "an unusually large volume of capital loans as opposed to loans for inventory purposes. With the rapid build-up of plant and equipment expenditures, there appears to have been considerable reliance on bank loans for construction and other capital purposes." Now, loans and inventories are resuming the close relationship that prevailed from 1946 through 1951. The reduction in total business inventories for the year which ended in September, 1954, amounted to $4.2 billion, of which $3. 4 billion were concentrated in manufacturing inventories. This reduction in inventories was accompanied by the drop in business loans discussed above. However, as business activity resumed its upward climb and as inventory liquidation came to a halt, business loan activity turned upward again. Loans outstanding of weekly reporting member banks of the Federal Reserve System increased from $20, 783 million in August, 1954 to $22, 347 million in December.

The need today seems to be largely for long-term capital to purchase fixed assets rather than for short-term capital for operating purposes. The result has been a substantial increase in new corporate issues and mortgages. A continuance of the capital spending boom will intensify the demand for the longer-term form of debt. It is noteworthy that the normal type of commercial bank credit is for short-term working capital for inventories; in the case of Small Business Administration loans, the longer-term credit is helpful in projects for capital expansion. 

Lack of Equity Capital 

A problem in the financial area which is very serious is the inability to help small business concerns obtain venture capital. 

Typical statements from the Small Business Administration Regional 

Offices are these: The Denver Office states, "From a financial assistance standpoint, the small business owner in this Region is faced with a never-ending problem of finding venture capital. The Boston Office says, "The inventor of a new product or idea finds it extremely difficult to find additional and new funds needed to bring the invention or idea to a state of completion." The Dallas Office says, "In many instances,

an analysis of small firms' financial statements discloses that they are under capitalized and need equity capital, which is hard to find. 

Lacking sources of equity capital, small businesses are attempting to obtain Small Business Administration loans, but cannot qualify. 11 The Small Business Administration, under its present authorities, is unable to provide such capital but is offering every assistance possible in counseling these concerns on the amount of capital required for a sound operation, suggesting procedures to be followed, and in directing them to possible sources. Negotiated vs. Advertised Contracts 

The Department of Defense continues to do most of its purchasing by negotiation rather than by advertising. As much as 88. 3 percent of the dollar value of new procurement in fiscal year 1954 was by negotiation. This method was utilized in more than 90 percent of all procurement actions. The preference for the negotiated method of purchasing unquestionably reduced the volume of possible contracting with small enterprises. The negotiation method tends to concentrate procurement with established sources, thereby eliminating from consideration competent firms which would be able to produce at lower prices if a public announcement of purchases permitted wider competition for contracts. It is generally agreed that, as a rule, small firms receive their fullest and most equitable opportunity to participate in

Government procurement under formal advertising procedures. At the same time, the increase in competition stimulated by advertised methods is generally accepted as productive of the lowest prices. 

The small business share of all advertised military procurement has increased from about onehalf in 1951 to nearly twothirds in fiscal years 1953 and 1954. On the other hand, small business received only

Subcontracting 

Reports received from the Small Business Administration's Regional Directors reiterate small business complaints about the decline in subcontracting opportunities. Our Cleveland Office states, for example, "For the concern doing general work without end products or specialized manufacturing method, conditions are worse. " 

The Chicago Office reports, "Consistently decreasing subcontracting opportunities, both commercial and Government, are creating serious management problems among small operators. Firms incapable of producing end items (component manufacturers) are complaining bitterly about lack of enough business to keep their shops operating profitably. " Our Boston Office states, "The continued

cutback of prime contracts has added to the decrease in subcontract opportunities."

There have been specific protests that major aircraft companies which are using Government-owned facilities and equipment are drastically curtailing subcontracting activities.

Factors which result in restriction of subcontracting opportunities are changed types of production and production schedules; high overhead costs of maintaining the plants of large prime contractors; the fear that too great a volume of subcontracts might reflect adversely upon the prime contractor; and the desire to maintain Governmentowned equipment in maximum possible production to avoid the risk of having it withdrawn.

The "weapons system" concept of procurement by the Air Force will tend to restrict opportunities for subcontractors, since it puts responsibility for delivery of a completely fitted aircraft on the air frame contractor. Many small producers who formerly were prime contractors for the Air Force say that they have found production virtually impossible in their new role. Further, they have cited instances where the major primes have used their new position to obtain engineering information in the guise of considering a subcontract to be awarded to a small business only to use this data in the manufacture of the particular item themselves. These matters have been brought to the attention of proper officials of the Department of Defense. 

New Procurement Policy

A Department of Defense Directive, No. 3005. 3, issued 

December 7, 1954, relating to the maintenance of an industrial mobilization base, shifted away from the single, lowest cost, most efficient producer concept which had previously been emphasized. 

The former policy meant a concentration of orders in a single supplier who was able to produce the defense item most efficiently. The new directive, however, listed nine considerations that would be written into procurement regulations to guide purchasing officials among them: Maintaining multiple sources of supply disbursed geographically; avoiding undue concentration in a few leading suppliers; preserving management experience and essential skilled labor forces; and using existing industrial capacity.

According to the Chairman of the Federal Trade Commission, Edward F. Howrey, it is not the number of mergers which is significant; it is their probable impact on competition. Merger activity has been particularly strong recently in the baking, paper, textile, dairy, chemical, automotive and primary metals industries. CHAPTER III FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE 

During the period August 1, 1954, to January 31, 1955, practically all phases of Small Business Administration's Financial 

Assistance Program showed increases over the preceding periods. 

This was true not only of loan approvals, but also of the other financial assistance activities, including counseling designed to solve the financial problems of small business concerns in other ways than through the extension of Government credit. The number of loans made under the Disaster Loan Program sharply increased because of the major role played by the Small Business Administration in repairing the damage done by Hurricanes Carol, Edna and Hazel.

During this 6-month period, the Small Business Administration made 560 business loans in a gross amount of $26, 959, 971 to assist small firms. This compares with 551 business loans in a gross amount of $31, 000, 562 in the previous 6-month period. In the Disaster 

Loan Program, 844 loans, amounting to $4,920, 747, were approved.

This compares with 124 disaster loans, amounting to $933, 902, made in the preceding 6-month period. To date, the Small Business Administration has approved 1,166 business loans for a gross amount of $61,716, 433, and 1,040 disaster loans, totaling $6, 082, 331.

The Financial Assistance Program has been aided by the development of close cooperation between the banks of the Nation and the Small Business Administration. To date, banks in nearly every 

State in the Union, as well as in Alaska, the District of Columbia, and Hawaii, have made loans to small business concerns with varying percentages of participation by this Administration. Through this cooperation and through direct loans approved by the Small Business Administration over $60, 000, 000 of credit has been made available to small business. Probably little more than $25, 000, 000 of the funds so provided will be withdrawn from the Federal Treasury. This $60, 000, 000 does not include outside financing made possible through this Administration's Financial Counseling Program. 

The presently authorized revolving fund of the Small Business Administration is $80,000,000. Of this amount $48, 675, 067 was committed as of December 31, 1954. It is expected that the uncommitted balance of $31, 324, 933 will be committed before the end of May 1955. 

Inquiries and requests for assistance by small business concerns are being received in our Regional Offices at an average rate of approximately 14, 300 per month.

As a result of simplification of procedures and staffing of Washington and field offices with experienced and qualified financial 29

personnel, the heavy backlog of loan applications which had accumulated during the previous semi-annual periods was sharply reduced in this period and is presently at a more normal level. The Administration's workload has been heavier in the past 6 months than during any other period due largely to several major catastrophes which required disaster loan assistance and the transfer of Reconstruction Finance Corporation disaster loans to this Agency for liquidation. In spite of this, the Small Business Administration has been able to accomplish more than in previous periods in keeping pace with new loan applications and in extending financial counseling services to small business concerns.

Financial Counseling 

Experience has shown that the small business which encounters financial difficulties generally thinks first of borrowing funds from whatever source is available, failing to recognize other possible courses of action which would eliminate the need for incurring debt. For the most part such enterprises are not financially able to employ specialists in financial management. It is in assisting these firms that the Adminis tration's Financial Counseling Program is most effective.

Whenever possible, experienced financial specialists in the Small Business Administration field offices assist the small firms to solve their financial problems by pointing out solutions which do not involve intermediate or long-term borrowing. Often the problem is undercapitalization of the firm, or perhaps a need for inventory liquidation, revised credit policies or changes in product pricing principles. 

A contemplated expansion by the firm may be too extensive or premature, its product line may be too diversified or too restricted, or its sales policies may need review.

These are only a few of the possible factors considered by the Small Business Administration in counseling small firms which have financial management problems.

Where a small firm does need intermediate or long-term credit or short-term working capital for general business purposes or normal growth, the Small Business Administration often can assist it in preparing a more effective request for funds to a prospective investor or financial institution.

Through offering advice and counsel on financial problems, facilitating transactions with private lending institutions, and providing other specialized services, the Small Business Administration has been able to assist many small business concerns. Exactly how many have benefited from the program is not known since the results of any advisory service are difficult to measure. On the other hand, many concrete examples of aid provided indicate that the benefits of the program are far-reaching:

1. A small woodworking concern in New England applied for a loan of $1,000 for 3 months to complete a contract to manufacture wooden boxes. The firm's local bank had declined to make the loan, although there appeared to be ample security and earnings. A Small

Business Administration financial specialist reviewed the application with a bank a few miles from the applicant's place of business and the bank agreed to grant the loan. 

2. A Midwest bank applied for a 75 percent deferred participation by the Small Business Administration in a loan of $110, 000 to a dry cleaning and laundry concern. After discussions between representatives of this Agency and the bank, the bank agreed to make the entire loan on its own, and to the satisfaction of all concerned. 

3. A West Coast concern applied to the Administration for a $50,000 loan, part of which was for debt retirement, part for purchase of new equipment and the remainder for working capital. After a Small 

Business Administration financial specialist had had several discussions with the firm's bank of account, the bank agreed to make a mortgage loan and an open line of credit available to the firm, thereby solving its financial problem. 

4. As a result of Small Business Administration counseling, a southern canning concern was able to obtain additional bank financing through a mortgage loan on its real estate and a working capital loan, secured by a pledge of warehouse receipts, whereas both types of collateral had heretofore been required in connection with the company's more limited borrowings which had become inadequate to handle increased volume. 

5. Through Small Business Administration counseling, a 

West Virginia company was able to obtain financing needed for expansion from a local bank and from sale of stock to some 300 local residents.

Two of the concerns, located in Georgia, obtained loans of $45, 000 and $70,000, respectively; the other firm, located in Florida, obtained a loan of $150,000. 

7. A Michigan concern needed funds to pay delinquent Federal excise taxes. Agency personnel suggested that a plan for the liquidation of the taxes be submitted to the Director of Internal Revenue, and have since learned that an equitable plan has been arranged which eliminates need for outside financing for the firm.

8. A Texas bank wrote an applicant for a bank-Small Business

The only major change made in basic loan policy during the past 6-month period was a reduction in the interest rate on disaster business loans from 5 percent per annum to 3 percent. loans had previously been made at the 3 percent rate. 

Lending Program 

Disaster home

The lending program of the Small Business Administration is divided into two main parts as set forth in Sections 207(a) and 207(b) 

of the Small Business Act of 1953. 

Section 207(a) of the Act gives the Small Business Adminis tration authority to make loans to small business enterprises when credit is not otherwise available to them on reasonable terms. The loans are intended to fill a gap in the financing provided small firms by private financial institutions and are designed to stimulate and preserve the initiative, independence and enterprise of small businesses. 

To the greatest extent possible, the Small Business Administration is striving to provide this credit in cooperation with private lending institutions. Its success in this is evidenced by the fact that about twothirds of all loans approved are in participation with banks and other financial institutions.

In order to expand the benefits to small business concerns and to promote a balanced national economy, the bank-Small Business Administration participation loan plan presents the greatest possible benefit to the participating institution consistent with the intent of the Small Business Act. For example, the plan enables a bank to broaden its lending activities, while maintaining desired liquidity of assets. 

The bank is assured a fair return on money loaned and is able to give better service to its depositors.

Section 207(b) of the Small Business Act of 1953 gives this 

Administration authority to make loans for the relief of owners of homes and businesses suffering losses due to floods and other catastrophes. 

Loans can be made only to the extent of actual losses not covered by insurance and, as in the case of regular business loans, cannot be approved if the required financing is otherwise available on reasonable terms. Because of the purpose of the loans as a rehabilitation measure, much more liberal credit standards are applied to disaster loans than is prudent for loans made under Section 207(a). Interest rates, too, are lower. But they are in reality loans which are repaid, and this plan seems much preferable to any one involving outright grants. There is constant collaboration with the Red Cross, to which cases are referred when there seems no possibility of repayment. On the other hand, the 

Red Cross refers cases to our field offices where repayment of advanced funds seems possible.

In the administration of its direct lending program under 

Section 207(a) of the Small Business Act, the Small Business Ad ministration is governed by the policies established by the Loan Policy Board and the requirements of the Act that "loans be of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment". 

The Administrator applies the most liberal interpretation of these policies and requirements possible, consistent with sound credit principles but at the same time is fully aware of his responsibility to protect Government funds. Therefore, each loan application is thoroughly analyzed and a loan is never approved or declined without full consideration of all factors concerned. The Agency's lending program is under continual study, and the area in which credit can be provided safely is being constantly reviewed.

Loan Disbursements 

At the beginning of the last semi-annual period, disbursements of business loans were running considerably behind loan approvals. In an effort to determine whether the Agency's loan closing procedures were responsible for this delay, the Administration sought the advice of several bankers and assigned staff personnel to banks to study their procedures. It was found that, in general, the procedures used by the 

Small Business Administration were similar to those used by most banks.

A further study of the situation revealed that in most instances, in the case of direct loans, the delay was due to normal problems of closing, such as title searches, awaiting completion of construction where interim financing was being utilized, etc.

It was determined that many of the delays by banks in making disbursements on approved loans were due partly to their lack of knowledge of the Small Business Administration closing procedures and consequent failure to recognize the similarity to their own. Therefore, Agency financial specialists within the Regional Offices have devoted a considerable amount of time to assisting the participating financial institutions in properly effecting loan disbursements. The result has been a substantial improvement in ratio of loan disbursements to loan approvals.

Any unusual delays in loan closings now encountered are almost always due to failure of the borrower to meet loan terms adequately and promptly. In the meantime, this Administration is continuing its efforts. to simplify the loan procedures, without at the same time sacrificing necessary and proper safeguards for Government funds.

During the last 6-month period, the Joint Determination Program of the Small Business Administration increased greatly in effectiveness. The Joint Determination Program The Joint Determination Program operated by the Small 

Business Administration in cooperation with the various military departments is directed toward obtaining for small business a fair share of Government procurement by restricting for competitive bidding or negotiation among small firms all or a percentage of certain individual military purchases.

To operate this program in the field the Administration has assigned 18 representatives to screen unclassified purchases in 34 major military procurement offices. These offices are listed in Appendix B. 

Small Business Administration representatives advise pro curement officials of these offices of proposed purchases which they believe should be set aside, in whole or in part, for competition among small business firms. Each such request for earmarking all or a part of a procurement for small business is reviewed by the military procurement officials and either agreed to, modified or rejected.

Improvements in Joint Determination Program Representatives of the Small Business Administration held numerous conferences with Department of Defense officials between October 21, 1953, and March 2, 1954, in an effort to obtain an agreement which would strengthen the Joint Determination Program and be still more advantageous to small firms. These conferences resulted in a new agreement between the Administrator of the Small Business 

Administration and the Assistant Secretary of Defense for Supply and 

Logistics which provided for even greater cooperation in behalf of small business. This agreement was issued as Department of Defense 

Instruction No. 4100.9, dated March 10, 1954. The agreement pro vided, for example, that procurements involving a minimum estimated expenditure of $10,000 or more would be made eligible for joint determination action, whereas the previously required expenditure had been $25,000 or more. It also contained the provision that the Defense 

Department would furnish bid sets, specifications and drawings on procurements under joint determination to Small Business Administration field offices. In addition, the Department of Defense agreed that eight other proposals by the Small Business Administration to strengthen the Joint Determination Program would be placed in the implementing instructions of the Army, Navy and Air Force. 

On the whole, good cooperation has been received from military purchasing offices since the Services issued implementing directives to DOD Instruction No. 4100.9 of March 10, 1954. Such cooperation is paramount to the success of any joint program to obtain a fair share of Government business for small firms, since, under the authority given the Small Business Administration in the Small Business Act of 1953, the Agency can recommend only, not demand, that suitable procurements be restricted to small business. 

The future success of the program depends upon the degree of cooperation received in carrying out the mandate of Congress as expressed in the legislation creating the Small Business Administration. 

Evidence of the better relationships with the Military Services is shown by the fact that for the 6-month period, August 1, 1954, through January 1955, only 3. 3 percent of the number and 

11.7 percent of the dollar amount of the joint determinations initiated by the Small Business Administration were rejected as compared with a rejection rate of 21.4 percent of the number and 22.3 percent of the dollar amount during the first 6 months of the Small Business 

Administration's operation a year earlier.

Although excellent relationships now exist between Small 

Business Administration representatives and Armed Services per son nel in most purchasing offices, there still are areas of misunderstanding concerning what specific items can be earmarked properly for small business. The main reason for rejecting joint determinations, as given by contracting officers, is an unwillingness to restrict entire procurements to small firms if it is believed that large firms might bid slightly lower than established small business suppliers. While it can be shown that no price differential can possibly result from agreement on partial joint determinations, there are many small procurements where partial joint determinations are not appropriate, as division will not produce more than one economical production run. 

On December 16, 1954, the Department of Defense announced the release of a revised directive outlining its small business policy. 

This directive and its implementation by the Armed Services procurement agencies is designed to provide for a more equitable opportunity for small business to compete for military contracts. 

The Small Business Administration was consulted during the formative stages of this directive and the final draft was forwarded to the Administration prior to its release. When implemented in the manner and intent in which it was formulated, the directive will provide for a more comprehensive and effective small business program by the Department of Defense. 

The most significant event in the history of the Small Business Administration for the improvement of our relationship with the Department of Defense occurred during the closing weeks of this report period. At the invitation of the Assistant Secretary of Defense for

Supply and Logistics, the Small Business Administration appointed a liaison officer with the Department of Defense, with offices in the Pentagon. The incumbent of this position operates at the Defense 

Department level and participates in all conferences and discussions and in the preparation of directives concerning small business activities of the Department of Defense and the three Services (Army, Navy and Air Force). Participation at the Defense Department's plan ning level by our liaison officer has already resulted in changes in military purchasing procedures which will benefit the small businessman seeking Defense contracts. It is hoped that future participation of our liaison officer in other Defense small business activities will serve to increase to an appreciable degree the small business share of the Defense dollar.

One of the main objectives of the Small Business Administration must be to increase the number of items that are acceptable for restriction to small business competition. This objective can be accomplished by seeing that the military bidders lists include a sufficient number of names of established or potential small business suppliers with ability to produce the items to be procured. also can mesh admirably with the program of the Department of Defense, announced December 7, 1954, to maintain a sound mobilization base and to achieve dispersal of its suppliers. This program also can be implemented by the Small Business Administration selective referral program under which competent small-business suppliers are provided information concerning military and civilian requirements at both the prime and subcontract levels.

Procurement Assistance Counseling Program 

One of the most important steps that small firms seeking Government business can take is to request placement on all appropriate bidders lists. In its Counseling Program, the Small Business 

Administration not only stresses this fact, but also helps the small firms to determine the proper bidders lists for their product or products. Agency staff members also guide small firms in requesting inclusion on the bidders lists. The Small Business Administration, by agreement with the Defense Department, may recommend additional small business concerns for inclusion in the list of bidders or firms to be solicited for any specific procurement which is suitable for performance by small business.

To be effective, counseling of small business must consist of general guidance, with specific answers to detailed procurement problems. Firms are advised that they can, upon request, receive from the Small Business Administration notification of available procurement opportunities suitable for their facilities in both the prime and subcontract fields. They are informed how bid sets, specifications and drawings may be obtained or where they are available for inspection. Each Small Business Administration office has a set of packing and packaging specifications available for reference and estimating purposes. These are valuable to small firms in preparing quotations on proposed Government purchases.

In addition to providing information on selling to the Government, the Small Business Administration, through its Regional and Branch Offices and its representatives in procurement centers, is called upon to assist small business in many other ways. For example, it provides advice and assistance in connection with:

1. Furnishing information on bidding technicalities in both advertised and negotiated procurements. 

2. Getting on specific bidders lists having inadequate small business competition. 

3. Expediting inspections of pre-production samples of components or completed end items. 

4. Getting products accepted for inclusion on qualified products lists. 

5. Obtaining bid sets, prints and specifications for specific procurements. 

6. Securing performance bonds. 

7. Obtaining progress payments or advance payments. 

8. Obtaining payment of invoices. 

9. Handling of claims. 

During the first 18 months of the Agency's existence, its representatives at military procurement centers supplied information on 4, 439 occasions to representatives of small firms concerning procurement and other problems. These Administration representatives also report that 4, 378 firms have been recommended to contracting officers for specific procurement opportunities since August 1, 1953. 

In the 7 months since July 1, 1954, personnel in Small Business Administration Regional and Branch Offices had 15, 800 inquiries from small firms regarding procurement assistance matters. 

In June 1954, a Procurement Information Center was opened in the Small Business Administration Branch Office in Baltimore, Maryland. This center has proved to be of great value to small business concerns. In September 1954, a Counseling Center was opened in the Washington headquarters of the Agency, so that representatives of small business firms may obtain expert counsel in procurement, financial and all other Small Business Administration services. Proposed purchases by Federal civilian purchasing offices in the Washington area, as well as those military procurements under joint determination, are classified and displayed in the center. 

Since it was opened in September, the office has been visited by 1, 247

persons seeking procurement and other information, and has received 1, 133 telephone inquiries.

Our counseling assistance is made more effective by the cooperation of other Federal agencies. Civilian agencies cooperate with Small Business Administration field offices by furnishing them with current invitations for bids, which the offices then bring to the attention of small firms. Small Business Administration Regional and Branch Offices also arrange to obtain current procurement information from Federal procuring installations in their areas. 

This enables the Agency to provide a wealth of procurement information to small firms. For example, the General Services Administration forwards information on all purchases to be made in its regions to the Small Business Administration Regional Offices serving the areas. The Foreign Operations Administration furnishes Small 

Business Administration field offices with advance information on export opportunities under programs financed by that Administration. 

This information includes descriptions of items, quantities, names and addresses of purchasing offices, and shipping instructions. 

Here is a specific case which illustrates the wide range of assistance which the Small Business Administration provides small business concerns in the fields of procurement, production, management and financial assistance:

The Dallas Regional Office of the Agency notified a Texas firm with only six employees of a pending procurement for minesweeping floats which was suited to its facilities. The owner of the firm visited the Small Business Administration office and an industrial specialist of the Agency reviewed the prints with him and counseled him on preparing his bid. Although the company was low bidder, its ability to finance the contract was questioned by the contracting officer. The owner was then advised to apply to the Small 

Business Administration for a loan for $100, 000 to process the contract. The loan was granted, and the award, for $386, 600, was made to the firm.

In the early stages of the contract a delay in production resulted because one of the small firm's suppliers was unable to furnish needed parts because of hurricane damage to his plant. 

However, the Small Business Administration worked with the Navy Department to overcome this delay and expedite shipments until full production was reached. The small firm was high in its praise for the advice and assistance given by the Agency during this critical period.

When the contract was half completed, the Small Business Administration notified the firm of an additional purchase of the same item by the Navy. The Navy agreed to award the new contract to the company provided that it was assured of financing to do the work and that the Small Business Administration would issue to the company a 

Certificate of Competency covering the contract. 

The Small Business Administration suggested that the company's fine record on the contract on which it was presently working justified.the new award without a Certificate of Competency, provided adequate finances were made available. The Navy agreed, and the Agency extended the maturity date of the present loan, authorizing use of the cash collateral account set up for the first contract to process the additional work. This made adequate finances available, as the first contract would be completed before production was started on the new procurement. The Navy then awarded the contract, which totaled $271, 031, to the small firm. The firm, which originally had only six employees, now has more than 40.

Solutions to Problems Occasioned by Insufficient Copies of 

Bid Sets or Prints 

A problem faced by Government procurement offices is the difficulty of determining how many bid sets and prints to reproduce.

When the Small Business Administration is advised by a small firm that it is unable to obtain a bid set with appropriate attachments, it invites the firm to inspect the bidding forms at one of the Agency's Regional or Branch Offices, or suggests other Government offices where the bid set may be available for inspection. The firm also is advised by the Small Business Administration to apply immediately for placement on the appropriate bidders list, so that it may receive bid sets for future procurements direct from the purchasing installation.

At times, insufficient copies of prints or drawings have had the effect of restricting the opportunity for small firms to bid on a purchase. To combat this problem, the Small Business Administration and some Navy procurement offices have adopted a plan whereby reproducible prints are forwarded to the Administration's Regional Offices. Firms wishing to bid on the items then may have the prints reproduced at their own cost, and may obtain a bid set from the Navy. 

This plan has proved very advantageous both for small firms desiring to bid on the purchases and the purchasing offices themselves. 

As a result of consultations with the Small Business Administration, the Defense Department has made a change in the Armed Services Procurement Regulations which also should help to overcome the problem of insufficient copies of bid sets. Under this plan, which is optional for procurement offices, advance notice of a procurement will be sent to firms on the purchasing agency's bidders list and also will be published in the "Synopsis of U. S. Government Proposed 

Procurement, Sales and Contract Awards", published daily by the U.S. Department of Commerce. It will be stated that all requests for bid sets received within 10 days will be honored. This procedure, when used, will result in firms having adequate advance notice to request Air Force bid forms. It also will allow the procuring agency to make a better determination of the number of bid sets which it should prepare.

Furnishing Additional Small Business Sources of Supply Another Small Business Administration program which benefits both small firms and purchasing offices is that of furnishing competent small business suppliers of items for which there is insufficient small business competition. This is done by having Small 

Business Administration field offices locate small firms in their areas which are competent to furnish items for which Small Business Administration procurement center representatives have indicated competition is needed. This increases participation in bidding by

small firms and often results in lower prices to the Government. 

Further, it allows small firms to compete for Government purchases of which they would otherwise be unaware. Small Business Administration Regional Offices have reported that since July 1, 1954, 2, 251 small business sources of supply have been furnished to procurement agencies in response to specific requests.

Subcontracting Program The Small Business Administration's Subcontracting 

Program is designed to assist qualified small firms in obtaining a fair percentage of the subcontracts let by prime contractors performing work for the Government.

The Administration urges prime contractors to place more subcontracts with small concerns and encourages them to do so by bringing to their attention the facilities of capable small businesses. 

The reduction in military spending and expansion of their own facilities has resulted in prime contractors seeking fewer subcontractors. 

Instead of subcontracting to a greater extent, the present tendency of many prime contractors is to keep work in their own plants or to place a larger volume with affiliated plants or subsidiaries. This is especially true in the aircraft industry, where a decline in Government orders and design changes in aircraft have caused manufacturers to retain in their own plants work of a type previously subcontracted. 

Such action has placed firms dependent on aircraft subcontracting in considerable jeopardy.

Tax Amortization Program 

The Government's accelerated tax amortization program is an incentive, in the form of tax relief, for concerns to expand defense production facilities. The Small Business Administration assists small concerns to obtain a fair share of the benefits of accelerated tax amortization by interceding in their behalf with the Office of Defense Mobilization and delegate agencies in order to obtain prompt

and favorable action on their applications for certificates of necessity. 

These certificates give firms the privilege of writing off, over a period of 60 months, all or part of the cost of new or expanded facilities.

The Small Business Administration performs two principal functions in regard to tax amortization.

At the request of the Office of Defense Mobilization and the delegate agencies, the Administration obtains information from the field pertaining to small business applicants. Past experience shows that many applications lack certain data necessary to reach a decision on them. Small Business Administration field offices have been particularly effective in obtaining additional information required by the delegate agencies to complete the processing of applications from small firms. 

In other cases, the Agency, while not participating directly in accumulating information in support of an application, is asked by a small firm to check on the status of its application, and to advise it of any adverse development or other circumstance of which it should be informed.

Both operations require continuing close liaison with the 

Office of Defense Mobilization and the delegate agencies. 

Although many expansion goals have been filled, the consolidation or suspension of others, participation on the interagency Ad Hoc Committee on Expansion Goals, and requests from small firms for assistance, provide a regular workload in this area for the Agency. 

An important intangible feature of the Agency's Tax Amorti zation Program is its effectiveness in assuring that the Office of Defense Mobilization and the various delegate agencies will give careful consideration to applications from small concerns. Production Pools. 

The Small Business Administration gives advice and assistance in the formation of small business production pools and formally approves their proposed operations in accordance with Section 217 of the Small Business Act of 1953. This approval is subject to the con currence of the Attorney General and the Chairman of the Federal Trade Commission. 

Production pools consist primarily of groups of small concerns voluntarily associated together to obtain and perform jointly, contracts for the production of articles, equipment, supplies, and materials or for the furnishing of services, needed for the defense effort. The firms have a mutual agreement as to their organization, relationship, and procedure.

The formation of production pools has greatly declined, as evidenced by the fact that no new pools have been approved during this reporting period, and only one formal request for approval is pending. One of the previously approved pools has disbanded and its immunity from Federal antitrust laws and the Federal Trade Commission Act has been withdrawn. This reduces the total number of approved pools to 28, with 605 member companies and approximately 28, 575 employees. No new members of existing pools were approved during this period. However, interest in pools continues, though to a limited degree. Small Business Administration Regional Offices have reported approximately 30 inquiries regarding the formation of

production pools during this reporting period. These are in addition to a number of direct inquiries to Washington. 

Of the 28 approved small business production pools, only 11 consider themselves active. The others are not actively seeking 

Government contracts but are maintaining their organizations on a standby basis.

The total number of contracts awarded to approved pools to date is 210, amounting to approximately $5, 807, 752. Of these, four contracts totaling $2, 701, 305 have been canceled for default.

The total number of awards shows an increase over the last reporting period of nine contracts amounting to $97, 752. In addition to prime contracts, pools have reported receipt of 50 subcontracts totaling $2, 770, 000.

Facilities Inventory for Small Plants 

An inventory of small business productive facilities is maintained on a decentralized basis in the Administration's Regional Offices. Small companies interested in registration are furnished a questionnaire on which they list their productive facilities and other pertinent information. On receipt of the completed questionnaire by the Regional Office, the data are reviewed and the company classified by major product or capabilities. The company is then coded for quick identification in accordance with the Standard Industrial Classification.

The purpose of the facilities inventory is to insure a means of quickly ascertaining the productive capacity of small plants in a given area. The data furnished by the company is carefully classified as to capacity, machine tools, number of employees, types of products, and other essential production factors. This information can then be evaluated to determine a manufacturer's industrial potential, including the kinds of production for which his facilities are now suited, and the types to which the facilities can be converted in case of full mobilization for defense. Many small firms now engaged in producing a peace-time, civilian item can, when the need arises, produce a wide variety of defense items which have no civilian counterpart.

During the last 6 months, the Small Business Administration Regional Offices have been actively engaged in adapting part of the registration data on each company to a mechanized operation to simplify referral and notification activities. This has been accomplished

by the use of stencil plates which contain a minimum of necessary identification and descriptive data on the company, and which can be used for addressing correspondence to it. From such records, the Agency's field staff can readily obtain skeleton information on individual concerns, without going to the main alphabetical files. 

These condensed records are maintained in Standard Industrial 

Classification code numerical order for rapid reference.

The inventory is now used principally in assisting the registered companies to obtain prime and subcontracts, in locating new sources of supply for the military services, and in locating scarce or specialized machine tools and equipment when calls for them are received.

Small Business Administration Regional Offices are authorized to cooperate with State and local governments, Chambers of Commerce, and trade associations in the collection of facility information in order to expand the inventory. This compares 

As of January 25, 1955, 27, 125 small plants were registered with the Small Business Administration. 

with a figure of slightly over 20, 000 at the end of the last semi-annual period. A program is now under way to enlarge this total. 

The increasing importance of the mobilization aspects of the inventory points up the need for registering more small firms that are removed from urban centers. This is now receiving major emphasis from the Agency.

The Small Business Administration plans to increase the number of firms in the facilities inventory to about 100, 000 when mechanized procedures have been developed to the point where a much larger inventory can be successfully handled.

Specifications Assistance 

A major problem facing small concerns is difficulty in bidding on Government purchases because of inability to obtain or review copies of applicable Federal and military specifications.

Experience has shown that many concerns receive bid sets without specifications; that in many cases the supply of specifications is exhausted at a particular purchasing agency due to a heavy demand in connection with a particular procurement; and that often the basic specification alone is included with bid sets, while all supplementary specifications are omitted. 

In the

Included in the Small Business Administration's continuing program of assistance in this area is the availability, at both the Washington and field offices, of four-volume reference sets comprising 392 packaging and packing specifications most often required by small concerns in selling their services and products to the Government. The Small Business Administration has established libraries of packaging and packing specifications selected and maintained specifically for the use of small concerns. period since July 1, an additional 41 packaging and packing specifications were obtained and added to the previously established reference sets. The library sets contain packaging specifications referred to most often in invitations for bids, including packaging and packing requirements for overseas shipment. The reference sets are used as a guide for Small Business Administration field personnel when answering inquiries about packaging problems or when discussing them with small firms. Moreover, when potential bidders are unable to obtain copies of particular packaging specifi cations, the Small Business Administration library set will frequently provide reference information in time to enable bidders to submit their bids before the bid opening date.

The Agency's field offices assist small concerns in obtaining copies of specifications from procurement agencies and provide liaison with other Government agencies concerning specifications problems. To make available information concerning the location of reference sets of principal Government specifications, the Small

Business Administration has published the "U. S.

Government 

Specifications Directory". 

This Directory lists more than 500 places where small business owners may locate quickly Government specifications and indexes needed to figure packing costs on Government purchases and to prepare bids. The Directory covers in detail the purchasing specifications used by the military and civilian agencies of the Government; tells how businessmen may obtain copies of particular specifications and specification indexes for continuing use; and provides pointers on using them. It is the only known directory of its type in existence, and is indexed alphabetically by States and cities to simplify the location of places where the specifications may be reviewed. The Small Business Administration analyses have resulted in numerous recommendations to General Services Administration for the deletion of possible restrictive or discriminatory provisions. 

As a further aid to small concerns, the Agency's Regional Offices have retained from bid sets received limited stocks of individual specifications and make these available to concerns which have failed to receive them with invitations for bids.

Products Assistance Program 

One of the most pressing problems of small business concerns is difficulty in keeping abreast of product and marketing developments. 

To help them in overcoming this handicap, the Small Business Administration maintains a Products Assistance Program. The program has a twofold goal: to assist in strengthening the independent small firms of the Nation, and to help in developing the actual and potential capacities of small business 

thus increasing the contribution of small firms to the national economy and to themselves. 

As contemplated, the Small Business Administration's product development activity has been of assistance to small firms which have been unsuccessful in obtaining Government contracts or which do not have the necessary facilities or are not interested in participating in Government procurement programs. It may also prove to be of increasing value to small business concerns in cushioning the shock of cutbacks in defense contracts.

In all the years before 1946, American industry spent $4.6 billion on organized research. In the 8 years following 1946, however, industry spent $9.5 billion on research--more than double the amount for the previous 170 years. The larger companies have been the main sources of these expenditures.

The Products Assistance Program of the Small Business 

Administration is designed to help small business concerns to draw upon the wealth of available information and data on product research. 

The program includes, but is not limited to, assistance in these areas: 

1. Product development 

2. Product marketing 

3. Possible new and improved products 

4. "Inventive" ideas 

5. Unpatentable ideas 

6. Government-owned patents

7. Privately owned patents 

8. Product redesign 

9. New processes

The cooperation extended by Government agencies, universities, and industrial groups has contributed in very large measure to the initial success of the program. Certificates of Competency The Certificate of Competency Program of the Small 

Business Administration has been in operation since the start of the Agency in August 1953. Through the program, small companies obtain Government prime contracts which they otherwise would be denied, due to disqualification by the procuring office for reasons of inadequate capacity or credit, or both.

A request for a Certificate of Competency is initiated by a small business concern when its low bid on a specified procurement has been rejected by the contracting officer. 

The small company submits its application for a Certificate to the Small Business Adminis tration Regional Office in its area. 

A careful and thorough plant survey and financial analysis of the company are then made by the Agency's field engineering and financial staff. Their report is then sent to the Washington Office for review and evaluation, and a decision as to whether the Certificate of Competency is to be issued.

This part of the program 

the processing of applications is probably the most important single factor in determining whether Certificates of Competency are to be of real help to small business. 

Applications are scrutinized with the greatest care in order to insure that only those companies that can actually produce are awarded 

Certificates. 

At the close of the current reporting period, the Agency had received a total of 126 applications for Certificates of Competency and had issued 59 Certificates. Contracts covered by the Certificates under which awards have been made amount to $5, 663, 705. Appendix 

C contains a list of Certificates of Competency under which contract awards were made, as reported for the period August 1, 1954, through 

January 31, 1955.

A majority of the cases to date have been in connection with contracts with the Department of the Air Force, followed closely by the Department of the Army. Military defense items covered by these certified contracts include rocket launcher assemblies, smoke generators, aircraft platform assemblies, grenade launchers, bomb slings, bomb racks, bomb trailers, boats and tents.

When it began operations, the Small Business Administration assumed responsibility for 91 active Certificates of Competency issued by its predecessor agency, the Small Defense Plants Administration. Of these 91 Certificates, 19 were still in active status as of 

January 31, 1955, and of the 19 cases, 15 were behind schedule in their production.

One contract for liners was finished approximately 2 weeks ahead of schedule, and the tent contract was completed on time. The second liner contract was delayed slightly due to materials shortages, but the contracting officer gave permission to finish the contract by utilizing small pieces of material on hand. 

Another Certificate case which is receiving very close follow-up attention involves an Army Chemical Corps procurement for smoke generators. The contractor is a small Virginia firm. 

Controversy over the manner in which the pre-award studies were conducted by the procuring agency eventually led to a hearing before the Subcommittee on Defense Activities of the Committee on Armed Services of the House of Representatives. A report on the hearing was published by the Subcommittee July 20, 1954. In its report, the 

Subcommittee expressed satisfaction with the thoroughness of the Small Business Administration investigation which resulted in issuance of the Certificate of Competency, and commented favorably on the adequacy of the analysis made in arriving at a decision favorable to the bidder. Extremely close liaison has been maintained by the Small Business Administration with the contractor, and the Agency will continue to provide any requested assistance until the contract has reached completion. This procurement amounted to $607, 101.04, which was later revised to $591, 545. 04, and the savings to the Government through award to the low bidder totaled approximately $80, 000.

The care exercised by the Small Business Administration in evaluating requests for Certificates is demonstrated by the ratio of approvals to applications; during the current semi-annual period, approximately two applications were denied for each Certificate granted. For the program to date, the ratio of approval is a little higher, being approximately 50 percent.

During the 6-month period, several cases came to light where the preliminary operations of the Certificate program appear to have resulted in contracts being awarded to small firms without the actual issuance of Certificates. In those cases, more complete and effective presentation of the small firms' qualifications by the 

Small Business Administration cleared up the contracting officers' 

doubts, and the contracts were awarded without the bidders applying for Certificates.

Better use of the Certificate of Competency Program can only come about through close cooperation between the Small Business Administration and the military agencies. As a result of 

Small Business Administration negotiations with the Department of Defense, directives were issued by the three Military Services requiring notification to the Small Business Administration when lowbidding small firms are about to be disqualified. 

Management Publications 

Every owner of a business is faced each day with decisions that must be made if the business is to continue to operate. Experience and training provide ready and certain answers to many of these problems. For others, additional facts and ideas are needed. 

Since the great bulk of business management problems have been successfully solved by some firms, the basic task of the Small Business Administration is to determine the nature of the broad problems themselves, and the solutions to them, and to present this information for the use of those who need it. Management publications are prepared with this purpose in mind. 

Care is exercised in selecting subjects which are of current interest to business owners. Authors are selected from among businessmen, professional personnel, and Government technicians who are acknowledged authorities in the subjects covered. The resulting publications are then made available to small firms through the field offices of the Small Business Administration, or, in the case of publications to be sold, through the Government Printing 

Office.

Management Courses for Small Business Owners 

The necessity for the small-business owner to concentrate on day-to-day operations prevents him from devoting much time to improving his over-all knowledge of business administration, or to learning more about planning, organization, control, and other phases of management. To assist him in becoming a well-rounded administrator, the Small Business Administration is encouraging educational institutions to provide short, practical courses in administrative management. The end purpose, of course, is to foster growth of small businesses, provide more jobs and achieve a still higher standard of living.

After almost a year of trial and experimentation, the administrative management training program of the Small Business Administration has been proved sound and is now being extended rapidly by schools of business, university extension divisions, evening colleges, and State distributive education departments. 

These administrative management courses are based upon two principles: (1) attendance is limited to owners and managers of small firms, and (2) subjects taught are limited to business administration, not operation. Most of the courses are offered in the evening, usually one night a week for several weeks, totaling 

16 to 30 hours of discussion, lecturing, or both.

The businessmen-students pay tuition, which covers the cost of the course. The educational institutions assume full educational and financial responsibility for all courses, and the Small Business Administration aids as a co-sponsor, often with the assistance of such local organizations as the clearing house association, chamber of commerce or junior chamber of commerce, local or State manufacturers, retailers or wholesalers associations, and individual trade associations.

Two experimental courses were held in the spring at Richmond, Virginia, and Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and the experience gained from these courses has been invaluable in the development of subsequent ones. Since the beginning of fall, 12 courses have been offered small-business owners, and additional courses are currently planned for later this school year or the fall of 1955.
