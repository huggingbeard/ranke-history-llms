PREFACE 

Can grammar keep its place in a curriculum geared to a streamlined age undergoing the upheavals of war? 

Yes! Basic training in language is so vital that high military officials have requested the secondary schools of the nation to provide adequate mastery of correct English. In addition to the needs of our country in these critical days, the continuing necessity of earning a living certainly requires proficiency in the everyday forms of speaking and writing. Furthermore, a firm grasp of good current usage provides the passport to poise and confidence in social contacts. To establish this solid foundation in language habits and skills, the ability to choose between right and wrong forms of expression, the study of grammar is indispensable.

To make this study more attractive and understandable, the author of GRAMMAR WITH WINGS has correlated the traditional definitions with the terminology of aeronautics so that youngsters will more easily perceive concepts and relationships in terms of that which they already know and love. The numerous exercises and illustrative sentences deal with contemporary affairs and the live interests of boys and girls, so that the application of 

GRAMMAR WITH WINGS will function in genuine situations.

The pilot of a sentence is called the subject, a word telling who controls or directs the action. The engine or motor is called the verb, a word describing the action or telling what happened. Just as a good airplanespotter must learn to recognize certain markings, you must learn to identify the subject, the verb, and the object of a sentence.

2. What did he do? 

3. Who or what received the action?

OMITTING THE SUBJECT (Giving Commands) 

When the captain of a Flying Fortress orders the bombardier to "lay eggs" in enemy territory, he simply says, "Let 'er go!" Of course, the complete sentence would be, "(You) let her go!"

The subject is omitted in giving commands because the person receives the order directly. In such sentences as these, the word you is understood to be the subject; this fact is shown by means of a parenthesis (you). 

Take this report to H.Q. 

Pass the ammunition. Halt! 

Fire whenever ready. (You) take this report to H.Q. (You) pass the ammunition. (You) halt! (You) fire whenever ready.

When asking people to do something for us, it is perfectly natural to omit the subject (you), but we should never forget to say please! Courtesy is never omitted.

COMPOUND SUBJECT 

(Pilot and Co-pilot)

Just as a plane may have more than one person to direct the controls, a sentence may have more than one subject to perform the action. They rank equal in importance. When a sentence contains only one subject, the subject word is called the simple subject. When two or more words are used as the subject of a sentence, the subject words are called the compound subject. 

Examples of Compound Subjects: 

1. Eisenhower, De Gaulle and Montgomery joined forces in 

North Africa to drive Rommel to the Mediterranean Sea. 

2. In order to meet the quota set by the military authorities, the Army and the Navy have relaxed certain physical standards for admission. 

3. Food rationing and black markets go hand in hand. 

ONE OR MORE VERBS 

(Single-engined or Multi-engined)

Some aircraft are driven by a single motor, others by more than one. Similarly, a sentence may have only one verb or several verbs to denote the action. A sentence carries more meaning when two or more verbs tell what happened.

Examples of Compound Verbs: 

1. The ship rose and fell with the gradual swelling of the tide. 

2. The baby gurgled and cooed contentedly in the crib. 

3. They dashed into the building, leaped up the stairs, and rushed into their classroom.

HELPING VERBS 

(S.O.S.) 

A helping verb is a short auxiliary verb which helps to form the tense or voice of the main verb in a sentence. There are only a few helping verbs in English: have, do, be, shall, will, and may. 

They are very useful in giving different meanings to the main verb in a sentence. In everyday conversation, we use contractions for these auxiliary verbs as shortcuts in speaking. These six little verbs are indispensable helpers in delivering the message.

Examples: 

1. The gremlins 'll get you, if you don't watch out. ('ll will) 2. They must've marched a million miles in the burning sun before they started to see friendly Arabs in every sand dune. ('ve have) 3. After they were rescued, the tall bearded man said quietly, "I'd better not talk about the incident." ('d=had) 4. Once a man becomes famous, the hounds of publicity won't camp anywhere else, except on his trail. (won't will not) 5. We'll arrive at about seven o'clock this evening. ('ll shall)

ACTIVE VOICE AND PASSIVE VOICE 

(Reversing the Engines)

The normal direction of a plane is forward, but one may sometimes reverse the engines. In the same way, a sentence may reverse the action of its verb so that the subject is the receiver of the action, not the doer. When this happens the verb is said to be in the passive voice. The usual form is the active voice. 

Examples: 

1. Active voice:

Passive voice: 

2. Active voice:

The man painted the house green. 

The house was painted green by the man. 

A Navy plane located Rickenbacker in the Pacific.

Passive voice: Rickenbacker was located in the Pacific by a Navy plane.

You have noticed above how the engines may be reversed so that a sentence written or spoken in the normal order (active voice) may be rewritten (passive voice). Here is the way to distinguish between active and passive. Voice is the form of the verb which indicates whether the subject acts (=active voice), or is acted upon (=passive voice). 

In the active voice, the person or thing used as subject performs an action against some other person or thing; hence, active. 

For example, "The camel stole the show during one sequence between Bob Hope and Bing Crosby in 'On The Road To Morocco.'" Notice that the subject (camel) performed an action (stole) against the thing (show); therefore, this sentence is in the active voice. In the passive voice, the receiver of the action becomes the subject by reversing the sentence thusly: "The show

was stolen by the camel during one sequence between Bob Hope and Bing Crosby in 'On The Road To Morocco." Observe that the object of the sentence in the active voice becomes the subject of the sentence when rewritten in the passive voice. The doer in the passive has to be indicated by the preposition by (by the camel).

TRANSITIVE AND INTRANSITIVE VERBS (Angles of Attack) 

Since only transitive verbs have voice, you must be able to distinguish between transitive and intransitive verbs. The verbs we have been studying in the active and passive voice are all transitive verbs. 

A verb that tells what happened to a person or thing is called a transitive verb. It nearly always has an object. For example: 

1. Excessive rains spoiled the crops. 

2. The store-keeper raised his prices. 

3. The mechanic fixed the flat tire in a jiffy. 

4. The Senior Class applauded the invited guest speaker. 

5. The nurse assisted the wounded boy to his feet. 

A verb that describes a state of being and has no object is called an intransitive verb. For example: 

1. The sun sets at six o'clock. 

2. The river meanders through lush meadows. 

3. Thank God we are alive today. 

4. This roast beef tastes delicious. 

5. The Indians traveled lightly but swiftly in their canoes.

COPULATIVE VERB (The Connecting Link) 

There is a type of intransitive verb that does a special job, that is, links the subject with another word in the sentence. For example, "Pasteur was a famous bacteriologist." In this sentence, was links the subject Pasteur with the predicate noun bacteriologist. Examine this group of words: "Vincent grew taller during the summer." In this sentence, grew links the subject Vincent with the predicate adjective taller. Such a joining or linking verb is called a copulative verb. The most common copulative verb in English is the verb be. Other copulative verbs very often used are these: become, seem, grow, appear, look, feel, smell, stay, taste. 

Examples: 

1. I am a meteorologist at LaGuardia Airport. 

2. Women become very talkative at a "hen party.” 

3. At night the moon seems to sail through the clouds. 

4. A cold shower makes you feel alert and hungry. 

5. My uncle regrets the fact that he stayed single. 

Special notice: Copulative verbs are always intransitive; that is, they describe a state of being and have no object. 

DISTINGUISHING BETWEEN DIRECT OBJECT AND PREDICATE NOUN OR PREDICATE ADJECTIVE (Plotting the Course) 

Can you plot the course of a plane? You must know the true compass heading and make allowance for wind drift. In a similar way, can you plot the course of a sentence? You must understand

the verb in order to discover which way the meaning is headed. 

If the verb is a transitive verb of action, the whole sentence points toward its mission or destination, the direct object. Example: "Market Wise won the first race." If, however, the verb is a copulative verb, the whole sentence is headed toward the word linked with the subject. This linked word is either a noun or an adjective, so this is called either predicate noun or predicate adjective. Examples: 

Predicate adjective: 

The Greek guerrillas were gallant even in defeat.

Predicate noun: 

In the famous mountain pass at Thermopylae, a new generation became heroes like their ancestors. 

Both the objects of active transitive verbs, and the predicate nouns or adjectives after copulative verbs are necessary to complete the meaning of the verb; hence they are called complements. 

In order to plot the course of the sentence clearly, you must be able to distinguish between direct object and predicate noun or predicate adjective.

COMPLETE SUBJECT AND COMPLETE PREDICATE 

(Piloting the Aircraft)

You recall that we compared the pilot of a plane with the subject of a sentence because each one serves to control or direct the action of the engine or of the verb. For example, the subject and the verb in the following sentence may be easily located:

Subject and verb: 

The modern Clipper ships used by the Pan-American Airways in transatlantic service are Boeing flying boats, the largest commercial planes in the world. 

We may continue the comparison by adding other factors: the pilot needs instruments to direct the plane; the engine needs a propeller, wings, and a tail structure to move the plane. In the same way, the subject has other words to describe the person or thing you are telling about; all together, the subject and related words are called the complete subject. The verb together with other words related in use and expanding the sense of the sentence is called the complete predicate. For example, in the same sentence we analyzed above:

Complete subject: 

The modern Clipper ships used by the Pan-American Airways in transatlantic service are Boeing flying boats, the largest commercial planes in the world. 

Complete predicate:

The modern Clipper ships used by the Pan-American Airways in transatlantic' service are Boeing flying boats, the largest commercial planes in the world. 

LOCATING THE COMPLETE SUBJECT AND COMPLETE PREDICATE 

Every word in a sentence is either in the complete subject or in the complete predicate. If there is any doubt in your mind about where a word belongs, use the following method.

Step 1. Find the action word (the simple predicate) and underline it twice.

The members of the team practice in the gym on rainy days. 

Step 2. Find the simple subject and underline it once.

The members of the team practice in the gym on rainy days. 

Step 3. Continue underlining once the words that describe or tell more about the simple subject. (These words are called modifiers.)

The members of the team practice in the gym on rainy days. (The words underlined once are the complete subject of the sentence.)

Step 4. All the words that are not in the complete subject must be in the complete predicate. These words we underline twice. Complements belong in the predicate. 

The members of the team practice in the gym on rainy days. 

He never comes late to school. 

Where did you get that hat? (You) Sprinkle the lawn this afternoon.

In the last seat of the last row sat Harry. 

The boys and girls in the back of the room laughed.

DIRECT OBJECT OF THE SENTENCE 

(Target for Tonight)

The destination or mission of a Flying Fortress is called its target. When the bomber has delivered its perilous cargo, it has accomplished its mission. In the same manner, a word denoting who or what receives the action of a verb is called the direct object of the sentence.

Examples: 

1. The saboteurs wrecked the troop train. 

2. The hungry shipwrecked sailors caught a shark following their raft one afternoon. 

3. The government rationed shoes to guarantee equal distribution of footgear among civilians. 

4. Some very good friends of mine donate a pint of blood to the Red Cross Blood Bank once a month. 

5. The girls in our school knitted fifty afghans for the men in the merchant marine service.

Remember that not every plane is on a bombing mission; some planes are on observation duty, while others may serve as fighter escorts. Similarly, not every sentence has a direct object. The only sentences that do have a direct object are those sentences containing a transitive verb. A transitive verb scores a direct hit every time. In order to locate the direct object in a sentence, ask yourself this question: "Who or what received the action of the verb?" For example, in the first sentence above, "Who or what did the saboteurs wreck?"—the answer is the direct object, train. 

In the fifth sentence, "Who or what did the girls knit?"—the answer is the direct object, afghans.

PREDICATE NOUN AND PREDICATE ADJECTIVE (Escort Planes) 

A squadron of fighting aircraft may consist of a formation of bombers supported by smaller escort planes to parry enemy attackers. Naturally these escort planes do not carry the burden, but they assist safe delivery of the bomb load. In like manner, there are words used in sentences which do not receive the action of the verb, but they assist in completing the delivery of the message or thought of the sentence. A noun used to complete the meaning of a copulative verb is called a predicate noun. An adjective used to complete the meaning of a copulative verb is called a predicate adjective. You can recognize a predicate noun or a predicate adjective by these markings: 

4. The predicate noun or predicate adjective is the same person or thing as the subject of the sentence, or it may describe the subject. b. The predicate noun or predicate adjective is linked or joined to the subject by a copulative verb (be, become, seem, grow, appear, look, feel, smell, stay, taste).

Examples: 

1. General MacArthur, became the commanding officer in the Pacific area. 

2. With their clever camouflage paint all over their person and outfit, the Japs appeared foliage in the dense jungles. 

3. If I were king, you would rule the world. 

4. Scientists have discovered a new type of synthetic rubber which looks authentic and lasts for many miles. 

5. During these hot summer days, an ice cream soda tastes delicious.

Examples explained: 

1. What did General MacArthur become?-predicate noun, officer, after the copulative verb, became. (Special note: A copulative verb does not show action, but describes a state of being; therefore, it does not have an object.) 

2. What did the Japs appear?-predicate noun, foliage, after the copulative verb, appeared. 

3. If I were who?—predicate noun, king, after the copulative verb, were. 

4. Rubber looks how?-predicate adjective, authentic, after the copulative verb, looks. 

5. Soda tastes how?-predicate adjective, delicious, after the copulative verb, tastes.

REVIEW OF THE  

Learn these definitions before doing the exercises that follow. 

A sentence is a group of words stating a thought. 

The subject of a sentence is the person or thing spoken about. 

The verb is a word describing action or state of being. 

The object is a word denoting who or what receives the action of a transitive verb.

The simple subject is a single word used as subject of a sentence. 

A compound subject refers to two or more words used as the subject of the same verb in a sentence.

A compound verb refers to two or more verbs used with one subject in the same sentence.

The complete subject includes the simple subject and all the words or phrases or clauses which modify its meaning. 

The complete predicate includes the verb and all the words or

phrases or clauses which modify its meaning or complete the sentence.

An auxiliary verb is a short verb which helps to form the tense or voice of the main verb in the sentence.

A copulative verb is a short linking or joining verb used to connect the subject with a predicate noun or predicate adjective in order to complete the sentence.

A transitive verb is a verb of action having a direct object. 

An intransitive verb is a verb describing a state of being and having no object.

The imperative mood, used when giving commands, omits the subject, but the word (you) is understood.

A verb is in the active voice when it shows the action of the subject toward an object.

A verb is in the passive voice when the person or thing used as the subject receives the action of the verb. 

The direct object of a sentence is the person or thing that receives the action of the verb.

The predicate noun is a noun used to complete the meaning of a copulative verb and describe the subject. 

The predicate adjective is an adjective used to complete the meaning of a copulative verb and describe the subject. 

An important concept: A sentence may contain all or some of the various parts defined above. There is absolute necessity for only one part, however, in order to maintain the definition of a sentence as a group of words stating a thought. That important part is the verb. The subject is understood in giving commands. 

The object will be lacking in the case of intransitive verbs. 

But the motive power of thought, the verb, must be the core of the sentence. Therefore, in all your writing or speaking, make sure you build your sentences around a verb.

CHAPTER II  

The words in our language are divided into eight groups according to how they are used in a sentence. They are called the Eight Parts of Speech. 

NOUNS 

A noun is the name of something; it may be a person, a place, a thing, or a quality. Nouns are classified as follows:

A proper noun names a certain person, place, or thing. It is always capitalized. 

On Thursday, President Roosevelt will address the Senate. 

A common noun names any person, place, or thing.

The boy walked to school with his friend. 

A collective noun names a group of persons or things.

The army paraded before a crowd of people. 

An abstract noun names a quality. 

Honesty is a virtue.

A compound noun consists of two nouns or a phrase used as the name of a person, place, or thing.

John Smith sent a salt cellar to his mother-in-law. 30

PRONOUNS 

1 A pronoun is a word used instead of a noun.

Pronouns are classified as follows: 

A personal pronoun indicates by its form whether it stands for the speaker (first person), for the person spoken to (second person), or for the person or thing spoken of (third person). 

The personal pronouns are:

First Person: 

I, we, me, us 

Second Person: you, thou and thee (in poetry, prayer, and the Bible) 

Third Person: he, she, it, they, him, her, them

A relative pronoun connects the subordinate clause in which it stands with another word to which it refers (an antecedent). 

The relative pronouns who and whom refer to persons, which and what refer to things, and that refers to either persons or things. 

He thanked the boy who taught him to swim.

I lost the book which (or that) I bought last week. 

An interrogative pronoun is used to ask a question. The interrogative pronouns are who, whom, which, what. 

Who does not understand the lesson? 

What time is it?

A possessive pronoun denotes ownership. The possessive pronouns are mine, ours, yours, his, hers, its, theirs, whose, thine and thy (in poetry, prayer, and the Bible). 

His is lost. 

This house is ours. 

Thine is the power and the glory.

A demonstrative pronoun points out something definitely. 

The demonstrative pronouns are this, these, that, those. 

This is the color. 

These are mine.

An indefinite pronoun points out something indefinitely. 

Common indefinite pronouns are all, any, each, either, few, some. 

All are here. 

Some are missing.

A reflexive pronoun is used as an object and refers to the subject. The reflexive pronouns are myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves. 

She hurt herself. 

He shot himself.

An intensive pronoun emphasizes its antecedent. The intensive pronouns are the same as those listed above under Reflexive Pronouns. 

I myself will do it. 

They themselves are to blame.

VERBS A verb is used as the simple predicate of a clause or of a sentence. Verbs are classified as follows: 

A transitive verb takes a direct object.

The teacher posted the marks of the honor pupils on the bulletin board.

An intransitive verb takes no direct object, nor is it followed by a predicate noun or predicate adjective. 

The men slept in hammocks during the entire voyage. 

A copulative verb (or a verb of being) is followed by a predicate noun or predicate adjective. 

His reaction to the suggestion was a mistake. (mistake is a predicate noun) 

The medicine tastes bitter. (bitter is a predicate adjective)

An auxiliary verb helps another verb. The common auxiliary verbs are be, can, do, had, have, may, must, shall, will. 

I have read many books about the South. 

Mary did not wash the dishes.

An adjective is used to describe or limit a noun or pronoun.

tall boy limiting: six days short man exciting game few people many hours

Adjectives are classified as follows: 

A demonstrative adjective points out something definitely. 

The demonstrative adjectives are this, these, that, those. 

This hat is mine. 

These books are his. 

An indefinite adjective points out something indefinitely.

Some common indefinite adjectives are any, each, some. 

We found some food on the table. Do you have any toys?

An interrogative adjective is used to ask a question. The interrogative adjectives are whose, which, what. 

Which hat is yours? 

Whose book is this?

A possessive adjective denotes ownership. The possessive adjectives are my, our, your, his, her, its, their, whose. 

This is my sweater. 

Where is your house?

She has three children.

PREPOSITIONS 

A preposition introduces a prepositional phrase. Some common prepositions are in, on, into, from, by, at, before, between,

with, within, under, over, among, to, above, after, upon, without, during. in the room on the bottom into the next store to Chicago In the above prepositional phrases the words room, bottom, store and Chicago are called the object of the preposition. The entire phrase should be considered as a unit. Every prepositional phrase acts as an adjective or an adverb and is commonly called either an adjective phrase or an adverbial phrase. 

The boys in our neighborhood sometimes play baseball in the street.

In our neighborhood is an adjective phrase modifying the noun boys. In the street is an adverbial phrase modifying the verb play. 

Often two phrases follow each other.

The gentleman sat [in the rear] [of the room]. 

In the rear is an adverbial phrase modifying the verb sat. Of the room is an adjective phrase modifying the noun rear. 

CONJUNCTIONS 

A conjunction connects two or more words, phrases, or clauses. Conjunctions are classified as follows: 

A coordinating conjunction connects elements of equal rank; that is, words with words, phrases with phrases, clauses with clauses. Some common coordinating conjunctions are and, but, for, either...or, neither...nor. 

Bob and Tom visited me. (and connects two words: Bob with Tom)

The dog is either in the kitchen or in the living room. (either...or connects two phrases: in the kitchen with in the living room)

The men are given a strenuous athletic training, but the women receive instruction in first-aid. (but connects two clauses: The men are given a strenuous athletic training with the women receive instruction in first-aid)

A subordinating conjunction connects a subordinate clause (or dependent clause) with its principal clause (or independent clause). Some common subordinating conjunctions are since, because, if, that, unless, whenever. 

We will go if it does not rain. (We will go is the principal clause; if it does not rain is the subordinate clause.) 

I help Dad whenever he needs me. (I help Dad is the principal clause; whenever he needs me is the subordinate clause.) 

INTERJECTIONS 

An interjection is used to express a sudden or strong emotion. Some common interjections are oh, ah, hurrah, alas, say, well. 

Oh, what a game it was! 

Say, who called me?

VERBALS 

Definition: Certain words which are derived from verbs are called verbals.

Verbals are forms of verbs that are used as nouns, adjectives,

or adverbs. They are really two parts of speech at the same time. 

There are three verbals: the gerund, the participle, the infinitive. 

GERUND 

Definition: A gerund is that form of the verb ending in ing, used as a noun.

A gerund acts as a verb and a noun at the same time. 

We enjoy singing.

As a verb singing shows action; as a noun it is the name of something and acts as the direct object of the predicate verb enjoy. 

Here is another example. 

We enjoy singing old familiar songs.

As a verb singing shows action and takes the direct object songs; as a noun it is the name of something and acts as the direct object of the predicate verb enjoy. 

We have learned that a noun may be used in a sentence as: 

1. a simple subject 

2. a direct object 

3. a predicate noun 

4. an object of a preposition 

5. an indirect object [=after the preposition (to)]

The following sentences illustrate a gerund used as each of these five functions of a noun. 

1. Dancing the rhumba to the tunes of a band is a great pleasure to many people. (dancing is the simple subject)

2. The women in the society enjoy playing cards in the afternoon. (playing is the direct object) 

3. My job at home is caring for the furnace. (caring is a predicate noun) 4. The man was arrested for speeding along the highway. (speeding is the object of the preposition for) 5. The Navy gives boxing a prominent part in its training program. (boxing is an indirect object of the preposition to understood)

The gerund is sometimes used in the passive voice. 

The batter certainly did not like being hit by the ball. 

The above illustrations of the gerund are in the present tense. 

There is a past tense to both the active and passive voice. The word having always precedes these forms.

He spoke of having seen the Yankees. (active voice; past tense)

We did not approve of his having been arrested. (passive voice; past tense)

Since a gerund is a noun, it must always be preceded by a possessive adjective.

The audience applauded his (not him) acting. 

He disliked your (not you) writing to me.

We objected to his (not him) whistling so loud. 

No one approved of their (not them) working overtime. 

The child did not appreciate my (not me) watching him.

PARTICIPLE 

Definition: A participle is that form of the verb ending in ing or ed, used as an adjective.

A participle acts as a verb and an adjective at the same time. 

The most commonly used form of the participle is the present tense (active voice). This is formed just as the gerund is formed -by adding ing to the present tense of the verb. 

We heard a singing canary.

This simple form of the participle is used frequently in our daily speech. We speak of a drinking glass, a paring knife, a bombing plane, etc. We regard them as ordinary adjectives when they precede the noun they modify. 

Sometimes we use the participle as follows: 

We heard a canary singing.

In the above illustration we have placed the participle after the noun it modifies, rather than before it. As a verb, singing shows action; as an adjective, singing modifies canary. 

We can develop the sentence further as follows: 

We heard a canary singing a beautiful tune.

As a verb, singing shows action and takes the direct object tune; as an adjective, singing modifies canary.

The present participle is also used in the passive voice, being+ past participle.

The boy being tired from walking sat down under a tree. 

The past participle preceding the noun it modifies is frequently used as a regular adjective: a broken promise, a burned match, a swollen finger, etc.

The perfect participle is having+past participle. 

Having ridden with him before, I declined his invitation for a ride in his car.

The perfect participle may be in the passive voice, having been+past participle.

Having been wounded in action, the soldiers were sent home on furlough.

Participles must be placed just before or just after the nouns they modify.

(Dirigibles and Balloons)

As you know from your study of aircraft, there are two kinds of lighter-than-air vessels: dirigibles and balloons. Dirigibles are steerable and, therefore, move under control of the pilot. Balloons are not steerable and, therefore, move without control unless they are tied in place. In a similar manner, there are two kinds of clauses: independent and dependent. The independent clauses are like dirigibles, in the sense that they are free to direct their meaning unassisted by other words. The dependent clauses are like balloons, in the sense that they must be attached to other words or else float aimlessly. An independent clause can take off under its own power and remain aloft without losing its way in conveying a message or idea. A dependent clause can rise in the middle of a page and drift whichever way the wind blows, but it cannot be relied upon to deliver a full message. In order to avoid aimless drifting of ideas in your speaking or writing, make sure that you attach each dependent clause firmly to the right word in the sentence. Thus, minor and supporting ideas will form part of the neat pattern of a balloon barrage.

An incomplete sentence is a fragment of thought. Usually an incomplete sentence expresses part of an idea, but cannot make complete sense because the subject or the verb may be missing.

OBSCURITY DUE TO CONFUSION OF PRONOUNS 

A pronoun is a word used in place of a noun; it is a kind of signal pointing toward the noun that it refers to. Sometimes the repeated use of the same pronoun mixes the signals so that the meaning of a sentence grows obscure. To help the reader or listener to understand the meaning more clearly, substitute names or direct discourse and omit the pronouns. Another way of clearing up things is to shift vague or misplaced antecedents to

positions nearer the pronouns which refer to them. Notice how the confusion in the following sentences may be straightened out by directing the traffic along one-way streets. 

1. Obscure: When Rommel and Montgomery met in North 

Africa, his motto was to drive him to the sea.

When Rommel and Montgomery met in North 

Africa, Montgomery's motto was, "Drive him to 'the sea!" 

2. Obscure: In playing soccer, you may hit the ball with your head after it bounces.

Clearer by shifting the dependent clause: 

In playing soccer, after the ball bounces, you hit it with your head. may 

3. Obscure: Tom likes baseball stories, and Margaret likes tales of knights and ladies, which keeps both of them happy.

Clearer by subordinating the independent clauses: 

While Tom likes baseball stories, and Margaret likes tales of knights and ladies, reading keeps both of them happy.

She put the money into her bag and closed it, but when she stepped off the elevator the money had disappeared.

FAULTY PARALLEL STRUCTURE 

What keeps a plane in level flight when the pilot takes his hands off the controls? One factor of stability is the parallel structure of the plane in wings and fuselage so that a plane momentarily caught in a gust of wind and out of control has a restoring tendency. You can imagine what would happen if one wing were out of balance! In the same way, a sentence will maneuver across a page and deliver its meaning safely, if the ideas are expressed in parallel form. Your job as a writer or speaker is to design your sentences so that they balance. To achieve this clearness of expression, you must avoid faulty parallel structure. Balance a phrase with another phrase, a clause with another clause, an infinitive with another infinitive, and so on. 

Look at the parallel structure in the headlines of today's newspaper. Notice one on the sports page similar to this: 

DODGERS ROUT PIRATES GIANTS TRIM CARDINALS

Turn to the political news section and find a headline like this:

SENATE VOTES TO ADJOURN AS HOUSE DECIDES TO CONTINUE 

Examine the business and financial section to discover fluctuations in the stock exchange expressed this way:

OIL RISES SHARPLY STEEL HOLDS FIRM 

Have you noticed the technique of balancing ideas in these headlines? 

1. The sports headline achieves parallel structure by balancing an independent clause with another independent clause in the simple order of subject, verb, object. 

2. The political headline balances two clauses, one of them independent, the other dependent; but the parallelism results from the use of an infinitive after the verb: votes to adjourn... decides to continue. 

3. The business headline balances two clauses by using an adverb after the verb in each case: rises sharply...holds firm.

THE SPLIT INFINITIVE 

A sure way of "cracking up" is to fly directly into a thunderstorm to split it in two as a short cut to an airfield on the other side. Another way of endangering your neck is to imitate a trapeze artist doing a "split" on a tight rope twenty feet off the ground. These two splits may prove fatal. A pleasant and harmless type is the "banana split” at your neighborhood ice-cream parlor. 

Is a "split infinitive" dangerous or harmless? Let's find out.

As you have already learned, an infinitive is the form of a verb introduced by the word to. They always go together like ham and eggs, or spaghetti and sauce. Now a split infinitive is an infinitive in which another word or phrase stands between to and its verb partner. For example: "to gracefully dance" for "to dance gracefully" "to modestly say 'no'" for "to say 'no' modestly" "to unlawfully sell" for "to sell unlawfully" "to safely get out of a spin" for "to get out of a spin safely" "to all of a sudden overheat the engine" for "to overheat the engine all of a sudden" Is it wrong to place a word between to and the verb which forms an infinitive? It is not wrong, but it is preferable not to separate the parts of an infinitive. Such separation is occasionally allowable in speech; less often in writing. You see, spoken English is freer because it is the unstudied and unpolished form. Written 

English permits time for polishing the expression of ideas and

making a better selection of language. Therefore, two neighbors talking about the black market may say, "The butcher tried to unlawfully sell meat at higher than ceiling prices." A newspaper reporting the same idea would avoid the split infinitive thus, "The butcher unlawfully tried to sell meat at higher than ceiling prices."

Sometimes the split infinitive serves to intensify the meaning: "This is to officially certify that John Doe did, alone and unassisted, take off from and return to Floyd Bennett Airport, this tenth day of July in 1943, thereby successfully completing his first solo flight." At other times the split infinitive makes for awkwardness: "He tried to quickly but bunglingly turn off the valve leading to the fuel system." Better: "He quickly but bunglingly tried to turn off the valve leading to the fuel system." 

How shall we conclude this discussion? Aviators say, "The best way to recover from a spin is to avoid getting into one." Writers say, "The best way to handle an infinitive is to avoid splitting it.” 

Go thou and do likewise.

DANGLING CONSTRUCTIONS 

To achieve the greatest aerodynamic efficiency, modern plane designers streamline all parts. Nothing dangles in air for whatever dangles stops the airflow and may create a hazard. The landing gear consisting of wheels and shock absorbers must be retracted when the plane is in flight. Filing down the protruding heads of rivets on the wings and the body of a plane yields an increase in air speed of 25 m.p.h. For the best possible performance, airplanes must avoid loose ends.

Similarly, when you design a sentence for communicating ideas in speech of writing, you should avoid dangling constructions of words, phrases, or clauses. A sentence must be cleancut and free

from all loosely attached expressions. The meaning must be clear and unmistakable. Each word, or phrase, or dependent clause should modify the proper element in a sentence. 

There are three remedies for dangling constructions: 

1. Change the structure from phrase to clause, or clause to phrase to make the meaning clear. 

2. Change the position of the dangling word, phrase, or clause in the sentence by placing it near the expression it modifies. 

3. Insert the necessary word which the dangling construction should modify. 

1. Dangling Prepositional Phrase

Incorrect: After one hundred hours of flying, the mechanic made a periodic inspection of the aircraft. 

Correct: The mechanic made a periodic inspection of the aircraft after one hundred hours of flying.

Reason: The phrase describes the flight of the plane, not the mechanic, and it should be placed near aircraft to make this meaning clear. 

2. Dangling Participial Phrase

Incorrect: Neatly packed in a corner of the cabin, we crawled on our hands and knees and discovered a parachute.

We crawled on our hands and knees and discovered a parachute neatly packed in a corner of the cabin.

Reason: 

Ask yourself, "Who was packed in the corner?"

Then the parachute must be attached to its own participial phrase by changing the position of the dangling phrase." 

3. Dangling Gerund Phrase

Incorrect: On dismantling the engine and propeller, it seemed very complicated. 

Correct: On dismantling the engine and propeller, we found it very complicated. 

Reason: 

The gerund phrase dangles without belonging to any particular word in the sentence. By inserting we found, the dangling construction becomes firmly fixed in the sentence. 

4. Dangling Infinitive Phrase

Incorrect: An altitude of fifteen hundred feet was insufficient to do acrobatic flying. 

Correct: An altitude of fifteen hundred feet was insufficient for the pilot to do acrobatic flying. 

Reason: The infinitive phrase to do acrobatic flying dangles because it lacks a subject, the pilot, to give it clear meaning. 

5. Dangling Adverb

Incorrect: When flying the "pylon eight" pattern proportionately, you must bank at the turns and during the short straightaway to compensate for wind drift.

Correct: When flying the "pylon eight" pattern, you must bank proportionately at the turns and during the short straightaway to compensate for wind drift. 

Reason: The dangling adverb proportionately makes sense when placed near the verb it modifies, bank. 

6. Dangling Elliptical Clause 

Incorrect: My flight instructor encourages me by waving when flying solo. 

Correct: My flight instructor encourages me by waving when I am flying solo.

Reason: 

The elliptical clause when flying solo dangles because it might refer to either the flight instructor or the pupil who is speaking. Inserting the words I am makes the meaning unmistakably clear as to who is flying and who is waving.

CORRECT USAGE OF ADJECTIVES AND ADVERBS 

Let's review.

COMPARISON OF ADJECTIVES AND ADVERBS COMPARISON OF ADJECTIVES 

When we compare persons or things in order to see which contains more or less of a certain quality, we express this relationship in three ways:

The positive degree denotes the simple quality. 

Mary is a bright girl.

The comparative degree denotes a higher degree of the quality. 

Alice is a brighter girl.

The superlative degree denotes the highest degree of the quality. 

Jane is the brightest girl.

To form the comparative of adjectives of one syllable, add er to the positive. Use more or less for adjectives of two or more syllables.

To form the superlative of adjectives of one syllable, add est to the positive. Use most or least for adjectives of two or more syllables. Positive: tall

Comparative: taller Superlative: 

unusual more unusual intelligent less intelligent tallest most unusual least intelligent 

Never use both comparisons together. prettier (not more prettier) prettiest (not most prettiest) slower (not more slower) speediest (not most speediest)

Examples: 

1. Margaret is prettier (not more prettier) than Valerie. 

2. Margaret is the speediest (not most speediest) typist in the class.

Use the comparative degree when two persons or things are compared, and the superlative degree when more than two persons or things are compared. 

Of the two students, Helen is the less (not least) ambitious.

AGREEMENT IN NUMBER 

A verb must agree with its subject in number. A singular subject requires a singular verb; a plural subject requires a plural verb. Be careful in such sentences as the following when the subject is followed by a word or group of words beginning with of, with, together with, along with, besides, including, in addition to, as well as, accompanied by, etc. The verb agrees with the subject word, not with the modifiers.

The package of books is (not are) too heavy for me to carry. 

The old man with his dog was (not were) lost in the woods. 

Frank, as well as Tom, eats (not eat) slowly.

This book, as well as those, is (not are) interesting. 

One of the Marines swims (not swim) 100 yards with a full pack.

Two or more nouns or pronouns joined by and require plural verbs and pronouns.

Tom and Mary are (not is) going to the movies. 

I am sure that Jack and Jim did their (not his) best.

Two or more nouns or pronouns joined by or or nor require verbs and pronouns that agree with the nearer subject. 

Either Joe or Bob is (not are) in the kitchen. 

Neither Dad nor the twins are (not is.) at home.

Neither Mary nor Alice did her (not their) homework. 

Either Sam or the girls forgot their (not his) books. 

Such pronouns as each, every, one, anyone, no one, everyone, anybody, nobody, someone, somebody, either and neither require a singular verb and are followed by a singular pronoun, even though apparently applicable to many people at the same time.

Neither of the boys was (not were) on time. 

Every student was requested to bring his (not their) textbooks to class.

Each player must pay a deposit on his (not their) locker. 

Errors may be avoided by shifting to a plural subject: 

All players must pay a deposit on their lockers. 

TRICKY CONSTRUCTIONS: (. . .one of the. . .that. . .) 

The phrase one of the (+plural noun) followed by a dependent clause (that or who) requires a plural verb and plural pronoun in the dependent clause.

The General Grant is one of the tanks that were developed to play their part in stopping the blitzkrieg. 

Jesse Jones served as one of the advisers who contributed their experience and ability to the President. 

Madame Chiang Kai-shek is one of the few brilliant women who have spoken words of wisdom and charity.

AGREEMENT IN CASE 

What does case mean? In grammar, case means the relation of a noun or a pronoun to other words in the sentences. In English, there are only three cases:

The nominative case shows that the noun or pronoun is used as subject of a verb.

The submarine made a crash dive. 

The objective case shows that the noun or pronoun is used as object of a verb or preposition.

The walkie-talkie relayed the message to the outpost. 

The possessive case shows that the noun or pronoun is used to indicate ownership.

The commander sacrificed his life. 

Where does trouble arise in handling these cases? Sometimes with verbs, prepositions, or infinitives these cases are misused by careless speakers or writers. 

For example, "between you and I" should be "between you and me" because the preposition requires the objective case, not the nominative case. "I like his (not him) cooking" because the possessive pronoun means you admire the food, whereas the objective case would put the cook himself right into the stew for a cannibal's pleasure! Here, you see, difference in case makes a difference in meaning.

VERBS-THE ENGINES OF A SENTENCE 

In the first chapter, we spoke of the subject as the pilot, and the verb as the engine of a sentence. The verb shows action or describes the moving force. The verb tells what is happening or being done. The verb is the motor or engine because it moves the sentence in the direction intended by the speaker. To convey a message, or make a statement, or ask a question, the verb is the most essential element in the sentence. Indeed, you may omit all other words except the verb, and the sentence will retain its basic meaning. For example: 

1. (a) Will you please go out of here at once! (b) Go! 

2. (a) The plane is out of control, so you had better jump right now. (b) Jump! 

3. (a) Do you really expect me to desert my ship just because you are having some trouble with the controls? (b) Desert?

Of course, a commanding tone or gesture, a pleading look or warning cry, can supply the verb with some of the full meaning contained in the context of words.

TENSE OF THE VERB 

(Timing the Spark)

The cylinders in an airplane engine are arranged to work in sequence so that the crankshaft continues to revolve without interruption. To accomplish this sequence of work, the distributor coil sends a spark to the cylinders in rotation by split-second timing. Timing the spark keeps the engine working smoothly and continuously.

Now, the word tense means time. So, in building a smoothrunning sentence, you must watch out for the sequence of tenses just as carefully as you time the sequence of sparks in the engine cylinders. Never let your sentences sputter or stall with a confusion of tenses. Learn to distribute the timing in your sentences so that the verbs work harmoniously. For example, don't say: “He had just finished warming the engine, when he notices there is insufficient fuel in the tank." Better: "He had just finished warming the engine, when he noticed there was insufficient fuel in the tank." Here is another example of poor timing or wrong tense: "He trembled to think of what might have happened, if he took off without refueling." Better: "He trembled to think of what might have happened, if he had taken off without refueling." Compare these two sentences to discover how tense changes the meaning: 

1. The bomb exploded where my plane was parked. 

2. The bomb exploded where my plane had been parked. 

In which sentence was the plane safe from bombing?

THE MAIN TENSES DEFINED 

Tense denotes the time of an action, as indicated by the form of the verb. 

The present tense indicates an action at the present time. 

We are planning a trip to the airport today.

The past tense indicates an action at some past time. 

We planned a trip yesterday. 

The future tense indicates an action at some future time. 

We shall plan a trip tomorrow.

The present perfect tense indicates an action completed at the present time. We have planned a trip already, and are about to start. 

The past perfect tense indicates a past before some other past action. action completed 

We had planned a trip before you entered.

The future perfect tense indicates an action that will be completed in the future. 

We shall have planned a trip before you will arrive.

AVOID THE FOLLOWING COMMON ERRORS IN TENSE 

Don't use the present tense when the past tense is required by the meaning. 

This morning Mother gave (not give) Robert a dollar. 

Ruth came (not come) home very late last night.

Don't use the past participle when the past tense is required. 

He began (not begun) his speech with an anecdote. 

Yesterday we swam (not swum) across the lake and back. 

Don't use the past tense instead of the past participle in forming the perfect tenses. 

Sam has gone (not has went) to the ball game. 

Have you seen (not have you saw) Jack?

Don't use the past tense when the present tense is required to express a condition necessarily true in both the past and the present.

The name of the movie we saw yesterday is (not was) "Murder at Midnight."

Don't shift tenses; use the present or the past, but not both. 

Wrong: He ate dinner and then goes to the movies. 

Right: He ate dinner and then went to the movies. 

Right: He eats dinner and then goes to the movies.

Special Note: In telling a story or describing an event, narrate all the incidents in one time sequence. Tell everything that happened in the past tense for consistency of time; or if you wish, you may relate the whole story in the present tense. Only make sure that you do not unnecessarily change the time as you proceed. Wrong: "The manager handed the boy a telegram to be delivered to his own mother. The telegram came from the War 

Department and contained the official announcement of his brother's death. The boy starts sobbing and refuses to deliver the message." Better: "The boy started to sob and refused to deliver the message." Thus, the entire incident is told in the past tense.

SHIFTING THE MOOD 

Mood is the manner in which a verb expresses an action or a condition.

The indicative mood is used to state a fact or to ask a question. 

John is ill. 

The apples are ripe. 

The mechanic inspected the plane. 

Is John ill? 

Are the apples ripe? 

Has the mechanic inspected the plane?

The imperative mood is used to express a command or to make a request. 

Go to the blackboard. 

Answer the third question. 

Please speak distinctly.

The subjunctive mood is used to express a wish, a supposition, or a condition that is contrary to fact. 

I wish I were (not was) home. 

If I were (not was) you, I would study harder. 

Suppose you were (not was) asleep.

John spoke as though he were (not was) president. 

Don't shift moods in a compound sentence. 

Wrong: The dishes should be washed, and then you want me to sweep the floor? 

Right: The dishes should be washed. Then you want me to sweep the floor? 

Right: You want me to wash the dishes, and then sweep the floor?

CHAPTER VII  BONERS 

If you accidentally put lubricating oil into the fuel tank of your car or plane, and poured the fuel oil into the lubricating system, the performance of the engine would soon declare your mistake. For even though fuel and lubricants bear strong resemblance, each type of oil fits its own special purpose. Similarly, in language, there are pairs of words bearing strong resemblance in sound or spelling which are sometimes misused. CHAPTER VIII RULES AND ERRORS IN  (Using the Right Signals) 

Why bother with periods and commas and exclamation points? Who invented all these rules anyway? Just as civil air regulations are necessary for safety in flight, so too are the rules for punctuation and capitalization necessary for clearness in writing. Punctuation marks separate words or sentences by means of recognized symbols. These signals to the reader are just as important as road signs to the car driver. Even in baseball the catcher and the pitcher must understand each other's signals when a batter stands at the plate. An error in punctuation may ruin a letter in the same way that a dropped ball may lose a game. 

Therefore, to play safely, learn to use the right signals whether flying, driving, or writing.

RULES AND ERRORS IN PUNCTUATION USE THE PERIOD [.] 

1. After declarative and imperative sentences. 

We hear America calling. Answer her. 

2. After abbreviations. Mr.

ERRORS IN USING PERIODS 

Do not use a period at the end of a phrase or a clause. A period marks the end of a sentence! 

Wrong: When we left the beach. 

Wrong: The water being too cold for swimming. 

Right: The water being too cold for swimming, we left the beach. 

Do not write sentences following each other with a comma for separation. Use a period to divide sentences. 

Wrong: We went to Uncle John's farm in the country, we had a wonderful time.

Right: We went to Uncle John's farm in the country. We had a wonderful time.

Do not use a period at the end of a question or exclamation. 

Wrong: He shouted, "How dare you!". She replied, “Me?". 

Right: He shouted, "How dare you!" She replied, "“Me?”

USE THE COMMA [,] 

1. To separate a series of words or expressions. 

We bought candy, cake, ice cream, and soda. 

We bought candy, cake, ice cream and soda. (comma before and is optional) 

2. To set off an appositive, unless closely related in meaning. 

Robert Simmons, my friend and classmate, is ill. 

My brother Robert Your uncle Joe Peter the Hermit

3. To set off a transitional word or phrase (e.g., therefore, nevertheless, however).

He was underage; consequently, the Navy discharged him. In addition (or Besides), he had forged his parents' signatures on the consent blank. 

4. To set off an absolute phrase (= a noun plus a participle).

The night being cool, we put on extra blankets. 

The vagrant having no funds, the judged lodged him in a cell overnight. 

5. To set off words in direct address. 

Frank, where were you? 

This, Uncle Joe, is my idea. 

6. To separate a direct quotation from the rest of the sentence. "I believe," he said, "that I will go." 

7. To separate the clauses of a compound sentence, provided the clauses are long or somewhat involved, and they are connected by and, but, or or. 

This is the first time that I have taken a dive from the ten-foot board, but I hope it will not be the last. 

8. After a long introductory dependent clause or extended phrase. 

If you want to play a game of checkers or ping-pong with me tonight, come to my house.

During the exhibition of prize rabbits, no cats or dogs were allowed to enter the building.

9. To set off a contrasting expression introduced by not. 

Peace is trampled upon in Europe, not because the people are militaristic, but because the rulers are selfish. 

10. To set off a non-restrictive clause (i.e., descriptive, but not essential). 

George Washington, who was our first president, was a great leader. 

11. To denote separation in dates and addresses. 

July 18, 1943

ERRORS IN USING COMMAS 

Use a comma, not a period, to separate an independent clause from a dependent clause. 

Wrong: We went to the movies. The weather being nasty.

Right: We went to the movies, the weather being nasty. 

Use commas to separate a series of words, phrases, and clauses.

Do not insert the commas before the first member of the series or after the last. 

Wrong: He is a tall, dark, and handsome, boy. 

Right: He is a tall, dark, and handsome boy. 

Wrong: I can recommend this boy, as being honest, conscientious, reliable, and of good character. 

Right: I can recommend this boy as being honest, conscientious, reliable, and of good character. 

Wrong: Sandwiches, soda, ice cream, cake, and candy, were served at the party. 

Right: Sandwiches, soda, ice cream, cake, and candy were served at the party.

Don't omit a comma in such sentences as the following, which may be misread.

Wrong: When the guests had finished eating several children friends of the family were brought in to sing their songs.

Right: When the guests had finished eating, several children, friends of the family, were brought in to sing their songs.

A non-restrictive clause is descriptive but not essential to the meaning of a sentence and is set off by commas; a restrictive clause is an essential part of the meaning of a sentence and is not set off by commas. The test of essentiality is, "Can this clause be omitted without changing the central meaning of the sentence?" If so, use commas. 

Wrong: General Mac Arthur who fought in the Philippines is now in Australia. 

Right: General Mac Arthur, who fought in the Philippines, is now in Australia. (a non-restrictive clause; set off by commas)

Wrong: Those pilot-trainees, who do not study, will fail their tests. 

Right: Those pilot-trainees who do not study will fail their tests. (a restrictive clause; not set off by commas)

1. To separate members of a compound sentence when no conjunction is expressed.

3. Between clauses of a compound sentence that are joined by transitional words (so, thus, hence, therefore, however, also, then, still, besides, otherwise).

CHAPTER IX SPELLING 

Since we live in an air age, we naturally speak and write in the language associated with flying. Knowing how to handle the vocabulary of aeronautics becomes as important as knowing how to maneuver a plane in flight. The Armed Forces strongly insist upon keeping log books and reporting scouting observations in clear correct English. Business houses demand 100% accuracy in all correspondence. Social notes and friendly letters require acceptable spelling mastery as an index of culture. 

Therefore, spelling claims our constant attention. In every field of human activity, words may be classified under two types: technical terms and popular phrases. It is desirable that we feel competent in dealing with both types. However, the pressure of time and other affairs makes us lean heavily upon the dictionary for guidance. The chief purpose in studying spelling is to make us conscious of word-formations and to prevent us from making serious blunders. Can you make a "three-point" landing, or do you flop onto the airfield in "pancake” style?
