Foreword

IN N PRESENTING this material to teachers and those interested in the Indians of this section of the North American continent I wish to offer apologies.

In the first place I felt that the work was not inclusive enough for publication in pamphlet form, however numerous requests have continually come to my attention for the work in a more convenient form. This booklet, therefore, is presented to meet such requests. 

Secondly, the work is by no means to be considered exhaustive, nor is it meant to imply that the last word on research in this locality.

I ask for the forebearance of critics, and the candor of those who find that I have erred in my interpretation of primary and secondary sources. The Antiquity of Man in America 

The antiquity of man in America has been under discussion for many years, yet today the problem is far from being settled satisfactorily. Nothing has been discovered that proves that any great antiquity is evident. 

Geological formations reaching far back beyond the Glacial Period show the remains of man and much of his handiwork. Therefore the American man would be at least 10,000 years old. However, beyond this approximate date no conservative student would care to accept the evidence as satisfying.

Attempts have been made to trace our Indians to Asiatic and European origin. It would be just as sensible in many respects to try to trace the European or Asiatic from America. We do have inclinations to trace these origins from the known to the unknown, as is evidenced by early traders who assumed that since they were subject to kings, all peoples were subject to kings and the Indians they believed should not be an exception to the established rule.

The first land surface to rear itself above the oceans is thought to be the Labrador coast. It is probable then that the appearance of man in America was earlier or just as early as man in either Europe or Asia. Due to climatic conditions he would naturally be slower to advance. 

The origin of our American Indians is a problem still unsolved. Students, unlike Alexander, still have this unconquered field before them.

It is believed that the land within the present boundaries of Somerset County was used mainly as a hunting ground by wandering bands of Indians. These are believed to be members of the Five Nations, whose central location was within the present boundaries of New York State. However, at many places in the county ample proof has been found of Southern Indian occupation: Flint

arrow tips, broken pottery, drills, burying grounds or cemeteries, and several mounds were found to contain skeletons of different tribes.

One of these villages, which was probably permanent, was located at or between the junction of Quemahoning Creek and Stonycreek, about seven miles north of Stoystown, near the Quemahoning Dam. Here on the farm of 

Samuel Holsopple many relics have been plowed up. On the top of an embankment Mr. Holsopple states that he has found evidence of primitive pottery, numerous flint chips and several clay tubes, probably used as calumets. 

Not far from this point the author picked up a number of arrow heads, a drill, an excellent specimen of stone axe and a skinning knife. The Bureau of American Ethnology,

Bulletin 30, states that this place was known as Kickenapawlings, or Old Town. But at the time of the Forbes expedition through Quemahoning Township this village was abandoned (1758). The village took its name from the chief. It was composed of a mixed group-Delawares and Iroquois and probably some Shawnees. It is mentioned on a map published in 1899 by the above mentioned institution. 

According to Heckewelder, Mahoning means "at the salt lick," while it is highly probable that Cunni, in which manner it was first spelled, means "place of the pines." 

The creek is bordered by numerous pines on much of its course. However, it is believed that Heckewelder was wrong. James LeTort, an early trader, in a signed report made October 29, 1731, to Governor Gordon concerning the tribes living on the Allegheny wrote that a Delaware Indian chief named Quoowahaunee lived on the upper reaches of one of the numerous creeks flowing into the Ohio. Probably the name Quemahoning is derived from this chief's name.

Another important site is found in Elk Lick Township. On a farm once owned by a Mr. Miller, and midway between his farm and the Maryland line, was a well-

preserved cemetery. From this cemetery several skeletons have been removed together with the implements and ornaments which usually accompained the body at the time of interment. This gives evidence of a village nearby, as the custom was to bury the dead at no great distance from the camp. It also gives us the assurance that the site was fairly permanent, as the savages sometimes carried the bones of their ancestors with them when they chose to move permanently. 

Manner of Burial

Their methods of burial are interesting. Some of the bodies were placed in vaults made by placing flat stones upright all about the corpse and then completing the burial by placing huge flat stones over the body and covering the whole with ground. Iroquois and Delaware tradition says that the bodies were first placed on high stakes, the body resting on a platform, where it was allowed to decompose. 

After the flesh had fallen from the bones, the latter were carefully gathered together and placed in a convenient hole in the ground.

Later, the same Indians buried their dead in caves, together with the nearest surviving member of the family, whether wife, brother, or sister. This unhappy living member was forced to stay with the dead. How long this practice continued no person seems to know, but it is told that once a wild animal, serpent, or other agency stole the dead body while the living watchman slept, and thereafter the Indians felt that this was a warning to bury their dead in the ground where they probably belonged. Many other methods seem to be popular in disposing of their dead. Some skeletons are found with their bodies bent double, others in a sitting position, while some are stretched full length in several feet of ground, having nothing more for protection. protection. But whether buried with pomp and glory, or whether thrown into the meanest grave,

if any personal belongings could be found they were promptly buried with the deceased for possible use in traveling through the long lane that connected this world with the Happy Hunting Ground.

General Characteristics 

The characteristics of the Indians in this section were the usual high cheek bones, flat nose, small eyes and straight hair. It seems that their stature varied. Their mortality rate was necessarily high, since living in such crude houses, skin clothing and poor food made them easy victims of pneumonia.

The children ran naked until 10 or 12 years of age, and then in summer wore little more than a cincture, or breech clout. However, in winter they usually had plenty of clothing, as the skins of animals were well furred and furnished no mean covering. 

Usually, when the babe was born it was as carefully cared for as the modern child, but their customs differed: 

Most of the Algonquin tribes dipped the newly born babe into icy spring water (this was to harden him for life) and then wrapped him firmly and tightly in a soft robe to keep his back straight and flatten his head to the proper proportions. Usually the infant was strapped to a flat board and thus held rigidly in place, whether he was carried about upon his mother's back, or propped against a tree trunk.

Some of the eastern tribes allowed the baby to spend its first night outside the wigwam; rain, snow, or what not. If the child survived, it was considered fit to remain in the village, if not it was thought a weakling and was of course buried, and the mother felt that justice has been done. Today we say such actions are savage, which they undoubtedly were, yet it was the only way they had to weed out the weak and unfit, savage though it was. 

If the child died, the mother would fashion a pair 10

of tiny moccasins from a piece of soft fawn skin, carefully embroidered with porcupine quills. These would be for the dead papoose, and would be carried on the empty cradle on her back until she believed the child to be old enough to walk. Then the mother would carry them to the grave where she would place them near the foot of the mound. These moccasins were for the feet of the papoose to walk over the rough ground to the Happy Hunting

Ground. The mother was then permitted to cease carrying the empty cradle, for the spirit of the dead babe had gone forward.

Not all the Indian's time was spent on the warpath, or devising ways and means of torturing captives. The Indian had other occupations to take up his leisure time, despite the fact that we have been taught to believe that about all the early red man did was fight, and sit about the wigwam smoking and telling stories of his hunting exploits.

The Indian warrior always took an active part in the home life. The making of canoes was scarcely ever left to the squaw which was popularly understood, but his was the backbreaking job of felling huge elm trees either in the autumn or spring of the year, when the bark would peel easily. The method of securing such choice material for canoes was in this manner. Usually considerable time was spent in selecting just the right tree for the purpose. 

If a small canoe was to be made, of course a small tree was felled and if a large canoe for the purpose of holding six or eight men chanced to be needed, a tree of ample girth was used.

After considerable time was spent in hacking down the tree the men set about loosening up the bark. This was done by pounding every square inch of the bark, then running a lateral slit up the trunk to desired length, and then with all available care the bark was slowly stripped out around the tree in such a manner that the entire piece came off without a break. The cross-pieces were added to 11

keep the bark apart, the ends were tied together with strips of green hickory or elm bark, and the ends were also gummed together with wild cherry gum, or any of the resinous gums found in abundance in our native forests. 

This canoe then was the much talked of birch bark, and a host of other names. True, the birch was used, but it had scarcely half the utility of the elm canoe. After the elm was somewhat dried it was light, waterproof, capable of holding upwards of a dozen men, and was entirely seaworthy.

There was always the everlasting job of looking for bear-trees. This part of the home life of course, fell into the hunting class, but after all it was carried on close to the home village. When the bear tree was located by means of the numerous scratches that appeared upon the bark, the men usually felled a small tree in such a manner that it would topple against the bear tree. 

Then several of the men made ready to oust the bear from his winter quarters. One man armed with nothing but a tomahaw and a blazing firebrand climbed the tree and poked the fire into the hole where the bear had entered. Then, he retreated rapidly and took up his bow and arrow to await the snuffing and smoke dazed animal. Upon his appearance all the men on the ground began shooting their arrows at it until he fell to the ground. 

The bear was then skinned and divided among them. 

The meat was taken to the wigwam and dried, sometimes by the men and sometimes by the squaws. There were usually many apologies to the animal after it had been killed, together with many promises to reverence its spirit. 

Usually the bear meat lasted a very short time. The entire group feasted until they could eat no more, and as stated above, what little was left was smoked and dried, while the men returned to the forest to find other bear or raccoon trees.

Their methods of living varied. Their food in this section consisted mainly of maize, squash, peas, beans and, 12

of course, the meat the country offered: 

Venison, bear steak, small game, and buffalo. The latter were scarce when the white man came. It is reported that the last buffalo was killed near Reid's City, some distance above Lambert's garage about a half mile above the Lincoln Highway. Wild ducks and geese figured in the diet as their feathers figured in their dress, more ornamental than useful. Their houses were scarcely more than sheets of birch bark placed over a skeletal framework of saplings. 

If the hunter chanced to be successful, his lodge was covered with skins. In the center of the lodge was a smoke hole, which could be covered during inclement weather. 

Imagine the distress of the inmates when this hole was closed and the smoke from the campfire could find no outlet!

Their couches were mainly placed several inches from the ground and were little more than a heap of furs, grass, or boughs of helmlock. In times of need, these skins were smoked or tanned by pounding the brains of a freshly killed animal into them. The hair was scraped from a portion of them, and many curious designs were painted upon them by using a kind of bluish clay found in abundance over the county. 

Penn's Letters on Indians

Much has been written about the Indian's house by early settlers and historians, especially William Penn. 

William Penn in a letter to the Free Society of Traders at London, says, in part, of the Indians, which at that time were mostly Delawares and Shawnees in his vicinity, "When the young women are fit for marriage they wear some manner of ornament on their heads, from advertisement. The age they marry at, if women, is about 13 or 14; if men, 17 or 18, rarely older. Their houses are mats, or bark of trees, set on poles, in the fashion of an English barn, but out of the power of the wind, for they are scarce- 13

ly larger than a man; they lie on reeds or grass. Their diet is maize or Indian corn, roasted in the ashes, or boiled with water, which they call hominie. They have likewise several sorts of beans and peas, that are good nourishment, and the woods and rivers are their larders." 

In the same report he mentions that in regards to their religion they believe a great king made them, and that he lives in a glorious country to the southwest, and that the soul of the good shall go thither and live again. They sacrifice the first fruits of the season, and the fattest buck generally goes into the fire where he is all burned. They bring also, to these festivals, or sacrifices, both black and white wampum, or small shells that were held to be of great value. A small present of this wampum was usually burned as a part of the rites.

General Religious Rites 

These rites were recently performed by the Seminole (runaway) Indians of Florida, which they choose to call the Green Corn Dance." It seems to be an accepted fact that most of the natives of North America practice many of the same religious rites. 

It is generally thought that the Indian spent his life killing off his neighbors and preparing his war paint and decking himself out in his finest array of feathers and knick-knacks to lead a scalping party. This is not so. Most of the savage's leisure moments were spent in recounting his deeds of valor, playing ball and other games. The little savage found a great delight in playing about the woods like a true child of the forest should, and in trying to kill his first game. The serious business of procuring enough food for his numerous progeny took up most of the Indian's time. Excepting if game was exceedingly plentiful the savage found time to plan a scalping party upon a neighboring tribe or clan. This gave the foraging party opportunity to secure women from other tribes for wives, and 14

usually some male captives were secured who were sometimes forced to serve their captor as slaves for a certain period time.

After all it is well to remember that the red man was the first to offer his hand of friendship. The Englishspeaking people accepted the proffered hand, and along with it, took the Indian's corn, and later stole his lands and occasionally his women. They finally drove the red man into the wilderness of the country, and, when that was no more, drove him into the wilderness of despair. A wilderness from which the ragged remnants of his once powerful tribes are slowly emerging to take their place beside their white brothers.

Indian Tribes---The Algonquin Group 

The name Algonquin, means "the place where fish are speared." This name was later to include names of certain tribes that at one time or another inhabited Western Pennsylvania, such as the Shawnee, Delaware, Chippewa, and several minor tribes.

Unlike their brethren, the Iroquois, the Algonquin were never able to effect an organization that would withstand the advancing white man. Finally by the treaty of 

Greenville, 1795, this group made their first land cessions. west of the Allegheny Mountains, and were subsequently pushed to the banks of the Mississippi, and beyond. 

It might be interesting to note that the Iroquois usually favored the English, while this group put their trust in the French. When the latter were driven from the country, the Algonquin went westward, and were forced to agree to the 1795 treaty. 

In 1794, "Mad" Anthony Wayne, scattered what had almost been a closely organized confederacy of this group, and from that time forward, they were so scattered that they were in no position to organize for a final stand against the white man. 15

Their chief deity appears to have been a fabulous great rabbit, but according to some historians, this was merely a symbol. They believed in the immortality of the soul, and had a rather well-founded theory that their deity created the earth and the animals and plants, but unlike the white man's god, there was no benevolence in their creator, who was inclined more to trickery and deception. 

The line of descent always emanated from the female side. 

Their chief was usually selected because of his noted skill and strength, and not from any kingly clan, as is sometimes supposed.

From this group of Indians, the white man first had knowledge of johnnycake, succotash, hominy, samp, and maple sugar. Gookin, in 1674 described their method of preparing food: "Their corn is generally boiled maize, or Indian corn, mixed with kidney beans, or sometimes without. Also, they frequently boil in this pottage, fish and flesh of all sorts, either new taken or dried, as shad, eel, alewives, or a kind of herring, or any other sort of fish. But they dry mostly those sorts before mentioned. 

These they cut in pieces, bones and all, and boil them in the aforesaid pottage. I have wondered many times that they were not in danger of being choked with fish bones; but they are so dexterous in separating bones from fish in their eating thereof, that they are in no hazard. Also, they boil in this frumenty all sorts of flesh they take in hunting, as venison, beaver, bear's flesh, moose, otter, raccoon, etc., cutting this flesh in small pieces, and boiling it as aforesaid. Also, they mix with the said pottage, all sorts of roots, as Jerusalem artichokes and groundnuts, and other roots, and pompions, and squashes, and also several other sorts of nuts and masts, as oak acorns, chestnuts and walnuts; these husked and dried, and powdered, they thicken their pottage therewith. Also, sometimes they beat their maize into meal and sift it through a basket made for that purpose. With this meal they bake bread, baking it in the ashes, covering the dough with leaves. 16

Sometimes they make of their meal small cakes which they boil. They also make a certain meal of parched maize. This meal they call 'nokake.'" 

Use of Ornaments

The Algonqin was very fond of ornaments and used almost anything that might draw attention to bedeck themselves. Higginson, in 1629, says: "Their hair is usually cut before, leaving one lock longer than the rest." 

Sometimes, one side of the head was shaved, leaving the other side quite long. Others shaved a strip back over the top of the scalp, thus leaving hair on the two sides. It appears that their chief motive was to look as frightful as they possibly could. 

Regarding the method of making the birch bark canoes, Zeisberger said: "They peel trees abounding with sap, such as lime, etc., then cutting the bark in strips two or three yards in length, they shaped them by placing stones about the bark, and drying the strips in the sun." 

Their houses of bark were so fashioned, as were some of their community houses. Father Hennepin in his writings said, "Their houses are made like long arbors and covered with double mats of flat flags."

Their method of burial was disclosed in the excavations made by the Pennsylvania Historical Society, in their activities in Jefferson township, Fayette County, when R. 

M. Engberg, archaeologist, in excavating several skeletons found that some of the bodies were doubled up, some stretched out lengthwise, and still others doubtlessly buried in a sitting position. One grave, that of a child, had been covered by a very large stone. Beside the skull were a number of drilled elk teeth, possibly used as a necklace. Another skeleton showed a number of bear teeth had been buried with the body, while still another skeleton was found without any implements or ornaments of any kind. The body of this latter skeleton was doubled up originally. 17

Somerset County Revelations 

Recent excavations made under the supervision of the 

Works Progress Administration at the Clouse farm and the Montague site shows ample evidence of the occupancy of several groups of Indians in the Somerset County section. Many of the graves and villages sites cause one to believe that these locations were used as hunting sites and a few as permanent villages. Twins were found buried in the same grave, and in the vicinity were found many other skeletons showing the possibility of a disease having struck down the members of this particular tribe. In concluding the Algonquin Indians a quotation from Morton on the type of houses and discussion on their wearing apparel is offered.

In 1637, Thomas Morton published his "New England Canaan" in which he described the manners and customs of the Indians. Of their houses and habitations Morton said in part: "The natives are accustomed to build them houses like the wild Irish; they gather poles and place the great end of them in the ground, placing them in the form of a circle and bending the tops of them together in the form of an arch, then bind them together with the bark of the walnut trees which is tough, so that they make the same round on top for the smoke to ascend and pass through. 

These they cover with mats and flags, reeds, or sedge finely sewed together with needles made from the splinter bones of a crane's leg, with threads made of Indian hemp which grows there naturally, leaving several places for doors, which are covered with mats, which may be rolled. up or let down. "The fire is always made in the middle of the house. 

They sometimes drag in a great tree and burn the end by degrees, shorter and shorter until all is consumed. "They sleep in three places in their houses, lying on mats of deer skin, otter, beaver, raccoon, and of bears, 18

all of which they have dressed and converted into good leather with the hair on for a covering. In this manner they lie as warm as desired. When they move they carry with them only the mats and furnishings. Neither do they spend all of their time in one place both winter and summer for that would make both fuel and food scarce, but like the gentry of Europe, move about from place to place as they please. They have meetings from several places where they exercise themselves."

Practically all the tribes lived in houses of this type during the hunting seasons.

Wearing Apparel 

Quoting from Morton on the wearing apparel of the 

Indians he wrote: "The Indians of these parts do make their apparel of the skins of several sorts of beasts and commonly of those that do frequent those parts in which they live; yet some of them, for variety, will have skins of such beasts that frequent the parts of their neighbors, which they purchase of them by Commerce and Trade. "These skins they convert into good leather, making the same soft and plume . . . . the hairy side they wear next to the body in winter time and in warm weather they wear the hair outward: they make likewise some coats of the feathers of 'turkies' which they weave together with twine of their own making, very prettily: these garments they wear like a mantle knit over their shoulders. They have another garment made of Mose (Elk or Moose) they make these skins white, and stripe them with size round about the borders, in form like a lace set on by a 'taylor'. . . . and mantels made of beaver and bear skins: they make shoes of Moose skins, which is their principal leather used for that purpose. For want of that leather they use deer skins . . . . They make stockings that come within their shoes, like a stirrup stocking, and is fastened 19

above at the belt which is about their middle; . . ... those garments they always put on when they go hunting to keep their skins from the brush: and when they have their apparel on they look like Irish in their trousers. A deer skin must have the tail on, which is wrapped about their bodies, fashioned into a pouch wherein they carry their fire making implements. "Thus with their bow in their left hand, and their quiver of arrows at their back, hanging on their left shoulder with the lower end of it in their right hand, they will run away at a dog trot until they come to their journey's end; and in this kind of ornament they do seem to me to be handsomer than when they are in English apparel. Their women and girls have shoes and stockings to wear likewise when they please, such as men have, but the mantle they wear to cover their nakedness with is much longer then that which the men use; for as the men have but one deer skin, the women have two sewed together at full length, and is so large that it trails after them like a great ladies train, and where the men use but one bear skin for a mantle, the women have two sewed together and when they change, they cast the new one over them and drop the other away for modesty . . . . which is to be noted in people uncivilized; therein they feeme to have af much modefty as civilized people, and deserfe to be applauded for it." (The last two lines were written as the text appeared. The letter "s" was written as "f" and all spelling in the above has been largely edited.-Ed.). 

The Delawares 

The original name of the Delaware, which latter. name was given by the white man, was Lenape, or Lenni Lenape, meaning original people. They were the most important of the Algonquin stock. The three great tribes were named Munsee, Unami, and Unalachtigo. Of these three, the Munsee was sometimes considered a separate tribe, so different was their speech and dress. 20

The Delawares were subjugated by the Iroquois about 1720, and subsequently they were forced from their old homes and were obliged to remove to the Susquehanna vicinity. Again, the encroachments of the white settlers caused them to move westward to and over the Alleghenies. 

However, some went south and joined the wandering Shawnee, while still others ventured northward, and later threw in their lot with that of the Ottawas, and later the French.

This last brings us to the story of one Captain Jacobs. 

When the Delawares felt the support of the French at their back, they turned upon their old enemies, the Iroquois, and asserted their independence; asserted that they had been wrongfully deprived of their lands in and about the eastern rivers, and with this new power at their back, they were the bitterest of enemies to their one time friends, the English. Captain Jacobs was a Delaware chief.

When Washington had his trouble with the French, in Fayette County, Captain Jacobs saw an excellent chance to strike back at the English for their part in robbing them of their lands. Accordingly, he joined Bejeau's forces, who had collected to retard Braddock's expedition, when they crossed the Monongehela River. Doubtless he and his followers thought they could pick off a few English scalps, with which to delight the squaws of the wilderness wigwams. And in this he was not disappointed. Instead of merely retarding the English, the combined forces of French and Indians turned the army into a complete rout, and the Delawares had a chance to glut their thirst for blood, and their empty belts with an abundance of English scalps.

The Indians failed to follow up their victory at once. 

They were delighted with the scalps and the abundance of plunder that lay waiting under their avaricious eyes. 

After the smoke of the defeat cleared, the Indians, so long under the hands of oppressors, felt some misgivings about 21

what they had done, and promptly fled until the wrath of the hated English, should blow over, and cool.

But as nothing like another army followed them, the Indians again became emboldened and fell upon the outlying settlements. Their thirst for blood and plunder was not yet sated, and they continued their horrible work. 

Both Captain Jacobs and Shingis learned that the English had put a price upon their heads, and fled to Kittanning, which means "On the large stream." Colonel 

John Armstrong marched against this stronghold on September 9, 1756. The Pennsylvanians surrounded the stronghold and killed or captured the Indians that had taken refuge in the village. Captain Jacobs and his entire family were killed.

Captain Jacobs' memory still lingers in Western Pennsylvania. Jacobs Creek in Fayette County is believed to be derived from this same Captain Jacobs, as is also Jacobs 

Cabins, on the Youghiogheny River. 

Bounty Paid on the Indians

By the Act of April 14, 1756, Governor Morris pro claimed that for every male Indian above twelve years of age, one hundred and fifty Spanish dollars, or pieces of eight, should be paid, provided he be taken alive. For every scalp taken from a male above the age of twelve, 130 dollars should be paid. For deliverance of any Indian, woman or child under twelve years of age, 130 dollars should be paid, and for the scalp of either of the aforesaid, there should be paid fifty pieces of eight. For the return, alive, of any English subject, there should be paid to the deliverer, the sum of 150 pieces-of-eight.

This reward failed to reduce the Indians, and not until 

1763-64, during Bouquet's Expedition to the Ohio, were the Delawares finally subdued. 22

The Mahicans 

Little is known of this group of Indians in so far as our own portion of Western Pennsylvania is concerned. To us they have never meant much more than a group of traveling people, who stayed here only long enough to hunt for a season, and then migrate slowly westward, where they finally made their home.

Mahican means in Algonquin language "A Wolf" or simply wolf. These Indians were known to the Dutch settlers, and were by them named the River Indians. As these Dutch settlers crowded them, they migrated to the 

Susquehanna River about 1730. Here they mingled with the Munsee and the Delaware Indians, and for the most part lost their identity, as this group combined came under the name of "Loups" or wolves. These newcomers to the above mentioned tribes were nicknamed "the Stutterers" because they spoke a strange tongue. Morgan writing of the Mahicans says that they had the same clans as the 

Delawares and Munsee: the wolf, turkey, and turtle, but were later subdivided into the wolf, dog, opossum (wapakwe), and little turtle (Gakpomute), the crane and the chicken.

The mode of dressing varied greatly among the Mahicans. Van der Donck quoted in Bulletin 30, p. 787 states "The men originally wore a breech clout made of skins, but after the Dutch came they wore a lap of duffels . . in addition to this they wore mantels of feathers . .

When the young men wished to look especially attractive they wore a band about their heads, manufactured and braided, of scarlet deer hair, interwoven with soft shining hair." "The women wore a cloth around their bodies, fastened by a girdle which extended below the knees, but next to the body, under this coat, they used a dressed deerskin coat, girt around the waist. The lower body of this skirt they ornamented with strips tastefully decorated with 23

wampum which was frequently worth from 100 to 300 guilders ($40 to $120). They bound their hair behind in a club, about a hand long, in the form of a beaver tail over which they drew a wampum-ornamented cap; and when they desired to be fine they drew around the forehead a band also ornamented with wampum, which was fastened behind in a knot. . . They wore bracelets curiously ornamented . . . . .They manifested grief at a death by cutting off their hair and burning it on the grave." 

Burial Rites 

Ruttenber, quoted from Bulletin 30, p. 787 said:- "The dead were usually interred in a sitting position. It was usual to place by the side of the body a pot, kettle, platter, spoon, and provisions; wood was then placed around the body, and the whole was covered with earth and stones, outside of which pickets were erected, so that the tomb resembled a little house. "Their houses were of the communal sort and differed only in length; they were formed by long, slender hickory saplings set in the ground in a straight line in two rows. The poles were then bent towards each other in the form of an arch . . . These houses rarely exceeded 20 feet in width but were sometimes 180 feet long." "Their religious beliefs were very much the same as other Algonquin tribes." 

The Shawnees 

Shawnee means "Southerner." This tribe was one of the leading groups in Pennsylvania. Students and scholars have tried to attach this tribe to some definite group, but have failed. According to their language they belong to the Algonquin.

The wandering habits of the tribe made their class- 24

ification a very difficult matter for early writers. Many of these historians stated that they belonged to the Delaware clan.

This tribe moved westward by invitation from the Cherokee; this move to the Alleghenies was made in order that they might unite with the Cherokee to fight against their mortal enemies, the Catawba. Since the English were very friendly with the Catawba, one can readily understand why the Shawnee were on the side of the French in 1758. These Indians openly boasted that they had killed more English colonists during the Revolutionary War than any other tribe.

The Shawnee first came into Pennsylvania about 1677 and first lived with the Conestoga along the Susquehanna River. In 1701 Opessa, the chief, made a treaty with 

William Penn, and fifty years later the Shawnees still kept the document in their villages in the State of Ohio. 

Since the Delawares called them their "second sons" they were allowed to live with them. The Shawnee in turn called the Delawares their "Grandfathers."

The Shawnee were constantly finding fault with their neighbors, both red and white, and were consequently ejected from these groups. However, Count Zinzendorf, a missionary, managed to keep them satisfied during much of the French and Indian War, but as before the 

Shawnee again disagreed with the Delawares in 1755-56 and migrated to Ohio. 

Historians and Indian legend say that the dispute was caused through an argument about a grasshopper. This illustrates their childish propensities, and gives one a point of view regarding their quarrelsome habits. 

After joining the French, the group fought the English and Americans for a period of nearly forty years. The end of the warfare coming with the treaty of Greenville in 1795. 

Tecumseh, and his brother, the "Prophet," were members of this erratic race. 25

There are approximately 1,000 Shawnee now living in the United States.

Most of the Indians found in and around Johnstown in 1755-58 were Shawnee and Delaware. Those at Kickenapawlings, at the junction of the Stonycreek and the Quemahoning Creek, were Shawnee and Delaware, while those in the vicinity of Shellsburg were Shawnee alone. 

The Munsee 

Munsee, (Min-asin--ink) means "one that keeps calling or sounding through the night." This was one of the three principal divisions of the Delawares, the others being Unami, and Unalachtigo. However, their speech differed so much that they have sometimes been classed as a separate division. The Munsee were the group of Indians who were defrauded of their lands by the infamous "Walking Purchase" and by it were forced to remove about 1740 to the Susquehanna River, from whence they were driven to the headwaters of the Allegheny River. Some of the 

Munsee lived here as early as 1724.

Due to the work of Moravian missionaries among the Munsee, they were commonly termed Christian Indians. 

The coming of the white man over the Allegheny Mountains drove the Munsee either to Canada, farther westward, or into the roving bands of marauding Indians that terrorized the Pennsylvania frontier for several years.

The Conestogas were driven from their eastern lands by the Iroquois in 1675, invaded the territory of the Conoys and forced that tribe up the Potomac and into western Pennsylvania. Later the Iroquois assigned them lands back on the Susquehanna river where they removed. Later they moved west with the Mahican and Delawares, and were absorbed by these tribes, and like the Conestogas and others joined clans and used the turkey, bear, turtle, and other signs for their signature after they arrived at Detroit for a last grand stand against the steady encroachment of their white brethren. 27

The Cheraws

Very little is known of this group of Indians. The white man made a steady war against them from 1540 up to and including 1768.

These Indians were first mentioned by De Soto in 1540, when the tribe lived in central North Carolina. He named them Xuala, Suala was the name by which they were known by the Cherokee. Early records of Virginia name the tribes Saraw, and still later "Cheraw."

About 1700 these Indians had moved close to the Dan River, near the southern boundary of Virginia where fields. several miles long were cultivated. Sometime during 1710, the Iroquois became dissatisfied with the tribe and drove them eastward, where the colonists of North Carolina declared war on them and made an application to Virginia for help. Governor Eden or North Carolina made the application, which was refused by Governor Spotswood of Virginia. The latter believed that Eden's men were the aggressors. In 1726 the Cheraws took refuge with their neighbors, the Catawbas. In 1759 a party of "Cherraws" 29

under their chief "King Johnny," joined the English in the expedition against Fort Duquesne. These Indians were. probably never natives of this locality, but are mentioned in the text because of their part in the capture of Fort Duquesne, and were a part of the band that traveled over the Forbes Road.

The Iroquois 

The name Iroquois means in the Algonkin language, "real adders." The tribes thus known were the Mohawk, Cayuga, Onieda, Onondaga, and Seneca. These names were known to our history as the Five Nations. Their name in their own language means "men of the long house" from the fact that they lived in houses made of birch or elm bark, and many of the tribal families lived in the same long-house. Sometimes, especially after the Iroquois began their war making, they built houses of logs, and made a runway on the interior, from which they could stand and throw rocks, and other missiles down on their enemy. 

It is believed by many authorities that this confederation of tribes took place no earlier than 1570. The Delawares called the Iroquois the Mingwe, and the other Algonquins named them Nadowa adders. The English gave them the name of Five Nations, but after 1722, they called them the Six Nations. The confederation, it is thought was brought about by their wars with the Algonquins and Hurons, then banding themselves together, they were better able to retain the lands they had gained. 

The Dutch gave them their first firearms, and thus they again were able to carry on their extensions for more land. To the south they pushed their claims as far as the territory of the Cherokee and Catawba, two tribes that resisted their advances, and held them in check. Some authorities say that their westward advance was broken by the Chippewa, but one legend from the Iroquois says: "The Iroquoian advance crept westward, adopting many 30

smaller tribes to themselves, such as the Eries, Andastes, and others. They became so fierce that the babies in their mother's lap were hushed from their crying by the declaration that the Iroquois were coming. Westward, the hated 

Iroquois advanced until they came to a tributary of the mighty Father of Waters. There they met a short stolid stranger. "Who are you?" questioned the Iroquois. "We are Sioux!' declared the strangers, on the opposite side of the stream. 'What do you here?' continued the Sioux. 66 6 "We hunt!' declared the Iroquois. "For what do you hunt?' asked the Sioux. "For men we hunt,' shouted the Iroquois, making ready their weapons. 66 ""You then, have found men. Prepare to fight.' "And they truly had at last found men! For hours the battle raged, and finally the Iroquois were defeated. 

Those who remained on the battlefield, were surrounded, the Sioux gouged out their eyes, burned holes in their tongues, cut off their ears, and otherwise mutilated them, and then sent the survivors back to the five council fires with the request that 'Next time send not women to fight the Sioux, but men!'"

Possibly their hatred for the French was the result of Champlain's foolish attack upon them, while he was hunting with a group of Algonquin Indians. They never forgave the French, but harassed them at every turn until the Five Nations were divided in their sympathies between the English and the American Colonies. All the tribes except the Onieda and about half the Tuscaroras joined the English. It is estimated that scarcely more than 3,000 warriors constituted the original confederation. 

Each of the Five Nations, by a cross division, were divided into eight clans, three of the most important being the Wolf, the Bear, and the Tortoise. The members of 31

each of these clans were spread out through the Five Nations and were forbidden to marry in the same clan. 

This seems to be the one thread by which all the nations clung together.

When the English arrived on the coast of what is now Eastern United States, they found the Delawares subject to the Five Nations, and therefore, unable to make treaties, bear arms, or have any voice in the counciis. They were known as "women" or "Grandmothers" of all the eastern Indians. So long as they did nothing spectacular to get back in the good graces of their fellow tribesmen, such as winning a great battle, etc., the duties of the Delawares at councils of the Confederation were lighting the fires, serving the warriors with the best cuts of meat, and other menial tasks. The Delawares were obliged to climb slowly up the social ladder to a place among the highest warriors. This may be the reason that so many Delawares were anxious to make a showing against the encroachments of the white settlers. It was their last stand for a chance to regain the respect of their fellow tribesmen.

(Tuscarora means "hemp gatherers")

In 1708 there were in North Carolina probably six towns and 1,200 warriors. The Surveyor-General Lawson of North Carolina wrote in his history of North Carolina. (1714) that while these people were mild, kind, peaceable, ingenious, and industrious, they were soon brutalized by the vices of the colonists." The women were debauched by the settlers, and some were kidnapped and sold into slavery. In protest the Indians petitioned the provincial government of Pennsylvania in 1710 to redress or help them with their difficulties. June 8, 1710, two commissioners met this embassy at Conestoga where they met Civility 32

and four other Conestoga Chiefs with Opessa, Chief of the 

Shawnee. Eight wampum belts were presented to the 

Commissioners. The first asked for Christian protection; the second appealed for protection for their children who might bring wood and water without danger of capture; the third asked that young men might hunt for meat for their wives, mothers, and old people in safety; the fourth asked for a lasting peace; the fifth asked that their villages be not destroyed; the sixth asked for peace for all the tribes; the seventh required a cessation from murdering and taking them "so that thereafter they would not fear a mouse, or anything that ruffles the leaves." By the eighth wampum belt, the tribes asked for an official path. between them and the people of Pennsylvania. (Min. of

It is well to reflect that the selling of Tuscarora Indians as slaves to Pennsylvanians and others was one of the actual causes of the Tuscarora War in 1711-13; the other main cause was the occupancy of Baron de Graffenried and his Swiss and Palatine people on their land. The 

Baron feeling that Lawson, the Surveyor-General, was dishonest, complained and remarked that the rough Carolinians were more cruel than the savages. The Baron was captured and offered the following for his release: 

Cloth jerkins; 500 grains of small shot; two bottles of rum and extras for the chiefs of the 10 or more towns, and promised to take no more land from the Indians. 

From this period the Tuscarora drifted northward into Pennsylvania and finally many of them filtered into New York State and became the sixth nation of the league of the Iroquois. (Doc. Col. Hist. V. 376, 1855). 

From the time the Tuscaroras left Carolina 90 years elapsed before they took up a friendly dwelling with the Five Nations in the North. The tribe on the upper reaches of the Juniata River were called Lackens or Lakens. 

It is extremely interesting to note the business of rum selling was a very lucrative trade for this tribe. In 33

1708, according to Lawson, rum had been recently introduced among the westward Indians. The Tuscaroras carried rum several hundred miles from the east in rundlets. They then sold it by "the mouthful." That is "so many mouthfuls for a dressed skin." It is said they never used other measure. It was the custom of the buyer to choose a man with a very large mouth to go with him to the rum market. Each mouthful was carefully emptied into a wooden bowl. The buyer could then drink at his leisure. Later, these same Tuscarora sold wooden bowls to the Indians in which they might empty their liquor. 

Oil and paint served two purposes. They usually anointed the body with a clarified bear oil mixed with ashes. This practice darkened their skin, and served to keep away the mosquitoes. When they wished to dress. very attractively they mixed with the bear oil a red powder made from a scarlet root found in the mountainous section of the country. 

These Indians, like their Iroquoian brethren to the north, were inveterate gamblers. They sometimes bet the very last material thing they had, and on occasion even bet themselves away, the loser remaining the winner's slave until he could be redeemed. Stick guessing games, the bowl and plum seed game, and a game played with chunky stones were famous pastime activities. 

This group of Indians were widely scattered throughout Pennsylvania, and for that reason have been included in this material. (Ref: Pa. Archives, I-XII; Minutes of the Provincial Council of Pa., Col. Rec. I, XVI; Lawson, History of Carolina, 1714, reprinted 1860). 34

Legends and Superstitions 

The term "Mythos" in Greek 

Greek means whatever is spoken by the mouth of man; thus in this material the name comes to mean that which is without a foundation of fact, and the reader should bear this in mind. 

The Indian at all times felt his relationship with other animals and realized that all beings must be of his kin, and as such it was generally treated. They believed in a supreme being, and often addressed their prayers before battle or when they were in dire need of food, to this unseen power. This is true, of course, only after the coming of the white man, for of any previous belief we have little or no accurate data. Then too, the Indian felt the powerful influence of thunder, lightning, heavy rains, the wind, and fierce storms. These were given a fair share of the sacrifices, which every Indian felt was necessary to appease the wrath of the particular "orenda." 

At times the Indians felt that by eating a portion of a particularly courageous animal or man, the courage thus shown would be transferred to the one eating of the portion. 

Alexander Henry, an Indian trader for sixteen years and captured by Indians, relates in his "Travels" published about 1808, that while a captive he witnessed his adopted brother, Wawatam partaking of a human feast. In Mr. 

Henry's own words, let him describe what he saw: "Shortly after, two of the Indians took one of the dead bodies, which they chose as being the fattest, cut off the head, and divided the whole into five parts, one of which was put into each of five kettles, hung over as many fires kindled for this purpose, at the door of the prisonlodge. Soon after things were so far prepared, a message came to our lodge, with an invitation to Wawatam to assist at the feast. of it. "An invitation to a feast is given by him who is master Small cuttings of cedar wood, of about four inches 35

in length, supply the place of cards; and the bearer by word of mouth supplies the particulars. "Wawatam obeyed the summons, taking with him, as usual, to the place of entertainment, his dish and spoon. "After an absence of about half an hour, he returned, bringing in his dish a human hand, and a large piece of flesh. He did not appear to relish the repast, but told me it was then, and always had been the custom among all the Indian nations, when returning from war, or on overcoming their enemies, to make a war feast from among the slain. This, he said, inspired the warrior with courage in attack, and bred him to meet death with fearlessness."

At another time, related in the same volume, Mr. 

Henry mentions their reverence for a rattlesnake's power. "I had been gathering wood for a fire when my attention was attracted by a peculiar noise. I cast my eye on the ground and there discovered a rattlesnake, at not more than two feet from my naked legs. The reptile was coiled, and its head raised considerably above its body. 

Had I advanced another step, I must have trodden upon it. “I no sooner saw the snake, than I hastened towards the canoe to procure my gun; but the Indians observing what I was doing, inquired the occasion, and on being informed, begged me to desist. At the same time they followed me to the spot, to the spot, with their pipes and tobacco pouches in their hands. On returning I found the snake still coiled. "The Indians on their part, surrounded it, all addressing it by turns and calling it their grandfather; but yet keeping at some distance. During this part of the ceremony they filled their pipes; and now each blew the smoke towards the snake, who, as it appeared to me, really received it with pleasure. In a word, after remaining coiled, and receiving incense, for the space of half an hour, it stretched itself along the ground in visible good humor. 

Its length was between four and five feet. Having remained outstretched for some time, at last it moved slowly 36

He states in his published account dated June 1, 1799 (Bourbon County, Kentucky-where he later moved from this section): "The Indians generally are of the opinion that there are great numbers of inferior deities, which they call Carreyagaroona, which signifies the heavenly inhabitants. These beings they suppose are employed as assistants in managing the affairs of the universe, and in inspecting the affairs of men; and that even the irrational animals are engaged in viewing their actions, and bearing intelligence to the gods. The eagle, with this purpose, with her keen eye, is soaring about in the day, and the owl, with her nightly eye, perched on the trees about the camp in the night; therefore, when they observe the eagle or the owl near they immediately offer sacrifice, or burn tobacco, that they may have a good report to carry to the gods. They say that there are also great numbers of evil spirits, which 38

they call Onasahroona which signifies the inhabitants of the lower regions. These, they say, are employed in disturbing the world, and the good spirits are always going after them, and setting things to right, so that they are constantly working in opposition to each other. Some talk of a future state, but not with any certainty; at best their notions are vague and unsettled. Others deny a future state altogether, and say that after death, they neither think nor live."

Mr. James Smith adds, however, that he could speak but imperfectly with them at this time, and could get no further opinions from them regarding their theological views.

Tobacco as first used by the natives of the new world is recorded by Ovideo in "History de las Indias," Vol. I page 130, 1851, wherein he writes: "On the arrival of the first Europeans in North America the natives were observed to make offerings of the smoke of some plant, to their many deities and spirits; by it disease was treated, and the smoke ascending from the pipe was regarded as an evidence of such an act as the sealing of an argeement or the binding of a treaty. Tobacco was likewise offered in propitiation of angry waters, to allay destructive winds, and to protect the travelers." He further adds that "The 

Indians of Hayti in the 16th century had the custom of taking fumigation for the purpose of getting intoxicated (which they call tobacco) with the smoke of a certain herb."

Hariot (Narr. of Virginia-repr. 1893) wrote in 1585: "There is an herbe which is sowed apart by it selfe, & is called by the inhabitants 'Vppowoc': In the West Indies it has diuers names, according to the suerrall places and countries where it groweth and is vsed. The Spaniards generally call it Tobacco. Many notables gave presents of tobacco from 1518 and later. Jean Nicot, a French ambassador to the King of Portugal, sent seeds of the plant to Catherine De Medici about 1559, and for his thoughtfulness the plant has subsequently received his name-Nicotiana-and later the name of the poison which it contains-Nicotine. 

But the Indian was not interested in finding out just where tobacco came from. To him it represented something very desirable, and for this reason our own Indians 40

here found it a likely gift to their deities. It was as before mentioned, offered to the rattlesnake as a peace offering. At other times the Indians threw portions of their scanty supply into roaring flood waters together with their favorite dog, whose legs had previously been tightly bound. 

It was offered to the eagle and the owl, to the spirits that guard the dead, and to all powerful 'orendas' or 'manitos.' 

Sometimes the Indian used the red willow bark he found along the creeks and rivulets. At other times he found occasion to burn the common mullein which shows its gray leaves on every bare hillside in western Pennsylvania.

Usually a certain pipe was used for ceremonial smoking. It was generally delicately carved from soapstone, and was sometimes fitted with reeds to blow smoke to the cardinal points, while others are excellent imitations of turkeys, human heads, and other totem figures. During any ceremony when the peace pipe or ceremonial pipe was being passed about the circle of squatting Indians, it was almost a disgrace to drop the pipe while passing it from one to another, and it was believed that the one dropping the pipe would suffer for his awkwardness.

Weapons

This subject of Indian lore has created by far the most interest among the laymen, first, because of the numerous examples of various types found scattered over our native hills, second, by the native curiosity one displays when the subject is mentioned.

There were three types of weapons used by natives of this vicinity-hammers, or tomahawks, arrowheads, and piercing instruments. In the first class we find some rare specimens. The tomahawks which have been found in 

Somerset County resemble very closely our own pole-ax. 

The writer found one recently that weighs approximately two pounds, has a nicely polished blade tapered to a very sharp edge. The upper portion is rounded and shows that considerable pains had been taken to have this portion accurately pecked out to make it perfectly balanced and attractive in appearance. Held between the thumb and forefinger it balances perfectly, and one shudders to think what would happen to one's skull, if it, fastened to an eighteen-inch handle, were brought down upon one's head.

Many other types of hammers or tomahawks have been found, but the one described above appears to have been the most favored. 

The second type of weapon, the arrow tipped with a flaked stone or flint, or other hard stone was by far the most common. It was projected by means of the well known bow, which every boy knows how to make from the sturdy branches of the ash, the hickory, or even the oak. The stone tips were sometimes gathered in the vicinity where they are found today, some were brought from other localities. Several dozen tips made from cannel coal have been found in the vicinity of the Quemahoning Creek in Somerset County, together with numerous flints, red, yellow, pink, gray, black, and sometimes a combination of these colors.

The size of these stones found along this creek range 43

from three and a quarter inches down to less than one inch. 

All the tips have exceedingly sharp points, jagged edges, and are properly notched to receive either the rawhide thong, or the split arrow shaft. There are, however, one or two exceptions, and that is a narrow needle-like point with jagged edges, sharp point and about two and one half inches long. The butt end is notched as if at one time it was loosely fastened to the end of a shaft.

This latter method seemed to be a favorite device for attaching the stone tips. When the wounded person attempted to withdraw the shaft, the arrow remained and rankled in the wound, possibly causing the death of the individual after much suffering.

The tips were not always fastened by means of thongs, but glue and various types of gum were employed to keep the stones securely attached to the shaft.

The bows were usually made of heavy wood. The average register of a bow was approximately sixty pounds. 

The bow string was drawn to the marksman's ear before the shaft was released. It is said by an authority that an arrow shot from such a bow would sometimes pass entirely through a deer or buffalo. The early Indians plucked all the hair from the right side of the face to keep the hair from getting entangled with the rawhide bow-string, and also wore a wrist guard made of dressed deerskin to keep the string from injuring the wrist upon the release of the arrow.

The piercing instruments were not used as extensively as the arrows or the tomahawks, and were possibly used if at all, infrequently about the wigwam for boring or sewing. 

However, these three weapons were not the only ones used by the Indians to defeat their enemeis. Mohawk 

Solomon told James Smith, during his captivity with the 

Mohawks, Conewaugas, and Delawares, the following story. Allow James Smith to tell the tale in his own words: "We came to a place where buffalo tracks were very plain in the sand, and I said it is surely buffalo tracks; he 44

said, 'hush, you know nothing, may be buffalo tracks, may be Catawba.' He went very cautious until we found some fresh buffalo dung, and then Solomon seemed satisfied that we were following buffalo. He then stopped and told me an odd story about the Catawbas. He said that formerly the Catawbas came near their hunting camps, and at some distance from the camp lay in ambush; and in order to decoy them out, sent two or three Catawbas in the night, past their camp with buffalo hoofs fixed on their feet, so as to make artificial tracks. In the morning, those in the camps followed after these tracks, thinking they were buffalo, until they were fired upon by the Catawbas, and several of them killed. The others fled, collected a party and pursued the Catawbas; but they, in their subtilety, brought with them rattlesnake poison, which they had collected from the bladder which lieth at the root of the snake's teeth; this they had corked up in a short piece of cane-stalk. They had also brought with them small cane or reed, about the size of a rye straw, which they made sharp at the end like a pen, and dipped them in this poison, and stuck them in the ground among the grass, along their own tracks, in such a position that they might stick into the legs of their pursuers, which answereth the design; and as the Catawbas had runners behind to watch the motions of the pursuers, when they found that a number of them were lame, being artificially snakebit, and that they were all turning back, the Catawbas turned upon the pursuers, and defeated them, and killed and scalped all those that were lame. When Solomon had finished this story, and found that I understood him, he concluded by saying, 'you don't know, Catawba velly bad Indian, Catawba all one devil Catawba.'" (Colonel Smith's Capitivity. 1799).

Arts and Crafts

The arts and crafts were in general very well developed. Much was gathered from nature for the immediate needs of the group, but many of the Indians were skilful workmen in environment where nature failed to supply their needs. Environment had much to do with the arts. 

In many places caves were used for dwellings, in other localities conical houses were made of the skins of animals, while in sheltered coves some tribes resorted to the use of bark from trees, and built rude huts. Farther to the north where the danger of attack from other tribes was a menace, the Long House was built to accommodate several families, and was usually surrounded by a palisade. Some of the excavations at the Clouse farm, Somerset County, show definite traces of post holes, probably used at one time to hold upright a portion of a palisade for defense. 

Since this locality was a fairly permanent settlement, the traces may have come from uprights for a long-house. 

The invention of cooking utensils was fairly well developed. Potters clay was used, as were numerous types of soft stone. Recently well preserved clay pots were unearthed in Somerset County. Rare indeed is a find of a wicker vessel. This type was made of woven willows and packed with native clay. Evidences found show that some of these types were baked. Present finds are generally broken remnants.

Clothing took up the time of several persons in each tribe. The dressing of skins was no small task, neither was the work of assembling the material. Early records state that the hard leather was chewed to make it pliable enough to insert a home-made awl. Every animal caught was a potential portion of some Indian's clothing. 

Records now extant show that there was no sharp distinction made among workers. There was apparently no desire on the part of any one in authority to enforce an 49

established division of labor. Natural inclination made one a canoe maker, an expert in stone work, or some other field of industry.

The Indians in this section were never expert beadworkers. Beads came from the rivers, therefore, the workers in this part of the State were more concerned with quill work, feather adornment, and work on flint imbedded in the limestone deposits. Metal workers in this district were not found. There has not been a single trace of metal being found in graves that antedate the arrival of the white man, either for ornaments or utensil.

Woodcraft: Sharp tomahawks were used to fell the pine and other soft wood. Canoes, portions of houses, and drying frames were constructed from trees. When flint failed to produce fire, wood was used. Many methods of making fire by means of two pieces of wood are known, and few Boy Scouts today would fail to make a fire if caught without matches in the forest. The rotary fire drill was a type of mechanical contrivance nearest our modern machinery age.

Root Craft: Many types of roots were used for food, medicine, to poison fish, and in many cases fine roots were utilized to make vessels for storing food. 

Stone Craft: Stone Craft is treated under weapons, and tools, and in that division shows that the Indian had developed a set of tools and weapons that were well suited to his environmental purpose. Very little stone carving is found in the county. 

Gouges: Gouges were stone implements resembling celts or adzes with the face hollowed out. Some writers thought they were used as spiles for tapping maple trees. 

Their use was probably to gouge out mortars. Most gouges were found east of the Allegheny Mountains. 

Hammers: To any primitive people, probably the stone hammer had first preference. Their purpose was to 50

strike, crush, or drive. Hammers were made of any hard or tough material, such as wood, bone, stone, and sometimes deer antlers.

The hammers were held in the hand and used to pound grain or nuts into meal, to stun fish, and to flake fragments from flint.

Hammers so constructed as to require a haft were used to kill and to quarry rock. The haft increased their effectiveness. Many types are found in this locality. 

Usually our local finds have a hatchet form and are smooth and highly polished. 

Hatchets: These metal objects were brought to the

Indians by Europeans. Colonial history insists that the hatchet was much sought after as an effective war implement, whereas the common stone axe or celt was used for domestic purposes (1 McCullough) from various sections. of the country stone hatchets were found with both blade and handle carved from a single piece of stone. 

The hatchet and pipe combination usually found in museums are of colonial period, and were manufactured as an article of trade. No counterpart of this type has ever been found in aboriginal art. Its use in actual warfare is doubtful. It was too light and could not serve a utilitarian purpose as well as a stone axe or iron hatchet. 

Saws Saws, usually serrated pieces of flint were employed in sawing various objects.

Borers: Many types of borers are found in Somerset 

County. In size they range from one inch to four and and even five inches in length.

The type most commonly found have a flat head to fit the thumb and forefinger. The remaining portion is graduated to a very sharp point.

Axes were of many types. Some were roughly made, while others had polished blades, and round heads. None were found having a drilled hole for handles; usually the implement was bound to a wooden handle by a rawhide thong. 51

Scrapers: Chisel like pieces of flint are found in many sections. Their size was varied to suit the individual taste of the maker. Practically all scrapers were made to fit either the fingers or the hand in a snug manner.
