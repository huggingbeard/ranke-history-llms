Foreword 

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) is a multilateral agreement subscribed to by over 80 governments, which together account for more than four-fifths of world trade. GATT is the principal instrument through which the United States works with other countries to reduce tariffs, eliminate nontariff measures, and remove other trade obstacles that handicap the free flow of international trade. The GATT is also representative of an evolving system for international trade. Since its entry into force in 1948, seven rounds of multilateral trade negotiations have been conducted under the auspices of the GATT. 

The most recent round of multilateral trade negotiations took place in Geneva from 1973 to 1979 and is known popularly as the "Tokyo Round." Without doubt, the Tokyo Round was the most comprehensive and far-reaching negotiation since the inception of the GATT more than 30 years ago. The Tokyo Round resulted not only in a significant reduction of world tariffs but also in six major agreements concerned with nontariff measures. Often referred to as "Codes," these agreements lay out new international rules for: subsidies and countervailing measures, antidumping, government procurement, technical barriers to trade (standards), customs valuation, and import licensing. The Tokyo Round also resulted in important agreements regulating other aspects of international trade.

Part One-The Code on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures Introduction 

In general terms, a subsidy is a bounty or grant (usually provided by a government) that confers a financial benefit on the production, manufacture, or distribution of a good or service. All governments, including the 

United States Government, maintain subsidy programs of one form or another. Some forms that subsidies may take are direct cash grants, credits against taxes, concessionary loans, and infrastructure services. The nature of any given subsidy is typically related in some way to the purpose it is intended to accomplish. mal Many subsidies employed by governments are intended to assist in attaining their national economic and social objectives. For example, most governments grant subsidies to foster the elimination of regional economic disparities. In the terminology of international trade, such subsidies are called "domestic" or "internal" subsidies. Governments also make use of subsidies as instruments of their international trade policy-primarily with the objective of stimulating national exports of goods or services. 

Naturally enough, these subsidies are referred to as "export" subsidies. 

A subsidy frequently affords a competitive advantage to nationally produced products in one country over unsubsidized production in another country-with the result being an artificially induced distortion to international trade. Under certain circumstances, the distortion to market forces caused by subsidies can be so severe as to cause or threaten real economic injury to competing industries in other countries. Recognizing that subsidies can have such effects, the United States and other countries have attempted to defend their domestic industries against subsidized competition through the use of special "countervailing duties imposed at the border. When they are levied, countervailing duties are assessed in an amount equal to the net subsidy borne by an imported good. Thus, countervailing duties are not retaliatory in nature but are intended to offset the competitive advantage provided to an imported good in international trade by the subsidy.

The Tokyo Round Agreement Summary 

The international agreement negotiated and accepted during the Tokyo Round¹-known popularly as the "Subsidies Code”—acts as the basic set of rules governing the use of both subsidies and countervailing measures by most of the world's major trading countries, including the United States. The Code entered into force on January 1, 1980. 

Insofar as subsidies are concerned, the Code flatly prohibits the use of export subsidies on industrial and mineral products, regulates the use of export subsidies on agricultural products and primary fishery and forest products, and sets out broad guidelines for the use of domestic subsidies. 

With regard to countervailing duties, the Code specifies that they are to be imposed in an amount no greater than the net subsidy borne by a product and then only where subsidized imports cause or threaten material injury to an industry in the importing country. To deal with the possibility that a signatory of the Code might nonetheless grant a subsidy in violation of the rules found in the Agreement, special rules are provided to guide the settlement of disputes. If a dispute cannot be satisfactorily resolved, the Code allows for special retaliatory countermeasures that might be authorized against the trade of a country which has broken the agreed rules.

Overall, the Code is a major improvement of the international rules found in the GATT that governed the used of subsidies in the past. Prior to the entry into force of the Code, the use of export subsidies on nonprimary products was prohibited only if the granting of a subsidy led to the sale of a product in export markets at a price lower than the comparable price charged for the like product to buyers in the domestic market. 

Further, the prohibition did not apply to export subsidies granted on primary mineral products!

The rules on the use of export subsidies for primary products have also been changed in the Code. Formerly, the rules stated only that export subsidies should not be granted on primary products in a manner that resulted in a country gaining more than an undefined "equitable share of world export trade" in a given primary product.

Prior to the Code, there were no specific rules at all concerning domestic subsidies. Finally, the lack of special dispute settlement provisions for subsidyrelated problems subjected these problems to adjudication in a general dispute settlement context that often proved incapable of resulting in the effective resolution of a dispute. 

While the new Code makes important changes in the international system for dealing with subsidies and countervailing measures, the effectiveness of this new system will not really be known until a body of case law has been developed through the practical application of the Code's provisions applicable to both subsidies and countervailing measures.

To summarize, the rules found in the Code prohibit the use of any form of export subsidy on nonprimary (or industrial) products and primary mineral products and limit the use of export subsidies on primary products so that they are not used in a predatory manner to displace the exports of other countries in world markets. Domestic Subsidies.-The Code recognizes that domestic subsidies are most often employed by governments to achieve objectives unrelated to international trade. As a result, the use of domestic subsidies is in no way prohibited by the Code. Instead, it gives broad guidelines that are intended to aid countries in the use of subsidies that might otherwise have an adverse effect on the international trade interests of another signatory to the Code. To avoid such adverse effects, signatories have committed themselves to a consideration of whether world trade conditions on the product to be subsidized are such that the granting of a subsidy might have undesired consequences. The results of this examination are then to be weighed against the internal objectives intended to be fostered through the use of the subsidy.

While the Code's general rules on the use of domestic subsidies are not very stringent, the important aspect of this section of the agreement is found in its recognition that domestic subsidies can have adverse effects on the trade of another country and that where these effects are found to exist, the affected country is free to pursue an appropriate course of action. For example, if a product benefitting from a domestic subsidy is exported to another country and its importation into that second country causes or threatens material injury to an industry in the importing country, countervailing duties may be applied to the product in question. 

Alternatively, the granting of an internal subsidy might have the effect of changing the competitive conditions within a country to such a degree that imports of a like product from another country are inhibited. Where a subsidy has this type of import substitution effect, affected exporting countries are free to seek a resolution of the problem under the consultation and dispute settlement provisions of the Code. Some hypothetical examples of problem-causing domestic subsidies are included in the appendix to this pamphlet.

The Code's Dispute Settlement Provisions 

The eventual resolution of any dispute between countries under the Code is governed by a "Committee of Signatories" to the Code made up

of representatives from each of the countries adhering to the agreement. 

Each country has an equal voice in the deliberations of the Committee. 

The Committee itself will meet twice yearly in Geneva, Switzerland; however, it can also meet at other times if a special meeting is requested by a party to the Code.

The first stage of the dispute settlement process calls for consultations. 

In this context, any country under the Code may request consultations with the signatory it believes to be granting a subsidy inconsistently with the provisions of the Code. Such consultations must be entered into as quickly as possible. The aim of these consultations is that the parties to the dispute will arrive at a mutually acceptable solution to the problem within 30 days in the case of an export subsidy and within 60 days in the case of any other subsidy. By mutual agreement, these periods may be extended. Such consultations typically involve a discussion of the facts of the matter by representatives of one or more agencies in each government. Although the consultations may be held in Geneva, they are more likely to take place in one of the capital cities of the countries concerned Where a mutually acceptable solution cannot be reached through normal bilateral consultations, either party is free to refer the dispute to the Committee for conciliation. During the conciliation process provided for under the Code, the members of the Committee review the facts of the case and encourage representatives of the disputing parties to develop a solution to their problem. It should be noted that, during its review of the facts, the Committee reserves the right to reject further consideration of those cases where it finds that there is no reasonable supporting evidence of the allegations made by the complaining party. 

If 30 days after the request for conciliation the matter is not resolved, the country alleging an inconsistent subsidy may request that a panel of experts be established by the Committee for the purpose of reviewing the "facts of the dispute. Typically, a panel is made up of three or five members who may or may not be government officials. In no case is a panel composed of representatives who are citizens of the countries involved in the dispute.

Shouy Panels are to be established within 30 days of a request for their establishment. A panel is then given 60 days to present its findings to the 

Committee. After reviewing the facts in a dispute, the panel prepares a report on the case which includes its conclusions as to whether or not a subsidy has been granted in a manner that is inconsistent with the Code's restrictions on the use of subsidies. The descriptive portion of a panel's report and at least an outline of its conclusions are made available to the parties to the dispute before being circulated to the Committee so that the disputing parties have another chance to resolve their differences before the matter is referred to the Committee as a whole. If it should still not be possible to resolve the matter, the panel's report would be circulated to the Committee for its consideration.

Following its consideration of the panel report, the Committee may make recommendations on a solution to the dispute within 30 days of its receipt of the report. Should the Committee decide that a subsidy is being granted inconsistently with the provisions of the Code-and its recommendations are not followed within a reasonable period of time-it is empowered to authorize appropriate retaliatory countermeasures against the offending party (taking into account the nature and degree of the adverse effects found to exist in the case). ماوه The logic behind this process is rooted in the belief that a country found in violation of the Code should first be given an adequate opportunity to eliminate the offending subsidy and, failing such action, retaliatory countermeasures are to be employed in an effort to persuade a country to comply with the Code. The exact nature of possible countermeasures is left unstated in the Code.

Countervailing Duties Under the Code 

Up to now, this pamphlet has concerned itself with the Code's antisubsidy provisions and the dispute settlement provisions that guide the international response to possible violations of the rules on subsidies. In resolving a dispute on a country's violation of the rules on subsidies, the action authorized by the Committee might be circumscribed to a large degree by the nature of a subsidy. For example, in the context of the Code's dispute settlement procedures, the use of an export subsidy on a manufactured product (prohibited by the Code) is much more likely to lead to the authorization of countermeasures than would the use of a domestic subsidy (the use of which is restricted only where there are demonstrable adverse effects on trade). However, a much different situation exists when we look at the circumstances surrounding the use of a countervailing duty. 

Under the Code, countervailing duties are intended to protect enterprises in an importing country against injury from subsidized imports. 

Countervailing duties may be imposed against subsidized imports into a country's market if these imports are found to be causing or threatening to cause material injury to an industry producing a like product in that country. In this context, it is immaterial whether the imported good benefits from an export subsidy or a domestic subsidy-both subsidies are equally "countervailable." WHITE Rules Governing Countervailing Duties.-The Code clearly stipulates the conditions under which countervailing duties may be imposed against subsidized imports. Under the Code, a government may begin an investigation into the existence of a subsidy only when it has sufficient evidence of the likelihood that (a) a subsidy exists, (b) there may be material injury to a domestic industry, and (c) there is a causal link between the alleged subsidy and the injury to the industry. Normally, these investigations are to be initiated upon a written request by or on behalf of the industry affected. However, in special circumstances, the Code does allow for the initiation of an investigation without a request if there is sufficient evidence of injury caused by subsidized imports. When an investigation is formally initiated, the Code requires that public notice be given and that the evidence of both the subsidy and injury caused by subsidized imports be considered simultaneously. The Code also requires that public notice be given at any point where the authorities investigating the case reach either preliminary or final determinations, whether or not the determinations are positive or negative. The Code specifies that, except in special circumstances, countervailing duty investigations should be concluded within a year of their initiation. 

If after sufficient investigation a preliminary positive determination is reached that a subsidy does exist and there is sufficient evidence of injury, investigating authorities are permitted to impose provisional measures against the imported products under investigation. These measures may take the form of provisional countervailing duties or they may be cash deposits or bonds equal to the estimated amount of the net subsidy borne by the imported product. Under certain circumstances specified in the Code, countervailing duties may also be levied retroactively on those imports entered for consumption not more than 90 days prior to the application of provisional measures. :8 

Only after a final determination has been reached that a subsidy exists and that subsidized imports are causing or threatening to cause material injury to a domestic industry can definitive countervailing duties be imposed on subsidized imports. When a definitive countervailing duty is imposed, the Code requires that it shall not be levied in an amount exceeding the amount of subsidy found to exist, calculated in terms of subsidization per unit of the product imported into the country. Further, countervailing duties may remain in effect only as long as the imported

products continue to benefit from subsidies and the countervailing duty is judged necessary to counteract the subsidization which is causing injury. 

Undertakings.—Under the Code, investigative proceedings in a countervailing duty case may be suspended or terminated without the imposition of provisional measures or countervailing duties if an agreement known as an "undertaking" can be satisfactorily negotiated between investigating authorities in the importing country and either the government or exporters in the exporting country. Typically, this would be a government-to-government agreement where the government of the exporting country would undertake to eliminate or limit the subsidy or take other measures concerning its effects with the aim of eliminating the possibility of injury to an industry in the importing country. The Code also allows price undertakings from exporting firms designed to eliminate injury, provided that the government of the exporting country consents to such an agreement.

Any undertakings that might be accepted are not permitted to remain in effect for a period longer than would have been the case had countervailing duties been applied. In addition, while the acceptance of undertakings permits a suspension of proceedings, an investigation of injury may still be necessary if it is deemed desirable by either the government of the exporting country or authorities in the importing country.

Part One of this pamphlet was devoted to an explanation of the international rules governing the use of subsidies, countervailing duties, and other countermeasures that are found in the Tokyo Round Subsidies 

Code. This section discusses the provisions of the Trade Agreements Act of 1979, which approved the U.S. acceptance of the Subsidies Code and serve to implement the Code in the United States.¹

International trade problems arising from the granting of subsidies to goods in world commerce can be dealt with on two separate, but related, "tracks." One track involves the use of countervailing duties by authorities in an importing country, while the second track calls for the Committee of Signatories to the Code to authorize countermeasures where a subsidy is granted inconsistently with the Code's provisions. It should bẹ noted that the track of relief followed in a given case depends largely on the circumstances surrounding the subsidy and its alleged trade effects. 

The countervailing duty track is essentially domestic in nature. So long as a given foreign practice qualifies as a subsidy, imports benefiting from the subsidy can be subjected to countervailing duties if they are found to cause or threaten material injury to a domestic industry in the importing country. The Code's provision prohibiting export subsidies on industrial products is unimportant for the conduct of the case, Further, aside from the consultation procedures under the Code, there is essentially no international involvement in the decisionmaking process followed by investigating authorities, so long as countervailing duties (or "untakings" or "suspension agreements") are employed in a manner that is fully consistent with the provisions of the Code. 

However, the first track (countervailing duties) is more restricted than the second track (dispute settlement and countermeasures authorized by the Committee) in that (1) a countervailing duty serves as an effective remedy only in addressing a situation that might prevail in the market of a country importing a subsidized product; and (2) material injury to a domestic industry must be proven before countervailing duties can be imposed.

However, it is possible that no agreement will be reached. At this point, the investigation would enter a crucial phase. The United States would invoke the multilateral dispute settlement provisions of the Subsidies Code described in Part One, Section 2.C and, in a manner of speaking, the case would go to court.

The provisions of Section 301 parallel the provisions of the international agreement. Under U.S. law, the Trade Representative must recommend to the President what action, if any, should be taken in the case: a. No later than 7 months after the initiation of an investigation if the

petition alleges that an export subsidy has been granted inconsistently with the Subsidies Code; b. No later than 8 months after the initiation of an investigation if the petition alleges that a subsidy other than an export subsidy has been granted inconsistently with the Code; or, c. No later than 12 months after the initiation of an investigation if the petition alleges a subsidy granted by a country that does not adhere to the Subsidies Code.

With the exception of the 12-month period allowed for cases involving nonsignatories, these time limits accommodate those found in the dispute settlement portion of the Subsidies Code and permit the Trade Representative's recommendations to the President to take into account the recommendations of the international Committee of Signatories to the Code. Countervailing duty actions, unlike actions taken under Section 301, serve as a remedy for only foreign subsidized imports into the U.S. mar ket. In addition, in most cases a countervailing duty will be imposed only after it has been determined that (a) a product imported into the United States benefits from a subsidy and (b) a domestic industry within the United States has been injured by reason of the subsidized imports. 

In countervailing duty cases that require a determination of injury to a domestic industry, the Office of the Deputy Assistant Secretary for Import Administration in the U.S. Department of Commerce investigates and determines the existence and amount of an alleged subsidy and the United States International Trade Commission (ITC) determines whether the affected U.S. industry is injured by reason of subsidized imports. The manner in which these agencies conduct their respective investigations is closely guided by U.S. law and regulations and certain decisions by each agency are subject to judicial review in the U.S. Customs Court.

Conduct of Commerce's Investigation 

Preliminary Determinations.—Generally, within 85 days after the date on which a petition is filed, Commerce is required to reach a preliminary determination as to whether there is a reasonable basis to believe or suspect that a subsidy is being provided to the merchandise that is the subject of the investigation. The time period allowed for reaching this determination may be extended to as long as 150 days at the petitioner's request or if a case is determined to be extraordinarily complicated. However, it is the expressed intent of the Congress (as expressed in the legislative history to the Trade Agreements Act) that a preliminary determination is normally to be reached within 85 days. Commerce must publish in the Federal Register its notice of the determination and the parties affected, and must deliver the determination to ITC and all interested parties involved in the proceeding. 

If the determination is affirmative, the amount of the net subsidy must be estimated and stated in the notice. In addition, an affirmative preliminary determination has several important effects. First, beginning on the date of the notice's publication, the liquidation of all entries of the merchandise subject to the investigation is suspended. Secondly, each District 

Director of Customs is directed to require a cash deposit or the posting of a bond or other security for each entry of the merchandise concerned equal to the estimated amount of the net subsidy applicable to the merchandise. Finally, if the preliminary determination of Commerce is affirmative, ITC formally begins its final investigation into the question of material injury to an industry at this point in the process. (ITC's Director of 

Operations, however, may have already continued informal investigative activities.) • Throughout this section, the time limits generally referred to will be those of a normal investigation.

Final Determinations.—Within 75 days after the date of a preliminary determination (in a normal case this would mean within 160 days of the submission of the petition), Commerce is required to reach a final determination as to whether a subsidy is being provided to the merchandise that is the subject of the investigation. Prior to making the final determination, Commerce will normally hold a hearing within 30 days after the preliminary determination is published, at which time an opportunity is provided for all parties to be heard before a designated official of the Department. Typically, these views would be heard orally and would be presented either by the interested parties themselves or by their counsel. 

Written views may also be provided, subject to certain conditions specified in 19 CFR 355.33.

As with the preliminary determination, notice of the final determination, the parties affected, and the estimated net subsidy, if any, are published in the Federal Register Copies are also provided to all interested parties to the proceeding and to ITC. 

If the final determination is affirmative, it will include information on the estimated amount of the net subsidy and the nature of the subsidy found to exist (e.g., whether the subsidy is an "export subsidy" or a "domestic subsidy"). If the preliminary determination was also affirmative, Commerce will refrain from issuing a countervailing duty order until it is notified of ITC's injury determination. If, however, its preliminary determination was negative, Commerce will at this point order the suspension of liquidation on the affected imports and direct the deposit of estimated countervailing duties or the posting of bonds or other securities in the estimated amount of the net subsidy. Also, the final ITC injury investigation would be commenced.

If the final Commerce determination is negative, the proceedings (both in Commerce and ITC, if the latter had begun an injury investigation based on a positive preliminary determination by Commerce) are terminated by the determination. Any suspension of liquidation that may then be in effect also is terminated, with all estimated countervailing duties deposited refunded, and all bonds or other security released. 

Countervailing Duty Orders.-In any case that requires a finding of injury to a domestic industry, Commerce will publish a countervailing duty order only after being notified by ITC that it has made an affirmative determination on the injury issue. In a normal countervailing duty case, Commerce will be notified of ITC's determination within 45 days of the final affirmative determination by Commerce. Commerce is required to publish a countervailing duty order within 7 days of being notified by ITC that an affirmative injury determination has been reached. In other terms, in a normal case, a countervailing duty order will be published within 212 days of the filing of a petition resulting in the investigation. 

A countervailing duty order directs Customs officers to assess a countervailing duty on the merchandise found to be benefitting from the sub-

sidy equal to the amount of the net subsidy determined or estimated to exist.¹º It also includes a description of the class or kind of merchandise to which it applies and includes information concerning "critical circumstances," if found to exist. Countervailing duty orders do not cover the products of any firm that does not benefit from the subsidy found to have been granted to other firms producing or exporting the merchandise in question.

Generally, a countervailing duty order will remain in effect for as long as the subsidized import covered in the investigation continues to benefit from a subsidy when exported to the United States. However, at least once a year, there is an administrative review of the determination made and the amount of any net subsidy is redetermined. An order can be revoked if, for a period of 3 successive years the imposition rate of the countervailing duty is zero or there are no imports subject to such duties.

The information found in the report is based on the results of questionnaires submitted to producers and importers, economic analysis conducted by the ITC staff, and information that may be garnered through a public hearing. The report contains preliminary findings of fact as suggested by the Director of Operations. The Commissioners are free to accept or reject any or all of the report.

“Undertakings" Eliminating Subsidies, Exports, or Injury 

The Subsidies Code provides for the suspension or termination, without imposing countervailing duties, of an investigation on the basis of certain "undertakings" by either the foreign government said to be providing the alleged subsidy or by the exporter(s) of the affected product. 

The U.S. statute provides that an investigation may be suspended upon acceptance by Commerce of an agreement that provides for elimination of the foreign subsidy, cessation of subsidized exports to the United States or-in rare instances-elimination of the injurious effect of the subsidized imports.

The statute imposes certain conditions upon the acceptance of such agreements. These include requirements that Commerce determine that suspension of the investigation is in the public interest and that effective U.S. monitoring of an agreement is practicable. In each case, as conditions for acceptance of agreements for suspension of an investigation, Commerce must notify and consult with the petitioner regarding its intention to suspend, including furnishing the petitioner with a copy of the proposed agreement no later than 30 days prior to the proposed suspension. 

Commerce must also give 30 days notice to all other parties to the proceeding prior to the proposed suspension and give the petitioner and all such parties an opportunity to comment on the record. 

Agreements Eliminating Net Subsidies or Subsidized Exports.—An in vestigation may be suspended at any time before a final determination by Commerce if the government of the country in which the subsidy practice allegedly occurs agrees, or exporters who account for substantially all of the imports subject to the investigation agree to either (a) eliminate completely the net subsidy on the imports, or (b) cease subsidized exports to the U.S. market within 6 months after the date on which the proceeding is suspended.

Agreements Eliminating Injurious Effect.-Where extraordinary cir-

cumstances are present-meaning that suspension of the investigation will be more beneficial to the domestic industry than its continuation and the investigation is complex-an agreement can be accepted that will eliminate completely the injurious effect of the exports to the United States of the merchandise that is the subject of the investigation. Such an agreement may be accepted from a foreign government if that government agrees to restrict the volume of goods exported to the United States or it may take the form of an agreement concerning the prices of the goods to be exported to the United States. In a price-related agreement, there must be guarantees that (a) the suppression or undercutting of prices of like domestic products by subsidized imports will be prevented, and (b) at least 85 percent of the net subsidy will be eliminated or offset with respect to each exporter subject to the agreement. 

The acceptance of an agreement eliminating injurious effect can be nullified by ITC, which, upon receipt of a petition, is required to determine whether the agreement has in fact eliminated completely the injurious effect of the allegedly subsidized imports. If the determination is negative, i.e., the injurious effect has not been completely eliminated, Commerce must proceed toward reaching a final determination of a subsidy after following the procedures normally followed in the case of affirmative preliminary determinations absent an agreement. 

Monitoring of Agreements.-Before accepting any agreement in lieu of completing a normal countervailing duty investigation, Commerce must provide for the effective monitoring of foreign compliance with the agreement. If an agreement is found to have been violated, Commerce must order the suspension of liquidation on unliquidated entries of the affected merchandise and, if the investigation was not completed, it must be resumed as if an affirmative preliminary determination had been made on the day that the investigation was resumed. If the investigation had been completed, a countervailing duty order must be issued. In either case, the petitioner, interested parties and ITC must be notified and a notice of the determination published in the Federal Register. 

Judicial Review in Countervailing Duty Cases 

The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 provides for judicial review in the United States Customs Court of a wide range of determinations that may be made by either the U.S. Department of Commerce or the U.S. International Trade Commission during the conduct of a countervailing duty case. Both affirmative and negative determinations are subject to judicial review and can be contested in the Court by any interested party who was a party to the countervailing duty proceeding. If the Court's decision on the matter is not in harmony with the published determination of either Commerce or ITC, the matter is remanded to the appropriate agency for action consistent with the final disposition of the Court.

Hypothetical Examples of Export Subsidies 

The following hypothetical examples of export subsidies are presented for illustrative purposes only. The fictional names employed for countries, legislation, and programs are not in any way intended to refer to actual countries or their programs. 

Example A: 

In October 1972, the Government of the Thalline Republic enacted the "Machinery Export Promotion Act" enabling the payment of direct government-to-industry grants designed to foster the development and expansion of Thalline machinery exports. The Act provides for government grants to exporters equal to 20 percent of a company's annual incremental earnings that can be attributed to export sales of machinery. Thus, if a Thalline company earned $10 million from machinery exports in 1973 and $15 million in 1974, it would be eligible for a government grant of $1 million in 1975 based on the increment in its 1974 earnings over those realized during 1973.

Comment: The "Machinery Export Promotion Act" is an export sub sidy of a type prohibited by the Subsidies Code. In its "Illustrative List of Export Subsidies," the Code includes "(a) The provision by governments of direct subsidies to a firm or an industry contingent upon export performance."

Example B: 

The copper industry in Nargon is totally owned and operated by the 

Nargon Government. Prior to 1975, government-run Nargon Copper em ployed a uniform pricing system for the copper it sold to domestic and international consumers of the metal. In 1975, however, the President of 

Nargon issued Administrative Decree No. 27, which authorized a 10 percent discount in the price of copper sold to copper consuming firms in the country who agreed to a 5-year commitment whereby they would export at least one-third of their total production of goods incorporating copper. 

Comment: Administrative Decree No. 27 is an export subsidy of a type prohibited by the Subsidies Code. In its "Illustrative List of Export 

Subsidies," the Code includes "(d) The delivery by governments or their agencies of imported or domestic products or services for use in the production of exported goods, on terms or conditions more favorable than for delivery of like or directly competitive products or services for use in the production of goods for domestic consumption, if (in the case of products) such terms or conditions are more favorable than those commercially available on world markets to its exporters."

Example C: 

Corporate income taxes in the Saturnine States are assessed at a rate of 45 percent of a company's profits. Currently, the Saturnine States Parliament is considering action on a bill that would retain current tax treatment for those companies whose profits result solely from domestic sales while reducing the rate of taxation for companies active in export markets. Specifically, the bill proposes to lower the rate of corporate income tax to 20 percent of profits for that portion of a company's profits attributed to export sales.

Comment: The bill under consideration would provide for an export subsidy of a type prohibited by the Subsidies Code. In its "Illustrative 

List of Export Subsidies," the Code includes "(e) The full or partial exemption, remission, or deferral specifically related to exports, of direct taxes or social welfare charges paid or payable by industrial or commercial enterprises." 

Note: Unlike examples A and B (above) the hypothetical bill in this example would not necessarily be in violation of the Subsidies Code where its benefits were realized by an exporter of primary products (aside from primary minerals). In such a case, the Code status of the program would depend on its impact on the exports of a given primary product from the Saturnine States.

Hypothetical Examples of Domestic Subsidies 

The following hypothetical examples of domestic subsidies are presented for illustrative purposes only. The fictional names employed for countries, legislation, and programs are not in any way intended to refer to actual countries or their programs. 

Unlike the examples given for export subsidies in the preceding section, no attempt is made in this section to portray a hypothetical subsidy as a violation of the Subsidies Code. Rather, this section is directed at illustrating the type of international trade effects that might flow from the use of domestic subsidies that may or may not result in an international dispute or a countervailing duty action.

Example A: 

In 1978, the Dorf Turbine Company announced its intention to build a $50-million turbine plant to serve the market in two neighboring countries, Nargon and the Saturnine States. Initially, the company was undecided as to which country would be more suitable for the location of the new plant since neither country imposed tariffs on turbines and the economic situation was comparable for both nations. Several other turbine manufacturers also were operating at the time in both countries. 

In early 1979, the Governor of Elysia, one of the 31 states of the Saturnine States, requested a meeting with the Chairman of the Board of Dorf. The Governor promised that if Dorf would locate the plant in 

Elysia, the state would provide the company with free land and would exempt the company from state income taxes for 10 years. In May 1979, Dorf accepted the Elysian offer and by January 1981, the plant was in full-scale operation. About one-half of the plant's production was marketed in Nargon.

In mid-1983, Spindown Turbines Ltd. of Nargon petitioned for a countervailing duty investigation on the grounds that subsidized imports of turbines originating in Dorf's Elysian plant were causing injury to the turbine industry in Nargon. Eleven months later, Nargon authorities announced their determination that the subsidized turbines had been found to be causing injury to the turbine industry and imposed countervailing duties on all imports of turbines originating in the Dorf plant. 

Comment: In this example, a domestic subsidy (in the form of free land and relief from corporate income taxes) was provided not by a central government of a country but by a subsidiary state government within the country. In all probability, Elysian authorities were less interested in the international trade impact of the subsidy than they were with fostering new investment within the state. Nonetheless, because subsidized exports of turbines to Nargon caused injury to the turbine

industry in that country, Nargon found it necessary to impose countervailing duties on the products in question. 

Example B: 

One of the Thalline Republic's principal exports to Nargon is computers.

Prior to the Tokyo Round of Multilateral Trade Negotiations, the Nargon tariff on computers stood at 10 percent ad valorem. In the trade negotiations, Thalline negotiators were able to secure a concession from Nargon providing for the elimination of the 10 percent duty as of January 1, 1980. In exchange for this concession, the Thalline Republic eliminated the import duty on small aircraft-a concession of principal interest to Nargon. Both sides expected that the tariff concessions would have the effect of fostering increased international trade in the products concerned. 

In April 1982, the Nargon Science Council published a report stating that the country was rapidly becoming an importer of high-technology products. The report warned that unless the Government took steps to foster innovation in Nargon industry, the nation's economy and international terms of trade would suffer a severe deterioration. Acting on the basis of the report, the Nargon Parliament passed the Research and Development Act of 1982, which provided for government funding of qualified R&D expenditures by private industry. The annual budget for the new program was set at $500 million, with 20 percent of this amount earmarked for R&D in the country's small computer industry. 

By mid-1986, a Nargon company, Cylon Data Incorporated, had achieved a major technological breakthrough in computer design that made possible significant reductions in the price for mainframe computers. Cylon had been assisted in this effort by grants made to the company under the 1982 Act. Beginning in 1987, imports of computers from the Thalline 

Republic began to fall off as consumers switched to the less expensive models produced domestically. The decline in computer imports continued in 1988 and 1989.

In the Tokyo Round, the Thalline Republic and Nargon became signatories to the Subsidies Code. Taking advantage of this fact, Thalline officials claimed in 1989 that Nargon had granted domestic subsidies inconsistently with the Code's provisions in that the R&D subsidies to the computer industry had import substitution effects that nullified or impaired the benefits to Thalline exporters of the Nargon tariff concession on computers. The dispute went unresolved to the Code's Committee of 

Signatories, where, after consideration of the report of the panel, the Committee authorized the Thalline Republic to withdraw its tariff concession on small aircraft, which had benefitted Nargon.

Comment: The Subsidies Code requires signatories to seek to avoid causing, through the use of any subsidy, nullification or impairment of the benefits accruing directly or indirectly to another signatory under

the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. A tariff concession clearly falls within this category of benefits. Further, the Code indicates that nullification or impairment may be demonstrated by the effects of the subsidy in displacing or impeding the imports of like products into the market of the subsidizing country. In our hypothetical example, the 

Nargon subsidy had effects that met both of the above tests.
