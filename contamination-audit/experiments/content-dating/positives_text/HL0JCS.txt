HIGHLIGHTS: REVIEWING FISCAL YEAR 1973 

Aviation began to feel the impact of the energy shortage and that fact added a new dimension to the picture of air transportation in fiscal year 1973. 

Scarcity of aviation fuel and the effort to conserve fuel resources affected route adjustment actions in the last half of the fiscal year, just as those factors would later have their effect on rate adjustments in the fiscal year to follow.

Generally, the Civil Aeronautics Board's route program aimed at greater operating flexibility for carriers by lifting unnecessary restrictions and realigning local service carrier route systems from complex segment networks into single segments. The Board continued to hear carrier applications to delete or suspend service at uneconomic points and, in a number of cases, approved substitute service by commuter airlines at small communities. 

Except for cases in progress and certain international awards, the CAB awarded no new or improved competitive route authority and instituted no new competitive authority route proceedings.

The Domestic Passenger-Fare Investigation, largest proceeding in the 

Board's 35-year history, moved closer to completion with the issuance of final Board decisions in the Fare Level and Discount Fares phases of the case.

In the Discount Fares phase, Board opinions found that youth standby, youth reservation and family fares were unjustly discriminatory, and that family and youth reservation fares were also unreasonable, and that they should be phased out by June 1, 1974. The Board also established criteria for evaluation of the reasonableness of promotional fares that might be proposed in the future. The Board heard oral argument on the procedure it should use in issuing its final order in the Fare Structure phase, the last phase to be decided in the case. Some remaining issues in the Joint 

Fares phase were to be decided concurrently with the Fare Structure phase and the Board at fiscal year's close was considering petitions for reconsidera tion in the Seating Configuration and Load Factor phases. 

The Board permitted on an experimental basis a new conceptual approach to air travel pricing, the "Demand Scheduling" proposed by Trans World Airlines, which permitted a type of advance reservation service designed to enable the carrier to operate at high load factors and, in turn, offer substantial discounts. The Board continued to monitor the service 1 P 1

which it deemed sufficiently contained to avoid adverse economic impact on the industry, were it to prove successful. 

In international fares, the devaluation of the U.S. dollar on February 12, 1973 prompted a London meeting the following month of the International Air Transport Association (IATA) traffic conference. The parties reached agreement to maintain the status quo in North Atlantic fares through December 31, 1973, with a general 6 percent increase in all fares for travel originating in the United States to compensate for the adverse impact of the devaluation. The CAB approved the agreement as the only feasible alternative in light of the approaching peak travel season and the public interest in having firm prices to be able to make firm travel plans. 

The Board also approved IATA agreements relating to Western Hemisphere and South Pacific services, concluding that the increases sought were warranted by the adverse effect of the dollar devaluation on revenues of foreign carriers operating in those areas and increases in U.S. carriers' operating expenses. But the corresponding impact of devaluation on the balance of revenues and expenses in North/Central Pacific service did not require any compensating adjustment, the Board concluded. The CAB also disapproved other proposed increases in North/Central Pacific fares on the ground that the carriers were operating more capacity than was necessary to serve the market adequately and that the increased fares would retard traffic growth.

The Board's Bureau of Enforcement, in the second year of its reorganization, marked the most vigorous and effective period in the Bureau's history. 

One result of that activity was the Board's acceptance of civil penalties totaling $410,900, in amounts ranging from $1,500 to $87,000. And that amount, which was a 103-percent increase over the $202,850 accepted in fiscal year 1972, was already almost equalled during the first nine months of fiscal year 1974.

Early in the fiscal year the Bureau of Enforcement held meetings with officials of several foreign governments to establish reciprocal procedures to deal with charter enforcement programs. Preventive enforcement programs were the aim of meetings conducted with air carriers and ticket agents offering ethnic charter flights to Ireland. The carriers concerned canceled more than 100 spurious charters.

The Board also established enforcement field offices at Anchorage, Alaska and Miami, Florida, and disclosed plans to establish others at New York, Los Angeles, Seattle, Dallas and Chicago.

Accounts and records of every route air carrier and suppemental air carrier received some coverage, with 97 regular audits performed. In addition to the regular audits, the staff conducted 70 special examinations, including surveys of the accounts, records, operations and facilities of 23 commuter air carriers and 14 air freight forwarders, with 33 other examinations consisting mainly of air carrier organization studies, subsidy rate support work, ticket pricing surveys and trade agreement transactions. 

During the year, subsidy accruing to eligible air carriers was $65.5 million, compared with $67.2 million for fiscal year 1972. The decrease was wholly related to the local service carriers, which received $61.1 million. 

The other $4.4 million accrued to the Alaskan carriers for services within the State of Alaska. No subsidy was paid to any other carrier group but at 2

the close of the fiscal year, Airwest, Frontier and Texas International had petitions pending for adjustments to past periods. 

Subsidy payments in fiscal year 1973 were made under Class Rate VI. 

The Board planned to establish a new rate (Class Rate VII) effective July 1, 1973, which was to have some innovative features that were expected to extend its life span and minimize future reopenings of the class rate. 

The Board approved, at the direction of the President, the acquisition of Caribbean Atlantic Airlines (Caribair) by Eastern Air Lines but dis approved the merger of American and Western Air Lines in an order affirmed by the President. Parties in one other merger and another acquisition case withdrew after the administrative law judges issued their decisions recommending disapproval. The Board also approved the merger of two intra-Alaska carriers.

Volume 45 of the Board's formal case reports, "CAB Reports," was distributed in June, 1973. Volumes 46 and 47 were in final stages of printing and were to be distributed in the new fiscal year. Volumes 48 and 49 were in various stages of publication during the fiscal year. 3

DOMESTIC AVIATION ROUTE ADJUSTMENTS 

A new dimension-the effort to conserve fuel resources- -influenced recent adjustments to the airline route structure in fiscal year 1973 and it will necessarily influence them in the immediate future. In addition route adjustments continued to be needed for financial strengthening within the industry.

In general, the Board's route program was directed toward improving the operating flexibility of carriers by removing unnecessary restrictions and by realigning local service carrier systems from complex groups of segments into single segments. The Board also continued to hear carrier applications for deletion or suspension at uneconomic points. In many instances, the 

Board approved substitute service operations by commuter carriers at small communities. 

Except for cases in progress and certain international awards, the Board awarded no new or improved competitive authority and instituted no new competitive authority route proceedings.

Unique area-type route investigations involving New England and Alaska are in progress, and the Board heard two cases involving, in effect, the transfer of authority from a trunk carrier to a local service carrier. One other application seeking that type of route adjustment has been filed.

DELETIONS AND SUSPENSIONS 

Trunkline and local service carriers continued to seek termination of operating authority at points that do not generate enough traffic to be served without financial losses. The Board balanced the financial consideration with other factors such as accessibility of the community to other modes of transportation, and proximity to an alternate airport-to determine whether to grant requests for suspension or deletion. Several suspensions and deletions were granted, either through show cause procedures or after hearings.

CONTROL AND INTERLOCKING RELATIONSHIPS 

Under a fiscal year 1970 amendment to the Federal Aviation Act, the CAB has jurisdiction over the acquisition of control by any person of an air carrier. An equity interest of 10 percent is presumed to be control unless the Board finds otherwise. (Public Law 91-62) and any person who acquires more than 5 percent of an air carrier's stock must report such holdings to the Board.

The CAB in fiscal year 1973, as in 1971-72, was involved to a substantially greater extent in ownership transactions affecting air carriers. 

During 1973 the Board approved, after hearing, the applications of Jet Capital Corporation to acquire a 26 percent stock interest in Texas Inter national Airlines, Inc. and of Alaska Continental Development Corpora tion to acquire up to a 46 percent stock interest in Alaska Airlines, Inc., and of several related groups of persons, foundations and companies to acquire Chicago Helicopter Airways, Inc. A request for acquisition of Southern Air 

Transport, Inc. by Stanley G. Williams was pending as the fiscal year ended. 

The new legislation has also involved the Board increasingly in the determination by a number of carriers to create holding companies for the purpose of diversifying their activities. During fiscal year 1973, the Board held hearings in the Air Carrier Reorganization Investigation (Docket 24283) to determine: (1) the relative benefits and detriments of air carrier diversification; (2) whether a reorganization proposal of Braniff Airways, Inc. should be approved and, if so, subject to what conditions, and (3) what action, if any, should be taken in respect to the already completed reorganiza tions of the Flying Tiger Line, Inc. and United Air Lines, Inc. 

Consistent with the guidelines established in the Long-Haul Motor Car rier-Air Freight Forwarder Investigation (Docket 16857) and the Household Goods Air Freight Forwarder Investigation (Docket 20812) decided in 

1969 and 1972 respectively, the Board exempted a number of control and 552-580 O-74-2

interlocking relationships between long-haul motor carriers and household goods carriers, on the one hand, and indirect air carriers, on the other. 

The Board also approved the acquisition by Reader's Digest Association, Inc. from Transamerica Corporation of all of the assets of the Foreign 

Study League, Inc., a study group charterer. In 1971, the Board has disapproved the control by Transamerica of Foreign Study League while it controlled Trans International Airlines, Inc., a direct air carrier, and ordered Transamerica to divest itself of the study group charterer. 

INTERCARRIER AGREEMENTS 

More than 700 intercarrier agreements on a variety of matters affecting air transportation were passed on by the Board during fiscal year 1973. 

Among the more significant were those adopted by the scheduled airlines through their trade associations—the International Air Transport Associa tion (IATA) and the Air Traffic Conference of America (ATC). 

In fiscal year 1971, the Board disapproved an IATA proposal to substitute for its unanimous voting requirement an 80 percent voting requirement for the passage of conference resolutions because the proposal might lessen the limited influence the Board has over international air transportation pricing policies through its approval or disapproval of IATA resolutions. In fiscal year 1972, the IATA members proposed, where unanimity was not achieved, a procedure where bilateral agreements could be executed. The 

CAB concluded that this proposal raised issues of considerable significance, such as whether the amendment would affect the Board's ability to pass effectively on IATA matters which required further examination through the holding of an oral argument. After oral argument, the Board in fiscal year 1973 found the resolution to be adverse to the public interest, noting that the availability of a less-than-unanimous alternative would lessen the willingness of carriers to make the necessary compromises, thereby reducing, perhaps substantially, the number of unanimous agreements-a force which historically has tended to reduce rates, or at least has helped to prevent rate increases. The Board said that by reducing the likelihood of unanimous agreement on rates the amendments could nullify this effect on the present IATA traffic conference regulations.

The Board also held hearings in the Air Traffic Conference Bylaws Investigation (Docket 23542), instituted to determine, among other things, whether the unanimous voting machinery of ATC should be maintained and, if not, whether a representative determination of the conference should be effected by different voting procedures.

The Board held oral argument in the Express Service Investigation (Doc ket 22388) and the Airline Industrial Relations Conference (Docket 23267). 

At year end both cases were before it for decision. The former investigation was instituted to resolve, among other things, the question of whether a long-standing Board approved agreement between all of the domestic certificated air carriers and REA Express, Inc., under which air express service is offered and provided to the shipipng public, should continue. Other issues are whether the services currently provided under the agreement are distinguishable from other air freight services; and whether other persons should be allowed to perform the type of services which have been offered

by REA alone for more than 30 years. The latter case involves the establishment of a body to serve as a common advisor, information clearing house, and, in some instances, representative of participating carriers in labor matters. 

In the supplemental air carrier field the Board deferred action, and sought public comment on a proposal of the International Air Carrier Association (IACA) to establish uniform standards and practices for charter flight eligibility as a means of improving present self-enforcement practices within the organization.

In the area of air taxi operators, the Board tentatively approved, subject to various conditions, a series of agreements between a major air freight forwarder and 34 air taxi operators regarding the provision of priority air freight services to and from various off-line points. 

CAB policy for some years, subject to various guidelines, has been to approve agreements for the provision of technical assistance by U.S. air carriers to the airlines of developing friendly foreign nations, and last year the Board approved provision of such assistance to Air Manila, the national carrier of the Philippines, and Nigerian Airways, the national airline of the Federal 

Government of Nigeria.

AIR TAXI OPERATIONS 

During fiscal year 1973, a total of 2,900 air taxi operators registered with the Board. Of that number, 230 were commuter air carriers which either transport mail or perform scheduled individually-ticketed air services. During the year, the Board amended the registration requirements to permit air taxis to register once every two years rather than annually. 

The Board concluded its investigation to determine whether the existing limitation on the size of allowable air taxi aircraft should be modified. A major result was the replacement of the existing gross maximum takeoff weight limitation of 12,500 pounds with a new limit that permits the use of aircraft with up to 30 seats and a payload capacity of up to 7,500 pounds. 

The CAB also issued eight exemptions to air taxi operators to use large aircraft in scheduled services or in specific geographic markets.

The Board proposed a rule which, if adopted, will amend its regulations to require all forwarders providing C.O.D. service to obtain and file with the Board a surety bond assuring the prompt remittance of monies collected on delivery of C.O.D. shipments.

Combined domestic and international forwarder revenues from air freight forwarding increased from $556,552,000 in calendar year 1971 to $654,096,- 000 in calendar year 1972, and air freight forwarders increased the tonnage they consolidated from 728,297 to 854,687. AIR CARRIER SCHEDULING 

The Board has no direct statutory authority over airline schedules, except in very limited circumstances, but in fiscal year 1972 it was asked for the first time to permit discussions aimed at limiting schedules in certain major markets with a view toward reducing schedule capacity, and thus improving operating economies, without unduly affecting service to the public. These discussions and others subsequently authorized resulted in agreements, which were temporarily approved by the Board, to reduce schedule capacity among competing carriers in four major east-west markets (New York/Newark-Los Angeles; New York/Newark-San Francisco; 

Chicago-San Francisco; and Washington/Baltimore-Los Angeles) and between New York/Newark and San Juan.

Last year, the Board authorized further discussions which resulted in extension of capacity controls in the same markets. On the occasion of its consideration of a further request for discussions of reductions in the four east-west markets, the Board observed late in the fiscal year that, based in experience gained in the transcontinental markets, capacity agreements constitute an immediately effective remedy for eliminating wasteful overcapacity in city-pair markets, and may, in conjunction, serve to dampen and ultimately reverse the chronic and persistent industry-wide tendency to operate unnecessarily costly capacity. Capacity reduction can also have a potential major impact on fuel consumption, the Board noted. 

During the first year of operation, the transcontinental agreement reduced by 9,900 the departures in the three coast-to-coast markets and by 2,700 the one-way flights in the Chicago-San Francisco market. That, in turn, the

Board observed, may well have resulted in a saving of upwards of 120 million gallons of fuel-more than 1.5 percent of the domestic trunk consumption for 1971. With results of this magnitude achievable in only four markets, the potential for system-wide reductions are significant. 

At year end, agreements extending the four east-west and the New York/ Newark-San Juan market reductions were pending before the Board, as were a new request by Continental Air Lines, Inc. for discussions of capacity reductions in the Chicago-Los Angeles and the Chicago-Denver markets, and a request by Trans World Airlines, Inc. for authority to discuss capacity reductions in all other competitive markets.

In another similar proceeding, the Board authorized all scheduled air carriers to hold discussions looking toward the possibility of agreements 12

whereby the carriers would establish standard flight times or cruise speeds, or other operating procedures that could result in fuel conservation. 

In a related proceeding the Board again permitted the airlines to engage in discussions designed to limit aircraft movements during peak hours at certain congested airports (John F. Kennedy International Airport, La- Guardia Airport, Washington National Airport and Chicago O'Hare Inter national Airport) in accordance with a 1968 Federal Aviation Administration rule limiting operations at such airports. The Board also renewed its approval of an agreement among the U.S. and foreign scheduled airlines to limit to 2,500 the hourly flow of passengers arriving at the International Arrivals Building at JFK, the limitation set by the Port of New York Authority after reviewing its facilities and the resources of the Bureau of Customs and other U.S. inspection agencies. 

TRAVEL AGENCY MATTERS 

The Board does not regulate directly the activities of travel agents but becomes involved in this area by virtue of its responsibility to pass upon certain types of inter-carrier agreements, including those adopted by air carrier trade associations relating to the selection, retention and remuneration of travel agents.

After deferring action and receiving comments from interested persons, the Board approved a resolution of the Air Traffic Conference of America relating to adherence by ATC agency applicants to minimum safeguards for protection of airline ticket stock, identification plates and standard ticket forms, and details for the securing by agents of passenger accommodations on particular flights.

A number of ATC agency matters, some of which the Board had deferred action and requested public comment on, were pending before the Board at the close of the year, including amendments to the Standard Agents 

Ticket and Area Settlement Plan, Agents Financial Responsbility, Agency 

Fees, Bonding of Agents, Air Tour Requirements and the formulation of procedures for participation of agency representatives in certain ATC deliberations. Also pending a proposal by the National Air Carrier Association (NACA), an organization of supplemental air carriers, to establish for the first time a program for the appointment and retention of travel agents.

Upper Volta CHARTER OPERATIONS 

Passenger charter flights between the United States and foreign countries (exclusive of Canada) increased by 4 percent in 1972, from 19,394 flights in 1971 to 20,074 flights (Exhibit 9).

The number of passenger seats flown increased from 3,593,049 seats in 

1971 to 3,712,736 seats in 1972, or 3 percent (Exhibit 10).

The Latin American and Caribbean market was the only charter market in which there was marked growth-flights increased 21 percent from 4,845 in 1971 to 5,858 flights in 1972, and passenger seats flown increased 23 percent from 702,936 seats in 1971 to 867,164 seats in 1972 (Exhibits 9-10). 

Transatlantic flights increased by 2 percent from 13,385 flights in 1971 to 13,640 flights in 1972, and passenger seats flown increased by 3 percent from 24

1) 

2,664,560 seats in 1971 to 2,741,374 seats in 1972 (Exhibits 9-10). The minimal growth in the transatlantic charter market is probably attributable to general economic conditions and improved enforcement action. Traffic actually decreased from 1971 figures in some markets-substantially in the cases of the Netherlands (passenger seats flown declined from 186,340 in 1971 to 117,689 in 1972 or by 37 percent), and the United Kingdom (passenger seats flown declined from 819,736 in 1971 to 643,308 in 1972 or by 22 percent), and by a less significant amount in the cases of Austria, Denmark/ Norway, Germany and Portugal. In markets other than these six, transatlantic passenger seats flown actually increased by 39 percent—by 111 percent in the case of Spain, where the increase was greatest (Exhibit 11). 

Transpacific flights decreased by 51 percent from 1,164 flights in 1971 to 576 flights in 1972, and passenger seats flown decreased by 54 percent from 225,553 seats in 1971 to 104,318 seats in 1972 (Exhibits 9-10). The decrease in the transpacific market was a result of the decline in U.S. military activity in Southeast Asia.

In the charter market between the United States and foreign countries, U.S. route carriers had the greatest overall increase in traffic-27 percent in flights and 33 percent in passenger seats flown. The share of the charter market carried by U.S. route carriers also increased significantly in 197240 percent of total flights, and 34 percent of passenger seats flown. 

Foreign route carriers also had a substantial overall increase in traffic in 1972-17 percent in flights, and 19 percent in passenger seats flown. 

Flights and passenger seats flown by U.S. supplemental carriers decreased by 9 percent, and for foreign charter carriers flights decreased by 25 percent, passenger seats flown by 23 percent.

The United States-United Kingdom charter market remains the largest market with 3,386 flights, followed by United States-Germany with 2,688 flights, and Spain with 1,747 flights. 

Flights (1972)

The 6,074 passenger charter flights between the United States and the United Kingdom and Germany accounted for about one quarter of the 20,074 passenger charter flights between the United States and foreign countries (Exhibit 11).

Transatlantic passenger charter flights accounted for 68 percent of the total number of flights, Caribbean and Latin America for 29 percent, and Transpacific for 3 percent (Exhibit 11).

BELGIUM-Negotiations in Brussels in September, 1972 resulted in the signing of a Memorandum of Understanding on charter services for three years. The Understanding permits U.S. and Belgian airlines to conduct charter flights without arbitrary restraints, including the need for prior approval, and establishes the regulatory regime which each side will apply to the charter operations of the other's airlines. The agreement included landing rights for Travel Group Charters (TGC's). 

CANADA-Two rounds of negotiations in Washington, in January and March of 1973, produced progress toward the conclusion of a bilateral charter understanding.

YUGOSLAVIA-Negotiations in October, 1972 in Belgrade and May, 30

in Washington resulted in the initialling, ad referendum, of the first formal bilateral agreement for nonscheduled air services. 

TGC/ABC UNDERSTANDINGS-After several rounds of negotiations in Washington and in Europe, temporary intergovernmental understandings have been reached on the acceptance and enforcement of Travel Group Charters (TGC's) and Advance Booking Charters (ABC's). Such understandings have been signed with France, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, and negotiated (pending signature) with Austria and Spain. In general, the agreements require: (1) the European country to accept as charterworthy traffic on U.S. originating flights which conform to the TGC rules including substitution and comingling (with other types), features generally not otherwise acceptable; (2) the United States to accept traffic conforming to the European country's ABC originations; (3) minimization of administrative procedures; (4) assignment of enforcement responsibilities; and (5) agreement to modify rules to prevent undue diversion from the scheduled air services.

BILATERAL RELATIONS 

The United States had in force in fiscal year 1973 a total of 72 bilateral air transport agreements (Exhibit 16.), including 16 agreements based on an assumption of rights and obligations by former possessions of agreements in force between the United States and the respective mother countries. 

Bilateral air transport agreements, the formal intergovernmenal basis for exchanging air rights with other countries, provide the framework for managing the bulk of the U.S. commercial air relations with other countries and for resolving problems and/or disputes. 

Where there is no formal bilateral agreement, as in the case of Argentina, the Philippines, Yugoslavia, Central America and certain Caribbean, Asian and African countries, air operations between the United States and the countries concerned are conducted on the basis of reciprocity or the unilateral grants of operating authority. 

The Bureau of International Affairs is divided into seven sections with four operating divisions representing specific world geographic areas, responsible for bilateral relations with the countries in these areas.

INTERNATIONAL CIVIL AVIATION ORGANIZATION (ICAO) 

In fiscal year 1973, a conference on the economics of route air navigation facilities and airports was held in February 1973, at the ICAO offices in Montreal, Canada, to review, refine and recommend to the ICAO Council, aviation facility and service financing principles and related matters for the guidance of the Contracting States of ICAO. The Board representative on the U.S. delegation to the Conference was designated the U.S. spokesman on airport matters. Among the many items considered by the Conference were: principles and factors applicable to determination of the costs allocable to international civil aviation; standardization on a method of allocating costs to international civil aviation; principles applicable to charging systems, and standardization of charging methods.

The Conference reaffirmed the existing ICAO principles on costing and cost allocation and the principle of gradualism in recovery. Also the U.S. proposals on a high priority for the ICAO Cost Allocation Study and the recurrent collection of air navigation financial and traffic data were accepted. 

Other U.S. proposals received consideration by the Conference, but were not accepted as Conference recommendations. The United States was successful in securing adoption of language indicating that, depending on State policies, no single party necessarily bears sole responsibility for resolving airport problems.

Facilitation Session 

The Eighth Session of the Facilitation Division of ICAO was held in

Other matters discussed were the impact of high-capacity aircraft on facilitation and the increasing application of electronic data processing techniques to air transport operations, particularly air cargo. 38

New Concept Tied to Efficiency 

Trans World Airlines proposed a new conceptual approach designated "Demand Scheduling," which contemplated a type of advance reservation service designed to enable the carrier to operate at high load factors and, in turn, offer substantial discounts. The carrier has until 60 days prior to the departure date to determine the departure time and flight itinerary which will provide it the greatest operating efficiency, at which time the passenger is notified of these specifics, which cannot then be changed. The Board permitted the program to become effective as a worthwhile and novel experiment, sufficiently contained to avoid an adverse economic impact on the industry if it proves unsuccessful. It intends to monitor the development of this service closely.

INTERNATIONAL FARES AND RATES IATA Meeting 

Activity in the international area was dominated by protracted efforts of the IATA carriers to agree upon a North Atlantic scheduled fare structure for effect from April 1, 1973. Meetings began in July, 1972 and continued throughout the first half of fiscal year 1973, concluding unsuccessfully in December. Most of the carriers were dissastisfied with the current fare structure which was considered to be producing insufficient revenue and responding inadequately to charter competition. But they reached no consensus on an appropriate solution and in January, 1973, carriers began making individual transatlantic tariff filings in anticipation of an open-rate situation on February 1.

The Board suspended the filings of numerous European carriers on the basis that the fare levels were so low as to endanger the already marginal economics of transatlantic service. At the same time, representatives of the 

Board and the Department of State initiated consultations with major European governments, pursuant to their bilateral Air Transport Agreements with the United States in an effort to arrive at a solution. These discussions produced no acceptable consensus. 

Effects of Dollar Devaluation 

As a result of devaluation of the U.S. dollar on February 12, 1973, the

IATA traffic conference convened in London in March and reached agreement to maintain the status quo in North Atlantic fares through December 31, 1973, with a general six percent increase in all fares for U.S.-originating travel to compensate for the adverse impact of the devaluation. The 

CAB approved the agreement as the only feasible alternative, in light of the approaching peak travel season and the public interest in having firm prices so that firm travel plans could be made. 

On transpacific routes, the carriers agreed to increases in most fares of about four percent, subject to an additional five percent increase in dollar fares to reflect devaluation. They also agreed to fares applicable within the 

Western Hemisphere which reflected increases averaging about 5 percent and greater emphasis upon individual, as opposed to group, travel in U.S.- 

Caribbean markets.

The Board approved the agreements relating to Western Hemisphere and South Pacific services, concluding that the fare increases were warranted by the adverse effect of the dollar devaluation on revenues of foreign carriers operating in those areas and increases in U.S. carriers' operating expenses.

The Board concluded, however, that the corresponding impact of devaluation on the balance of revenues and expenses in North/Central Pacific service did not necessitate any compensating adjustment. The Board also disapproved other proposed increases in North/Central Pacific fares on the ground that the carriers were operating capacity in excess of that nec- 44

essary to serve the market adequately and that the increased fares would retard traffic growth.

In the formal investigation of IATA Cargo Rates (Docket 20522), decided in February, 1973, the Board found international freight rates to be unduly preferential and prejudicial to certain United States port cities.

ECONOMIC ENFORCEMENT 

Fiscal year 1973, the second year of its reorganization, marked the most vigorous and effective period in the history of the Bureau of Enforcement. 

The Bureau concluded 1,298 compliance actions through informal means. 

In its new litigation program, it instituted 78 formal proceedings including 23 civil penalty cases, 22 formal complaints, obtained 33 cease and desist orders. The Bureau also obtained two federal court injunctions and filed two amicus curiae briefs. In fiscal year 1973, the Board accepted civil penalties totaling $410,900, in amounts ranging from $1,500 to $87,000. 

That was a 103 percent increase over the $202,850 accepted in fiscal year 1972. Most violations involved charter regulations and tariff requirements. 

Field Investigations 

The Bureau's Investigation Division conducted numerous field investigations into such problem areas as ticket discounting practices in the North Atlantic travel market, fraudulent tariff practices of air freight forwarders in Miami, joint fares, unauthorized air transportation by indirect air carriers, unauthorized air transportation by private flying clubs, and compliance with the Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z of the Federal Reserve Regulations.

The ticket discounting investigation, made in conjunction with enforcement officers of the International Air Transport Association, checked flights operated by 16 foreign air carriers and two U.S. air carriers. (See 

Discounting, page 57.)

THE ENFORCEMENT PROGRAM 

The CAB enforcement program during the year dealt primarily with four areas of carrier activity: charter violations, tariff violations, misleading advertising and unauthorized air transportation.

The relative volume of enforcement activities by type of complaint and entities involved in such complaints are shown in the chart on page 59.

In early fiscal year 1973, the Board met with officials of several foreign governments to establish reciprocal procedures to deal with charter enforcement problems. ACCOUNTING AND REPORTING 

During fiscal year 1973, the accounting and reporting regulation function continued to be faced with demands for development and change in the Board's information systems to assure their integrity and to provide the Board with the type of information needed to reach timely, informed decisions. Significant responsive regulatory actions that were implemented or proposed are highlighted below.

Accounting and Disclosure Standards for Lease Transactions 

Toward the end of fiscal year 1972, the Board adopted new disclosure requirements for leasing transactions against a backdrop of urgent concern over the inconsistent pattern of reporting vital financial and operating aspects of lease commitments and the absence of leadership by authoritative professional accounting bodies in tackling the problem. The Board recognized that action as only an interim measure and it now has under consideration a proposal which would expand these disclosure requirements to enable users of air transport statements to better assess the present and prospective effects of lease commitments on cash flows, debt/equity ratios, financial position and operating profit investment bases. 

The proposal would also establish accounting standards for recording the present value of required lease payments due in future periods-standards which have long been needed to make meaningful comparisons between air carriers which finance flight equipment acquisitions with debt or equity capital and those which obtain such properties under long-term noncancelable leases.

Growth of Cost of Vacation Pay and Similar Benefits 

During the past decade, there has been a rather pronounced increase in the costs of vacation pay and similar plans which compensate employees directly for time not worked. Between 1962 and 1972, the total annual vacation liability of the certificated route carriers rose from 44 million to 195 million dollars. Of particular significance is the indication that specific features of vacation pay plans employed often influence both the accounting for and presentation of such costs in financial statements. 

Substantial accounting issues surrounding this subject are whether the costs of vacation and similar benefits should be allocable to the time period 61

over which they are "earned," to the accounting year in which the entitlement "vests," or to the accounting year in which the benefit is "exercised." In recognition of the substantial diversity that exists, the Board issued a proposed rule with the objective of narrowing these differences, providing guidance for the appropriate accounting and, most important, placing on carrier management the obligation to be sure financial statements are understandable, complete, unimpaired and sound. 

In a related action, the Board adopted a regulation which requires submission of a statement of accounting practices and procedures for employee pension plans, as well as an annual filing of a duplicate copy of a Department of Labor form which discloses the actuarial assumptions used in computing a carrier's annual pension benefit costs for funded plans. 

Air Freight Forwarders and Air Taxi Operators 

The Board today is confronted, as it was not a year ago, with mounting evidence that: (1) certain air freight forwarders are receiving preferential treatment from direct air carriers as well as direct carrier financing; (2) financial failures of marginal air freight forwarders are causing substantial financial losses to the shipping public; (3) U.S. international air forwarders are facing increased foreign competition which threatens to squeeze them out of markets outbound from the U.S., and (4) an increasing number of long-haul motor carriers are penetrating the air forwarder industry with potential diversionary effects. 

Noting the emergence of these critical problems and the expanding number of forwarders, the Board reexamined the adequacy of its existing reporting system for this carrier class. That evaluation led it to formulate an entirely new set of reports and uniform system of accounts which was to be circulated to the air freight forwarding industry through a notice of proposed rulemaking.

On December 27, 1972, the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) released its Air Taxi Safety Study, which includes, among other things, a review and analysis of air taxi accident data and an identification of predominant accident causal factors. The report pointed out that the absence of certain basic data made it impossible to compare the level of air taxi safety with that of other types of air transportation with any degree of statistical accuracy. The NTSB made several remedial recommendations to the Board and the Board prepared a proposal which would expand the existing reporting requirements for both air taxi operators and commuter air carriers to provide NTSB with the statistical means for measuring the safety standards of these classes of carriers. In providing for their reporting, the proposal also will require certain other basic data that the Board believes would be desirable in complementing these and the operational data presently reported.

Expenditures for Federally Directed Antihijacking Measures 

During fiscal year 1973, the carriers began incurring significant unavoidable costs in carrying out Federally-directed security measures, and the Board was faced with a situation never encountered before. The security 62

tit art om. costs could not have been anticipated by the Board in the fare-level phase of its Domestic Passenger Fare Investigation (Docket 21866) and, because the security measures were considered temporary and outside the scope of normal business activities, the problem was considered one of recovering identifiable costs of performing a unique function by means of assessing a special charge, rather than one of fare level. It became essential to develop accounting and reporting requirements and related security costs to assure a sound basis on which to evaluate the special security charges. 

Passengers Traveling Under Reduced Rates 

It became increasingly clear during the fiscal year that a significant divergence existed among carriers in accounting and reporting for passengers traveling under reduced-rate transportation authorized under section 403b of the Act, resulting in distorting passenger revenues, revenue passengermiles and yields.

The passenger revenue classification is intended to encompass revenues from transportation services offered to the public. It is not intended to cover revenue received from airline employees, officers and directors, or others who are traveling under reduced-rate transportation authorized by section 403 of the Federal Aviation Act and Part 223 of the Board's Economic Regulations, or from travel agents, cargo agents and tour conductors traveling at reduced fares.

In general, the carriers are under no common-carrier obligation to provide such services for this noncommercial traffic and presumably they do not provide capacity for such persons. To achieve uniformity in this respect, the Board sought comments on a proposal which would exclude persons traveling at reduced rates from the passenger revenue classification and record them in an "other operating revenue" classification. 

Travel Group Charter Enforcement 

To tighten up the provisions of the previously enacted Travel Group Charter Regulation related to enforcement, the Board amended its regulations to require advance filing of prospective passenger name lists on a prescribed form (CAB Form 372a). The direct air carrier also must use an officially stamped exact copy of that form in verifying the identity of enplaning charter participants. The amendment also requires the direct air carrier, in verifying the identity of enplaning travel group charter participants both in domestic and international markets to use a document bearing an identifying number as a source of verification and to enter the document number on the passenger list.

Audit Procedures 

Audit procedures employed to examine air carrier accounts and other records were revised further in fiscal year 1973 with a view toward increasing the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the CAB audit program. 

Financial and statistical data contained in carrier reports filed with the Board were analyzed and evaluated for indications of major changes in accounting policy, unexplained cost trends, inconsistent reporting, and noncompliance with applicable accounting and reporting regulations. Computer application facilitated the reviews and evaluations, the results of which were incorporated into audit programs for use by Board auditors at carrier offices.

Audits Verify Compliance 

Accounts and records of every route air carrier and supplemental air carrier received some audit coverage. The 97 regular audits performed included 20 audits of subsidized carriers, 25 audits of supplemental carriers, and 52 audits of nonsubsidized route carriers. The audit objectives were to verify compliance with the Board's economic regulations, particularly those embodied in the Uniform System of Accounts and Reports, to report any apparent uneconomical operations and extravagant management practices and to evaluate the financial condition of supplemental carriers. 

The major accounting and reporting subjects examined and reported on to the Board during fiscal year 1973 were investments in nontransport activities, formation of and transactions with charitable foundations, compensating bank balances required by lender banks as a condition of granting credit, depreciation of property and equipment for regulatory purposes, accounting for foreign exchange gains and losses, denied boarding compensation, bad debt losses, and basic financial indicators of the supplemental carriers. 

In addition to the regular audits, 70 special examinations were performed which included surveys of the accounts, records, operations, and facilities of 23 commuter air carriers and 14 air freight forwarders. The remaining 33 examinations consisted primarily of air carrier reorganization studies, subsidy rate support work, ticket pricing surveys, and trade agreements transactions (exchange of transportation for advertising).

The commuter air carrier and air freight forwarder surveys marked the first on-site examinations of this group of carriers. New and improved audit procedures, including more research and planning in the headquarters office and continued use of the public accountants' working papers and reports, generated the audit capability for making the surveys.

CONSUMER AFFAIRS 

The Board has been striving to improve the overall quality of air transportation. Through its Office of Consumer Affairs, it monitors consumer sentiment and provides assistance where required to satisfy the needs of the air traveling public. 

During the past fiscal year the Office handled a record number of individual complaints, 12,868. There were an additional 433 specific inquiries made by Members of Congress in behalf of complainants-187 from the 

Senate and 246 from the House of Representatives. 

Through the use of a computer, the Board has been able to expedite the handling of complaint letters and initiate investigations. Because of the effective use of the computer, 90.9 percent of the 5,358 complaints received in the July 1 to December 31 period were dispatched to carriers within the first 10 days of receipt. In the second half of the year, 99.2 percent of the complaints were dispatched within 10 days. 

In the first half of the fiscal year 55.7 percent of the complaints were processed and closed within 30 days. In the second half of the year, 48.8 percent were closed within a month. Since March, 1971, direct and indirect air carriers have paid in excess of $500,000 to passengers and shippers who sought assistance in resolving disputes arising out of, among other things, oversales, fare misquotes, baggage problems and delayed COD shipments.

Over 65 percent of those complaints leading to cash payments were for $100 or less.

The Board's Office of Consumer Affairs analyzes the various types of complaints and has used its analyses to initiate a number of rule making actions. It also has met publicly with carrier managements to effect changes in procedures that have benefited the traveling and shipping public. 

Publication during the fiscal year of two guidebooks-"Air Travelers' Fly-

From time to time, meetings have been arranged for students, professors, businessmen and city officials with appropriate Board staff members so that such visitors can receive current information related to their special interests. During the past year the Office also was responsible for arranging presentations to the Board by State and Congressional delegations concerning air service problems. 

The Office is kept informed on all matters of public record before the Board and is constantly aware of air carrier applications affecting service in a particular market so that it can keep Members of Congress up to date on Board activities involving their states or districts. The Office also assists with air transportation problems experienced by their constituents as a 552-580 - 74 - 6 71

Litigation during fiscal year 1973, for the most part, consisted of direct statutory review of orders of the Civil Aeronautics Board in the U.S. Courts of Appeals. In addition six complaints also were filed against the Board in the U.S. District Courts. 

Fifty-nine proceedings were instituted against the Board, compared with 31 in the preceding year. At the close of the fiscal year, 34 petitions for review had been disposed of, 52 were pending. In the preceding year 21 petitions were disposed of and 27 were pending at year end. 

An important case pending in the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit at the close of fiscal year 1972, Air Line Pilots Association, International v. Civil Aeronautics Board, 475 F. 2d 900 (C.A.D.C. 1973), involved a challenge to the Board's approval of an agreement among American Airlines, Trans World Airlines, and United Air Lines providing for reduction of scheduled seating capacity in four major domestic markets for approximately one year. While rejecting contentions that a hearing was required to consider the antitrust implications of the agreement, the court remanded the case to the Board for further consideration of the impact of the agreement "on carrier employees." But the court permitted the Board in its discretion to continue the agreement in effect pending the necessary reconsideration. On remand, the Board supplemented its prior findings in accordance with the decision of the court and reaffirmed its approval of the agreement (Order 73-4-13). No appeal was taken from the order on remand.

The District of Columbia Circuit also heard two appeals from the Board's approval of agreements among the member carriers of the International 

Air Transport Association. In Aviation Consumer Action Project v. Civil 

Aeronautics Board, C.A.D.C. No. 72–1492, decided January 31, 1973, petitioner challenged the Board's approval of IATA agreement which increased from $25,000 to $50,000, the maximum fine which could be assessed by the association against a member carrier for violation of any of the various "traffic conference resolutions" of the Association. In affirming the order the court found no showing of error in the Board's determination that the voluntary inter-carrier penalty agreement was neither precluded by the civil penalty provisions of the Federal Aviation Act nor adverse to the public interest.

The second case, American Importers Association v. Civil Aeronautics 

Board, 473 F.

LEGISLATIVE PROPOSALS 

The CAB is required by the Federal Aviation Act to include in its annual report such recommendations for additional legislation as it believes to be necessary for discharging its regulatory functions. The Board's present legislative proposals for 1974 are encompassed in the following program. 

1. Protection of Air Transportation Consumers During Airline Labor-Management Disputes 

Legislation is needed that would provide procedures for safeguarding air transportation consumers' interests during an airline labor-management dispute without discarding the orderly bargaining processes of labor and management.

In the CAB's opinion, the emergency dispute provisions of the Railway Labor Act are inadequate for dealing with labor disputes in the airline industry. Rather than encouraging serious bargaining, the Act discourages it. Since the parties to a dispute know that an emergency board may be appointed by the President to investigate and report respecting the dispute, they tend to demand—and offer—respectively more and less than they are willing to accept. Furthermore, the parties are inclined to look upon the recommendation of an emergency board as a basis for further bargaining on their part since the recommendation is not binding on them. 

The CAB proposal would amend the Federal Aviation Act so as to provide that if an airline dispute were not settled within 30 days after the submission of a report by an emergency board established by the President under the Railway Labor Act, the President could invoke a "final offer selection" procedure. Under this procedure, final offers of each party would be submitted to a neutral panel. This panel would select, without alteration, the most reasonable of these offers as the final and binding contract to settle the dispute.

Although the existing emergency strike provisions of the Railway Labor Act would continue to be applicable to airline disputes, the final offer selection procedure would encourage the parties to draw their respective positions closer together rather than pull further apart. The parties would compete to make the most reasonable and most realistic final offer-one which would have the best chance to win the panel's endorsement. This procedure would have the virtue of providing finality without containing those aspects of compulsory arbitration that are inconsistent with free collective bargaining. Legislation has been drafted. 

2. CAB Assessment of Civil Penalties 

Legislation is needed that will permit the Board by order to impose civil penalties for violations of the economic provisions of the Federal Aviation 85

Act, or Board regulations thereunder. The Federal Aviation Act now provides that a person violating any such provision of the Act is thereby subject to a civil penalty. However, while the Board may compromise a civil penalty action, the penalties can actually be imposed only by a U.S. district court in a proceeding instituted there. This procedure has had a substantial adverse impact on the Board's ability to ensure compliance with the requirements of the laws which it has the responsibility for enforcing. 

The CAB proposal would empower the Board, upon notice and hearing, to order the payment of civil penalties up to the maximum amount fixed by statute. A Board order imposing a penalty would be subject to review by the U.S. Court of Appeals. Failure to pay a penalty ordered by the 

Board would subject the person involved to an action in a U.S. district court for recovery of amounts owed to the United States. Legislation has been drafted. 

3. CAB Court Representation 

Legislation is needed that would grant the CAB the right to representation through its own counsel. The judicial review provisions of the Federal 

Aviation Act do not expressly provide that the CAB may participate in court proceedings through its own counsel as a matter of right. This right would be comparable to that exercised by other independent regulatory agencies. Legislation has been drafted 

4. Inspection of Ticket Agent Records 

The CAB should be given authority to inspect the records of ticket agents. 

The Federal Aviation Act presently gives the CAB such power with respect to air carriers. Since the Board does not possess similar power with respect to ticket agents, it must rely on the cooperation of the agents in enforcement problems involving them or upon the time consuming subpoena procedure. This has resulted in serious difficulties. In many instances ticket agents have refused to make their records available, which has resulted in delayed enforcement action and lost opportunities to obtain important documentary evidence of violations of the Act (S. 2889, H.R. 4212). 

5. Rebates by Ticket Agents 

Legislation is needed which will make it clear that the civil penalty provisions of the Federal Aviation Act are applicable to "ticket agents" who grant rebates. Both air carriers and ticket agents are subject to the criminal penalty provisions of the Act for knowing and willful grants of rebates. 

Air carriers also are prohibited by certain of the economic regulatory provisions of the Act from rebating, and thus are subject to the civil penalties applicable to violations of the economic provisions (pursuant to the civil penalty provision that was subsequently added to the Act). Since ticket agents are not, in terms, prohibited from rebating by the economic provisions, the anomaly arose whereby ticket agents are arguably immune from civil penalties for the same actions that would subject them to criminal 86

eac start Stro penalties. The CAB proposal would amend the economic provisions of the Act so as to specifically prohibit ticket agents from charging their customers either more or less than the tariff amount (S. 2889, H.R. 4212). 

6. Notice of Tariff Changes 

Legislation is needed that would amend the Federal Aviation Act so as to require a carrier to give at least 45 days' notice of any proposed tariff change and to require the Board, in the event that it decides to suspend a proposed tariff for interstate or overseas air transportation, to do so at least 15 days prior to the time the tariff would otherwise go into effect. The present 30-day notice period allows the Board insufficient time to review and consider complaints requesting suspension of proposed tariffs. The number of procedures that must be accomplished within the 30-day period often takes up most of that period, and in such circumstances the question of whether the operation of a tariff should be suspended cannot be resolved until a few days before its effective date. Thus, the status of a tariff proposal can be uncertain until immediately before the carrier proposes to make it effective. This, in turn, can result in serious inconvenience for the traveling and shipping public and for the affected carriers. 

Although the Board would continue to have only 30 days within which to evaluate complaints requesting suspension of tariffs in interstate or overseas air transportation since the Board's suspension power would terminate 15 days before the expiration of the 45-day notice period, both the air traveler or shipper and the carrier would benefit from amendment of the Act in this manner. In the case of tariffs in foreign air transportation, the Board would be provided with an additional 15 days within which to study and analyze tariffs since the Board would not be required to suspend a tariff of this nature at least 15 days before its effective date (S. 1873, H.R. 10881).
