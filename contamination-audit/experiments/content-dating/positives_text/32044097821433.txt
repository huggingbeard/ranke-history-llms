In compiling this Final Report of the Legislative Council Committee of the 79th General Assembly, we have endeavored to keep this booklet small enough for you to carry it in your pocket, yet with sufficient content to advise you of the work undertaken by the Council with regard to each study and the reasons which caused us to arrive at our conclusions and recommendations. You will note that in many instances we have referred to statements and material on file in the office of the Council and have stated that this information and copies of bills designed to carry out our recommendations are available to members of the 80th General Assembly. We have followed this plan because we realize the impracticability of including all this material in one booklet. We hope that you will feel free to call on us for any background information mentioned in this report. Your Council and its subcommittees have met on many occasions during the past two years. Our study meetings have always been open to all members of the General Assembly, and copies of material filed have been sent to legislators at regular intervals. We have endeavored at all times to remain completely non-partisan in our approach to matters affecting our state government, and the enclosed reports and recommendations are made in that spirit and represent the unanimous agreement of all Council members. 

Good government can be achieved only by a thorough knowledge of the facts and problems confronting us. We sincerely hope that the work of the Council is assisting you in this respect and that this report will enable you to pass on the matters we have studied with the feeling that the facts have been developed by your own agency, the Legislative Council.

The Council is making the recommendations contained herein in obedience to requests of the 79th General Assembly. We are giving you our best judgment as to how the 80th General Assembly should dispose of these problems. The decision is in your hands. 

Yours very truly, James L. Bomar, Jr

THE LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL 

The Legislative Council Committee exists by virtue of acts of the 78th and 79th General Assembly contained in Sections 3-401 

through 3-406 of the new Tennessee Code Annotated and its 1955 Supplement. From the date of its creation in 1953, the work of the Legislative Council was watched with great interest by students of government, and when the General Assembly convened in 1955 the Council had already been accepted as a vital arm of the legislative branch of our government. Recognizing this, the 

79th General Assembly called on it to make studies and recommendations on some nineteen separate subjects and provided necessary funds with which to conduct these studies. 

Because of the huge task assigned it, the staff has been gradually increased until it now has an Executive Director, an Assistant Director, a Study Analyst and four stenographers, all of whom stand ready to assist the members of the General Assembly in their work. 

The Council itself is composed of members of the General Assembly representing both major political parties of the state and its membership is equally distributed among the grand divisions of the state.

LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL COMMITTEE 

3-401. Creation-Composition.-There is hereby created a legislative council committee hereinafter referred to as the council, which shall constitute a special committee of the general assembly. It shall be composed of the speaker of the senate, the speaker of the house of representatives, three (3) senators to be appointed by the speaker of the senate, not more than one (1) of whom shall represent the same grand division of the state and not more than two (2) of whom shall be members of the same political party, and six (6) representatives to be appointed by the speaker of the house of representatives, not more than two (2) of whom shall represent the same grand division of the state and not more than four (4) of whom shall be members of the same political party, plus two (2) senators, appointed by the speaker of the senate, neither of whom shall be from the grand division in which the appointing officer resides, and two (2) representatives, appointed by the speaker of the house of representatives, neither of whom shall be from the grand division in which the appointing officer resides. Within sixty (60) days after the convening of each regular session of the general assembly, the speaker of the senate and the speaker of the house of representatives shall name the members of the council and the members so appointed shall serve until the end of their terms of office, and until their successors are appointed and qualified. A vacancy in the appointive membership of said council shall be filled by the officer making the original appointment. The members of the council shall elect a chairman and a vice-chairman and such other officers as they deem necessary. (Acts 1953, ch. 215, Sec. 1 (Williams, Sec. 172.26); 1955, ch. 37, Sec. 1; 1955, ch. 223, Sec. 1) 

3-402. Meetings-Subcommittees-Record of proceedings.- 

The first meeting of the council shall be held in the senate chamber at the state capitol on call of the speaker of the senate within fifteen (15) days after their appointment. At its first meeting, the council shall provide for adoption of rules of procedure and employment of necessary clerical, technical and professional assistance. Thereafter the council shall meet at such times and places as the council may deem necessary; provided, that it shall meet at least once in each quarter year and shall meet at any time upon the call of the chairman. Eight (8) members shall constitute a quorum but any motion or action of the council must be authorized by a majority vote of the total number of members to which the council is entitled. The council may create subcommittees consisting of its own members or other members of the general assembly or such citizens having special knowledge on a particular subject as the council may determine, to study and report on assigned subjects. Any members of the general assembly shall have the right to attend any of the sessions of the council or of its subcommittees and to present his views on any subject under consideration, but only members of the council shall have

the right to participate in any decision which the council may make, and only members of a subcommittee shall have the right to participate in any decision which a subcommittee may make. 

The Council shall keep an accurate record of all proceedings, make periodic reports to the members of the general assembly, and keep said members informed of all matters which may come before the council, the actions taken thereon, and the progress made in relation thereto. (Acts 1953, ch. 215, Sec. 2 (Williams, Sec. 172.27). 

3-403. Staff.-The council may employ such personnel, including an executive director who shall be a graduate of an accredited college or university with training or practical experience in governmental problems, as it may deem necessary for the execution of the functions of the council and its subcommittees, and shall determine the qualifications and fix the compensation of such personnel. The council may, in effectuating the provisions of this chapter, contract with any public or private agency or educational institution for research studies or the gathering of information. The council or any subcommittee may call upon any state department, agency, or officer, or agency of any political subdivision for such facilities and data as may be available and state departments, agencies and officers, and agencies of political subdivisions shall cooperate with the council or any subcommittee to the fullest possible extent. (Acts 1953, ch. 215, Sec. 3 (Williams, Sec. 172.28). 3-404.

Duties.-The council shall have the following duties: 

(1) To furnish all members of the general assembly with adequate research and, in cooperation with the attorney-general or in any manner which the council may determine, bill drafting facilities. (2) To maintain in the state capitol for the use and information of the general assembly, legislative reference, library, and bill drafting facilities, and to collect, summarize and index information relating to legislation and legislative policies. The council may provide for such other office space as it may deem necessary. (3) To prepare studies on important problems for the consideration of the legislative branch of the state government either on its own initiative or as directed by the general assembly or either house thereof. (4) To study and analyze state revenues and expenditures and the problems and functions of the various departments, agencies and officers of the state and to make reports thereon to members of the general assembly. (5) To submit a report of its findings, conclusions and recommendations to the governor and each regular session of the general assembly not later than seventy-five (75) days before the 10

opening of such session. It may submit with its report drafts of such bills as it may deem advisable. (6) To assist individual legislators in collecting data and drafting bills. (7) To report on the content and status of pending legislation and to prepare manuals and directories. (8) To receive messages and reports in person or in writing from the governor or any other state officials. (9) To cooperate with similar agencies of other states, with the council of state governments, and with other interstate research organizations. (10) To perform all other duties the council or the general assembly may deem essential to the efficient carrying on of the work of the legislative branch. (Acts 1953, ch. 215, Sec. 4 (Williams, Sec. 172.29). 

3-405. Assistance from other agencies.-The council may call upon the attorney-general, state comptroller, state planning commission, state library, state university and all other state departments and agencies for assistance and advice; and it shall be the duty of the attorney-general to render opinions, and give counsel and assistance to said council on request of the chairman of said council. (Acts 1953, ch. 215, Sec. 5 (Williams, Sec. 172.30). 

3-406. Expenses and salaries.-The members of the council and of any subcommittee established pursuant to this chapter shall be paid a per diem of ten dollars ($10.00) for each day of meetings of the council or of any subcommittee attended by them, and shall be reimbursed for all necessary traveling and other expenses incurred in the performance of their duties. All payments to members, payments to witnesses attending hearings or meetings under process, salaries of employees and operating expenses shall be paid by the director of accounts upon written authorization and direction of the chairman or vice-chairman of the committee. (Acts 1953, ch. 215, Sec. 6 (Williams, Sec. 

172.31); 1955, ch. 37, Sec. 2.) 11

STATEMENT OF POLICY 

When the Legislative Council of the 78th General Assembly made its report in the fall of 1954 it decided to include a "Statement of Policy" so that it would not be necessary to repeat certain questions and conclusions in discussing each study. Most of these questions and conclusions concerned the need for revenue to finance recommendations growing out of our studies. In an attempt to state a position on this matter, the Council of the 

78th General Assembly stated, in part: 

66 the Legislative Council has concluded that its function is not to recommend new taxes-this being a prerogative peculiarly vested in the corporate body of the Legislature itself" "Instead, the Legislative Council's duty rests solely in the careful and thorough study of the problems that confront our State government, within the framework of the mandates issued by the 1953 Legislature, and to recommend the objectives it feels should be attained in these various fields of study. The final attainment of these objectives, each irrefutably coupled with additional revenue requirements, lies within the wisdom of the duly elected representatives of the people" The present Legislative Council now finds itself in somewhat the same situation. We do not, however, believe that a simple reaffirmation of the above statement (although we continue to subscribe to it), can fully discharge our responsibility to the members of the General Assembly. For that reason we wish to make some suggestions concerning the overall financial policy of the state government, as established by the General Assembly. 

Federal Road Program 

In reporting the facts concerning the new federal road program, we said that our study had revealed a need for new funds in excess of $35,000,000.00 during this and the next biennium in order for the Department of Highways and Public Works to carry on its present program and at the same time match all funds made available under the new federal road program. A part of this money is needed immediately. 

We did not recommend how this money should be raised because the Highway Study Commission, authorized by the 1953 Legislature, now has under way a study on highway revenues and financing. It is our understanding that, prior to the convening of the 80th General Assembly, this Commission will file recommendations with the Governor that will set forth a detailed plan or plans for financing this program on a sound basis. 12

Other Services and Revenue Needs 

Our recommendation that the counties share of the cost of the welfare programs be reduced will cost the state approximately $460,000.00 annually. Recommendations for expanded services will, if adopted, require general fund appropriations of approximately $500,000.00 annually. We are of the opinion that current revenues are sufficient to cover these amounts without the necessity of tax increases.

There are, however, many departments of our State government that we have not studied. We are reasonably certain that some of these departments will request additional funds for expanded services. If this situation does arise, we suggest that the 80th General Assembly carefully weigh all requests and that any extension of services or a reduction of local contributions be kept within existing revenues. 

Education 

This brings us to a question that has been debated across the state for many months: "WHAT WILL THE 80TH GEN- ERAL ASSEMBLY DO FOR EDUCATION?" 

The 1955 General Assembly increased the retail sales tax from 2% to 3% and pledged the additional 1% "specifically and exclusively to educational purposes." The Council believes that it was the intention of the 1955 Legislature that the revenue from this additional 1% sales tax be used to enrich the program of education, over and above the cost of the program for the 1953-55 biennium. We are of the opinion that we should carry on our educational program during the next biennium without any further tax increase.

We have studied the yield from the 1% sales tax increase and are of the opinion that it will produce enough revenue during this and the ensuing biennium to enable the state to continue its present program of education, absorb all increased costs occasioned by rising attendance, teacher experience and training, etc., continue our present program of capital improvements for education, financing the cost of such improvements in our colleges, universities and special schools for the remainder of this and the ensuing biennium on a "pay-as-you-go" basis and at the same time provide for an increase in teachers' salaries. 

We recommend that the 80th General Assembly: 

1. Appropriate sufficient funds to maintain our present educational program. 

2. Appropriate $2,775,000.00 to finance the remainder of this biennium's capital improvement program for colleges, universities and special schools, totaling $7,025.000.00, from revenue rather than by bond issues. (Most of the revenue going to the sinking fund for repayment of bonds comes from 13

gasoline tax revenues. We believe that these improvements should now come out of revenues pledged for educational purposes.) 

3. Appropriate at least $7,025,000.00 from revenue for capital improvements in our colleges, universities and special schools during the 1957-59 biennium. (This is the same amount that we will spend for this purpose during this biennium.) 

4. Appropriate $2,700,000.00 for the 1957-58 fiscal year and $5,450,000.00 for the 1958-59 fiscal year for salary increases for our teachers. Based on the Council study, when these appropriations are made for the benefit of education, all estimated available revenues derived from the 1% increase in the sales tax will have been fully appropriated for educational purposes. 

Surplus 

We are informed that unappropriated funds in the treasury of the state totaled $35,502,780.63 on June 30, 1956. This appears to be a rather large sum at first glance, but when we realize that the State of Tennessee is now spending more than $300,- 000,000.00 annually, we see that this amount would run our state government for only a few weeks. 

It is true that money from tax sources is steadily coming into the treasury, but these same funds are constantly being paid out in accordance with the appropriations of the General 

Assembly. Unfortunately, neither income nor expenditures can be divided into equal installments to provide a balanced flow of income and expenditures for each week and month of the year. 

For this reason-just as it is necessary for us, in our personal affairs, to anticipate those weeks and months when we will have heavy expenses and attempt to lay a little by in order to be able to meet an insurance premium or to buy a new winter coat or to get clothes and shoes for the children to begin the fall term of school-so it is with the State of Tennessee. There are certain times during the year when authorized expenditures are much greater than at others. One of these times is in the month of August when the State sends one-third of its appropriation for the minimum school program to the cities and counties of the State, so that they may have sufficient funds to open their fall term. As the cost of education grows, the amount of this payment increases each year. In order to meet this ever-recurring obligation, the state must have enough money in its treasury in August of each year or else borrow money to open each school term. We are informed that, at the present time, it is necessary for the state government to begin its fiscal year on 

July 1 with at least $25,000.000.00 in what is now called the

Working Capital Fund, in order to meet seasonal demands such as that referred to above. (If the cost of the minimum school program and other government services continues to rise, and 14

all indications are that they will, this Working Capital Fund must be increased, because the August payments will become larger each year.)

In considering what amount might then be available for expenditure, from unappropriated funds in the treasury, we ought to subtract $25,000,000.00 from the sum of $35,502,780.63 mentioned earlier.

It might seem at first glance that this tremendous amount of money is available for increased appropriations for services, salaries, etc. However, we do not see surplus funds as being available for salaries and operating expenses that will recur annually, but rather for capital improvements and as a cushion in the event of a sudden decline in state revenue. 

It has long been our custom in Tennessee to finance the cost of capital improvements for carrying forward programs of advancement in mental health, higher education, correctional institutions, highways, etc. through the issuance of bonds. We believe that whenever surplus funds are available in the treasury, over and above the Working Capital Fund, we would do well to use these funds for capital improvements, instead of issuing bonds.

We, therefore, recommend that the present surplus, $10,- 

502,780.63 plus any amount that may accrue in the future, over and above the Working Capital Fund, be used for capital improvements such as those mentioned above, thus keeping the bonded debt of the state at the lowest possible level. 

We further recommend that all acts authorizing bond issues for capital improvements contain a section providing that in the event it is determined that surplus funds are available, over and above the Working Capital Fund, the bond authorization shall be cancelled and surplus funds used to make the improvement.

A sound financial program is one of the first essentials of good government. Our state has recently had its bonds advanced from an "A" to an “AA” rating. We believe that we are on a sound basis at the present time and are proud of the part the 

General Assembly has had in this achievement. 15

INDETERMINATE SENTENCE LAW, PARDONS, PAROLES AND PROBATIONS 

On the day when two or more family groups began to live together as a tribe or clan, man began to wrestle with the problem of punishment for crime. This problem is one of the greatest mysteries with which the mind of man has been faced. Wherever men have begun to think about life and its meaning, to contrast the good and the bad, the pleasant and the unpleasant, they have been compelled to ask themselves what good they are accomplishing when they punish their fellowman for his crimes. That punishment should be remedial, that it should be punitive-these are explanations which readily occur to men, and are accepted by some as being a complete answer; but they leave unexplained whole areas of human experience and countless instances where punishment seems to work no good and to avenge no sin.* 

Tennesseans have made progress in this field. We have advanced from the day when the stocks and the lash were standard punishment to our present day system where an attempt is made to rehabilitate those who violate the rules of our society. 

Many theories have been advanced and many cures tried. 

However, except for the fact that prisoners today are better fed, clothed and housed and more humanely treated, Tennesseans cannot look at the results of their system and honestly say that much headway has been made in their efforts to rehabilitate. 

Our citizens realize that society must be protected, and that in some instances the only way to give law abiding citizens the protection to which they are entitled, is to confine the criminal for life, if need be. However, a good look at our penal system and at the rate of crime in our state causes our citizens to wonder if the doctrine of retribution has answered the age old question, "How can man correct his brother so as to make him a better man?" Hate begets hate; retribution produces resentment and a purpose of revenge; and long periods of confinement usually make men worse instead of better.

It was with these thoughts that the 79th General Assembly of Tennessee enacted Senate Joint Resolution No. 42, by which the Legislative Council was: "authorized, empowered and directed to make a study of Indeterminate Sentence Law and its operation, as well as the broad question of Pardons, Paroles and Probations as now in effect in the state . . . 

A number of people from over the state with knowledge of and interest in this subject were invited to appear before the 20. * See Theodore H. Robinson, The Epistle to the Hebrews (London, 1933), 16

Council on July 6, 1955, and present facts and opinions on this subject. Appearing before the Council at that time were: Hon.

Many statements, documents, and reports, including the report by the Tennessee Bar Association, and a Model Probations

Act were filed by those who took part in the study. All of these reports, as well as the statements filed by many of the above named participants, are on file in the Legislative Council Office, and available to the members of the 80th General Assembly. 

We found that one fact stood out in the study-a pressing need for a probation system in Tennessee. At present the state has none, and all who appeared at any time during the study agreed that this was the most outstanding need. A great majority favored the administration of such a system under the Board of Pardons and Paroles, making it a Board of Pardons, Paroles and Probations, which was its former title. 

It was further agreed by those who participated that the probation system should have a Chief Probation Officer, and one 17

probation officer for each criminal circuit of the state. These officers, among other things, would make investigations for the judge in cases where probation was being considered. 

A study of the present Indeterminate Sentence Law brought forth many differences of opinion. Those who oppose the present law say that neither the people generally nor the jury trying a case under its provisions understand this law; that where two people are convicted of the same offense, yet one is of more serious consequence or caused more harm to an innocent person, the jury took notice of this by providing a longer maximum sentence in the more serious case, yet each, under the present law, would be eligible for release at the same time.

Those favoring the present law say there is no reason, if the judges properly charge the jury, for such misunderstanding to exist; that although two people convicted of the same offense are eligible for release at the same time, even though the jury gave one a longer maximum sentence, they are only eligible for release (whether they are retained or released is a matter that must be decided by the Board of Pardons and Paroles); that this power of the Board is often instrumental in keeping the prisoners in line while incarcerated, as well as affording an incentive for those confined to work toward their own rehabilitation. 

Other matters which were suggested by those participating in the study, which came within the proper scope of the study were: 

1. That the Board of Pardons, Paroles and Probations be made a permanent board and the terms of the membership staggered so that each governor will appoint one member for a four-year term with the authority also to fill any vacancy during his term. 

2. Extend and broaden the powers of the Board of Pardons, Paroles and Probations. 

3. Provide proper supervision of parolees. 

4. Extend the trial judges' authority in parole matters. 

5. Clarify the criminal laws of the state, both statutory and common law. 

6. Make the Indeterminate Sentence Law more explicit so that it can be understood by the public, and particularly by the juries.

In addition to the above matters, the members of the Board of Pardons and Paroles asked that consideration be given to the following suggestions: 

1. That the probation system be broad. Mr. Crow stated that records indicate that 85% of first offenders placed on probation never become second offenders. 18

2. That the present law be changed to provide that in all cases where a man is to be released at a definite time, the Board be given the power to release him one year before that time in order to insure employment and proper supervision for at least one year after his release from an institution. 

3. That the Secretary of the Board of Pardons and Paroles not be eligible to serve as a member of the Board. 

4. Extend the trial judges' authority to suspend sentences for grand larceny, housebreaking, and burglary, and provide that supervision be exercised over those offenders receiving suspended sentences for the period of the suspension.

After full, fair and impartial consideration of all these matters, the Legislative Council recommends to the 80th General 

Assembly: 

1. That no changes be made in the Indeterminate Sentence

Law at this time. The Council does, however, wish to call attention to the fact that there was considerable difference of opinion expressed on this.

There are valid arguments on both sides, but since no satisfactory substitute plan has been proposed, the Council has made the above recommendation. One solution that appears worthy of consideration was offered by Mr. Crow. He suggested that the present minimum sentence under the Indeterminate Sentence Law be abolished and that the minimum sentence in all cases be 

1/3 or 1/4 of the maximum sentence imposed by the jury trying the case, and in no case less than one year. 

2. That a probation system be established, with a Board of 

Pardons, Paroles and Probations, and administered under the Department of Corrections. 

3. That a sufficient appropriation be made to provide a Chief 

Probation Officer and an additional officer for each criminal circuit of the state, and that high qualifications for these officers be required, and that these officers also serve to supervise all persons whose sentences are suspended by the criminal judge. 

4. That the Secretary of the Board of Pardons, Paroles and

Probations not be a member of the Board, and that the Board be made a permanent Board of Pardons, Paroles and

APPREHENSION, SEGREGATION AND TREATMENT OF SEX OFFENDERS 

The Legislative Council was directed by House Joint Resolution No. 26 of the 79th General Assembly to study our laws on apprehension, segregation, treatment or punishment of sex offenders and report its findings to the 80th General Assembly. 

Chairman Bomar appointed the following legislators as a subcommittee to conduct the study: Rep. T. J. Davis, Chairman; 

Rep. Walter I. Forrester, Secretary; Sen. Joe Spencer and Sen.

Cuyler Dunbar. 

The subcommittee held three open meetings in Nashville, at which times persons with knowledge of and interest in the laws and practices in this state dealing with sex offenders appeared and discussed the problem. Those who appeared and made statements or filed written statements were: Dr. C. J. Ruilmann, State 

Commissioner of Mental Health; Whitworth Stokes, President of 

Tennessee Mental Health Association; J. E. Hogan, State Department of Education; Attorney General George McCanless; Gen. 

Phil Canale, President, and Gen. Carlton Loser, Executive Vice 

President of the Tennessee Attorney General Association; and

Dr. O. S. Hauk, Director of Central State Hospital. 

Copies of the laws in other states on this subject were obtained for use in the study. These laws were studied closely with a view toward improving the law in Tennessee. 

The Legislative Council found that at present sex offenders are not segregated from other prisoners; no treatment for them is offered; in most instances, as soon as they have served the sentence imposed for whatever crime they have committed, they are released on the public in no better condition than when apprehended, and often to commit the same crime or one of like nature. 

Segregation and treatment of all such sex offenders would doubtless be desirable. However, the Legislative Council feels that until some system is established that could determine which offenders could be helped or cured by treatment, a special institution would not be justified because of the cost of such program. 

Many states are attacking the problem; none have successfully solved it.

Medical science holds little encouragement for the cure of sex deviates. The pattern of sexual behavior is usually established early in life during the teenage period. These patterns are hard to change once developed. Few sex deviates even change the manner of deviation. A homosexual usually will deviate only in that manner. The same is true of "peeping Toms," exhibitionists, 29

sodomists, etc. According to those who have made a study of the subject, there are methods of treating various types of sexual deviates; a good per cent of the very young deviates are completely curable, but few of the adults. We understand that there are two broad classifications of sex offenders. One is, by nature, a timid person, usually capable of doing no real harm to another. 

In this group comes adult homosexuals, "peeping Toms," exhibitionists, etc. The other type is aggressive and often does not confine his criminal activities to sex offenses. This type usually has contempt for the law and is usually more a criminal than a sexual psychopath. Few of this last type can be cured, and, in most instances, the penitentiary is a more appropriate place for confining them than in an institution for sexual psychopaths. 

Among this type are rapists, child molesters, etc.

When these abnormal sex habits are fully developed, the only sure protection for the public is confinement of the sex deviate. 

The Legislative Council believes that some sex deviates can be cured, others helped by treatment to the extent that they would not be a menace to society if released, and other are incapable of rehabilitation and would offer a serious threat to society if released. The Legislative Council believes that this last group should be retained in an institution and regarded as a specie of insane persons.

The Legislative Council believes that psychiatric examinations should be given all who have been convicted of sex offenses soon after they are admitted to confinement, and that those found treatable should be given treatment. We further believe that no person convicted of a sex crime should be paroled from prison unless it be certified by the Department of Mental Health that the person released would not endanger the public, and on extreme and dangerous cases, provision should be made for continued confinement after the expiration of the sentence on the grounds of insanity. If this were done, this state would be taking a forward step in meeting the problem. We further believe that there are other type criminals who could be benefited by psychiatric treatment, and these should also be examined with a view toward treating them. However, since the first of this type treatment would be more or less a pilot course, and since this study was expressly authorized for studying sex offenders, we believe that such program should be commenced with this type offender and the results weighed before adding other offenses to the program. 

Therefore, we recommend to the 80th General Assembly: 

1. That the Pardon and Parole Board should have no power to parole a person who was convicted of a sex crime until such time as the Mental Health Department certified that he could be released with safety to the public. 30

2. That all persons confined in the state penitentiary for sex crimes shall be examined after commitment to the penal institution by a psychiatrist or psychiatrists from the 

Mental Health Department. 

3. That treatment and segregation be required for all prisoners convicted of sex crimes where the Department of Mental Health believes that treatment may effect improvement in sex behavior. (This can be accomplished by executive order and interdepartmental cooperation.) 

4. That the Commissioner of Corrections be required to have persons sentenced for sex crimes examined by the Department of Mental Health not more than one year nor less than six months prior to the time they are to be released, and if it is the judgment of the representative from the Department of Mental Health that it would not be to the public interest to release such person, that such offender be retained on the grounds of insanity, with proper safeguards of his constitutional rights. 

5. Provide for segregation of pronounced sex deviates in the penitentiary at the earliest possible date. 

6. Appropriate funds not to exceed $100,000.00 to be allotted by the Governor to the various departments to provide for the examination, treatment and segregation of persons convicted of sex crimes. 

7. Provide for a further study of not only sex offenders, but of all convicted offenders where psychiatric treatment could reasonably be expected to aid, cure or rehabilitate, and also a study of means to apprehend, commit and treat sex offenders prior to the conviction for criminal sex acts. 31

SMALL LOAN AND INDORSEMENT BUSINESSES Pursuant to Senate Resolution No. 26 of the 79th General 

Assembly, the Legislative Council was directed to study small loan and indorsement businesses and report its findings and recommendations to the Eightieth General Assembly of the State of Tennessee.

People interested in the subject were invited to appear before the Council at its regular meeting in January, 1956. The following persons appeared either before the whole Council or its subcommittee and made or filed statements during the course of the study: Arch Northington, Commissioner of Insurance and Banking; L. Buchanan Loser, Attorney for the Department of Insurance; Homer Clark, Superintendent of Banking; Ira Whaley, for 

Consumer's Finance Association; W. R. Holt, for Tennessee 

Credit Union League; George F. McCanless, Attorney General; 

Hoyt Bryson and James Burns as attorneys for W. G. Seaton and 

Herbert S. Dykes; Robert P. Scruggs, Jr., for Tennessee Consumer Finance Association; Ben O. Duggan, Attorney for Beneficial Finance Company of Chattanooga; Richard Dance, W. G. 

Seaton, H. S. Durham, Albert Williams, Collett Mayfield, and Walter Wood.

Despite the fact that invitations were made through the newspapers for interested people to appear or send in information, all the above named persons, with the exception of Collett Mayfield and Walter Wood, were either state officials called in by the Council because of information they had on the subject or attorneys and agents for the small loan or indorsement busi- nesses.

After the January meeting, Chairman Bomar appointed the following as a subcommittee to continue the study: Sen. Carroll 

Oakes, Chairman; Sen. James Diamond, Sen. Henry Gupton, Rep. 

James H. Cummings, and Rep. Eugene Pennington.

It appeared throughout the study that people interested in a change were hesitant to come forward. In fact, those desiring change were noticeable by their absence. The small loan and indorsement companies were well represented at all the meetings, but appeared satisfied with matters as they now exist and prepared to resist change.

The subcommittee met three times in addition to the January meeting of the whole Council and on each occasion the meetings were open to the public. 32

After full review of all the information by the subcommittee. and review by the Council, we found the following to be the situation in Tennessee:

There are two small loan acts in Tennessee: (1) The Small 

Loan Act of 1925 as amended in 1937. This act originally provided for the lender to collect 6% interest on loans, plus 3% per month charges. The 1937 amendment lowered this 3% per month charge to 1% per month. As a result of this amendment, practically all agencies doing business under this Act withdrew, either because it would not allow a fair return on the money loaned, or for reasons not appearing to the subcommittee. Consequently, only seven or eight businesses are now licensed to operate under this Act, and only one of these is actually operating in the state under its provisions. (2) The second act is the 

Industrial Loan and Thrift Act of 1951. It appears that this 

Act allows a reasonable return for loans under its provisions, but these loans are in amounts up to $2,500.00, and are seldom made for small amounts like $25.00 to $150.00.

As a result of the 1937 amendment to the Small Loan Act, the indorsement and brokerage companies came into existence during the mid-1940's. Indorsement businesses are agencies that do not actually lend money but provide the security for a loan. from a bank or other lending agency, for a charge. Usually these are small loans ranging from $25.00 to $150.00 but not limited to these amounts. Supposedly, the beneficiaries of these loans are those who need small amounts and have no security to offer in order to get the loan from a regular lender. As a result, they pay the actual lender the lawful rate of interest and, in addition, pay the indorsement company a charge for securing the loan. 

These charges customarily run, together with the interest, about 13% per month on $25.00 or $50.00 loans. Some run higher than this, and some few lower. It was estimated by Mr. Loser that the companies who use a more or less standard charge will usually average about $4.99 return per account per month. Of this, the average cost would be $2.67 per account per month; the average loss $.38 per account per month, leaving a gross profit by way of interest and charges of $1.94 per account per month. 

The loan broker performs a similar service for the borrower by merely arranging the loan for a charge. His returns are comparable to those in the indorsement business. 

There are no laws in the state now to regulate these businesses at all. So long as they merely secure the loan or arrange for it, there is no limit, other than that which competition provides, on the amount that can be charged.

At the present time, the authority of administering the Small Loan Act of 1925 as amended and the Industrial Loan and Thrift 

Act of 1951 rests with the Department of Insurance and Banking. 33

This department is also charged by law with administering the insurance laws of the state. They have only three investigators. 

The insurance field work is very heavy and is entirely separate and apart from the small loans business. These investigators have little time to devote to investigating complaints regarding the operations of the small loan and indorsement business and have practically no training for such work. As stated earlier, there are no provisions for regulating the indorsement and brokerage businesses, unless they invade the small loan field. As a result of our study, we believe that the most apparent evils lie in these type businesses. We believe that almost everyone recognizes that the situation is not wholesome and that corrective measures should be taken.

Since there is no statute applicable to the indorsement and brokerage businesses, it is felt that an act should be passed regulating them. Considerable time was spent discussing the action taken by other states in this field and trying to reach a conclusion that would permit us to recommend a law that would establish rates of interest and other charges. The Council finally concluded that, although, (1) these small lenders have become a part of our economy and, from a practical standpoint, a necessary part, still (2) we do not have sufficient information. available to recommend a law establishing rates to be charged. 

Because of this lack of information, we could not at this time. justify any rates which we might recommend.

It is not our intention nor do we believe it is the intention of the General Assembly to tax or regulate anyone out of business, nor to favor one type of business over another. It is our desire, and has been from the outset, to find a solution that will permit the operation of such businesses as are needed to make loans to needy people provided they operate without taking undue advantage of the acute need of the borrower. Should we recommend low rates of interest, fees, and charges, and thereby cause this type business to seek other avenues, as was the case when the 1937 amendment was made to the 1925 Small Loan Act, then we would not have performed our true task. On the other hand, to set a rate near what the agencies are now charging would, in effect, be condoning the present rates, which, in some instances, we believe to be exorbitant. However, we feel that to longer delay any planned method of solving the problem would avail nothing, and that even if equitable rates cannot be determined at this time, some measure of control can be had. 

We feel that, in order to properly regulate these small loan and indorsement and brokerage companies, it will be necessary to create a new division that will be entirely devoted to these matters and not leave this field as the stepchild of an already overworked department. It is our further thinking that, because of its relation to banking and the similarity of the training 34

required for the personnel in banking and small loan and indorsement and brokerage companies, it would be preferable to place such a division under the Department of Banking rather than the Department of Insurance and Banking.

We believe that this new division should be known as the Division of Small Lending Agencies of the Department of Banking and that the administration of the Industrial Loan and Thrift Act of 1951 should be transferred to this division, so that the division would have supervision over all agencies of this type. The new division would, therefore, take jurisdiction of the companies operating under the Small Loan Act of 1925, as amended; the 

Industrial Loan and Thrift Act of 1951; and of all indorsement and loan brokerage companies. The Act to be drawn should be broad enough to cover any new companies performing similar services under a different name.

The establishment of the aforementioned division with limited control, plus its authority to investigate the small lending companies (especially the indorsement and brokerage companies), and to require certain records to be kept, etc., should within a short time, and certainly before the 1959 session of the Legislature, enable this division to have valid data on which equitable rates, fees and charges could be based. 

Another solution which has been presented to us would provide for the division but give the Director power to adjust the rates until they were equitable. We question the wisdom of the 

Legislature delegating this much power to a new division at the present time.

Another solution suggested was the levying of a gross profits tax on the companies so that the higher the margin of profit, the higher the tax rate. Some feel that this might be developed, with study, into a workable solution, but we are not in a position to make specific recommendations on this now.

All who appeared or filed statements seemed to be in agreement that there is a pressing need for statistical data and for a strengthening of the Workmen's Compensation Division of the 

Department of Labor. All seemed to agree that this Division is doing the best job possible with the limited budget available. 

Many of those who appeared or filed statements contended that there was a need for the establishment of a Workmen's Compensation Commission and that the present functions relating to our Workmen's Compensation Act carried on by the Divi sion of Workmen's Compensation, the Courts, the State Treas urer, the Department of Insurance and Banking, the Division of Workshop, Factory, and Elevator Inspection, and the Division of Vocational Rehabilitation should be unified in this organization. 

Under such a system, it was suggested that appeals to the court for a review of the decisions and awards of the Commission be taken directly to the Supreme Court, to achieve a quick determination of the case. It is the opinion of the Legislative Council that such a provision would be unconstitutional since it has been held by the Supreme Court that appeals from similar bodies must be tried before a lower court because the Supreme Court has only appellate jurisdiction and cannot try a case except by the 37

record made in the lower court. Thus, if a Commission were created, a contested case would, if taken to the Supreme Court, require three hearings before being finally decided the original hearing before the Commission; a review by certiorari in the 

Chancery Court; and a final appeal in the Supreme Court. Under our present system, only two hearings are had the original hearing before the Chancellor, Circuit, or County Judge; and the appeal in the Supreme Court. So, if a Commission were created, instead of shortening the period between the filing of a claim and a final decision, that period would be lengthened by providing for one additional hearing.

Those favoring the change to a Commission form pointed out the fact that the state has a 2% tax on premiums collected by insurance companies in general, and a 4% tax on those writing workmen's compensation. It seems that this additional 2% on workmen's compensation premiums was originally intended to provide funds for the administration of the Act. This additional 

2% tax brought the State of Tennessee $324,831.00 during the year 1954 and, during that year, the expenditure of the Division of Workmen's Compensation amounted to only $32,271.06. Therefore, it was insisted that the Division is entitled to such money as is needed for its proper operation under the original intention of the Act.

Those opposed to a change in the present method of administering the Workmen's Compensation Act contended that there was no fault in the system itself, but the trouble was and is in the failure on the part of the state to make adequate appropriations for the Division of Workmen's Compensation and that the Workmen's Compensation Act could be administered to the satisfaction of all if this division were given an adequate appropriation.

No estimates were made as to the exact cost of operating a Workmen's Compensation Commission; however, figures were submitted showing appropriations in the following states that have commissions: Georgia-$194,000.00; Indiana―$131,131.00; 

Kentucky-$229,000.00; Oklahoma-$118,940.00; and Wisconsin $216,977.00.

Your Council has examined all statements filed, in an attempt to make recommendations that will be of benefit to both employer and employee and has come to the conclusion that there is nothing wrong with our present court-administered system of workmen's compensation that cannot be quickly remedied by an adequate appropriation. We are of the opinion that a change to the commission system would call for an annual appropriation of from $150,000.00 to $200,000.00, and we cannot, in our own minds, justify such an expenditure or such a radical departure from our present system, even though 43 of our sister states have followed this course. 38

We do believe, however, that an increase in the appropriation for the Division of Workmen's Compensation is fully justified. 

We have been advised by the Department of Labor that an increase of approximately $50,000.00 annually would enable them to add eleven employees to this Division and purchase certain equipment needed by the Division, and that with this additional personnel and equipment the Division of Workmen's Compensation would be able to revise their filing system and office procedures, accumulate needed statistical data, offer better service to the employers and employees of the state, carry on an educational program, and thoroughly check on all employers to insure compliance with the law and thereby increase state revenue, while, at the same time, improving administration. 

We believe that, before completely revising a system of administration in the field of workmen's compensation that is engrained in our governmental structure by almost 40 years of usage, we should first exert every effort to make that system meet our needs. We are of the opinion that the Division of 

Workmen's Compensation has never had a sufficient appropriation to do its work adequately. 

We recommend that the appropriation of the Workmen's Compensation Division of the Department of Labor be increased $50,000.00 annually, thus making a total annual appropriation of $82,271.06 for this Division. (The cost of this improved service would still represent a savings of from $70,000.00 to $120,- 

000.00 annually, over the estimated cost of a Commission form; and in the opinion of the Council accomplish the same results.) 

We suggest that both employers and employees lend their full support to the Division for the next two to four years; helping it to collect statistical data; carry on an educational program in the factory and workshop; and improve its services to management and labor alike. If, after such a period of time, any segment of those most vitally affected by the administration of this Act are dissatisfied with its operation and administration, we recommend that another study by the Legislative Council be made with particular emphasis on the desirability of changing our form of administration to a commission form. 89

From the study thus conducted, the Legislative Council found that: 

1. The state agency responsible for hotel and restaurant inspections is the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspection headed by E. W. Adcock, and that this division is within the Department of Conservation. 

2. Many counties and cities have local ordinances which require inspection of hotels and restaurants, principally from the standpoint of health and sanitation. These local inspections are in addition to and have no relation to state inspections which look to the condition of buildings, facilities, equipment and many other things, as well as standards of health and sanitation.

After considering the matter of inspection, the Legislative Council came to the conclusion that the state should continue its system of inspections, and that local units of government should not be excluded from the field, so long as their inspections do not in any way weaken those of the state, are independent of the state, and meet the needs of the local community. 

We feel that, although there is some overlapping of services, the local inspections serve a good purpose.

We therefore recommend that no action be taken to abridge the right of local units of government to exercise their authority 48

to make inspections, but we suggest that our Tennessee Department of Public Health call to the attention of local departments of public health the fact that they are actually representatives of local governmental units and urge them to make known this fact when they inspect hotels and restaurants within their jurisdiction.

Another question which arose and was considered was whether the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspections should be under the Department of Conservation or some other department. 

The Legislative Council feels that if it were recommending an entirely new system of hotel and restaurant inspections at the state level, it could well justify placing this responsibility in any one of three state departments; namely, Health, Agriculture or 

Conservation. We are of the opinion that the vast majority of 

Tennesseans look upon the present system of grading as one designed to denote the degree of cleanliness that may exist in such places of business, and for that reason we would be inclined to recommend that the Department of Public Health be charged with this responsibility, since this department has the trained technicians needed for a health service of this type. Logical and well supported reasons might also justify placing such a responsibility in the Department of Agriculture, since they have trained field men who examine and grade much of the food coming into eating establishments.

We are, however, faced with the reality that we already have a system of state inspections; that this system has some 186 check points, ranging from the manner in which dishes and glassware are washed to the conditions of plumbing connections, toilet facilities, storage rooms, etc.; that an effort is made not only to improve health standards for the protection of all our people, but to provide attractive and adequate facilities that will cause tourists who pass through our state to trade with our hotel and restaurant operators. There is no indication that any substantial segment of the hotel and restaurant industry is dissatisfied with the manner in which inspections are being conducted, nor is there any indication, save one which will be noted later, that the interests of the public or the hotel and restaurant industry are not being adequately protected under the present plan of inspection. Because of the facts listed above and the additional fact that representatives of the hotel and restaurant industry stated to the subcommittee that their relations at the present time with the inspecting authority, the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspections under the Department of Conservation, are good; that the law is being fairly enforced; that the division is self-supporting under the present system of fees; and that any change in administration would be a step backward for both the industry and the state; plus the further fact that the 

Commissioner of Public Health has stated that in his opinion a simple transfer of the division to any other department of the government would not improve the service but would in fact hurt 49

it, we say that the facts presented by authorities concerned indicate that no legislation is presently needed in this field and that the matter of inspections should be continued without change. 

We would like to urge a continuation of the present program of close cooperation in this field between the Department of Public Health and the Department of Conservation, and we suggest that the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspection might strive to improve its working relations with local health departments..

One matter has come to our attention that we feel should be the subject of consideration by the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspection. We have found that the vast majority of the citizens with whom we have talked look upon our present system of grading restaurants as one designed primarily to protect the health of those persons who avail themselves of the opportunity to eat in the restaurants and cafes of our state. While it is true that those restaurants receiving an "A" grade have met the high health standards established by the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspection, yet we find that it is possible for a restaurant to attain such a score on that portion of the inspection relating to health and sanitation as would entitle it to an "A" rating and yet be downgraded to "B", "C" or "D" because of the fact that they are unable to provide those facilities that make for physical comfort or for visual attractiveness. 

It seems to us that when an operator meets those standards of sanitation and cleanliness that will entitle him to a grade of "A", he is entitled to have the public so informed, although the grade he holds, based on the entire check list provided by the division, is below the grade of "A". We do not believe that legislation is necessary to effect this change, but we do urge that in those instances where an operator meets the conditions relating to health and sanitation that would entitle him to receive an “A” grade but does not qualify therefor because of other deficiencies, the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspection should indicate, in an appropriate place on their usual rating card, that the restaurant has met the requirements for a grade of “A” from the standpoint of health and sanitation. 66 The hotel and restaurant associations and the director of the Division of Hotel and Restaurant Inspection made certain recommendations concerning the need for amending existing laws and enacting new laws for the protection of the patrons and operators of the hotels and restaurants of our state. The Council wishes to acknowledge with thanks the cooperation of all these persons and the genuine interest that prompted them to make their recommendations. However, since these recommendations were in the main directed toward matters not directly related to the scope of this study, we have decided not to recommend specific action but we do suggest that members of the 80th General Assembly give their serious consideration to all recommendations having the united support of these three groups. 50

Mr. Morgan filed his "Conclusions and Recommendations" growing out of the study for the consideration of the Council; the conclusions and recommendations as filed by Mr. Morgan are 

The field study findings do not bear out the contention that property taxes in Tennessee are, on the average, unduly burdensome. The "effective" tax rate in the median Tennessee county is less than 67c per $100 of actual value (which would mean a county tax of less than $67.00 on a home or farm worth $10,000). When it is recalled that this $67.00 payment includes school tax as well as county tax properand when comparison is made with property taxes elsewhere -it is evident that the "average" Tennessean is not being "taxed to death." 

However moderate the property tax paid by the "average"

Tennessean, the study proves that large numbers of Tennessee taxpayers are overtaxed. The inequalities in assessment are so numerous and so extreme as to guarantee that outcome. 

The uppermost bars on the charts on pages 47-99 give some notion of the relative number of overtaxed taxpayers in each county and city included in the equalization studies. 55

The purpose here is to suggest specific changes in statutes and in administrative organization and procedures designed to secure greater equality of treatment for all taxpayers. 

Equalization of assessments is a two-level operation. The first-and simpler-aspect is that of equalizing between counties. This portion of the equalization job must be accomplished by the State itself-at the State level. Let us deal with it first.

Inter-County Equalization 

Average assessment levels in the 21 counties studied varied from 11 per cent of actual value to 49 per cent of actual value. In no county did the average level even approach the 

100 per cent standard established by law. Inter-county equalization is important in only one respect at present-the allocation of public utility valuations among counties. But since the present distribution formulas are based upon a proven fiction that all property in all counties is assessed at "actual cash value"-some positive action to insure greater equity between counties is needed. I suggest the following as a means of solving this complex problem: 

1. That the "actual cash value" standard be repealed; that instead, county courts and city councils be authorized to establish any uniform percentage (up to 100 per cent) of current market value as the basis for assessment. 

2. That an administrative branch of the State Equalization Board be established, which shall have as one of its duties the determination of the average assessment level in each county each year. 

3. That the State Equalization Board and the Public 

Service Commission be authorized and directed to use the above state-determined ratios as factors (along with the other factors presently used) in the formulas by which public utility valuations are allocated among the counties served by each public utility. 

4. That the composition of the State Equalization Board be changed by adding two members representing local levels of government. (Two to be named from lists submitted by the Municipal League and County Services Organization.)

Intra-County Equalization 

Equalization of assessments within the county or city is a great deal more difficult to accomplish than the equalization between counties discussed above. The range of ratios in any county or city studied is much greater than the range of aver- 56

ages among counties studied. For example, in County "M" (p. 70 of the study) the range of ratios is from 1% of sale price to 1000% of sale price, while the range of county averages is from 11% in the lowest county studied to 49% in the highest county studied. 

Intra-county equalization must be solved at the local level. 

Whether it is likely to be solved entirely with local resources is a highly debatable question. The 1947 General Assembly granted counties (and cities) authority to contract with and pay outside firms for reappraisal or reassessment work. But in the succeeding nine years, only three counties and one or two cities have used that authority. I suggest that technical assistance and general supervision of local reassessment or reappraisal programs be made available by the State to those counties (and cities) requesting such assistance. The administrative branch of the State Equalization Board, mentioned previously, would be the logical agency to provide this service. 

There is a strong belief that local units would use State assistance more readily than they use the services of commercial appraisal firms-even though the saving in money under a cooperative state-local arrangement might not be too significant.

Almost everything done by local assessing personnel is related one way or another to intra-county equalization. The intra-county equalization problem will therefore be treated from this point on under the appropriate sub-headings. 

Record Systems 

The minimum record system which county assessors are presently required to maintain is wholly inadequate. To remedy this weakness I recommend: 

1. That the State Equalization Board be authorized to prescribe standard forms for use by all county assessors and county equalization boards. 

2. That the valuations of land and improvements be required to be shown separately on all forms used either by assessors or trustees. 

3. That as early as practicable every county be required to develop a set of tax maps and an indexing system to supersede the present "bounds" system of real property identification. The indexing system should not be based in any way on civil district boundaries but should be related to official county maps. 

4. That the State Equalization Board, through its administrative branch, be authorized and directed to work with such counties and cities as may request its assistance in developing tax maps, property inventory card 57

systems and the other adjuncts of a modern assessment record system. 

5. That county registers be required to report all deeds, deeds of trust and real estate mortgages to the State Equalization Board, on forms prescribed by the State Board, with a copy to be furnished to the county assessor. 

6. That all deeds be required to list the full consideration. 

7. That all deeds be required to be recorded within six months of the date of execution.

Part-Time Assessors 

The notion that assessment is a part-time job-and a political sinecure-is a hangover from the 19th Century. All present evidence points to the fact that the day of the parttime assessor is long past, that the property tax burden of the mid-20th Century cannot be satisfactorily allocated by an untrained, part-time operator. In the average Tennessee 

County there is a sufficient volume of work to justify a fulltime assessor and one or more full-time deputies, if the work is done properly. In many cases the obvious trouble is that the counties are not spending enough money on the assessment function. The assessor has been virtually starved to death-in salary, in needed equipment and help, and in public esteem. In those cases where the volume of work is sufficient to justify a separate assessment office at all-and this includes the majority of Tennessee counties-the first requisites are better salaries and more adequate help and equipment.

In some of the very small counties it might be desirable to combine the duties of assessor with those of the trustee. 

This arrangement has been in operation for many years in Texas and in some other states so it is not an untried arrangement. It might be necessary to rearrange the work schedules somewhat but that is no major task. After all, if there isn't enough work for one full-time assessor, there probably is not a full-time job for the tax collector. It is certainly more businesslike to have one office profitably busy the year round rather than to have two half-busy offices. Personnel The survey indicates grave weaknesses in the selection, compensation and training of assessing personnel. To remedy some of these weaknesses, I recommend: 

1. That assessors be nominated by the county judges and appointed by the county governing bodies instead of being elected by the people. 58

2. That county governing bodies be given authority to fix salaries and travel allowances of assessors and deputies. 

3. That the State Equalization Board be charged with the development and administration of training courses designed to help assessors and deputies improve their performance. 

4. That the State Equalization Board be directed to develop and administer qualifying examinations and a certification procedure for persons seeking to qualify as county assessors, provided that the 1957 incumbents shall be deemed to be qualified without examination. 

Merchants Ad Valorem 

I recommend that property subject to Merchants' Ad 

Valorem tax be assessed by county assessors and the tax thereon be collected by county trustees at the same time, in the same manner, and at the same assessment level as is used for the assessment and collection of real and personal property taxes, and that the same review and appeal procedures apply to all three classes of property. 

Municipal Assessors

I recommend general legislation to authorize any municipality by ordinance, to establish an independent assessment system for municipal tax purposes, without regard to the assessment used by the county; provided that the municipality shall at the same time establish a city equalization board, and provided further that municipal assessors and taxpayers shall have the same right of appeal to the State Equalization Board that county assessors and county taxpayers have.

Small Counties 

In recognition of the fact that some counties are too small to form an effective or economical assessment district, I recommend: (a) That two or more counties be permitted, by resolution of the county courts involved, to form inter-county assessment districts of sufficient size to justify the retention of full-time staffs, if they so desire. (b) That counties below a designated minimum population be permitted, by resolution of the county court, to transfer the duties of the county tax assessor to the county trustee (or county register). 59

Personal Property 

The property tax has virtually become a tax upon real property only in Tennessee. This unfortunate and unfair situation arises principally from an ambiguous provision in the Tennessee Constitution which requires the exemption of $1,000 worth of personal property in the hands of each taxpayer. I recommend that the subcommittee consider proposing the following amendment to the Tennessee Constitution:

Delete from Article II, Section 28, the following: "One thousand dollars worth of personal property in the hands of each taxpayer" and insert instead the following: "household and kitchen furniture, tableware, musical instruments, wearing apparel, jewelry and other personal property of similar character used in the taxpayer's own household." 

Insert after the word "privileges" the following: "and intangible personal property."

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL 

The Council, as a result of its public hearings and the splendid research carried out under the direction of Mr. Morgan, has concluded that many changes and reforms are needed in the field of tax assessment at the local level. The undesirable situation revealed by our study of local tax assessments has not come about in the last year or in the last five years, however, but is the result of many years of drifting in the wrong direction. 

We have carefully weighed and thoroughly discussed every recommendation made by those who appeared before our group, and have given special attention to those recommendations made by our study director, Mr. Morgan. We must say that there is merit in every suggestion made to us and that time and need may justify the enactment of each suggestion. We are of the opinion, however, that any change in a governmental function as vital as the field of property tax assessment, some phases of which have been in existence for over 100 years, should be approached with caution and by degrees, so that the orderly procedure of government will continue with the least possible interruption and so as to avoid any violent change in wellestablished customs.

We believe that the people of Tennessee should have full opportunity to study and analyze the factual report entitled, 

"Property Assessment Administration in Tennessee-1955-56", made to the County Tax Assessment Subcommittee of this Council by Cecil Morgan, before any drastic changes are made in our tax assessment structure. (To achieve this end, we have had enough copies prepared to furnish one to each member of the 60

Legislature and such other public officials and citizens as may have an interest in the matter.) We have included in this report a list of all recommendations made by Mr. Morgan as a result of his study. We believe that time will see many of these recommendations enacted.

One of the greatest weaknesses of our present system of county tax assessments is the failure to have some central agency to which the counties can turn for assistance when problems regarding assessments arise. The only governmental unit capable of furnishing such service is the State of Tennessee. Such a service might be furnished through the Comptroller's office, the 

Department of Finance and Taxation or the State Equalization 

Board. Since the State Equalization Board concerns itself wholly with tax assessments we believe it is the proper place to locate a service. We recommend that an administrative agency be created under the State Equalization Board to give assistance to local units of government upon request. The services of this agency should be gradually expanded so as to provide real and personal property tax assessment manuals, assist in making county wide reappraisals, hold training schools, assist in the preparation of tax maps, interpret assessment laws, and many other functions that will aid local tax assessors in achieving more thorough and equitable assessments.

We believe that this agency should conduct, at least biannually, such studies as are necessary to determine the average assessment in each county of the state so that a determination may be made from time to time regarding progress being made by the counties toward inter-county equalization. 

For a number of years now the state has not levied any tax on locally assessed property, yet the State Equalization Board is composed entirely of state officials who hold their position because of the office to which they are elected or appointed, so that their duty as members of the State Board of Equalization is exofficio. We believe that local units should be represented on this board since these are the units of government most vitally affected by any action of the State Board of Equalization. 

We recommend that the membership of the State Board of Equalization be increased by two, one member to represent the cities of the state and one to represent the counties. We recommend that these members be appointed by the Governor for terms of four years each and be qualified by experience in matters relating to local tax assessment.

Should the 80th General Assembly accept the recommendations of this Council, we believe that such acceptance will be a great stride forward along the road that must be traveled by our state to achieve the better system of assessments at the local level that we believe the vast majority of our citizens desire. 61

We believe that within two to four years the good results of these recommendations will become evident and that the needs for further revision, if any, will develop into a clearer picture. 

As the report prepared by Mr. Morgan is studied by our citizens and as factual data is developed and analyzed by the administrative agency under the State Board of Equalization, it is entirely possible that future legislatures will, within the next 10 years, see the need for adopting other measures to aid assessments at the local level. It may well be that the people of our state will find that they cannot achieve the degree of equity in assessments that they so earnestly desire within the framework of our present Constitution and may see fit to amend it as to the assessment of real and personal property. 

We feel that the enactment of the recommendations listed above will go far toward correcting the ills of our present local tax assessment problems and that the people of this state and their representatives in the Legislature can be trusted to achieve such future reforms as common sense and good judgment and the desire for equity may dictate. 62

NEED FOR ADJUSTMENT 

It seems apparent to your Council that the Legislature which enacted the original bills authorizing the welfare programs must have given due consideration to the question: "What is an equitable division of the cost of these programs?" It seems natural to us, following this line of reasoning, that some adjustment should be made in the allocation of these costs when, at the end of almost 20 years, it develops that the income of the State of Tennessee is now approximately five times greater than at the time the programs began, while the income of the counties has tripled during the same period.

EFFECT OF RECOMMENDED CHANGES 

Let us now look at the effect these recommended changes will have on our county governments and our state government. 

Counties: 

It might seem at first glance that relief for the counties is long overdue, however, relief has been given the counties on two occasions since the original welfare bills were enacted in 1937. In the year 1939 a portion of the Alcoholic Beverage Tax was returned to the counties for the purpose of paying the contribution required to be made by the counties to the Old-Age Security Fund. 

This purpose is clearly set forth in Paragraph A, Section 57-135, Tennessee Code Annotated. Again in 1947, by public chapters

REVIEW OF APPLICATIONS AT THE LOCAL LEVEL 

We have reviewed rather carefully the contentions of those who recommend discontinuing review of all grants by the county judges of our state.

We are fully cognizant of the fact that this duty on the part of the county judges submits them to some political pressure. 

We are also well aware that this power of review could be used as a political instrument. We have considered the suggestions that the duty might better be performed by a committee of local persons who hold no political office. However, after considering all these matters, it is our conclusion that the entire program profits greatly by this review of all grants at the local level by the judges.

There is no substitute for the intimate knowledge that local officials have of the background, family connections, personality and needs of our people. Many times the case worker who visits an applicant may be from another county and although she may, and usually does, reach a correct decision based on good rules of social work, it is entirely possible that some collateral matter, known only to the local person who reviews the grant, would be most helpful in reaching a final decision. After thorough deliberation we have decided that the review of applications at the local level is a governmental function; that by and large the county judge is the person best qualified to do this for his county. 

We therefore recommend no change in this part of our law. Our decision in this matter is not based on the contribution of the county but on the fact that we feel the local government has a responsibility and that the county judge is the proper person to execute it.

Civil Service Appointments and Tenure Let us now look to appointments and tenure in the classified service under Chapter 32, Title 8 of the Tennessee Code Anno tated. This Chapter provides that incumbents in newly classified positions may be given a noncompetitive test within one year after establishment of said position. If this test is passed, the employee shall be certified as a regular employee. If the employee fails to meet the standards, he shall be dismissed from his position within sixty days after said certification of unfitness, or within sixty days after the establishment of a list of eligibles.

To prohibit, in exceptional cases, the dismissal of an employee who has rendered satisfactory service, the appointing authority certifies said employee should be retained, in which case he is retained without acquiring the status of a regular employee. If, however, he is found to be qualified for a different position of no higher rank, and he is so employed in said division, he shall be deemed to be a regular employee. 75

This provision eliminates those employees who are totally unfit, but at the same time makes allowance for a certain small number of employees who might not be able to pass a written test, but due to other qualifications, such as length of service and experience, should be retained in an unclassified category. 

The Act provides for promotion and entrance examinations with factors for adjudication of eligibility being enumerated fully and adequately, and also provides a manner for giving notice of these examinations.

The Director of Personnel is charged with the duty of establishing the qualifications for admission to any test, and also has the authority to strike the name or reject the application of any person who is unfit due to lack of qualifications or good moral character.

The Act takes into consideration the earned consideration of veterans because of their government service, and allows a five point preference on their earned rating if not disabled, and a ten point preference in the event they are disabled. The spouse or widow of a disabled veteran is also granted consideration and given a ten point preference on her earned rating, provided she has not remarried.

An appointing officer who passes over an eligible veteran and selects a non-veteran shall file with the director the reasons for doing so, which reasons will become a part of the veteran's record. 

In addition to these considerations, when reductions are being made in any part of the classified service, a person entitled to military preference in appointment shall be the last to be discharged or dropped or reduced in rank or salary, if his record is good, or if his efficiency rating is equal to that of any employee in competition with him who is retained in the service of his department.

The Act provides that the rating of each test shall be completed within four months, and that each person competing shall be given written notice of his final earned rating, his relative standing on the list, or of his failure to attain a place on the list.

The Act also provides for the establishment and maintenance of promotion and employment lists and establishes what we think is a fair limitation of time in which said list shall remain in force, being not less than six months, nor more than two years. 

Whenever an appointing authority proposes to fill a vacancy in the classified service, he shall submit to the director a statement showing the positions to be filled, the duties thereof, the official station and the necessary and desirable qualifications of the person to be appointed thereto, and shall request the director to certify the names of persons eligible for appointment to 76

such position. The director shall thereupon certify to the appointing authority the names of three eligibles for such position and the appointing authority is required to appoint within ten days one of those whose names are certified to fill the vacancy. 

The appointing authority, when filling more than one vacancy, shall fill each vacancy from among the highest three names remaining on the list, in accordance with the above provision. 

We make special mention of this provision due to the fact that it has been suggested that in the first instance it should be made a specific provision of the Act that the director be required to submit the three highest eligibles.

The wording of the Act is certainly indicative of the fact that the first three names as certified must be the three highest as appears on said list. It would be inconceivable to think that the next three highest eligibles could be submitted, if in the first instance the three highest had not been submitted. 

The Act further provides for preference in filling an appointment by reinstatement, when the former employee resigned in good standing, and within certain time limits. 

The Act provides for a probationary period of employment on the part of every person appointed to a position in the classified service, which probationary employment shall continue for a period of not less than six months.

The appointing authority must give the employee at least one months trial before removing him, which provision gives the employee an ample opportunity to acquaint himself with the particular work, and also protects the standards of state employees by permitting his discharge at any time after the first month in the event his habits, work and dependability are uncertain.

The Act appears, in all respects, to be adequate and sufficient in its relation to dismissal of an employee, with proper leeway being granted the appointing authority to conduct his personnel problems in a manner equal, in most respects, to private enterprise, and yet affording the employee opportunities and privileges not afforded those employees in private enterprise.

construed, and have been construed as meaning "good of the service" and not "good of the political organization."

After such dismissal, the employee is afforded two remedies: (1) In the event the employee concurs in the reason for dismissal or recognizes the reasons for such action, and, if the director determines the reasons for dismissal given by the appointing authority show that such dismissal does not reflect discredit on the employee dismissed, the name of such employee shall, if he so requests, be placed on the appropriate re-employment list or lists. (2) The employee shall also have an opportunity to file with the appointing authority a written statement regarding the proposed dismissal, a copy of which shall be filed with the director, and also perfect his appeal.

We now take up the last portion of the Act. This section acts as a bulwark against arbitrary and discriminate abuse of administrative power and authority in the administration of state personnel in the classified service. 

This section is also considerate of the wisdom of having administrative authority vested in a department head, which department head is looked to by a higher authority for the carrying out of certain mandatory and obligatory duties. 

Any regular employee who is dismissed or demoted may appeal to the Commission within fifteen days after such action is taken. Upon such appeal, both the appealing employee and the appointing authority whose action is reviewed shall have the right to be heard and to present evidence with technical rules of evidence not applying. We feel that the fifteen days allowed the employee to perfect his appeal, plus the ten days notice prior to such dismissal action, or a total of twenty-five days notice is ample for any employee to gather the necessary data, information or witnesses to be in a position to fairly present his case to the Commission. It is also to be noted that technical rules of evidence are dispensed with, which would afford the employee an opportunity without the benefits of expensive counsel to get all facts before the Commission. 

If the Commission finds that the action complained of was taken by the appointing authority for any political or religious reason, the employee shall be reinstated without loss of pay. 

The Legislative Council feels that this provision of the Act is self-explanatory, ample, far reaching and zealous in its protection of any state employee from the abuse of arbitrary or malicious firing and dismissal for political or religious purposes. In the event the Commission finds the employee was dismissed because of political or religious reasons, the Act is explicit in that the employee shall be reinstated. If it is necessary for the employee to resort to the courts for proper redress, the court can look 80

behind any reason which might have been given by the appointing authority, and if in the sound discretion of the court it is found that said reason is an artifice or subterfuge, or fails to designate or denote the true intent of the appointing authority, and that said true intent is political or religious in nature, the true intent will prevail for all practical or legal purposes, and the court will so order the reinstatement of said employee and any loss of pay actually sustained by said employee. (A recent ruling of the Chancery Court for Davidson County, Tennessee, bears out the feeling of the Council on this question and affords us with a precedent upon which we base our reasoning.) 

In all other cases, the findings and recommendations of the Commission shall be submitted to and considered by the appointing authority who shall make the final decision disposing of the appeal, which decision shall not be reviewable by any court. 

In those cases where the appointing authority is over-ruled by the Commission, after appeal by the employee because of facts presented, possibly unknown to the appointing authority at the time of dismissal, and the appointing authority concurs in the ruling of the Commission, the employee is reinstated without loss of wages or salary. When any regular employee is dismissed and not reinstated after appeal, the Commission may direct that his name be placed on an appropriate re-employment list, which direction shall be enforced by the director.

There is the belief in some quarters that Code Section 4-407 of the new Tennessee Code Annotated negates the whole idea of civil service.

This Code Section stems from the Reorganization Act of 1937 as amended by the Public Acts for 1939, Chapter 11, Section 

4. The Section reads as follows: 

4-407-Discharge of Employees-Compensation 

The Governor may strike from the state payroll any state employee appointed by him or his appointee, and may fix, increase or decrease the salary of such employee, provided, however, that the salaries of the commissioner and other officers, as set out in Section 8-2301, shall be fixed as therein provided. 

In no case, however, shall any compensation for personal services be fixed in excess of the amount of money appropriated by the General Assembly for personal services in any department or agency of the state government. 81

We have reviewed this code section in the light of Article 2, Section 3; Article 3, Sections 2 and 14; Article 6, Sections 3 and 5; Article 7, Sections 1 and 4; and Article 8, Section 2 of the 

Constitution of Tennessee for the purpose of enlightening ourselves as to the power of appointment and other powers of either the executive, judicial or legislative branch of government. The case of Richardson vs. Young, 122 Tennessee Reports, Page 483, is the leading Tennessee case on this subject. Therefore, we will set out herein the text of this case, it being contended therein that the power of appointment to office is a political power and may be conferred upon either of the departments without violating any constitutional prohibition. 

The Supreme Court said: 

"The Constitution does not define in express terms what are legislative, executive or judicial powers. Theoretically, the legislative power is the authority to make, order, and repeal, the executive, that to administer and enforce, and the judicial, that to interpret and apply, law. "In the writings of Montesquieu and other political scientists, much consulted when the first American constitutions were framed and adopted, it is said, in substance, that the absolute separation of the legislative, executive and judicial departments was essential to a republican form of government and necessary for the perpetuation and maintenance of the political liberties of the people. But the framers of the Federal Constitution, and those of a great majority, if not all of the states, including Tennessee, notwithstanding the objections of some of the ablest statesmen of that day, departed from this theoretical division of the powers of government, and in many important matters vested in each of them powers and authority that in strictness would belong exclusively to the others; and in some instances all departments are vested with the same power, to be exercised concerning different matters, and this is especially noticeable in the vestiture of political powers."

The Court says further in this case, in discussing the Constitution of 1796, the Constitution of 1834 and the Constitution of 1870: "The provisions of the former constitutions, in this respect, were substantially the same, the only material difference being that under them the legislature was vested with much more power of appointment. The Legislature, under the broad powers given it in the first constitution, and those vested in it by Article 7, Section 4 of the last two constitutions, has always exercised the power of appointment to office created by statute, or conferred it upon the people, the courts, the governor and others, as it deemed best. The governor 82

has accepted and exercised the power of appointment when conferred upon him absolutely or jointly with the senate. 

There is not only an utter absence in the constitution and the constitutional and legislative history of this state of anything that indicates that the power of appointment was considered an executive function, or that it was intended, except where expressly stated, to be vested in the governor. On the contrary, there is much to show that the framers of our organic law did not so treat it or intend to so vest it. We have no difficulty in coming to the conclusion that this power under the constitution of this state is not an executive function, inherently in the executive department when not otherwise expressly vested, but a political power which consistently, with the distribution of powers of government, may properly be vested in either the legislative, executive or judicial departments by the General Assembly. The power being, as we have held, political and not executive, it may be conferred upon either of the departments without violating the constitutional prohibition relied upon."

We have cited the above case due to the fact that we think it clarifies to some extent the power and authority of all three branches of government, and particularly the question at hand. 

It appears, from our reasoning, that the Legislature has all power of appointment not specifically set out in the Constitution. 

It further appears that the legislative bodies in the past have deemed it wise in certain instances to exercise their prerogative by delegating certain powers to the executive branch of government, with the power of appointment of certain officers and civil employees being one of these powers so delegated. 

In passing the Civil Service Act of 1939, the Legislature again exercised its prerogative by limiting the power of the chief executive as to the appointment and dismissal of certain employees by those serving under him. The Legislature saw fit, however, to expressly prohibit any provision in the Civil Service Act of 1939 from having the effect of repealing Code Section 4-407 as heretofore set out in full. 

We take the position that past legislative bodies have acted judiciously and wisely, and legislators, being representative of the people, have carried out the dictates of their constituents by delegating the authority and power of the Legislature to such other branches of government and in such manner as is manifestly to the best interest of the State as a whole. 

The Council has heretofore in this report, at various times, stated that we felt the legal provisions of the Civil Service Act to be sufficiently broad in scope to protect the employees from unwarranted dismissal, political favoritism or unjust and unfair working conditions. At the same time, it affords the employee 83

an opportunity for continued advancement in classification and compensation, and a basis of solid security for the building of a lifetime career in state service.

The Council has also had to study the Act through the eyes of the employer, which, of course, is the State of Tennessee and the taxpaying citizens therein. We must necessarily conclude that the public has an important interest in any law affecting employees who are compensated out of public funds. 

We can think of no greater evil that could come to any form of government than to set the employees of that government upon a pedestal so high that they could not hear the voice of the people who are paying their salaries and, therefore, requesting of them certain services in return.

Present day government is an intricate and multifarious business operation, and it is beyond the reasoning of this Council to think that a final and absolute authority should not be vested in some one person to determine whether or not an employee or prospective employee should be employed or dismissed. 

As a result of the study we have made of the civil service laws, the oral testimony submitted at open hearings and the written statements filed by the Department of Personnel, the Department of the Budget and the League of Women Voters, we respectfully submit the following recommendations:

That the Legislature give serious consideration to any request made by the Governor for an increase in the budget of the Department of Personnel and the Department of the Budget. 

The approval of a slightly larger budget would allow these two departments to employ two or three additional competent and trained personnel.

The Department of the Budget could use additional competent employees to become entirely familiar with the work program of each department of state government, and the personnel needs therein. We feel that this knowledge on the part of the Department of the Budget would save money through the elimination of unnecessary positions and would at the same time enable the Budget office to better determine the need for adjustments in salary scales. Such a program would afford the state the opportunity of keeping pace with private industry in recruiting personnel.

The Department of Personnel could use additional competent employees for the following purposes: (a) To more ably and accurately prepare a classification plan for positions in state service. (b) To more fully study the duties of a position before it is allocated to the various classes. 84

(c) To more fully and accurately determine the title of any new position and apply a salary range commensurate with the duties of that position, and (d) To revise or abolish any of the above classification allocations which have become outdated because of changes in the economy.

The above will permit the Department of Personnel to at all times know exactly whether or not any employee is erroneously classified, allocated or receiving compensation unjustly. 

We next recommend that at such time as sufficient funds are available for the additional competent personnel, as outlined above, that an outside agency be employed by the State to examine the work positions of all employees and set up a table of organization for each and every department and office. 

The Department of Personnel and the Department of the Budget could both use this table of organization as a nucleus for starting a true and efficient method of handling work programs as submitted by the various departments. 

After the above recommendations are put into actual practice, with the sufficient personnel in the Department of the Budget and the Department of Personnel to administer their duties toward each state employee in the classified and unclassified service and in conformity with the table of organization as established by the outside agency, we feel that a program should be instituted to quickly extend the provisions of the Civil Service Act to employee not presently covered. 

Our last recommendation is for the purpose of recognizing faithful service rendered by public servants. We recommend that the Department of Personnel establish a system within their classification and compensation plans whereby an increase in pay can be accorded certain employees who have reached the peak salary in their position and who have worked for the State over a given number of years. In other words, we recommend a system of advancement in pay, in grade, based on longevity. 85

The Legislative Council has realized from the outset that a study of so broad an application and requiring so much detailed work could not be completed before the 1957 session of the Legislature. The target date for the completion of the study is 

June 30, 1957, and barring unforeseen circumstances, that is a reasonable date on which to expect completion. The final plans for each phase of the study have been approved as heretofore outlined, and copies of these plans are available in the office of the Legislative Council to the members of the General Assembly.

The fact that the survey cannot be completed in time to be useful to the 1957 General Assembly should not deter the Legislature from making improvements in our program of education that are obviously needed. On the other hand, we believe that major changes in the structure and organization of our school system should await completion of the survey. 

The Council suggests that the 1957 General Assembly do as much as possible toward meeting the obvious needs of our schools (both public and higher education) by providing the resources which will be needed to maintain the present school program in the face of rising school enrollments and by continuing to improve the level of education in the State. It firmly believes that major changes in the structure and organization of either of the school systems should await completion of the survey.

LEGISLATIVE PROCEDURES 

The Legislative Council has, since its organization in 1953, carried on a continuous study of legislative procedures in an effort to improve and make more workable the rules of both houses and to recommend steps that will insure a greater degree of efficiency in the legislative branch of our state government. 

Early in 1955, Chairman James L. Bomar appointed the following 

Legislative Procedures Subcommittee: Rep. Eugene N. Collins, Chairman; Sen. James P. Diamond, Sen. McAllen Foutch, Rep. 

James H. Cummings, Rep. Barton Dement, and Rep. Fred I. Womack.

The Council and its subcommittee, in deliberating on this matter, had the advice of Rep. I. D. Beasley (now deceased); L. 

Buchanan Loser, Chief Clerk of the House; Gaines Lollar, Journal 

Clerk of the House; Harry Phillips, Secretary, Tennessee Code

Commission; and Weldon White and Clarence Kolwyck of the 

Bar Association of Tennessee, as well as present and former members of the Tennessee General Assembly, and the staff of the Council.

The Council noted that the trend during the past two or three sessions has been toward uniformity in Senate and House rules. 

We believe that this is good since many members of the General Assembly have served in both houses and all will profit by a similarity in procedure. The Council believes that this trend should be encouraged and that the time will probably come when it will be desirable to revise and renumber the rules of both houses so as to insure general uniformity without infringing on the right of either house to determine its rules of procedure as guaranteed by the constitution. Members of the General Assembly will recognize that the recommendations herein are designed to achieve three goals: (1) To clarify present rules, (2) to eliminate restrictions that might hamper free legislative decisions, (3) to achieve uniformity in the rules of the House and Senate wherever practical or possible.

SUGGESTED RULE CHANGES 

1. Rule 9 of the House of Representatives authorizes the Speaker or Chairman of the Committee of the Whole to clear the lobby in the event of disturbance or disorderly conduct. The Council feels that this power should be extended to cover the gallery of the House of Representatives. WE RECOMMEND, therefore, that 

House Rule 9 be amended by adding in the second line thereof, immediately before the word "lobby," the words "gallery or," so that House Rule 9 will read as follows: 102

"In case of any disturbance or disorderly conduct in the gallery or lobby, the Speaker or the Chairman of the Committee of the Whole shall have power to order the same to be cleared." 

2. We find, on examining the rules of both houses, that House 

Rule 21 and Senate Rule 59 refer to persons who shall be admitted to the floors of the respective chambers. House Rule 21 makes it incumbent upon some member to demand that the floor of the House be cleared, while Senate Rule 59 denies the floor of the Senate Chamber to all except those properly authorized. We believe that the Senate Rule on this matter is superior to the House Rule, and for that reason WE RECOMMEND that Senate Rule 59 be substituted for House Rule 21, so that Senate Rule 59 and House Rule 21 will read as follows: "The Sergeant-at-Arms shall admit no person into the Senate Chamber (House Chamber) except the officers of the State, members of the House (Senate), ex-members of the Senate (House), officers and members of the Senate (House), and reporters properly authorized to represent the various newspapers of the country; and all visitors and spectators shall have access to the gallery so long as they shall preserve order; but the Speaker shall have power to suspend this rule at pleasure, unless otherwise directed by the Senate (House)." 

3. House Rule 40 and Senate Rule 30 deal with motions to reconsider. The right given in the House, prior to 1953, to a member voting with the prevailing side to move reconsideration and request that the same be entered on the Journal and thus obtain exclusive control of the bill under consideration for the next two succeeding days of actual session, no longer appears in present House Rules, but is given in present Senate Rule 30. It is our feeling that this right should be restored to the rules of the House. 

We believe, however, that most of paragraphs 2 and 3 of Senate Rule 30 are outdated and contribute nothing to the procedure now followed in that body, therefore, WE RECOMMEND that the first paragraph of Senate Rule 30, plus the words "and no motion to reconsider a reconsideration shall be in order," be substituted for House Rule 40, so that House Rule 40 and Senate 

Rule 30 will read as follows: "When a question has been made and carried in the affirmative or negative, it shall be in order for any member voting with the prevailing side to move for a reconsideration thereof, at any time the same day or the two next succeeding days of actual session. If the member moving the reconsideration shall request that same be entered upon the Journal, it shall be done, and he shall have exclusive control of same during that day, and the two next succeeding days of actual session; and same shall be a privileged motion, taking precedence, when called up, over all other business. Thereafter, it may 103

be called up and disposed of by any member of the House; and no motion to reconsider a reconsideration shall be in order." 

4. House Rule 53 and Senate Rule 37 refer to the duties of the 

Committee on Committees, and are identical. We think that the 

Committee on Committees (sometimes referred to as the Calendar Committee) has worked well in both Houses and that it has done much to promote orderliness in the consideration of legislation. Since an ample and orderly procedure is provided elsewhere in the rules of both houses for changing or suspending rules, as well as for recalling bills that have been in a committee for more than seven days, we believe the last sentence of House Rule 53 and Senate Rule 37, which reads, "provided, however, that a majority may, at any time, suspend rule 53 (37)" is out of harmony with other House and Senate rules and tends to threaten orderly procedure, as well as the rights of the minority. WE 

RECOMMEND that the last sentence of Senate Rule 37, and the last sentence of House Rule 53, be stricken in its entirety, so that

Senate Rule 37 and House Rule 53, as amended, will read as 

"S.R. 37 (H. R. 53) All bills having been considered and reported out of the Committee to which referred, shall be referred to the Committee on Committees, whose duty it shall be to announce each day, under the Eleventh order of business, a calendar, listing the order of bills to be considered on the following day. Should the Senate (House) not_complete any day's calendar, the bills remaining unconsidered shall go to the top of the calendar on the next day that General Bills are considered. The Committee on Committees may set aside certain days when the 12th and 13th order of business will not be called." 

5. House Rule 56 and Senate Rule 41 deal with the right to recall a bill from Committee. Under House Rule 56, any action on a bill by a committee, even though taken in committee and not reported to the House, can be interpreted to prevent recall, though a majority of the members of the House favor passage of said legislation. We feel that the first paragraph of Senate Rule 41 is superior to the first paragraph of House Rule 56, although we would recommend that the word "any" be inserted in the first paragraph of Senate Rule 41 between the words "in" and "committee" and that the words "a majority of the members to which the Senate (House) is entitled under the constitution" are more appropriate than the words "a majority of the members of the Senate (House)", so that this paragraph would read, "After any bill or other matter shall have been in any committee for seven (7) days, the same may be called for by a majority of the members to which the Senate (or House) is entitled under the constitution, and placed upon the calendar of the Senate (House).” 104

We believe that a more orderly and deliberate process can be insured for bills that are recalled from committee under the provision of the first paragraph of these two rules if certain changes are made in the second paragraph of both rules. Under the present procedure in both houses, when a bill is recalled from a committee it is placed at the top of the calendar and is subject to immediate consideration under the regular order of business. 

Ordinarily, when a motion is made to recall a bill from committee, many of the members are unprepared to take final action although they may have been convinced that the ends of justice demanded that the bill be freed from the committee in which it was being held. We suggest that so much of the second paragraph of 

Senate Rule 41 and House Rule 56 as reads "and be subject to immediate consideration under the regular order of business" be deleted and that the words "on the next day on which general bills are considered" be inserted in lieu of the deletion. WE, THEREFORE, RECOMMEND that Senate Rule 41 and House 

Rule 56 be changed so as to read as follows: "S. R. 41 (H.R. 56)-After any bill or other matter shall have been in any committee for seven (7) days, the same may be called for by a majority of the members to which the Senate (or House) is entitled under the constitution, and placed upon the calendar of the Senate (or House)." "When a bill has been recalled from a committee to which it was assigned by the Speaker, or from the Committee on Committees, it shall be placed at the top of the calendar on the next day on which general bills are considered." 

6. House Rule 66 and the first sentence of Senate Rule 51 deal with parliamentary motions designed to kill or reject a bill. Our 

Constitution provides that "After a bill has been rejected, no bill containing the same substance shall be passed into law in the same session." On occasion, members who are opposed to some particular legislation will introduce a bill on that subject and later make, or have made, a motion, on first or second reading, designed to reject the bill and thus in effect, if said motion is adopted, defeat all similar legislation for the entire session. Those familiar with parliamentary rules recognize such a motion as perfectly legitimate, under existing House and Senate Rules. 

When the members are informed of what is taking place, it cannot be said that such a motion tends to defeat the will of the majority, however, bills are seldom debated on first or second reading and on most occasions slight attention is given the call by the Clerk for these two orders of business. For that reason 

WE RECOMMEND that Senate Rule 51 and House Rule 66 be amended by changing the "period" at the end of the first sentence thereof to a "comma" and inserting the following words: "provided, however, if any motion is made to this effect on first or second reading, then the bill, or resolution, and motion shall lie 105

over one day.", so that the first sentence of Senate Rule 51 and

House Rule 66 will read as follows: 

"S. R. 51 (H. R. 66)-A motion "To postpone indefinitely," or "To strike out the enacting clause of a bill," or "Resolving clause of a resolution," if concurred in, is equivalent to a rejection, provided, however, if any motion is made to this effect on first or second reading, then the bill, or resolution, and motion shall lie over one day." (No change is contemplated in the second paragraph of Senate Rule 51.) 

7. It has come to the attention of this Council that some states maintain a written record of the rulings of their Speakers on points of order. It seems to us that such a procedure is desirable for three reasons: (1) A record of such rulings would tend to explain the rules to new members, (2) such rulings, if published in the rule books at each session, would be of tremendous assistance to future Speakers, and (3) the adoption of such a rule would cause a body of legislative law to be built up in Tennessee. 

WE THEREFORE WISH TO RECOMMEND the adoption of the following rule by each house of the 80th General Assembly: 

The Clerk of the Senate (House) shall enter on the Journal all rulings by the Speaker on Points of Order. The Clerk or any member may request that the Speaker submit such ruling in writing at the close of the day on which the ruling was made.

The 1955 General Assembly adopted a new code. Present plans call for the inclusion of a compilation of the Public Acts of 1955, sometimes referred to as the "1955 Cumulative Supplement," into the official edition of the new Tennessee Code by the enactment of one act during the 1957 session. Until this act is passed, any general bill, introduced during the 1957 session, that seeks to amend a law enacted during the 1955 session would refer to a 1955 Public Chapter number, while any act designed to amend a law that existed prior to 1955 would refer to a section in the new Code. of the new Tennessee Code Annotated be given first priority by the 1957 General Assembly and that all members of the Legislature defer final action on their bills until this act becomes law. 

Printing the Public Acts of 1957It has been customary for a contract to print the Public Acts of each session of the Legislature to be awarded several weeks after adjournment, so that these acts are usually not available in bound volume until late in the following year. As a result of this procedure, legislators, judges, and other public officials are without the benefit of the official Public Acts for many months after they become effective.

By following this plan, official copies of most of the acts passed during the 1957 session would be in the hands of the officials mentioned above prior to adjournment. It would not take the printer long after adjournment to print and assemble the bound copy of the Public Acts and deliver the same to the Secretary of State for distribution in the manner prescribed by law.

ORGANIZATIONAL SESSION OF THE GENERAL ASSEMBLY 

Those who observe legislative procedure in Tennessee have long realized that each Legislature consumes practically all of the month of January in organizing. The 79th General Assembly enacted 347 general bills. The first of these bills was not passed until January 25, 1955, and only 14 were enacted during the entire month of January.

Some may wonder what members of the Legislature do during this time, but experienced legislators know that the first two or three days are consumed in electing the officers of both houses, following which employees must be selected from the executive branch to handle the clerical jobs connected with the session. The speakers are not presumptuous enough to select their committees prior to election and this is a time-consuming job if qualifications, party affiliation, and residence are properly considered. It is therefore usually about three weeks before the members are organized and ready for business. 

In an effort to bridge this gap, many states have adopted the procedure of holding a short organizational meeting prior to the regular session. Under such a plan the speakers and clerks can be elected, rules adopted and resolutions authorizing services passed in a matter of two or three days; following adjournment, the speakers have time to name committees, select the employees of the executive branch who are to serve the Legislature, make arrangements for the various legislative services, and when the assembly convenes in regular session, the assembly is ready to operate on a full-time basis from the first day. 

With these matters in mind, the Legislative Council recommends that the Governor convene the 80th General Assembly in extraordinary session at some time during the months of November or December for the purpose of organization. If this plan works, and we have every reason to believe it will, we hope that all future Governors will continue the plan. 109

LOBBYING Pursuant to House Resolution No. 60 of the 79th General 

Assembly, the Legislative Council made a study of lobbying. 

Soon after the adjournment of the 79th General Assembly, the Council directed its staff to gather information on lobbying, including the laws in other states on that subject. Among the materials thus collected was a treatise on state lobbying laws and regulations prepared for the Council by James P. Guenther, a law student at Vanderbilt University.

At the regular meeting of the Council on September 5, 1955, it reviewed all the information referred to above and found, among other things, that Tennessee is one of ten states which does not now have a lobbying law or regulations other than the general law against bribery.

At the conclusion of this first hearing, the Council requested its staff to attempt to secure a model act that would: (1) Require lobbyists to register (2) Require lobbyists and their employers to file expense accounts, and (3) Prohibit lobbyists from accepting employment on a contingent fee basis.

The staff reported that no such model act existed. 

The Council Chairman then, on February 16, 1956, appointed a subcommittee to make a further study of the laws in other states and of the need for a lobbying law in Tennessee. This subcommittee was composed of the following members: Sen. Henry

Gupton, Chairman; Sen. Carroll Oakes and Rep. T. J. Davis. 

The subcommittee studied the federal law on the subject and the laws of the following states: Ohio, North Carolina, Nebraska, Wisconsin, Virginia, Kansas, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, Michigan, Connecticut, Kentucky, Maryland, Indiana, South

Carolina and Mississippi. From this study it was found that: (1) The principal purpose of lobbying laws is to publicize the activities of lobbyists. (2) Of the 38 states which now have lobbying laws in one form or another, 29 require registration of lobbyists. (3) Nineteen (19) of the 29 states requiring registration also require the filing of expense accounts. (In 3 of these, both the lobbyist and his employer are required to file expense accounts.) (4) Twenty-two (22) states prohibit contingent fees for lobbyists. 110

(5) The Secretary of State in most of these states is responsible for the registration. In some states this responsibility is with the Attorney General, while in Oklahoma and Florida it is done by designated officers of the Legislature. (6) Several states require a registration fee of lobbyists ranging from a nominal fee to $250.00 in Georgia. (No one has registered in Georgia since 1941 as a result of the high fee.) (7) A great number of the states having lobbying laws require that this information be furnished members of the Legislature or published weekly in journals of each house. (8) A survey by the Associated Press in April, 1955, disclosed that 21 state legislatures had under consideration bills for the regulation of lobbists of the 21, ten (10) took no action. 

Since it is recognized that this matter directs itself principally to the members of the Legislature, present and past members of the Legislature were consulted.

After reviewing the laws of the other states and giving consideration to the lobbying activities during the past sessions of the Tennessee Legislature and to suggestions and recommendations of present and past members of the Legislature, the Council recommends to the 80th General Assembly that: (1) Legislation be enacted which would provide that: (a) Lobbying, as such, be regulated.

All who were consulted during the study agreed that a great deal of confusion would be eliminated if lobbying were regulated even to a small degree. (b) Lobbyists, properly defined, be required to register. 

It was brought out that often members of the 

Legislature are approached by strangers and asked about their stand on pending legislation. Questions of this type are often answered in an offhand manner, before all of the information available to the member has been considered, and with no thought that he would be quoted. Later it developed that the person making the inquiry was a lobbyist, had not identified himself as such, but had quoted, and sometimes misquoted, the member. Some lobbyists have been known to approach a member and question him on a bill or a proposed bill, and if his opinion at that time were favorable to the lobbyist, he would report to his employer that he had that member lined up, implying in some instances that his, the lobbyist's, influence had been used. All agreed that the lobbyists should be registered so their identity could be ascertained by the members of the Assembly. 111

(c) Contingent fees be outlawed.

It was suggested that some lobbyists accept employment with the agreement that the pay for their services would depend in whole or in part on their success in promoting or defeating certain legislation in which the employer was interested. 

Unwholesome practices often arise from such agreement. For example, the lobbyist might contract with his employer to oppose legislation on a given subject, should the issue arise, and might then be able to persuade a member of the Legislature to introduce a bill to make sure the issue did arise. 

Later he might persuade the member to withdraw his bill, thereby being in a position to collect a contingent fee without the member ever realizing that he had been used to carry out this well laid plan. All agreed that this should be outlawed. 

The only other provision given serious consideration at this time was that set out earlier in the report which would require lobbyists and their employers to file a statement of expenses. 

There are arguments in favor of such a provision as well as against it.

It was the feeling of the Council that, as in most matters of new legislation, the basic law should first be enacted and tried out before too many requirements are made. It might very well be that if the recommended law is enacted it will be found that filing a statement of expenses would be desirable; if so, that can be done by amendment. However, the Council does not recommend this provision until the law has been established as above set out and put through the test of a session's trial period. 

We further recommend that: (2) Each week during the session of the Legislature a list of the registered lobbyists be published in "The Legislative Record," together with all pertinent information required of each lobbyist on the registration. (3) More strict enforcement of the Senate and House rules governing lobbyists, particularly as these rules relate to the activity of unauthorized persons upon the floor of each chamber. 

The last two recommendations would not require legislation. 

A suggested bill has been drafted providing for compliance with the first recommendation. Copies of this bill are on file in the office of the Legislative Council and are available to the members of the 80th General Assembly.

It is the belief of the Legislative Council that if the above recommendations are carried out, a great deal of confusion caused by lobbyists during the session of the Legislature would be eliminated and the dignity of the legislative body would be enhanced. 112

Council through the Purchasing Department with the International Roll-Call Machine Corporation of Richmond, Virginia for the installation of an electric roll-call system in the Senate chamber with rental-maintenance for 1957. The cost of this installation will be $7,500.00 and the machine will be ready for operation by December 1, 1956.

DISPOSITION OF STATE RECORDS Pursuant to House Joint Resolution No. 38 of the 79th Gen eral Assembly, the Legislative Council was directed to make a study on the disposition of state records. 

On May 4, 1955, Chairman Bomar appointed the following legislators as a subcommittee to conduct this study: Sen. Henry 

Gupton, Chairman; Rep. Douglas S. Henry, Jr., Secretary; Sen. 

T. R. Ray and Rep. Weldon Burrow. The members of the subcommittee met on three separate occasions, heard much testimony about the problems confronting our state in this field, and personally visited our state archives.

During the course of the study, the following persons appeared before the subcommittee one or more times and furnished information regarding the problems under consideration: Dan 

M. Robinson, State Librarian and Archivist; William T. Alderson, Senior Archivist; Robert T. Quarles, Jr., Director of the 

Archives Division; Eleanor J. Burt, Senior Librarian; and James 

M. Glasgow, Assistant Attorney General. Much of the information contributed by them revealed how other states are meeting this problem.

The following statement of the problem was developed through public hearings and personal inspection of the Library and Archives Building: 

(1) Since June, 1953, the State Archives has received an average of 300 standard-sized file drawers of new records each month. (2) At this rate, available storage space in the new State Library and Archives Building will be completely filled in about two years. With the expenditure of around $75,000 for additional shelving, this could be extended to three and one half years. (3) Microfilming all records appears to be too expensive and is not a satisfactory solution to the problem. To microfilm the accessions of the last two years alone, not to mention records received prior to that date, would cost $540,000. 

To microfilm new accessions at this current rate would cost $18,000 per month. These figures are based on recent cost estimates made by the federal government, and include film, labor, equipment, and all other related costs. (4) A large portion of records now held by the Archives, or that are currently being created by the departments of state government, do not possess sufficient value to warrant their permanent retention. Included in this group are duplicate and triplicate copies of some records of permanent value, 117

the preservation of one copy of which, either in original or microfilm form, would satisfy the need for documentation of the agency's work. (5) No original or most complete record should be destroyed until the statute of limitation has ceased to apply. 

The enactment of statutes of limitations is solely a legislative function. (6) In the disposition of records, adequate safeguards must be established to prevent the destruction of records that have: (a) legal; (b) fiscal; (c) administrative or (d) research value. (7) The disposition of records could probably be administered most effectively by a small board or commission of responsible state officials whose position and experience peculiarly qualify them to perform the duties imposed upon the board.

We recommend the passage, by the Legislature, of an Act designed to establish a Public Records Commission composed of the Attorney General, the Comptroller, the State Historian, the 

State Librarian and Archivist, and the Executive Director of the

Legislative Council, as permanent members; the President of the 

Tennessee Historical Society, as a non-voting member; and the head of the department, commission, board or agency which created the records under consideration, as a temporary nonvoting member.

A commisison, composed of the membership set forth above, would be able to review all records from the standpoint of their legal, fiscal, administrative, and research value, and would still have some relation to the legislative branch of government. The president of the Tennessee Historical Society, though not a voting member, would insure public representation at meetings and the Commission would be his forum for the airing of any grievance concerning plans for unwarranted disposal. 

We recommend that the proposed Public Records Commission be authorized to determine and order proper disposal of state records. 

Proper disposal can be accomplished either: (a) By direct State legislation authorizing disposal of selected items, or (b) Through a Board or Commission established by a legislative act which provides certain safeguards as enumerated under our third recommendation. 

We strongly recommend the latter course. 118

We recommend the following limitations on the powers of the Commission: 

(a) No record shall be destroyed without the consent of the head of the department, board, or agency which created the same, followed by the unanimous approval of the voting members of the Public Records Commission. (b) No original or most complete copy of a record shall be destroyed before the applicable statute of limitations or nine years, whichever is more, expires. (c) No record concerning public or private lands shall be destroyed. (d) The Commission shall not have the power to destroy any judicial or legislative record.

In keeping with the mandate of the 79th General Assembly that legislation be prepared on this subject, we have, with the assistance of the Attorney General's office, drafted a suggested act.

Copies of this proposed act are in the office of the Legislative Council and are available to all members of the 80th General Assembly.

We believe that the adoption of the recommendations made herein will result in the saving of thousands of dollars to the taxpayers of the state, and that with the recommended safeguards, nothing of value will be destroyed. 119

MISCELLANEOUS 

Council Budget for the 1957-59 Biennium

The present annual budget of the Legislative Council is $60,000.00. We have carried on 18 studies during this biennium with our present staff and believe that we could undertake as many as 24 during the next biennium without impairing the quality of our work. Should the Council be assigned more than 

24 studies by the 80th General Assembly, or should you assign us a study of such magnitude that additional technical and research employees would be required, it will be necessary to ask for additional funds.

We therefore recommend a Legislative Council budget of $60,000.00 per year for the next biennium, provided the Council is not assigned more than 24 studies and provided further that none of the studies require a special staff. 

The Legislative Council desires to direct the attention of members of the General Assembly to the following matters without making specific recommendations thereon: 

Hospitalization of the Indigent.

A study committee on Indigent Hospital Services representing the Tennessee State Medical Association, the Tennessee Chapter of the American Cancer Society, Incorporated, the Tennessee Hospital Association, the Tennessee County Judges Association, and the Tennessee Heart Association has filed with the Council statements concerning the need for broadening our present indigent hospital services law to include cancer and heart cases capable of rehabilitation and requesting that the present appropriation be increased from $150,000.00 per year to at least $1,500,000.00 for the biennium. We have not made a study of the conditions leading to this request. We do, however, urge the members of the General Assembly to give careful consideration on Indigent Hospital Services.

Air Pollution. 

Dr. R. H. Hutcheson, Commissioner of Public Health, has requested that the Council consider the problem of air pollution as presently known in Tennessee. He has advised that there will be an increasing need for serious consideration of this matter as the use of fissionable material and radioactive isotopes, by private industry, private research workers and others not officially under the control of the Atomic Energy Commission, becomes more common. Since it had no directive from the General Assembly, the Council has not studied this problem. However, we recognize merit in Dr. Hutcheson's request and recommend careful consideration to any request for such a study. 120
