Introduction 

Despite a generation of industrial safety activity, no standard training text was available 7 years ago when the Division of Labor Standards was asked by various State labor departments to help train factory inspectors. To meet the need, R. P. Blake, the Division's senior safety engineer, in collaboration with other staff specialists, developed Industrial Safety Subjects. 

Constantly revised and expanded on the basis of actual industrial experience, this text, published in mimeographed form, was used in 1941 when the War Safety Training Program was initiated by the Division in cooperation with the Office of Education. More than 

50,000 key supervisors and worker representatives in war plants have now completed 96-hour basic courses in 116 engineering colleges throughout the country.

Widespread demand for this textual material has led the Division to print it under the title Safety Subjects. It is hoped that this book will continue to serve as a source of basic information on industrial accident prevention.

CHAPTER I Introduction to Industrial Safety 

Annual toll of industrial injuries. Cost in time money. Injury distribution by industrial classifications. Over-all improvement in national accident record. Gains in large establishments. Small plant problem. Value and practicability of safety. Outlook for immédiate future. 

1. During 1942 approximately 18,100 Americans were accidentally killed at their work. Another 102,600 suffered permanent injuries that ranged from permanent total disablement to such relatively minor injuries as the partial loss of a finger or a permanently stiffened finger joint. Approximately 2,147,000 more were injured to an extent that incapacitated each from his work for more than one day. 

The average duration of such incapacity was approximately 15 days each. 

2. This wastage of human values constitutes a serious national problem-a problem which is intensified at a time when manpower and skills are critically scarce. The killed and injured included a heavy proportion of those having special skills, rare abilities, hardwon knowledge, fine training, or valuable experience; many were young people of exceptional promise; the majority were heads of families. Data in sufficient detail for complete analysis is lacking but the base rates charged for workmen's compensation insurance constitute proof that the injury rate in many of the skilled trades is high. Since these insurance rates are based on the most recent 

5 years' experience in each trade or occupation, they furnish rough but relatively dependable gauges of the chance of disabling injury in each such trade or occupation, as measured by the costs (to the insurance underwriters) of the injuries that have actually occurred in each. 

3. While the loss of our productive capacity due to the death of these 18,100 workers cannot be measured definitely, it is obviously large. Equally unmeasurable, but perhaps even larger is the productive loss due to the 102,600 permanent disablements. For while other workmen replace and ordinarily become as valuable as those killed, each permanent disablement means some loss of productive capacity throughout the remainder of the working lifetime of each disabled workman. The 2,147,000 temporary total disabilities involved the loss of approximately 32,000,000 man-days of working time. Nor should we overlook the human suffering involved. The sum total of misery hidden behind these figures should of itself be sufficient to justify every practicable preventive effort by management, labor, and government jointly and severally.

11. It should be emphasized that no direct comparison can be made between the annual national accident totals and the Safety Council frequency figures. The one figure is a total of occupational injuries while the other is the rate of their occurrence in the employment of member firms of the Council. 

Where the Gains Have Been Made. 

12. Certain highly significant facts appear from an analysis of available data, namely: (a) Many large firms in most branches of industry have reduced their injury rates to a fraction of the former figure. (b) A few entire industries have shown great gains. Examples--steel, automobile, public utilities, cement, petroleum, railroads. (c) Small firms as a class have shown relatively few gains.

13. Despite the fact that American industry is noted for its giant corporations, the latest (1941) data from the Bureau of Census reveals that some 69 percent of the workers in America's shops and factories are employed by firms with 500 workers or less. 

14. Large farming establishments are rare. In trade and service there are a number of large firms, but small ones are the rule. 

15. The large bulk of construction in normal times is carried on mainly by small firms. Even in large construction projects requiring a considerable labor force, much of the work is handled by subcontractors, most of whom employ relatively few persons. Almost all such allied services as painting, heating, building maintenance and repair, demolition, etc., are rendered by small firms or small job contractors. 

16. Mining, while having a number of great corporations, is likewise made up mainly of moderate-sized or small firms. 

17. Only in the public-utility, petroleum, and transportation industries are large firms the rule. Incidentally, all three of these industries have made great reductions in accidents. 

18. In view of these facts, it appears conservative to estimate that not more than 25 percent of our workers are employed by firms employing more than 500 persons. And, since the greatest strides in accident prevention have been made by large firms, at least 75 percent of our labor force has been "little touched by safety."

Small Establishments the Major Problem. 

19. The progress already made by large firms as a group in reducing their injury rates makes it appear that their further progress will have relatively little effect upon the national injury toll as a whole. Many of them have attained what appears to be an irreducible minimum, and the majority of the others, at least in the manufacturing, transportation and public-utility fields, are not very far from that goal. 

This is not true of the four major industries which show the highest injury rates, namely, logging, lumbering, heavy construction, and mining. The logging and lumbering industries have, as a whole, shown little interest in safety, though individual firms in each industry are improving greatly. The construction industry has made great progress, but has much more to do. Mining, once probably our most hazardous industry, as measured by its accidents, has shown great and steady improvement under the safety leadership of the U. S. 

Bureau of Mines. 20. Obviously then, any large reduction in the annual injury toll must come through greatly improved safety performance in the multitude of small firms and businesses which, despite the prominence of our industrial giants, still account for a large majority of our industrial employment. The influences and methods that have produced so great an improvement in the safety performance of large firms have not been successful with small firms generally. Why? A completely satisfactory answer has not been found. However, the major factors appear to be: (a) The small firm cannot employ full-time safety personnel. (b) The small-firm executive carries a complex load and lacks the aid of a technical staff.

While it is vitally important to continue and expand this organized promotional effort, supplemental means must be applied to provide a close personal safety contact with each of the multitude of small managements which together employ the great bulk of American workmen. 

22. The farming industry extends this problem. The spirit and practice of safety must be carried not only to millions of farmers and their families, but also to a considerable mass of seasonal and migratory labor. Farming yields a heavy total of injuries. In only a few 

States is agriculture under workmen's compensation legislation, and there only on a voluntary basis. Increasing mechanization appears to be increasing the flow of serious injuries and deaths from the farm. 

Lessons From Safety Accomplishment to Date. 

23. It should be remembered that the prevention of work injuries is a relatively new technique. Preventive effort by management rests upon the fundamental realization that such injuries constitute a serious waste of human and material resources. This realization which gave rise to the National Safety Council, continues to be the driving force behind the safety movement. 

24. The technique of safety has been developed by trial and error and proven by results achieved. It can safely be said that while continued improvement and refinement of method is to be expected, the knowledge as to how to reach and maintain top safety performance is ample for the job to be done; the problem is primarily one of securing adequate application of tried and proven methods. 

25. Listed below, in briefed form, are the more important conclusions to be drawn from industrial safety accomplishment to date. 

The list, while not complete, does present lessons of outstanding importance to those who are seeking to continue and expand the work of industrial injury prevention. (a) The accomplishment of numerous firms in every major branch of industry proves that disabling injuries can be reduced to a figure that closely approaches elimination; (b) It pays to do so; (c) The expenditures required are relatively small; (d) Some degree of hazard is associated with every form of activity; therefore, the highest degree of accident elimination can be achieved only by careful, painstaking attention to safety in

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

every form of activity carried on in any given establishment or undertaking; (e) Accident prevention does not rest upon involved theory or special technical skill. Instead, it depends mainly upon safety-mindedness by management and men. Safety-mindedness may be defined as "the ever-active attention to safety in every detail of each day's work;" (f) If properly applied, the knowledge and resourcefulness possessed by every industrial organization, large or small, is adequate to bring its safety performance into accord with best practice and keep it there; (g) Any management, regardless of size of the establishment, type of industry or undertaking, or financial condition, can eliminate the majority of its work injuries; (h) Each injury results from the combination of a physical hazard and human error. The correction of either will usually prevent the injury but top safety performance can be had only by eliminating or reducing every physical hazard to the maximum degree practicable and, in addition, using every feasible means of controlling all work habits and practices in the interest of safety. 

Predictions as to the Immediate Future. 

26. Predictions as to future trends are always hazardous. This is particularly applicable to times of rapid industrial change. A steady high rate of orderly industrial activity favors safety. Fluctuations in volume, changes in process and methods, shifting employment, unrest these and their like bring higher accident rates. However, the war emergency has greatly expanded safety interest and has compressed into the period of the emergency gains that would otherwise have required a far longer time to accomplish. It seems reasonable to believe that much of this war-awakened interest in safety will live on into peacetimes. Perhaps it is not too much to predict that the curve of our annual accident waste will turn downward substantially and continue downward to a level that no longer constitutes so serious an indictment of our industrial civilization.

1. This discussion is not a chronological account of the development of the industrial safety movement in this country. Instead, it is purposely limited to a consideration of certain phases of that movement, a review which will be helpful in understanding the problems and conditions faced in the present drive for further progress in reducing the nationally serious toll of occupational injuries and deaths. 

Realization of the Problem. 

2. Probably the occupational injury rate has been high throughout 

American history. Certainly the job of developing a new country from a wilderness is a hazardous one, and under the conditions amid which this country was born and grew, a high rate of accidental injuries and deaths would be accepted as a matter of course. The growth of large industrial establishments called attention to the injury toll, for with the concentration of large numbers of workmen in one place, an injury rate which would attract little attention if applied to an equal number of workmen scattered among many small firms (or on the farms or in the woods), produced in each large plant a stream of injuries that was impressive. Undoubtedly, also, the great industrial expansion with the markedly increased use of machinery (all of this with little or no thought for safety) that followed the depression of the 1870's did result in an increase in the injury rates in heavy industry, particularly deaths and permanent disabilities. Even today, in spite of our reasonably adequate knowledge of accident prevention and the safety-mindedness of many of our industrial leaders, rapid industrial expansion under pressure for increased production will, unless proper measures are applied, result in a notable increase in injury rates.

Relief for the Victims of Industrial Injuries. 

3. When the attention of the public began to be focussed upon the growing numbers of workers disabled or killed by industrial accidents, the first reaction was to do something to assist the victims financially, rather than to stop the accidents which were the cause of their plight. 

Under the common-law system, the primary basis for damages for personal injury was negligence; that is, the injured worker was denied recovery unless he could prove that the accident which caused the injury was the result of the employer's negligence. But even if the

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

employer were negligent, he could still escape legal responsibility for the payment of damages if he could prove any one of his three "common-law" defenses. His first defense was that the accident had resulted in part from the negligence of the injured worker himselfthe defense of "contributory negligence." His second defense was that the accident was in part the result of the negligence of a fellow worker of the injured-the "fellow servant rule." And the third defense was that accident resulted from a hazard which the employee knew, or should have known, when he took the job, and that he had, therefore, assumed the risk of such an accident when he went to workthe defense of "assumption of risk.” 

4. Employers' liability laws were the first product of the attempt to ease the burden on the injured worker who was seeking financial recovery from his employer. These laws left untouched the grounds for recovery-the negligence of the employer. Their sole intent was to modify the common-law defenses so as to permit greater ease of recovery when the employer has been proven negligent. This type of legislation is still applicable to many workers in trades, establishments or industries not covered by workmen's compensation laws. It is still the sole basis of recovery for seamen and employees engaged in interstate railroad transportation. Particularly in railroading, the liability law is coupled with safety laws and rules, violation of which by an employer is evidence of his negligence. 

5. But substantial numbers of injured workers and of dependents of killed workers still failed to receive any form of financial recovery, and in the early years of the twentieth century proponents of relief for the victims of industrial accidents turned to a new type of law which had been developed some 20 years before in Germany and which by then had spread to most of the countries of Europe and to England-workmen's compensation. The fight for the adoption of workmen's compensation in the United States was led by organized labor, materially assisted by the press, which played up railroad and mine disasters, and by a growing number of prominent persons interested in economic and social reform. The first workmen's compensation act in America was passed in 1908 when the Federal Congress, as the result of a direct recommendation of President Theodore

Roosevelt, adopted a law providing benefits for injured workers in certain hazardous Federal employments.. 

6. Within the next few years, the subject was discussed and debated throughout the Nation, and in 1911 the States began adopting workmen's compensation laws. Ten years later 42 States and Alaska, Hawaii, and Puerto Rico had enacted such laws, and the Federal Government had provided workmen's compensation for all of its civilian employees. Federal laws were also passed to provide workmen's compensation for longshoremen and harbor workers, and for private employees in the District of Columbia. In 1943 but one lone 

State-Mississippi-remained without some form of workmen's compensation law. 

7. The various compensation acts in this country differ considerably, both in detail and as to the completeness of protection which they offer and to the workers they affect. In no State are all workers covered, and farm workers and domestic servants are generally with-

9 out the law. In general, however, all of them accomplish (for those covered) the following: (a) Eliminate negligence as a factor in determining the right of an injured worker to receive benefits, by providing that the employer shall pay compensation to each worker injured in the course of his employment, regardless of fault. (b) Provide at least some medical care to repair the physical and/or mental harm caused by the injury and to hasten the return of the worker to his job. (c) Establish a fairly exact method of computing the monetary benefits to be paid to an injured worker, tying the amount of the benefits to the wages of the injured man and providing a minimum benefit which is supposed to represent at least a subsistence income. (d) Provide that benefits shall continue at least during a period of readjustment in cases of permanent total disability and death, and in the more liberal acts, during the entire period of disability of the injured workman or the dependency of the widow and children of the deceased workman.

Workmen's Compensation and Accident Prevention. 

8. Some interest in industrial accident prevention existed prior to the enactment of workmen's compensation laws. Attempts to prevent the loss of life and limb and the misery resulting from industrial accidents were made by some employers mindful of their responsibility to the workers. Employers' liability laws, by at least threatening to make recovery of damages easier, stimulated the use of machine guards and safety equipment in some cases. But workmen's compensation legislation provided the first real stimulus to industrial accident prevention and is primarily responsible for the movement as we know it today. 

9. The reason for this is simple. Under the common-law and employers' liability laws the primary question was-whose fault was it? The industrial injury was of no apparent cost to the employer unless the accident was solely the result of his negligence. His job was mainly proving that he didn't have to pay when a worker was injured. Under workmen's compensation the employer has to pay regardless of whether or not he is at fault. An accident happened causing injury to a workman who was performing his job-that is the sole basis for compensation. So the only certain way to avoid paying for work injuries is to prevent them. This factor has been directly responsible for the work done in the field of industrial safety by insurance companies and by employers, both individually and through service organizations. Also, by focusing attention on the incidence and cost of industrial accidents, workmen's compensation laws have been indirectly responsible for the extension of prevention work by the States. 

10. Insurance Companies and Safety.-Insurance is a basic part of the workmen's compensation system. Only through insurance can employers in general guarantee payment of benefits to injured workers, and in all States but one, employers coming under the workmen's compensation law are obliged to insure unless they are permitted by law to carry their own risk by proving themselves financially able to assume the liability directly.

11. The insurance company, be it a stock company or a mutual (either private or public) is interested, of course, in holding down its losses. Unduly large losses would jeopardize the financial solvency of the company, and consistently low losses are necessary if the stock company is to show a profit to its stockholders and if the mutual is to pay dividends to its policyholders. Thus insurance companies became interested in assisting their policyholders in accident prevention work early in the history of workmen's compensation. 

12. In addition to the technical assistance which the insurance companies offered, methods were devised for trying to make safety "pay" for the policyholder in the form of immediately lower insurance premium rates. This monetary benefit for accident prevention is inherent to some extent in mutual insurance, since the surplus remaining after losses and expenses is distributed among the policyholders. To offer similar inducement to policyholders in stock companies and to give further incentive to mutual policyholders, a device known as "merit rating" was adopted to obtain reductions in premium rates for policyholders. The first type of merit rating used was "schedule" rating, under which the reduction in premium rate was computed on the basis of the policyholder's excellence in providing physical safeguards. This proved unsatisfactory because guarding alone, while vital, is only one part of prevention. The system now in general use is based upon the actual accident experience of the policyholder and is called "experience rating." 

13. The effective work of the insurance companies has been confined mainly to the larger establishments. The cost of providing technical assistance in accident prevention makes it very difficult for the companies to service adequately plants whose premiums are small, and these small plants cannot expect to receive much reduction in premium rates through either dividends or merit rating. 

14. Safety Programs by Large Employers.-A few large employers established safety departments or made some other definite form of attack on industrial accidents even before the passage of workmen's compensation laws. Since many large employers became "self-insurers" under workmen's compensation, that is they paid benefits and provided medical care directly, their interest in reducing the toll of accidents within their own plants was increased with the passage of workmen's compensation laws. By 1913 the movement had become so widespread that the need of some sort of national organization was felt, and the National Safety Council was formed. 

15. National Safety Council.-The activities of the Council, originally established to deal solely with industrial accidents, now embraces the work of reducing the number and seriousness of accidents of all kinds. Many of its members are representatives of manufacturing, public utility and transportation firms, and of workmen's compensation insurance and liability insurance companies, whose main interest still centers on industrial accidents. In the public safety field, where the chief activity is the prevention of automobile accidents, the members are mainly local safety councils, chambers of commerce, automobile clubs, schools, and city, State, and national governmental departments. 

16. The Council seeks to promote safety largely through the compilation and distribution of information concerning the causes and methods of prevention of accidents. Much of the information is re-

11 ceived directly from reports made by Council members, while other information is the result of special surveys and studies made on the highway or in the industrial plant. The information thus gathered is presented to Council members and others in publications, through graphic posters, or by the development of elaborate accident prevention plans. The Council does not manufacture, sell, test, or approve safety devices; neither does it inspect factories or write insurance.

The Council maintains a staff of safety technicians to render an informational service to its membership. Its library on safety is the largest and most comprehensive in existence. In brief, it may be said that the Council furnishes leadership, inspiration, and information to the organized safety movement in America.

18. Safety Promotion by Governmental Agencies. In addition to the actual work of safety inspection by States, various governmental agencies have been engaged in the distribution of information concerning industrial accidents and occupational diseases and in promoting safety in a general way. Notable among such governmental activities are: (a) Collection and publication of information by the Bureau of Labor Statistics of the U. S. Department of Labor. Through coverage many times that of the National Safety Council, and through scientifically worked-out methods of sampling, the Bureau figures are believed to be more truly representative of the general accident experience of industry than any other compilation. (b) State-wide safety contests sponsored by State labor departments or workmen's compensation boards. Those in Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and Ohio are typical. 

(c) The Safety and Health Unit of the Division of Labor Standards of the U. S. Department of Labor. The activities of this unit cover the publication of information concerning the causes of and methods of preventing certain industrial injuries, and the training of State factory inspectors in order to provide higher performance by State enforcement agencies. More recently the unit has been designated as Washington headquarters of the National Committee for the Conservation of Manpower in 

War Industries.

19. State Safety Inspection Services.- Workmen's compensation laws were usually accompanied by legislation requiring the correction of hazardous equipment (chiefly the safeguarding of hazardous machinery) and providing for inspection to enforce the law. This type of legislation rests upon the theory that prevention is chiefly a job of eliminating hazardous conditions, a goal to be attained only by compelling industry to correct physical hazards. Actually, of course, the great majority of employers never were, and are not now, indifferent to the safety of their employees. Failure to establish and maintain safe working conditions and good safety practices is usually due to ignorance as to what constitutes good practice in accident prevention. 

20. Considering the handicaps under which the safety departments of the States have been forced to labor, they have done much good work, particularly in securing at least minimum attention to machinery safeguarding and to the correction of the more obvious physical hazards. One of the handicaps faced by the industrial safety inspection units in general is a lack of information concerning the incidence and seriousness of accidents. In most of the States reporting of accidents is required only for purposes of the workmen's compensation law. The information in the reports is not designed to give adequate information on the causes of accidents, which is vital to a prevention unit. Furthermore, since the information is primarily for use by the compensation unit, reports are usually required only for accidents causing disability beyond the "waiting period" for benefits, which may be as long as 2 weeks. Often, too, there is a lack of coordination between the compensation and prevention units which results in the failure of the latter to receive even the limited information obtained by the compensation agency. 

21. The State accident prevention units have been handicapped, too, by a lack of funds and have been bedeviled by constantly changing personnel where a merit system is not in effect for State employees. 

In some States where copies of accident reports are furnished to the inspection agency, the latter is able to make no use of the information because of inadequate personnel, and the reports accumulate in a file. 

22. However, the trend is toward increased effectiveness of State safety inspection. A vital change in viewpoint is taking place, and more emphasis is being placed on promotion and service as contrasted with enforcement. It is now realized that good safety performance can be accomplished in a plant only when the management wants safety enough to apply itself diligently to getting it. In spite of the difficulties of securing accurate accident cost determination, the clear presentation of those costs is necessary in order to make employers as a class more accident-cost conscious so as to stimulate them to active accident-prevention effort. The more these costs can be shown, the greater the incentive to prevent.

Compensation and medical costs can be obtained from the insurer. The foreman, the master mechanic, the timekeeper, the official in charge of production, the injured employee, and those with or near him at the time of the accident, should all be interviewed. 21. The public is becoming increasingly safety-minded. In recognition of this fact the advertising of many leading firms is being devoted in some part to the effect of their products upon the safety of the public and to the safe conditions under which the product was made or handled. 22. Fine safety performance can be of great value in improving employee morale. Furthermore, the safety program itself is very valuable in fostering mutual understanding and cooperation between management and men and among all members of the organization. 

The oft-repeated saying, "There is something about safety work that gets under one's hide," expresses a vital truth. Whenever a management, any management, joins wholeheartedly with its employees in the effort to eliminate injuries to them at their work, it does much to bring out the better side of the human nature of both.

23. On the other hand, an atmosphere of discord breeds accidents. 

Labor troubles bring higher injury rates. Increased likelihood of accidents is associated with any major change in operations or in any resumption of operation after a shut-down, and when the upset is caused by or accompanied by any serious disagreement with the employees, the probability of accidents is further increased until such time as the spirit of full cooperation for safety is restored. Accident Cost to Employees. 

24. Industry does not bear the cost of accidents alone. The heavy financial loss to workers should also be kept clearly in mind. As pointed out earlier the National Safety Council estimates that during 1942 the wage loss to workers injured on the job amounted to some $620,000,000. Millions of workers are not covered by workmen's compensation laws-farm workers, workers in small plants which in many States are exempted from the workmen's compensation acts, workers in establishments whose employers have chosen (as they have a right to do in many States) not to come under the workmen's compensation law, and domestics. Furthermore, workers entitled to compensation receive only a fraction of their wages. The advocated payment of two-thirds of wages is not met in all States, some of which provide for as little as 50 percent. And the operation of an absolute weekly dollar maximum on compensation benefits reduces for high wage earners the percentage of wages received to far below the percentage stated in the act. The "scheduled" payments for permanent partial disabilities average far less than the loss of earning power occasioned by the disability. And the death of a wage earner usually means financial disaster to his dependents under even the most liberal compensation acts. From the wage earner's viewpoint there is no doubt that "safety is better than compensation."

1. There is much confusion in the use of the word "cause" as applied to accidents. To the preventionist the cause of an accident consists of the defects, or actions, or lack of action, that should be corrected to prevent a recurrence. Such terms as handling materials, falls, burns, etc., commonly listed as accident causes, are not causes at all. Handling materials is an activity out of which many accidents come, but the cause of each accident is a hazardous condition or something that some person does or fails to do in connection with the activity in question, or in most cases a combination of these. Falls lead to injury, but the preventionist wants to know what condition, act, or omission led to the fall. Burns are injuries, not causes. 

2. As an aid to clear understanding, "accident" as used herein is defined as "any occurrence that interrupts or interferes with the orderly progress of the activity in question." An accident, according to this definition, does not necessarily involve human injury. Indeed, most accidents do not and remain unrecorded, except on the cost sheet, for it is obvious that all interruptions and interferences increase costs, whether or not the amount of the cost increase can be measured. 

It is equally obvious that if we can make any properly planned job or any activity go exactly as planned, we will eliminate all accidents and with them all injuries.

Accident Causes. 

9. Causes may be subdivided into mechanical and material faults, unsafe acts of persons and the reasons for those faults or unsafe acts. 

Several factors are always involved when an accidental injury occurs.

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

They occur in a given sequence to produce a final result. Heinrich 2 has likened these factors to several dominoes standing on end so that when the first is tipped over it will cause the rest to fall successively. 

Injury is the final domino. Preceding the injury there is an occurrence, that is the fall of a person or an object, or contact with a machine, and so forth. Preceding the occurrence that produces the injury there is in every case either the unsafe act of a person (such as using a file without a handle, wearing loose clothing around machinery, and so forth) or an unsafe physical or mechanical condition (unguarded machinery, protruding nails, a tool in disrepair, and so forth) or both. Back of these things are the reasons for the unsafe act or hazardous condition. So we have our train of dominoes on end and culminating in an injury. The basic idea in prevention is to discover the causative factors the key dominoes and remove them, thus breaking the sequence and preventing the injury. 

10. The following classification of basic causes has received wide acceptance. It can serve as an excellent guide in analyzing accidents and accidental injuries. However, valuable as this analysis has proven, it does not bring out the fact that first attention should always be given to eliminating or reducing the physical hazard or fault. În nearly every case of accidental injury, both a physical hazard and faulty behavior will be found. Studies by a committee of the American Society of Safety Engineers showed that in some 80 percent of a thousand cases studied the physical hazard could either have been eliminated or reduced by practicable means. It is obvious that had this been done in each case the faulty performance would have been very much less likely to lead to injury. Therefore, the sane common sense course is to reduce physical hazards by every reasonably possible means and then make every effort to eliminate faults of behavior also. 

Undoubtedly, if we could find a worker who would never make a wrong or incorrect move he could operate the most hazardous machinery unguarded without getting hurt. Comforting as such a viewpoint may be to those who are unwilling to spend money generously in the interests of safety, it is not the way to eliminate preventable injuries. The best safety records are being made and maintained by those who use every effort to discover and correct all unsafe physical conditions and in addition painstakingly and continuously train and stimulate every employee to work safely.

11. The 15 to 85 Percent Fallacy.-Many studies have been made to determine the proportion of accidents chargeable to physical hazards as against those chargeable to unsafe acts (faulty behavior). The ratio usually quoted is 15 to 85 percent. Actually such ratios have little meaning or value in the work of prevention. First of all such studies by attempting in each case to fix only the principal cause of injury ignore the fact that most injuries result from a combination of physical hazard and faulty behavior and that it is usually possible to improve both. Furthermore, the reduction (elimination where possible) of the physical hazard will reduce the likelihood of injury from faulty behavior. Finally, such ratios, resulting from data covering an entire State or a large number of insured risks and including both extremes and every gradation between are of little practical value for use in the individual establishment. Analysis of plant accident records show that while certain fundamentals are of general applicability, the "firing line" detail of prevention is largely governed by conditions in each plant or operation. 

12. On the other hand, authoritative presentation of such ratios does much harm by unduly emphasizing "carelessness" and obscuring the importance of correcting hazardous conditions. The "not-yet safety-minded employer" accepting the 15-85 (or similar) ratio naturally reasons, to quote a specific instance, "I'll go after the 85 percent first and take care of the 15 percent after I get the carelessness thing licked. Why spend a lot of money on only 15 percent of the job?" 

13. Another factor-often highly important in appraising statistical evidence is the fact that in most cases the wrong act is made glaringly evident by the injury that results. The mechanical fault, however, is seldom so evident; in fact, unless a careful safetyminded study is made of the occurrence, the correctible hazard will often escape notice. An instance of this is furnished by the experience of a leading employer, whose accident record has been steadily improving for some years and is now at least representative of good practice in his industry. On a large battery of small stamping presses, his standard practice was to provide means of feeding that made it unnecessary for the operator to reach into the danger zone, but he had not provided an enclosure so complete as to make this impossible. 

An outside safety engineer called upon to advise with the management

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

on the furtherance of its already good safety work, discovered on studying the accident record that two amputations charged to personal fault had occurred on these presses. Investigation showed that simple ram enclosures, at a cost of only a few dollars per press, would have prevented them in spite of the faulty behavior of the operator. 

Since this can happen in a better-than-average plant, it is evident that it must be fairly common in establishments whose management is not highly safety minded. The majority of injury reports to compensation authorities are not based on adequate investigation as to cause; the descriptions are seldom adequate; reports are commonly made as a routine duty by clerical personnel without responsiblity for safety. 

As a result there is a very great factor of uncertainty in such data and since, as already pointed out, superficial investigation of accidents often misses correctible hazards, analysis based on compensation reports give too low a figure for mechanical sources. CHAPTER V Safety a Responsibility of Management 

Management's responsibility. What management must do. Control of physical conditions practical. Control of work practices. Safety and production. Fundamentals of top safety performance. Safety a continuing responsibility. 

1. If really good practice in the elimination of preventable accidents is to be reached and held in any establishment, top management must accept full and definite responsibility and must apply a good share of its attention to the task, just as it does to any other undertaking of vital importance. Every kind of work that men do involves some degree of hazard, and every uncontrolled hazard, if given time. enough, will produce its share of injuries. But proper attention to 

T safety will result in the elimination of almost all the injuries that would otherwise occur, regardless of the industry, the type of operation, or the occupation in question. In management is vested all authority, the determination of policies and executive direction; from management must come the drive for safety. Management must want to eliminate injuries badly enough to make accident prevention a vital part of all activities. Prevention must be given continuous attention along with such matters as cost, quality, and production. 1 2. Very briefly, the more important definite things that management must do to prevent accidents may be set down as follows: (a) Provide safe plant, equipment, and tools. (b) Safeguard all machinery. (c) Place no new machinery or equipment in operation unless full attention has been paid to its safety. (d) Plan and arrange all processes and operations with careful attention to safety. (e) Maintain a system of inspection to discover correctible hazards. (f) Maintain safety-minded supervision. (g) Train, educate, and stimulate its employees to follow safe methods of work and to take a sincere interest in the safety of themselves and their fellow workers. (h) Investigate each and every accident to determine how best to prevent a recurrence. (i) Make full and prompt report to the proper authorities of all cases of injury. 

3. The fact that physical conditions in the workplace are the responsibility of management is obvious. That it is always within the power of management to improve those conditions in a degree adequate to safety is not so obvious, but it is just as true. Accident prevention is good business, since the cost of accidents is always greater than the cost of prevention. Thus, the control of physical conditions to 27

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

insure safe performance is well within the power and the financial means of any firm that is able to continue in business at all. 

4. A moment's reflection will make it equally clear that management, which must provide whatever training and supervision may be necessary to insure a level of worker performance adequate to satisfactory production, must similarly accept responsibility for the control of work practices essential to safety. 

5. The relationship of safety to modern quantity production methods should be clearly understood. Quantity production rests upon careful planning for and accurate control of all operations. 

Worker injury occurs only as the result of faulty planning or faulty operation and therefore a high degree of safety is a byproduct of successful quantity production. Safety must be made an essential and an integral part of every operation and activity if truly successful quantity production is to be attained. The truth of this statement has been demonstrated again and again by comparing injury occurrence with the day-by-day curve of production. The tendency of the injuries to cluster about the interruptions to production is most marked. 

6. It might seem that this would not apply to employees not directly in production as, for instance, maintenance and repair forces, yard labor, warehousemen, etc. It does apply to them, however, provided their injury occurrence is compared with their production rate. For example, maintenance forces may, at the cost of a high accident rate to themselves, through working under high pressure on plant down-time, keep the machinery in fine operating condition, thereby enabling the production forces to turn in an efficient, injuryfree performance. But if the production of the maintenance force is segregated and analyzed, it will be found to have been retarded by each injury that was suffered. 

7. This relationship between orderly operation and injuries is covered in a widely accepted definition of accident which, while varying somewhat in wording, is essentially "an accident is any interruption to the orderly carrying on of the work." Under this definition, if a worker is injured there is an accidental injury. Only a small proportion of accidents yield human injury, but it is obvious that no injury will occur unless there is an accident. 

8. At this point, it is well to summarize much of the foregoing by listing the fundamentals necessary to top safety performance. They are: (a) Proper management attitude. Management must be intensely safety-minded. The prevention of injuries must continually be a part of the day-by-day thought and action of each member of the executive and supervisory forces. (b) Elimination of physical hazards. Their discovery and elimination to the maximum practicable degree must be accepted as fundamental to good safety practice. (c) Control of work habits and practices. This control is also fundamental. It must not, however, be considered a substitute for proper attention to physical hazards. (d) Organization for safety. Regardless of the form this organization may take, the purpose to be accomplished is the development and maintenance of effective teamwork for safety throughout the entire personnel, a joint effort to eliminate injuries.

29 9. Management responsibility can be fully expressed only through executive leadership. This leadership must be supplied by top management-the chief executive. Otherwise, safety will be regarded as secondary (or worse) to whatever matters the chief executive does concern himself with. Any person disposed to question this should ask himself what anyone in the employ of another pays most attention to-The thing or things his superior appears to want most from him. 

That is, he does if he values his job. So in any establishment, most attention is given to whatever top management, as expressed by the chief executive, appears to want most. There is no substitute for executive leadership. 

10. Almost numberless examples could be given to prove this if space permitted. It is sufficient, however, for our purpose to point out that without exception in the case of each of those firms that have established and maintain the accident records representative of best performance, the top management (the Chairman of the Board or President) exerts personal leadership and shows keen personal interest in safety. Conversely, when one finds indifferent or poor safety performance in an establishment, he will find if he can reach the top executive that the latter takes little interest in safety and, in fact, leaves "all that" to someone else. 

11. Many "salesmen of safety" in an effort to "make a sale" have promised that if a top operating executive would but give "just a little time" (a half hour or perhaps only 5 minutes) to start some set plan or program going and then spend another similar fraction of time per month to keep it going, the plan would do the rest. Actually any such approach is false. It sells safety too cheaply. It negatives the statement that safety is of major importance. Good safety performance is not to be had so easily. Executives expect to give considerable personal attention to each important activity under their charge. 

Asking for 5 minutes of an executive's time is merely asking for his autograph, not his executive functioning.

Injury Rates. 

1. An accident is an unexpected occurrence. When human injury is involved, we have an "accidental injury." The use of the term "injury" as meaning "accidental injury" was officially approved as

2. The old saying that "The proof of the pudding is in the eating" applies to plant safety. Each injury is in itself proof that some hazard or combination of hazards has not been adequately controlled. 

Therefore, the flow of injuries in any plant or operation constitutes the only final measure of safety in that plant or operation. A bad injury record is proof of unsafety. Perfection in safety could only be claimed for entirely injury-free operation. To use the injury record as a measure of safety performance, however, it is necessary to. know how often the injuries occur (that is, the rate at which they occur) and how serious they are. 

3. We must, of course, say exactly what injuries are to be used in measuring safety performance. Shall we include all those that require dressing or only those that cause temporary disability? The accepted practice (American Standard) is to include as a basis of measurement only those injuries which disable the injured worker for more than the day or shift during which he is injured. Anything less is not regarded as a lost-time injury and is not counted in figuring injury rates. When lesser injuries are included in measuring safety performance, the desire to make a good showing works against the effort to secure faithful reporting of minor injuries for treatment and infections result.

21. Bad housekeeping is a major factor in accident causation. 

Good housekeeping is a major factor in the efficient production of goods of high quality. The relationship is so intimate that it can safely be said that the standard of housekeeping a management maintains may be taken as an excellent gauge of its competency. 

22. 26. 28. Accident reports covering as long a period as possible should be studied in detail to discover at least: (a) The relative importance of the various injury sources. (b) The condition, processes or activities that produce injuries. (c) Extent of recurrence of each kind of injury or accident in each type of activity or operation. (d) Repeaters (individuals repeatedly injured). (e) Information leading to means of preventing recurrence. 

29. Since all information necessary to prevention could eventually be obtained from a complete account of the details leading up to the accident, each accident should be carefully investigated and the findings recorded in full. The appraiser of safety performance should study all available accident records to determine their value from this viewpoint.

CHAPTER VII Finding and Correcting the Hazards 

Importance of eliminating hazards. Means used in locating the hazards. 

Supplementing detailed checking to check major relationships and trendsclassification by sources, types and causes-computing injury ratescause analysis of accidents. Corrective action essential. 

1. As indicated in Chapter IV, multiple causative factors are involved in nearly every accident. Thorough investigation frequently reveals more than one hazardous condition; more than one behavior fault is commonly involved. Since accidents and the resultant injuries can and do come from every type of acitvity carried on in any plant or undertaking, complete elimination of injuries requires the finding and correction or adequate safeguarding of all conditions and practices that might yield accidents. We cannot expect to achieve perfection, but numerous establishments in nearly every important branch of industry have come so close to that goal that an average of only one employee (or even less) out of every three can expect to be the victim of a disabling work injury during a normal working lifetime in such establishments. 

2. The problem then is to find the hazards. Once these are recognized and appraised, suitable corrective action can be taken. Of course, the start should always be made with the planning and layout of the plant and the operations to be carried on in it. (See Ch. XI.) 

But with plant and process established, specific activities should be carried on to find the hazards. These are: 

(a) Plant Inspection (Ch. VIII). 

(b) Job Safety Analysis (Ch. IX). 

(c) Accident Investigation (Ch. X). 

3. Plant inspection is chiefly valuable in discovering hazardous conditions and arrangements, though experienced inspectors familiar with operating methods can also often discover practices that may lead to injuries. 

4. Job analysis is chiefly of value in determining safe work methods and in setting job standards in the interest of safety, but thorough job analysis will also bring to light many hazardous conditions not foreseen when the work was laid out. 

5. Accident investigation is in a sense a post mortem. Every accident constitutes proof that some hazardous condition or unsafe practice, or both, had gone uncorrected. We seek through planning to incorporate the maximum of safety in the new set-up. Through job safety analysis and plant inspection we seek to discover and appraise all factors that might lead to accidents and correct them or guard against them before the accident. But in spite of our best efforts some hazards go undetected; some unsafe practices are not caught or go unrecognized; at times we err in our appraisal. So we carefully investigate the accidents we fail to prevent in order to wring from them 37

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

every possible bit of information that will help us to improve our performance. 

6. These three activities-job analysis, inspection, and accident investigation-deal with hazards and accidents in detail; they do not show major relationships or indicate trends. So we classify the accidents to discover the relative importance of the various accident sources, injury types and causes (Ch. IV). We calculate the injury rates in order to measure our performance, make helpful comparisons and keep account of the trend (Ch. VI). At regular intervals we analyze and summarize the accident data (Ch. XXII) in order to get a definite picture of our safety performance so that we may know where and in what degree to press for improvement. Cause analysis (see 

American Standard Z16.2-Compiling Industrial Injury Causes) pro vides a simple method of determining basic causes and of recording the data in a manner to make it permanently usable for statistical purposes. 

7. All of these activities are of little value unless followed by effective corrective action. Therefore, it is necessary for the responsible executive to set up specific procedure designed to bring the proper corrective action in each instance and in every respect. In wellmanaged establishments, wherein spheres of responsibility are clearly assigned, activities well coordinated, supervision good, policies well defined, this is not difficult; the corrective measures are applied as a part of the day-by-day operating procedures. But if operations are not so well organized, securing adequate corrective action may prove the hardest part of the safety man's job, and it may be necessary to set up a specific form of safety organization designed to compensate for the lack of system in operative procedures. The inspector must, therefore, obtain and evaluate pertinent information, develop practicable recommendations and present them in such fashion that suitable action will result. 

An inspection that brings no action aimed at improvement is wasted. 

3. Since the inspector's time is limited, a complete appraisal of all phases of safety performance is usually out of the question. The inspector must apportion his time carefully and decide what things are vital to his purpose and how far to look into each. While details vary widely as among different industries and even among establishments in the same industry the fundamentals of safety apply to all. 

Some important factors are not subject to quick appraisal, but the competent inspector can quickly appraise the following fairly well: (a) The accident record. (b) Housekeeping. (c) Orderliness of operations. (d) Maintenance of machinery, equipment, and tools. (e) Machine guarding. (f) Condition of floors, stairs, walkways, etc. (g) Safe accessibility to all places where men must go. (h) Lighting, heating, ventilation. (i) Personal service conveniences. (j) Adequacy of personal protective equipment. 5. As regards item (k), the important thing is to make sure that the management is aware of the possibilities and has taken suitable precautions. In the case of pressure vessels, this means competent periodical inspection and tests. As a further example, work in tanks or bins to be safe must always take into account the specific conditions existing. The inspector should at least make sure that management is aware of such hazard and is using means of control in accord with best practice. 

6. The items listed above are general in nature. The following paragraphs give practical advice on specific points, drawn from widespread experience by competent inspectors of long service. 

7. Get all possible pertinent information about the plant before you call. Does it participate in organized safety activities? Does the management rate as able and successful, or the reverse? Is it a local business, or under distant control, etc.? 

8. Get acquainted with the executive in charge. Since safety is so vitally dependent upon management attitude, the most important person to know is the president of the company. If he is not located at the plant or visits it only rarely, then take the highest executive active in the plant's management. Let him know that your purpose is prevention and that you are there to work with him to help him prevent employee injuries and save him money. The length of the discussion and what to discuss will depend upon his reaction. To be competent, an inspector must not only know his subject, but he must be salesman enough to sell it to sometimes unwilling executives. 

9. Find out what the injury rates are. If the management does not calculate its rates, call for the accident folder and determine at least the frequency rate. Discuss the significance and value of injury rates and compare the rate found with rates representative of good practice. If the records are not adequate to permit this, make it clear that adequate accident records and their proper analysis are fundamental to good safety performance. 

10. Before going out into the plant make note of any accident cases that seem to justify further investigation in the plant. Clues of the greatest value are usually furnished by the accident reports. 

11. Find out what study is made of injury causes. Systematic and immediate investigation of each occurrence to determine its causes and to guide preventive action is so definitely routine procedure in all progressive establishments that its value is not open to question. 

12. Find out how carefully and competently such investigations are made. Usually this can be done without giving offense simply by asking for an explanation of specific cases at the places where they occurred. Ask to be shown just what occurred in each case. However, be very careful that in the process neither you nor the demonstrator gets hurt, as has been known to happen on such machines as saws and punch presses.

17. It is vitally important for an inspector to watch his own conduct, particularly if he is clothed with governmental authority. 

Under no condition is an inspector ever justified in taking the attitude that his knowledge is superior to that of the plant man. Nor is he ever justified in assuming that any management or representative of management is unwilling to obey the law or is indifferent to the safety of his employees. "Inhuman" employers or executives are rare. The overwhelming majority of injuries in industry are the result of a lack of safety-mindedness on the part of both management and men and in no way justify a condemnatory attitude on the part of the inspector. 

His job is to find out what the conditions are in each establishment for which he is responsible and secure improvement where improvement is needed. Since preventable injuries cannot be eliminated unless the active interest of the management is aroused, it is the inspector's job to arouse it. The official inspector is there to render a service to the workers, to their employer, and to the public. When he finds, as he will sometimes, an employer who persistently refuses to cooperate, it is time enough to resort to the use of force. Authority to compel action is a weapon which should be held in reserve until all other measures have failed. Competent and experienced official inspectors rarely have to use force, and when they do, they generally feel that such action is almost as much a reflection on them as it is on the employer. 

18. The inspection visit should always be concluded by a conference with the responsible executive, because effective action to improve conditions will depend almost wholly upon his acceptance of the findings. If such a conference cannot be held at the time, a definite appointment for a later date is the next alternative. If this is not possible, dependence must be placed upon a report. A very carefully prepared report should follow every such inspection and it is most satisfactory if made out following a discussion with the responsible executive, otherwise it is likely to be partly in error, place emphasis wrongly, run counter to managerial policy, or otherwise lack in effectiveness. 

19. Unsafe work practices can be discovered and satisfactorily corrected only by continuous watchfulness and painstaking training and education. The inspector should, of course, keep on the lookout for unsafe practices and discover what is being done to discover and eliminate them, but the usual plant inspection visit is far too limited to permit discovery of more than an occasional instance. Often, however, the action, or lack thereof, taken on such occasions will tell the experienced safety inspector much about the adequacy of plant safety leadership.

Continuing In-Plant Inspection. 

20. Everything in every establishment deteriorates through wear and tear of use and passage of time. Conditions change, processes are modified or replaced by others. All of these things bring or increase hazards. And even the most carefully laid out plant will have some unforeseen or overlooked hazards. Therefore, every

43 establishment should maintain a system of safety inspection suited to conditions. 

21. The detail involved in such an inspection procedure is considerable but the points requiring consideration in setting up an inspection system are quite simple, namely: (a) What is to be inspected? (b) How often is each thing, process, or area, to be inspected? (c) Who is to inspect? (d) Who is to supervise the inspection activities? (e) What reports and records will be needed? (f) What provision should be made for corrective action? 

22. Literally everything in or about a plant should be inspected in some degree and at some time. The usual procedure is to draw up a schedule. A safety device on whose unfailing functioning the workman's safety depends (a two-hand control on a press or a quick stop on a rubber mill) should be inspected and tested at least once each shift; the entire machine should be checked over perhaps weekly or monthly; completely overhauled annually as a rule. The findings of the safety inspector may be more important than production requirements in deciding when overhauling is advisable. 

23. The inspector must have a definite and thorough knowledge of the hazards that might be involved. A lively appreciation of the responsibility involved is also vital. Good judgment is necessary. In the case of highly specialized and inherently hazardous equipment, such as steam boilers or elevators, technical knowledge and skill of a high order are necessary. Insurance underwriters provide this type of service as do many of the State and city governments. 

24. The safety director will commonly have charge of safety inspection, or it may be under the master mechanic, the plant engineer or the production superintendent. The important point is that inspection must be under the direction of a responsible executive who will give it supervision adequate to keep it functioning effectively and not let it fall into a rut. 

25. Reports and records should be as simple as possible. The minimum in records necessary to enable the supervisor to keep account of the functioning of the inspection service will prove to be best. 

26. Provisions for corrective action will be governed chiefly by the organizational set-up. A master mechanic with broad authority to keep everything in good condition can take prompt and effective action on all findings. A safety director having inspection under his supervision may only be able to recommend action, although, if his standing with the supervisory staff is what it should be, this may be sufficient. Prompt and adequate corrective action is the goal to be secured. Whatever means will best insure that should be chosen. 

27. Definite and quite complete standards to guide the inspector should always be set up. In many cases, for example (abrasive wheels, welding sets), those furnished by the manufacturer will probably be adequate. Many standards must be set up for the plant, however. 

These should be representative of at least good practice as indicated by applicable American Standard Safety Codes, Safe Practices Pam phlets, or other authoritative sources.

CHAPTER IX Job Safety Analysis 

Job analysis—its development and extent. Job analysis and safetydiscovery of physical hazards and hazardous actions--determination of worker qualifications and proper equipment establishment of job safety standards. Planning of nonrepetitive jobs—maintenance, housekeeping, and incidental. 

1. Job analysis.-Its methods and technique have been developed in minute detail as an essential part of production control. It involves the accurate analysis and detailed description of each job in terms of duties, tools, methods, and working conditions, supplemented by a brief description of such personal requirements as age, sex, health, education, and specialized skills, if any. 

2. However, the detail of job analysis is of less interest to the safety engineer than whether or not analysis has been made, and if made, whether or not the improvements indicated by the analysis were carried out and the job filled by a worker carefully chosen to meet the indicated requirements. 

3. A high degree of safety is a byproduct of quantity production operation because of the thorough planning and control that is the basis of satisfactory quantity production. However, only a small part of industry is on a quantity production basis and in even the most modern of quantity production plants, there are many nonrepetitive jobs; for instance, maintenance and repair work. The extent to which it is practicable and desirable to routinize such work will vary with conditions. However, from the safety viewpoint, few plants have done it adequately. A well-known production engineer says that there are relatively few plants in which analysis and control of even the repetitive operations have been carried as far as would be profitable from the standpoint of efficiency alone, much less from the standpoint of safety. It is certainly true that in the bulk of nonmanufacturing industries relatively little has been done in the way of job analysis. 

4. The safety engineer need not undertake to master job analysis technique as the production engineer uses it. However, he should know what the procedure consists of and be able to analyze specific jobs or operations sufficiently to discover the hazards involved and suggest appropriate means of correction. 

5. The purposes of job analysis as the safety man views it may be set down in detail as: (a) Discovery of existent physical hazards. (b) Discovery and elimination or safeguarding of motions, positions, or actions that are hazardous. (c) Determination of the qualifications required for the safe performance of the work, such as physical fitness, motor skills, special abilities, etc. (d) Determination of equipment and tools needed for safety. 45

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

(e) Establishment of standards requisite for safety, including instruction and training of workmen involved. 

There is a common misconception that safety interferes with production, but the fact is that the demands of production need never preclude adequate safety measures. Greater safety usually means greater production largely because it involves better control. 

6. In the absence of job analysis, certain types of physical hazards are likely to be overlooked until an accident occurs. These include: (a) Position of fixed objects against which workmen may injure hands, feet, head, etc., in certain operations. Examples:

Sharp corners on machine frames, lack of adequate head room in setting up machines where close vision is required, fixed objects within area needed for convenient handling of articles to and from a machine or work bench. (b) Tools or equipment not fully adequate to or safest for the purpose. Examples: Chisel too long or too short, faulty tool design, need for more extensive use of jigs, more complete guarding of moving parts. (c) Faulty layout of "handling" operations likely to yield hand or foot injury or strain from lifting. 

7. Motions, positions, or actions likely to yield injuries would include: (a) Reaching over moving equipment or objects. (b) Reaching with hand, foot, or tool beyond range of clear vision. (c) Motion too rapid for conditions. (d) Off balance positions. (e) Incorrect posture in lifting or in handling a tool or heavy or awkwardly shaped objects. (f) Hazardous position of work or machine or of the workmen in relation to either or both. (g) Hazardous position of workmen in relation to one another. 

8. The determination and setting down of qualifications required for each job permit much better placement of workmen and reduce accidents from such causes as: (a) Strains from overlifting. (b) Hand injuries from slow reflexes or poor coordination or both. (c) Stature not suited to the work, poor hearing where audible signals are used, poor vision on close work or where visual signals are used. (d) Objectionable personality traits (in jobs where team spirit is important) that would interfere with smooth teamwork and result in accidents. (e) Fatigue from physical or mental strain. 

9. Desirable standards, in addition to specifying tools, equipment, order of operations, and qualifications of worker, should set forth the training that should be given to the worker new to the specific job. 

This training should be systematic and complete for all operations involved. It should be given also to old employees when they are

17. The cleaning work involved in good housekeeping is often a "stepchild" in the sense that it seldom receives as careful attention from management as production activities do. Upon investigation, the safety engineer often finds that the job of cleaning up and keeping clean is assigned to an otherwise not very useful employee who is given a "brush, a cart, and a pail" and set to work, and thereafter "goes his appointed rounds" with very little supervision. Actually, since the maintenance of a reasonably good standard of housekeeping necessitates the periodic cleaning by one means or another of every- S thing in the plant and getting into every nook and corner of the plant, the safety of men doing this type of work demands careful planning and analysis. Analysis of this type of work should particularly cover: (a) The completeness of safeguarding of gears, shafting, belts, and other moving parts under machine frames and in out-of-theway places. Injury records show clearly that such high-hazard devices as projecting set screws, gears, and shaft ends are never adequately guarded by position. (b) Protection against falls, particularly in case of window cleaning and other overhead cleaning. (c) Protection against the activities of others. This applies to clean-up work carried on in conjunction with repair jobs, especially during plant shut-downs. Usually in such case, two or more crews (under separate foremen) are operating, and, unless there is joint preplanning and close cooperation, unforeseen hazardous situations are likely to arise. 

18. Finally the occasional jobs, usually classed as construction, should not be allowed to escape careful planning and analysis. They have, in common with major construction work, the factor that makes the construction industry one of the three or four highest hazards of all major industries, namely, variety. No two jobs are ever exactly alike, and the range of hazards is very wide. Therefore, such work should be carefully planned in advance and the possible hazards should be carefully set down and considered; adequate methods of protection against them decided upon; adequate and safe equipment provided; careful and thorough instruction given; and close supervision maintained. 

19. In summation, job analysis as applied to safety may be defined as "planning, analyzing hazards, arranging operations, providing equipment, instructing, and supervising in a manner and to a degree necessary to maintain control adequate to safety throughout the job."

CHAPTER X Accident Investigation 

Purpose and procedure of accident investigation. Accident investigation and cause analysis distinction. Investigation, inspection, and analysis, plus corrective action-basic in accident prevention. Basic principles. 

Correlation of investigation and inspection. Inspection personnel. 

Conduct of investigation—promptness, types of accidents, completeness and thoroughness. Need for checking all hazardous conditions. Importance of corrective action. 

1. The purpose of accident investigation is to discover hazardous conditions and practices so that further accidents from similar causes can be prevented. This is accomplished through (a) detailed investigation of each individual accident to discover the causative factors involved, (b) analysis of the factors found, and (c) recommendation for corrective action based upon the investigation and analysis. 

Accident investigation should not be confused with cause analysis as applied in cause coding¹ whose purpose is to standardize accident cause data so that accumulated cause data may be more definitely useful in planning and guiding the preventive program. This chapter presents briefly the principles that should be applied in investigating industrial accidents. 

2. The idea of seeking to fix blame should definitely be avoided, for where it is allowed to enter, an attitude of "covering up" develops, which makes it difficult, if not impossible, to get all the facts. If every person involved-foremen, workmen, investigators-knows that the sole purpose is to prevent and not to blame, there will be no incentive to withhold essential information, and the investigator will have all the facts to aid in further preventive activities. 

3. If, in any given establishment, a policy of thoroughly investigating each accident is adopted, a fund of information on prevention will gradually be built up. If this practice is accompanied by systematic and thorough inspection to discover hazardous conditions, and if the findings of the accident investigations are consistently applied to the work of inspection, its effectiveness will continually increase. When investigations and inspections are adequately supplemented by corrective action, accidents steadily decline. This fact is so true that investigation, inspection, and analysis 2 leading consistently to corrective action, may properly be termed vital foundation stones of accident prevention. 

4. Accident investigation is a simple procedure, but certain principles should be followed if maximum results are to be secured: (a) Common sense and clear thinking are prime requisites.

An investigator must be able to collect his facts, weigh the value of each, and reach conclusions justified by the evidence. (b) Familiarity with the equipment, operation, or process should be at least sufficient to permit an understanding of the possible hazards in any given situation. (c) An understanding of the type of condition or situation likely to yield accidents is also necessary. (d) Neither the investigation nor the investigators should be under the control of the foreman or other supervisor involved, because few persons can be unbiased and objective about a situation or condition involving their own work. The attitude should be one of cooperating with the foreman to discover and secure the correction of the causative factors. (e) Each clue should be investigated fully. A conclusion that appears reasonable will often be changed by exploring a factor of apparently little importance. (f) Since both a physical hazard and an unsafe act are present in the great majority of accidents, both should be investigated fully. Every effort should be made to find means of eliminating the physical hazard. Similarly, appropriate means of correcting the unsafe practice should be sought. (g) No investigation is ever satisfactorily completed unless definite recommendation is made for corrective action. (h) More than one person should investigate, on the principle that "two heads are better than one." (i) Promptness is essential. Conditions may change quickly and details are soon forgotten. Finally, and vitally important, is the fact that the immediateness of the investigation will be taken by the workmen as indicative of the importance the management attaches to the matter of their safety. (j) Every accident should investigated in appropriate degree whether or not it is serious, since "chance" is often the sole difference between a trivial accident and serious or even fatal injury. 

Who Should Make Investigations? 

5. In general, it makes little difference who investigates accidents, if the preceding principles are followed insofar as they are applicable in each case. Practice varies considerably as follows: (a) Safety engineer.-Obviously limited to the relatively few establishments having full-time safety personnel. Unless supplemented by other inspections, this investigation has the drawback indicated by items (b) and (h) of paragraph 4. (b) Main safety committee. This practice has the advantage of competency, but the very serious disadvantage that the members of such a committee are busy men, and unless the chief executive is insistent, they will rarely feel that they can spend the time necessary for thoroughness. They should, however, maintain close touch with the work of accident investigation and themselves investigate particularly important cases as well as those in which there is any question as to the findings. (c) Foreman.-While the foreman involved should personally investigate every accident to workmen under him in order to improve his own supervisory performance as well as to aid in pre-

(e) Special standing committee. In some plants a standing committee is set up for accident investigation only, made up of members chosen for this specific purpose. (f) Combination of the above.-A typical instance that is yielding excellent results comprises joint investigation by the safety engineer, the foreman involved, and a three-man committee of workmen, with a separate written report from each of these investigative agencies. The safety engineer has the further duty of including in his report a discussion of any differences in findings, together with his conclusion and recommendations. 

6. The work of accident investigation and that of inspection should be closely correlated. However, the extent to which it is practicable to have the two activities carried on by the same personnel will vary with conditions. Inspection should be a continuing activity scheduled to cover plant and equipment as frequently as may be necessary to insure the prompt discovery of unsafe conditions; therefore, it is usually assigned as a full or part-time duty of persons carefully selected and trained for the purpose. It may be under the supervision of the master mechanic, the safety engineer, if there is one, the chairman of the main safety committee, or the chief plant executive. A full-time inspector may be, and often is, a member of an accident investigation committee.

8. Theoretically, every accident and near accident should be investigated thoroughly because each of these, had circumstances been slightly different, might have yielded a serious injury. However, the number of minor injury cases and hazardous occurrences is so great that thorough investigation of all would be too expensive and time-consuming. Therefore, the practical procedure is: (a) Immediate full investigation of all lost-time cases, and of such minor injuries as appear to involve serious hazards. (b) As thorough investigation of minor injury cases and nearinjury occurrences as conditions justify by the safety engineer or the standing accident investigation committee (item e, par. 5). (c) Maintain a continuing analysis of operations and jobs to discover and permit the correction of hazardous practices and conditions. (This is properly a part of job safety analysis, but its connection with accident investigation is so intimate that it should be mentioned here.) (d) Investigation by each foreman of every accident and all near accidents that he can discover. He should not only incorporate in his own procedure the lessons learned but should pass them on (preferably by written record) for general use. 

9. Every person who might have information bearing on the accident being investigated should be interviewed and a full record made. Signed statements are rarely advisable, however, since many workmen are suspicious of any attempt to have them sign anything, associating signed statements with the idea of blame and punishment. 

Sufficient accuracy usually can be obtained by a careful investigator without resort to signed statements. 

10. Thoroughness should characterize the work of investigating accidents. There is a constant temptation to carry the investigation only so far as is necessary to discover one means of prevention. But in the majority of accidents more than one hazardous condition or practice is involved. For example, a man feeding metal strip to a punch press through a limited opening was struck on the heel by a small casting that fell from a passing truck. He threw his hand up and it was caught and crushed between the guide and the ram. The investigators concerned themselves with the reason for the incorrect loading of the truck and recommended suitable action. However, a later checkup investigation by others (even though several days had elapsed) disclosed the following additional conditions. Additional corrective action was taken as described: (a) The truck was the four-wheel box type being pushed, though the box was so high that the man pushing it could not see over it.

Corrective action. A pull-type of truck was substituted. (b) There was a hole in the aisle flooring which had caused the truck to jounce.

Corrective action.-The floor was repaired and a check-up

11. Every accident investigator should constantly guard against the fact that the error of behavior (the human fault) is usually made glaringly evident by the accident, as in the above example, while the accompanying physical hazards may be hard to find. In each instance, careful search should be made for every hazardous condition that can be corrected in whole or in part. For example, a mechanic was sharpening a broadbitted tool on the side of an abrasive wheel. It caught and pulled his hand against the wheel, which ground the flesh and some of the bone off the knuckles of two fingers. Investigation disclosed the fact that he violated a rule prohibiting side grinding and also failed to use the tool rest. The findings covered only the matter of securing better observance of the safety rules. The general superintendent was not satisfied and, with the master mechanic, made a check-up. He found that the tool was so much wider than the face of the wheel that it was very difficult to sharpen it properly on the face. 

The grinder was of the double arbor type, so the remedy was to substitute for one wheel a type of wheel having a broader grinding face. 

Also, wider flanges were installed to lessen the temptation to do side grinding. 

12. Anything in the nature of browbeating of witnesses should be avoided. Even though sometimes effective in securing admission that completes the evidence, it will quickly defeat its purpose by making workmen unwilling to tell freely what they know." 

13. No pertinent information should be withheld or covered up.

An attitude of discovering and making use of the facts for the sole purpose of prevention is vital and should be maintained at all times. 

Corrective Action. 

14. Since the sole purpose of accident investigation is to guide preventive action, action must be prompt and thorough; otherwise it will be largely wasted. Certain things are vital, namely: (a) Prompt consideration of every recommendation and prompt compliance with those accepted. (b) Full explanation of the reasons for the rejection of recommendations not followed. (c) Full explanation of any necessary delays in corrective action. (d) Whenever a previously uncaught correctible physical hazard is found, the entire plant should be inspected for similar hazards. (e) Each discovery of a hazardous practice or procedure should constitute a warning that a similar practice may be in use by others.

Therefore, in every establishment there should be a definite, systematic means of transmitting every safety lesson to all to whom it can possibly be of benefit. The search for safety information and ideas helpful to safety should include all possible outside sources, such as contacts with State, Federal, and insurance inspectors, visitors from other firms, engineering consultants, sales engineers, attendance at safety meetings, etc. Finally, all of this accumulated knowledge should be applied to the maximum in such activities as purchasing, planning new plant or equipment or processes, employee training, and day-by-day operating processes.

Planning and Control. 

1. The accurate control of every element of production is basic in mass production technique. This involves the planning of every detail, layout, arrangement, and installation to accord with the plan; job standards accurately defining the skills and other worker qualifications required; training to develop, improve, or refine those skills; supervision adequate to direct, guide, and instruct the workmen; and maintenance adequate to keep all equipment functioning efficiently. 

Such procedure eliminates most hazards and hazardous practices; perfect planning and control would in theory eliminate all accidents; many firms have come very close to this goal; all could approach it and undoubtedly would if the value of so doing could be made clear to them. 

2. In spite of the remarkable advances and tremendous achievements of American industry, only a relatively small proportion of our industrial undertakings have had the benefit of good planning; few have control adequate for top safety performance. However, in even the worst of these, good safety performance can be attained by persistently carrying on a program of improvement based on realistic appraisal of conditions. Occasionally, a major reconstruction and rearrangement is practicable, but usually financial considerations limit expenditures to relatively modest sums month by month and year by year. This emphasizes the importance of carefully planning the safety program to make the most out of little. 

3. Easily detected conditions indicating lack of planning and systematic procedure are: (a) Unduly large amounts of materials "in process." (b) A glut of materials at certain points, a lack at others. (c) Crossing or intermingling of paths taken by materials "in process. 

(d) Traffic jams. (e) Inadequate working space about machines. (f) Inadequate storage spaces for materials. (g) Badly placed key locations such as toolroom windows, washrooms, control stations, etc. (h) Undue accumulations of waste and scrap. (i) Processes not in orderly sequence. 57

4. The safety engineer can help by suggesting specific improvements based on careful analysis of conditions. As safety technician, his recommendations must be in accord with sound operating procedures. Impractical suggestions will quickly discredit him. He should be vigorous in his advocacy of improvements of whose value and practicability he is sure, but he should be very careful not to go beyond his knowledge of sound production techniques. Every safety engineer should study at least one good text on plant and process layout in order to have some understanding of the principles involved and the techniques commonly applied. 

5. While the layout and arrangement of a plant, or other industrial undertaking involving plant and equipment, is a job for engineers having special skill in that field, the fundamental principles that should be carried out in providing for the maximum in worker safety are easily understood. Every industrial safety engineer should keep these principles clearly in mind for two important reasons, namely: (a) Sooner or later he will almost certainly have a voice in laying out a new undertaking or in making changes in an existing installation. (b) He will be better able to detect existing hazards before they yield disabling injuries. 

6. There is much need for more attention to worker safety in designing plant and equipment, in laying out the process, and in arranging equipment. The engineering profession as a whole is not as yet giving adequate attention to worker safety. Engineering colleges are barely beginning to show an interest in including this subject in their regular curriculums, the extent to which engineers specializing in the field of design and layout include safety in their planning, has been determined by the demands of their clients. 

Fortunately, the larger proportion of large new plants are for safetyminded firms who demand safety in the planning. Also, the fact, already noted, that a good degree of safety is a byproduct of quantity production also applies to the planning of the plant and equip- But it applies with less force to the relatively standardized design of plant building offered by firms specializing in preplanned industrial building. The chief fault is not in the building plan itself as much as in the fact that the whole project is seldom planned through sufficiently in advance. Instead the main features of building and arrangement are decided upon, the buildings ordered and further details fitted in with many compromises. Safety frequently suffers in the process.

Plant Layout and Process Arrangement. 

7. Plant layout, when applied to the preplanning of an entire plant, deals with such major relationships as size, shape and type of buildings, positions of buildings, arrangements for flow of materials to, from, and throughout the plant, provision for needed services, etc. Carried to its ultimate conclusion, layout planning should include, in quite fine detail, the placing of all fixed equipment and provisions for the flow of materials within the plant. Layout planning merges imperceptibly into process arrangement. 

8. Process arrangement, as the term implies, deals with the layout of each process and the interrelationships among the various processes

The relationships between plant layout and process arrangement are so intimate that in discussing the health and accident hazards involved, it is best to deal with them together. 

9. The details to be considered by the planning engineer or architect, or both, as the case may be, in providing for the maximum in worker safety are many; in fact, in a large plant making many products, or a single complicated product, they may seem to be almost endless. However, the types of hazard are few and the planner who is thoroughly familiar with conditions out of which accidents come, can by faithful adherence to a few basic principles produce a plan having a high degree of built-in safety. These principles may be set down as: (a) Adequate space. (b) Safe access. 

(c) Safe maintenance. 

(d) Adequate air and light. (e) Adequate services. (f) Provision for expansion. 

10. Congestion is a prolific accident producer. So is lack of head room. The planner cannot prevent the misuse of space provided, but he can and should provide space adequate to the full needs of equipment and operations planned for. Commonly found faults are: (a) Inadequate working space about machines or equipment: In practice the spaces necessary can be determined by laying out on the drawing board the necessary radius of action about each machine or piece of equipment and spacing them so that these areas do not overlap with one another or with areas required for storage, traffic services, etc. (b) Inadequate head room, particularly over moving equipment, such as electric overhead traveling cranes, and tall apparatus requiring servicing from the top, such as many types of stills, ovens, steam boilers, etc. Clearances should be such that no person riding on or working on top of such equipment can get caught against or strike his head against building members. The toll of worker fatalities from this source is a grievously long one. 

The cost of a single fatality would usually add many feet to the height of the average industrial building if included in the plan. (c) Inadequate space for storage. Since executives are likely to think of storage space as nonproductive space, they tend to try to hold it to a minimum. As a matter of fact, the cost of handling into and from inadequate storage spaces is in general so high that through a reasonable period of years, the first cost of fully adequate storage space is usually a highly profitable investment. Inadequate storage space yields bad housekeeping, increases the difficulties of handling materials and articles, contributes to the fire hazard and increases accident hazards. 

Provision for adequate storage space should include: (1) Incoming materials. Allowance should be made not only for maximum production requirements but also for advance buying to take advantage of favorable price conditions or to guard against anticipated shortages. (2) Materials in process. Failure to provide adequately for intermediate storage is a serious fault in many lines of manufacturing and in numerous plants in all lines.

Machinery Layout. 

17. Much of the foregoing material applies to the arrangement of machinery and fixed equipment. However, certain additional points of importance to safety should be added, namely: (a) Each machine should be so placed or so served that the traffic (both of men and materials) to and from it fits smoothly into, rather than interferes with, the general scheme of traffic. 

Example: Woodworking machine served by four-wheeled push trucks. Loads to and from the machine should not cross other lines of traffic. (b) Machine position should always be that affording maximum safety to those operating or working the machine. (c) Maintenance problems should be foreseen and planned for. 

This should include: (1) Oiling and cleaning. (2) Changing machine parts. (3) Adjustment or repair of drive as motor, belt, clutch, gears. (4) Major overhauls.

The injury rates of the above activities are generally high. A heavy proportion of the injuries reported are in most plants chargeable in whole or in part to inaccessibility, inadequate clearance, and failure to provide for the safe use of equipment and tackle necessary in major overhauls. (d) Hazards involved in handling the materials to, at, and from each machine require careful consideration. Such matters as weight, shape, size, and character of articles or material in question are taken into account but often inadequate consideration is given to worker safety in their handling. (e) Lighting requirements should be foreseen and provided for to assure: (1) Light of adequate intensity. 

(2) Freedom from glare. 

(3) Freedom from shadows. 

(f) In the case of machinery or equipment that produces fumes, smoke, dust, vapors, or heat that may be hazardous or create serious discomfort, measures of control suited to the conditions should be included. Added as an afterthought the means of control of these hazards are likely to be unduly expensive.

Importance of good housekeeping. Housekeeping "cleanliness and order." Need for careful planning and continuous follow-up. Housekeeping and fire prevention. Primary responsibility of management. 

Details of good houskeeping handling, storage and placement, piling, tools, waste disposal, aisles and storage space, provision for leaks, drippage and spillage, layout of machinery and equipment. Hazard of projecting nails. Need for housekeeping under peak production demands. 

Need for over-all cooperation. Clean-up campaigns. Housekeeping committee and inspection. Over-all housekeeping control. Cleaning methods. 

1. Housekeeping is one of the most important factors in accident prevention. Men trip over loose objects on floors, stairs, and platforms; they are hit by articles falling from overhead; they slip on greasy, wet, or dirty floors; they run against projecting poorly piled or placed materials; improperly piled or supported materials fall on them; they step on or tear their hands on projecting nails. 

2. Housekeeping is more than cleanliness. It is cleanliness and order. A place is in order when there are no unnecessary things about and those that are necessary are in their proper places. If it is clean also, floors nonslippery from grease, etc., light bulbs and windows clean, walls, etc., kept well painted, machinery and equipment kept neat and in order, and if aisles are properly marked and operations are carried on in an orderly manner, we have good housekeeping and good order. Incidentally, where we find these conditions, we find competent management and a low accident rate as well, for good housekeeping and orderliness are fundamentals of good management. 

3. Good housekeeping cannot be attained by an occasional grand clean-up and setting-in-order. It must be planned for and continuously pushed. When one undertakes to secure improvement in housekeeping, the first thing to obtain is a thorough clean-up; but unless the management accepts the idea of getting at least a reasonable degree of order and system into all operations, conditions will soon again be as bad as before. Lasting improvement will result only if, in addition to a clean-up, management will adopt a policy of searching out and correcting the conditions that produce the dirt and disorder, and will undertake to plan all operations systematically. 

4. Bad housekeeping is a major cause of fires, for a very large proportion of plant fires start in rubbish or in oil-soaked clothes, or are made more serious by the presence of unnecessary flammable or readily combustible materials on which to feed. 

5. While every employee should wish to do his part toward keeping his workplace clean and orderly, it is primarily a duty of management. For the management must so plan that all operations will be orderly; provide storage places for materials, racks or holders for tools and portable equipment or containers for materials in process; and in 573065-44- -5 63

general see that each day houskeeping and order are maintained as fundamentals of all that is done. Finally, management must train each employee to do his part, for a single bad housekeeper in an establishment not only leaves much disorder in his path, but also sets an example disruptive of the morale of all. 

6. The more important details of good housekeeping and order are: (a) Handling, storage, and placing of materials, articles, and supplies. Satisfactory control requires careful consideration of all factors of tonnage and volume of materials to be handled, the amounts required at the successive steps of the process, allowance of the spaces necessary, and the methods to be used in handling and transporting the materials and articles. (b) Methods of piling materials and articles.--These must be worked out for each class of material or article. Important points are: (1) Height of pile.-Dependent upon character of material and consequent danger of toppling, the means used in piling and removal, the traffic nearby and interference with sprinkler operation. (2) Strength of support.-Allowable floor loads should be posted. (3) Evenness of support and its continued stability.—These factors apply particularly to yard piling where uneven ground, nonhomogeneous ground or moisture may cause toppling, and on first floors or in basements where floors may slope or flooring sag. 

(4) Location. Aisle traffic or the presence of work benches or machines may make it necessary to limit the height or character of materials to be piled in otherwise desirable space. (5) Piling small articles.-Containers suited to the nature of the articles are commonly used. These usually take the form of wheeled or truck pick-up containers that receive the articles from work bench or machine. A useful modification for such articles as small cans are trays that themselves may be stacked. (6) Pipe or other long stock.-Suitable racks aid handling; projecting ends should be protected by location, railings, or barriers. (7) Mechanical aids.-The use of "strong-arm" methods instead of some form of stacker, piler, hoist, or carrier is a fault commonly found. Progressive management is continuously seeking better methods of piling and incidental handling. (c) Tool housekeeping.-Racks and holders suited to the size and shape of the articles, should be provided for all hand tools, jigs, and such machine fixtures as chucks, cutters, blades, etc.

Frequency of use, value of article, possibility of damage, and the system of maintenance used will determine the location of such racks. Obviously, tools frequently used at a bench or machine should be provided with racks or holders on the bench or at the machine. Wheeled racks holding a set of the tools and parts needed at a given machine or station are often very helpful.

A common mistake of management is to add machines or other production units to already well filled floor space thereby not only increasing the accident hazard but often decreasing the efficiency of the whole room due to overcrowding. 

7. Projecting nails present a serious hazard. Nails left projecting when kegs, barrels, or boxes are opened constitute definite invitations to injury. So do nails in planks or boards. Nail punctures are particularly likely to become infected, often by tetanus (lockjaw) germs. 

When kegs or barrels are opened, the nails should either be drawn or bent over and the points hammered into the wood with hammer and metal block. They should be drawn from boxes or driven home. 

Nails should be drawn from boards and planks, for if the lumber is ever used again they will have to be pulled out. Nails left in planks often ruin saws and may cause serious accidents. Even if boards removed from boxes, crates, forms, etc., are to be burned, it is better to draw the nails in order to avoid nail wounds in the handling. 

8. When demand for production increases, there is a constant temptation to relax such housekeeping rules as those covering the piling of materials and articles. Sometimes there may be no alternative, but usually other means of relief can be found. If the management authorizes the relaxing of any such rules, the return to the proper condition should be required at the earliest possible moment. 

Regardless of production necessities, material should not be piled too high or in such a manner as to constitute a hazard. If the operator of a machine requires a definite amount of space for safety in ordinary times, he needs it even more when under pressure of demand for maximum production. 9. The interested cooperation of the entire working force is necessary in housekeeping as in every other phase of safety work. Management must first set things in order generally and provide whatever equipment or other specific aids may be needed. Many firms use special clean-up squads that go from department to department in orderly routine. Specific duties (window washing, emptying waste cans, sweeping, etc.) are preferably assigned to certain men. This is in the interest of both safety and efficiency as the safest and best methods can thus be more effectively worked out and practiced.

Employee interest and cooperation may be stimulated by such announcements as: "Clean-up week begins Monday, August 22. Everybody get ready for the grand clearance. Old clothes, greasy rags, waste, and junk must go.'

13. All sweeping should be either by vacuum or with the use of dust inhibiting sweeping compounds. In some cases, wet methods are applicable. Where toxic substances are involved, vacuum sweeping should be used. If this is not practicable, special precautions suited to the nature of the toxic substance in question and the other conditions involved should be taken to safeguard all workers who might be exposed.

CHAPTER XIII Maintenance and Safety Maintenance for safety vs. maintenance solely for production. Poor maintenance as an injury cause. Appraising maintenance. Need for safety in maintenance operations. 

1. Machinery and equipment must be maintained in good condition to obtain satisfactory operating results. In even relatively wellmanaged establishments, however, there is very often a failure to maintain adequately those parts of equipment and plant not vital to production. This includes both portable and fixed equipment, buildings, safety devices, etc. When this condition is found, management must be sold on the importance of maintenance for safety as well as production. 

2. Instances in which poor maintenance may lead to accidental injury are numerous. The following will be suggestive: (a) Floor maintenance. Roughness, slipperiness from wear, holes, splinters, and poor patching contribute heavily to the two sources of injuries that are commonly most important numerically, namely, "handling" and "slipping, tripping, and falling. Also such defects often contribute to "machinery" injuries. 

(b) The condition of all portable equipment on which men climb or stand or from which they work is important from a safety standpoint. This includes portable ladders, steps, horses, scaffold planks, etc. Unless the management is actively interested in safety, such equipment may not be well maintained. (c) Tools defective through use are prolific sources of injury.

This applies not only to the familiar hand tools as chisels, wrenches, etc., but also, because of their increased use, to powered hand tools such as grinders, drills, etc. (d) Unless properly maintained, machine guards and safety devices not only fail to protect, but, through giving a false sense of security, may be worse than no protection at all. (e) A higher standard of maintenance than is necessary for production may be vital to safety. For instance, clutch or shifter wear may cause unexpected starting of a machine. (f) Electric wiring becomes unsafe not only through use but from temporary repairs, alterations or additions. "Temporary” jobs tend to become permanent unless carefully limited to necessities and immediately made standard when the emergency has passed. (g) A high standard of maintenance is necessary to the safety of such equipment as elevators, cranes, slings, chains, and tackle, pressure vessels, extension cords, and personal protective equipment. For such equipment, a definite schedule of inspection should be worked out and rigidly adhered to. 67

3. A complete study of maintenance requires much time and a detailed knowledge of safety. However, the experienced inspector can easily learn enough about the standard of maintenance in a plant during the course of an ordinary "over-all" inspection to appraise it reasonably well. Significant details easily observed are: (a) Hand tools. Note the conditions of hand tools of all kinds on benches, at machines and in use. Find out whether systematic means of tool maintenance is in use. Note whether defective tools are scattered around or promptly removed for repair or scrapping. (See Ch. XV.) (b) Observe electric wiring, control switches and boards, and particularly extension cords. Watch for "temporary" wiring and find out how long it has been there. Not only is it important that control apparatus and electrical equipment be kept in good condition, but it must also be designed for safety and well constructed. (For typical defects see Ch. XVI.) (c) Listen to the sounds of operating machinery. Well designed, properly maintained machines have certain characteristic operating sounds which are readily recognized. Other sounds, such as squeaks in shafting, rattles in power presses, and chatter in woodworking machines, are indicative of maintenance faults. (d) Determine whether or not there is any definite, systematic plan of inspection of such equipment as slings, chains, elevators, cranes, pressure vessels, machine guards, etc. Sometimes such systems are installed but not kept up. The knowledge of the person in charge of such inpsection concerning the date of last inspection and of defects found will be indicative. Questions pointed to some specific defect, such as "When was that crane recabled last?" help reveal the situation. (e) Notice the housekeeping and orderliness in the shops or other sections of the maintenance department. Bad housekeeping and disorder seldom accompany good maintenance.

Safety in Maintenance Work. 

4. Repair and maintenance work, particularly in highly mechanized industries, industries using much heavy machinery, and chemical industries, usually carries the greatest inherent hazard and the widest variety of hazards of any work in the establishment. For instance, a large corporation manufacturing a wide variety of machinery and equipment reports as follows for a typical year: "Production workers (average number 27,000)-Frequency 5.0. Maintenance men (average number 2,300)-Frequency 22.2. The frequency rates of the production workers in the various plants and important producing departments ranged from 0 to 18.1, while in the same plants and departments, the frequency rates for maintenance men ranged from 5.5. to 77.5."

Control in maintenance work lies in: 

(a) Careful, thorough planning of each specific job and the methods of handling the work. (b) Adequate equipment suited to the kind of work or type of job.

69 (c) Careful maintenance of all equipment. (d) Foresight in anticipating hazards that may be encountered and in devising means of control. (e) Supervision suited to the hazards of the work and the safety mindedness of the workers. (f) Selection of men with the judgment, mental alertness, and physical qualifications necessary for the work. (g) Training suited to the capabilities and experience of the man (personalized training). (h) Careful attention to the adequacy and maintenance of personal protective equipment and its proper use. 

5. These items are never adequately cared for unless the supervisor of maintenance is interested in safety, and is given the necessary authority and financial support to meet the needs of the work. Managements that fail to realize the importance of good maintenance may regard maintenance costs as an unproductive expense to be held to the minimum. It is noteworthy that, without exception, painstaking attention to maintenance is found in establishments whose accident record is first class.

Common hazardous operations and preventive measures. Safety and efficiency. Symptoms of bad handling methods. Importance in small plant. 

1. In most industries the handling of materials, articles, and equipment is the chief source of injuries. Every type of material and every article handled in or about the plant must be studied, and consideration given to tonnages involved, character of material or article, unloading from car or truck, storing, means of handling in process, preparing for shipment, shipping. The handling of incidental supplies and equipment should also be looked into, for at times this work will involve considerable hazard. 

2. The methods of preventing handling injuries may be classified as: (a) Planning and arranging operations and methods to eliminate hazardous situations and insure adequate control at all times. 

(b) Substitution of mechanical handling for manual handling to the maximum practicable degree. (c) Careful placing and training of personnel. (d) Adequate and competent supervision. (e) Provision of suitable protective equipment. 

3. Handling costs are an important factor in nearly all types of industrial undertakings. The details involved vary endlessly. The analysis and adequate solution of problems involved require a high degree of professional competence. As is the case in the field of quantity production as a whole, a good degree of safety is obtained as a byproduct of low-cost operation. It is the poorly planned or unplanned and the poorly controlled handling operations that yield high injury rates. 

4. Handling apparatus has become highly diversified and its development still continues. Much safety is built into such equipment though the safety man must always examine even equipment of the newest design for overlooked hazard points and the need of guards. 

Comments in Chapter 18 on the need of better attention to foto in designing and building machinery is often applicable to handling equipment. 

5. There are few activities in which physical adequacy and the faithful use of safe methods of working are more important than in the manual handling of materials. Strains, bruises, lacerations, and crushed hands or feet are common if the workmen are in xperienced or untrained. Experienced and properly trained workmen can avoid these injuries.¹

2. Hand-tool injuries are relatively numerous in practically all branches of industry. They result chiefly from the use of tools that are defective or not suited to the immediate purpose and from unsafe methods of use. Methods of correction are simple and can be carried out by any foreman or other supervisor. Tool defects are common.

Probably the chief reason for this is that they seem of little consequence to persons who are not safety-minded. Their real importance. is shown by the fact that safety-minded employers give hand tool safety careful and continuous attention. Experience proves that hand tool injuries, like all others, can practically be eliminated. The faults. that produce most tool injuries are so dependent upon the attitude of management and so identified with the working habits of the men that halfway measures will not be successful. All tools must be made safe and maintained in this condition, and proper methods of use must be followed.

But it can all be done with the minimum of human injury, and since the methods that eliminate accidents are always in the interest of production, quality, and economy, it is as much the supervisor's duty to see that men work safely as it is to see that they work effectively. CHAPTER XVI Low Voltage Electrical Hazards 

Hazards of ordinary shop lighting and power circuits.

Application of 

National Electrical Safety Code. Commonly found hazards and cor rective action or safe condition. 

1. This discussion deals with the voltages ordinarily used in shop fighting and power circuits. It is not intended to apply to the voltages commonly used in power transmission or to such special applications of electricity as sign lighting, radio transmission, the use of induction coils, or to static electricity. 

2. A person receives an electric shock whenever any part of his body becomes part of an electric circuit. In such case, he is subject to injury in two ways-from nerve shock which, if great enough, will cause the stoppage of lungs or heart or both, and from the heating effect of the current which may cause severe burns chiefly where the current enters or leaves the body. For any given voltage the injury suffered depends upon the amount of current, the course which it takes through the body and the length of time the victim is in the circuit. The amount of current flow will depend upon the voltage of the circuit contacted and the resistance of the circuit of which the victim's body is a part. The amount of current carried by the conductor one comes in contact with (the current carrying capacity of the circuit) is of no importance whatever, provided it carries enough current to injure. Since one-tenth of an ampere or less may kill, all ordinary circuits have sufficient capacity to kill. For example, the inherent hazard of a 10,000-ampere circuit is no greater than that of an ordinary lighting circuit if the voltages are identical. 

3. The average reasonably dry, clean, nonmetallic floor has enough resistance so that a person standing on it is not likely to receive a severe shock fr om a circuit of 220 volts or less. But one can never tell whether or not a floor is sufficiently clean or dry and free from nails or other conducting material. If the floor is wet, if one's clothing is wet, if one is sweaty and in a tank or on a metal floor, the resistance of the path through the body is likely to be as low as 250 ohms which might allow a fatal shock to be received from a circuit of as low as 30 volts. Also, he may get "across the line." Wellauthenticated instances of deaths from circuits of 50 volts or less are on record. 

4. The fact that electric shock of any intensity instantly produces involuntary muscular contraction is of vital importance. This muscular contraction may break the contact, thus freeing the victim, but more often it causes him to grasp the conductor tightly and unless rescued, he will lose consciousness and slowly die. Involuntary muscular contraction of hand and fingers has been produced_experimentally by a current of only one-thousandth ampere. Certainly then, any voltage above 25 volts must be considered hazardous under conditions favorable to low resistance contact. 77

1. Slipping, tripping, and falling constitute major sources of injury throughout industry. Falls are commonly classified as "on the same level" or "to a lower level." 4. Therefore, the safeguarding of mechanical apparatus to the greatest degree possible is of first importance. No experienced safety engineers will disagree on this point. However, the salesman of safety will find it necessary to prove this point repeatedly, for managements and executives who are not actively safety-minded cannot be expected to have such facts. They are more likely to be guided by the often repeated statement (correct and valuable as it may be when properly used) that in general not over 15 percent of injuries are attributable to mechanical causes. Unfortunately, this statement is often used as an excuse for not providing necessary safeguards. 

5. The safety man charged with the double duty of seeing that the workplace is made reasonably safe, and, what is more important, of "carrying the gospel of safety" to not yet safety-minded executives, must not only present the true picture but also give convincing reasons why first attention should be given to physical hazards. Such reasons include: (a) Whenever a hazard can be reduced or eliminated by means such as better design, mechanical safeguards or better construction, the gain is positive. Human habits and practices in the interest of safety are difficult to establish and maintain, but mechanical gains are permanent.

(b) As shown in tables 2 and 4, machine injuries are much more likely to involve amputation or other permanent disabilities than those from other sources. (c) Workers know that unguarded machinery is hazardous. 

They also know that guards cost money. An employer who fails to spend any reasonable amount to remove a visible hazard cannot expect his people to believe him when he says that he really wants to keep them from getting hurt. If they do not believe in his sincerity, they will not give him the cooperation that is absolutely necessary in order to reach and maintain really good safety performance. 

6. Machinery safety can logically be divided into two parts-the guarding of transmission machinery and the guarding of the point of operation. By transmission machinery is meant all moving parts that by their motion carry power from the prime mover to the machine using it. Point of operation is the point or area in which material is shaped, formed, cut, abraded, or otherwise worked on by the machine.

Transmission Machinery Guarding. 

7. The safety code approved by the American Standards Association, "Mechanical Power Transmission (B-15)" has received national acceptance. Most State codes follow it. This code covers the general details of such guards effectively. However, a few general principles in applying transmission guards deserve emphasis: (a) Guards should be substantial and firmly installed. (b) The most satisfactory material for all ordinary conditions is metal. Perforated or expanded metal or screen reinforced with angles, or pipe is usual practice. Wooden guards lack strength, become rickety, require frequent replacement, and contribute to fire hazard. In special cases the presence of corrosive fumes may make them seem advantageous. However, that is usually a poor excuse for the use of wood, since fumes that attack metal usually attack men's lungs and the proper action is usually to eliminate the fumes. The first cost of wooden guards is low but replacements soon increase the cost. (c) All machine controls must be conveniently accessible; also, guards must not interfere with oiling or adjusting the machinery. Instead, they should provide protection to the oiler or adjuster. Guards should be so assembled and secured as to facilitate their removal and replacement in case of repairs. (d) Guards should have no sharp edges, nor should they constitute tripping hazards or obstructions. (e) Guards for belts, overhead gears or other overhead parts subjected to considerable strain, should be strong enough to hold the part should it break. (f) Complete enclosure of the moving part to at least six feet above the floor or working level is the minimum standard for reasonable safety. (g) All gears, regardless of size or position, should be completely enclosed. The record of injuries from gears supposedly out of reach is so bad as to show clearly that the only safe treatment for gears is complete enclosure. Furthermore, enclosure

None but the fully submerged type should ever be used. Keys should never be left projecting. 

Point-of-Operation Guarding. 

This 

8. Positive mechanical means of eliminating machine hazards should be applied wherever possible and to the maximum possible extent. One commonly encounters the attitude that it is sufficient to guard a machine so that an operator faithfully obeying carefully worked out rules of safe operation can escape injury. attitude is wrong and is responsible for a heavy proportion of the injuries connected with machine operation. Every uncontrolled hazard, however remote, will produce its quota of injuries and even the most careful operator will at times do the wrong thing or fail to take some necessary precaution. Furthermore, many machine operators are neither carefully selected nor adequately trained, and in many establishments supervision is neither safety-minded nor adequate. 

9. The proportion of point-of-operation injuries that are permanent is high (see table 4)-loss of hands, fingers, or the hopeless maiming of bones, muscles or nerves. They are almost wholly preventable. 

Also, they are expensive. For instance, New York State data for a typical year showed that its compensated injuries from the operation of woodworking machines averaged $488 compensation per injury as against $330 per injury for all compensated injuries. 

10. Not only must a machine guard give maximum protection, but it must not interfere with operation or create or contribute to a different hazard. For instance, the guard about the operating zone of a punch press, if not solid, should not have horizontal members in front of the ram because the motion of the ram past them may cause eyestrain. 

11. Whenever possible a guard should be an integral part of the machine on which it is used. Sometimes the frame or some other part of the machine may easily be extended to cover a gear or a sprocket, or to close up a space where a pinching action may occur, between either two moving parts or a moving part and a fixed part. If this is not practicable, the guard should be made removable. It is a mistake, however, to make it too easy for machine operators to remove guards. 

Many firms follow the practice of so interlocking machine control and guards as to render the machine inoperable unless the guards are all in place. Guarding of existing machines must be provided for by means of extra attachments. This should not be the case with new machines. Designers should arrange to have guards built into or on the machines. Dependence should not be placed on adding them as an afterthought. Effective guards can best be included in designing machines to be used for special operations. It is almost always practicable to do this with machines that are used continuously for standard processes. 

12. User safety should always be a vital consideration in the design and manufacture of any machine. Not only should the manufacturer build the maximum of safety into the machines he produces, but point-of-operation guards should be considered vital parts of each

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

machine and be designed and constructed to function as effectively and as long as any other part of the machine. Standard models and prices should include guards. While the manufacturer must meet his customer's wishes and can manufacture only that which he can sell, he can and does habitually do much to guide the judgment of his customers. Some manufacturers are doing excellent work along these lines to advance the cause of safety, but in the main they have not yet realized the important part they can play in building safe machines and fostering the demands for them. The problem is basically one of consumer demand. The great majority of machines are ordered with little or no thought for the safety of the operator; or, if his safety is thought of, the purchaser expects to apply any needed guards later. 

In other words, safety is usually an afterthought and gets only "after" attention. This is evidence that in spite of the great progress that has been made in industrial safety, the majority of employers do not yet realize that machine (as well as other) injuries to their employees can be almost entirely eliminated, and that they can profit financially by doing the things necessary to eliminate them. 

13. State factory inspection agencies can greatly improve this condition by: (a) Insistence upon machines being guarded before their use is allowed. (b) A systematic procedure within each inspectional agency whereby the inspector not only emphasizes the above requirement to each employer whose plant he inspects, but furnishes him with all possible information on the hazards of the specific machines, and how these hazards can be met. (c) Encouraging and requiring the practice of including suitable safety specifications in all purchase orders. (d) Contact manufacturers and encourage them to design and build the maximum of safety into all new machines. The designer and builder of a machine is in the best position to guard it properly, for he knows every operation for which the machine is designed and its capabilities. 

14. Manufactured Guards.-A considerable business has developed in the manufacture of guards for application by the user. As would be expected, not all of these are as carefully designed, as well made, or as effective as they should be. However, there is, and, for some time at least, will be, an important field for the manufacturer who specializes in the production of guards and safety devices of high quality. 

15. Home-Made Guards. In advocating home-made guards, safety engineers should be cautious for the following reasons: (a) The average home-made guard looks like what it is-a makeshift, an afterthought, poor in appearance, only partly effective. Such a guard testifies to a lack of management's safety mindedness. (b) Injuries ascribed to defective or inadequate guards are frequent. While numerous examples of excellent home-made guards are to be found, high quality on the average is not to be expected where guards are designed and made by mechanics of widely varying abilities in establishments of all sorts.

91 at Ot IS (c) The reputed low cost of properly designed and well made home-made guards is often incorrect. Usually, if all costs are included, the total proves to be high rather than low. 

16. Too much emphasis is placed on keeping guard costs down and far too little on keeping their effectiveness high. Economy that produces a less than "safest possible" guard and thereby eventually yields an amputation is the reverse of true economy. 

17. The above facts constitute warning that home-made guards to be effective must be properly designed, well made and suitably attached. Furthermore, they should be finished attractively and durably. The same principles apply, of course, to the "commercial" guard and to those furnished by the manufacturers of the machine. 

The safety man should never oppose home-made guards as such nor advocate commercial guards as such. He should urge and insist that guards from whatever source be carefully designed and well made. 

If it is not practicable for the inspector who advises a management to make its own guards, to furnish detailed drawings, he should set down clearly the conditions to be met and the qualities the guard should have. 

18. Why Guards Are Opposed.-Employees on piece work often oppose guards through fear that they will cut their production or make their work harder. Employers often have the same view. Workmen who "learned" on an unguarded machine usually disbelieve in and resist guards, but those who "learn" on a guarded machine rarely prefer to operate the machine without guards. Actually, the correct kind of guards invariably increase production through enabling machines to be operated more steadily or faster, or both. 

Machine Feeding. 

19. The great majority of point-of-operation injuries occur during the process of feeding and result from the necessity of reaching into the "danger zone”—the area in which at some point of the machine cycle the hands (or other part of the body) can be caught. Obviously, then, the first thing to seek to do is to make it unnecessary to reach into the danger zone. This is done by automatic or semiautomatic feeding. However, reaching into the danger zone should be made impossible, as well as unnecessary, in order to prevent injury due to thoughtless action. If a piece of the material being fed sticks or gets out of place, it is almost instinctive to "make a quick grab" to correct it. Complete enclosure so arranged that the machine must be shut down to remove the material is the best means of prevention. 

20. In automatic feeding, the parts or material are stacked or placed in a hopper or roll and carried to the point of operation mechanically. The attendant merely keeps the machine supplied and removes the accumulated product and shuts the machine down when necessary for any adjustment or repairs. Since each such set-up must be "tailor-made" for the material involved, they are expensive and therefore practical for long runs only. It is chiefly the automatic machine, however, that has made possible the production of a very wide range of articles at a very low price for each. Injuries on automatically fed machines are usually due to attempts to clean or adjust machines in motion or when setting up for a run.

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

21. For certain material and for shorter runs fully automatic feeding is not practical and semiautomatic feeds are used. Each piece of material is placed by hand in a feeding device which carries it to the point of operation. Most accidents with semiautomatic feeds result from the occasional improper placement of material in the feeding device. An attempt to correct this while the material is nearing the point of operation often results in fingers being caught. 

This can be prevented only by enclosing the danger zone so that the operator cannot reach into it. 

22. For very short runs or with material not subject to mechanical handlings, hand feeding is used; that is, the material is placed at the point of operation by the operator's hand. It is the most dangerous of all machine operations because any error of motion that the operator makes is likely to result in injury, usually in an amputation. However, effective safeguards can be provided for all such operations and machine operation without them is never justified.

Common Metal Working Machines. 

27. Power Presses.-No single type of guard has yet been devised that is practical and effective for all kinds of presses, or even for all kinds of work that may be done on a given press. On long runs, the use of automatic or semiautomatic feeds, together with enclosure of the danger zone, is practical. It is the short run that is a problem. 

To develop special means of feeding for a run whose length is measured only in hours is usually too expensive and too time-consuming to be practicable. However, every press operation can be made reasonably safe in one way or another and at a cost that is not excessive. Management must be keenly determined to eliminate injuries from press operation and must adopt and enforce the rule that no press shall be operated unguarded. Every management that is competent to operate power presses efficiently can learn to safeguard them so effectively and operate them so safely that injuries will practically be eliminated. 

28. The punch press furnishes an outstanding example of the

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

point already emphasized, i. e., that complete safeguarding should always be included as a part of design and construction. Problems of mechanical feeding on short runs, so difficult and expensive when considered as afterthoughts, are readily solved in the design of the press and its accessory equipment. Feeding devices, such as dial feeds or sliding or rotating dies, always expensive and often impractical to add to standard press models, can usually be included in the design of the press at moderate increase in first cost. Thereafter, the guarding cost per run for short run work is limited practically to the cost of jigs to hold the pieces being worked on. 

29. Effective guards rarely fail to increase production because they make possible steadier operation and often permit higher speeds. 

An operator will operate his machine more steadily when he realizes that he cannot reach into the danger zone. Also, press speeds are limited by the speed with which the operator can perform the various motions that go to make up each cycle. The simpler and fewer the motions he has to make, the higher the press speed can be. Similarly, safety is advanced by reducing and simplifying the motions necessary. 

Safety must be a vital consideration in deciding how each press job is to be done, in designing the dies and in setting them, as well as in the operation of the press itself. 

30. Every phase of press work, including setting up, yields its quota of injuries. However, the forming, or second, operation is most productive of injuries. These occur when the operator is adjusting a piece or removing it from the die. A quick motion to set a piece right, a little delay, a failure to coordinate-each of these produce injuries. Here incorrectly placed guards may prove little better than none. A guard, to be effective, must either prevent the worker from reaching into the danger zone, or, if this is not practicable, make it impossible for him to reach into the danger zone (or leave his hand there) during the part of the cycle when injury can occur. Not only must the front of the press be fully guarded but also the sides and rear, for the operator may reach in from the side and other workers may reach in from side or rear. 

31. In most cases two types of guards will be sufficient, one for blanking operations and another for forming, or second, operations. 

However, the more common types of guards are briefly described below. Again it should be emphasized that no press should be considered completely or satisfactorily guarded unless provision is made. for whatever variety of guards may be necessary to safeguard all operations performed on that press. The purchasing agent should keep this in mind when ordering a press and should never regard an order as complete unless guarding is specified, regardless of whether the guards are provided by the press manufacturer, the die maker, or the purchaser. Safety engineers should insist upon this attitude and should, insofar as their authority permits, not allow presses to be placed in operation unless satisfactory provision is made for safety.

34. Danger Zone Enclosure.-Full enclosure of the danger zone is necessary with both automatic and semiautomatic feeding. The maximum opening in or about the enclosure should be three-eighths inch in width unless the clearance from moving parts exceeds 4 inches. 

In no case should openings exceed 1 inch in width. The enclosure should protect the entire length of travel of the ram. There should be no shear points between the guard and any moving part. 

35. At least the portion of the enclosure in front of the ram should consist of vertical members only, because eyestrain may be caused by the motion of the ram back of horizontal members in the operator's line of vision. 

36. The enclosing guard is often attached to the stripper or the die and since it must be suited to the size and shape of the die, the die maker or die setter must have a large share of responsibility for the job of safeguarding. He must be safety-minded and ingenious in carrying out the purpose of management to eliminate power press injuries. The foreman must cooperate fully to provide necessary

Where the chance of parts or stock jamming or sticking makes it desirable, the guard or a section of it may be arranged to be readily slipped up or aside, but in such case it must be so interconnected that the press cannot be tripped unless the guard is in place. Also it must not be possible to raise, or open, the guard with the press in operation. The guard, or its gate, must not in itself constitute a pinching or striking hazard. 

38. Automatic Feeding.-Full automatic press feed and take off with complete fixed enclosure of the danger zone is most applicable for continuous or very long runs. It gives a very high production rate and low cost. The details of the feed and take-off will vary with the properties of the stock and characteristics of the work done, but in general, flat stock or wire is coiled and fed from the roll; thin flat pieces are fed by friction rolls; and thicker pieces by plungers. 

39. Semiautomatic Feeding.-There are five types of semiautomatic feeds, namely: (a) chute feed, (b) slide feed, (c) sliding dies, (d) dial feeds, (e) revolving dies. 573065-44- -7

40. In chute feed the pieces are placed by hand in a chute along which they slide, or are pushed, to the die. This method has been found successful with a great diversity of shapes and sizes. Often in changing over from other means of feeding, it is necessary to reverse the dies and incline the press. 

41. In slide feeds the parts are stacked in a suitable carrier and pushed into the die by a hand-operated plunger. 

42. Sliding dies are so mounted as to slide toward the operator for safe feeding and then return to position under the ram. 

The die may be operated by hand or foot power or mechanically. 

43. In dial feeds the operator places the pieces in suitable holders on a dial whose rotation, timed to the stroke of the press, carries them under the ram. 

44. Revolving dies are a modification of the dial feed principle in which two or more dies are so mounted as to revolve about a fixed center to carry the parts under the ram. 

45. In determining the details of guarding, the whole layout of the operation must be considered. The method of feeding, the design of the dies and the means of ejecting the pieces are all interdependent, and each has a bearing on the problem of safeguarding. Obviously, then, the close cooperation of die designers, die makers and die setters and plant executives is necessary if the maximum in safety is to be achieved. That power press injuries can be prevented is proved by the fact that many are doing it. 

46. Manual Feeding.-Methods of safeguarding used in connection with manual feeding are: Enclosure of ram; limitation of ram stroke; gate guard; two-hand control; sweep guard; light beam; "pull out" or safety harness; use of special tools. 

47. Where the size and shape of the material or parts being processed permit, insertion through limited openings in ram enclosures may be used. However, the requirements in paragraphs 34 to 37 should be met. A chief difficulty in highly diversified work is that many different shapes and sizes of enclosures are necessary; in fact, each die should have its own guard which, of course, increases the cost. Ingenious and farily successful attempts to meet this have produced guards built up of rods individually adjustable to accommodate a wide variety of work. Such guards must be correctly adjusted, however, and should be so locked as to prevent their being tampered with by the operator. 

48. For many operations using thin stock, presses whose stroke can be closely limited are used. The maximum opening between ram and die or stripper should not exceed three-eighths inch. 

49. Ram enclosures which, or a section of which, move out of the way to permit insertion of the piece and return to their protecting position before the operating clutch can become engaged are termed gate guards. They should meet the requirements of paragraphs 34 to 37. When connected with the tripping mechanism only, gate guards do not guard against repeats due to clutch faults and an effective nonrepeat device should be provided. Nonrepeat devices are usually connected to the foot treadle but they may be hand operated and arranged to trip the press as they reach the protecting position. 

They must not in themselves constitute a hazard such as pinching, striking, or shearing.

The arrangement must be such that neither control can be held or tied down and the press operated by the other. Positive means of preventing repeats through faults of the clutch or the controls should be provided. On presses requiring more than one attendant there should be two controls for each. 

51. A sweep guard, as its name implies, moves across the point of operation to sweep the operator's hand out of danger as the ram descends. Although they are accepted generally by State codes, as well as by American Standards Association Code on Power Presses, they have serious limitations. At high press speeds they strike too hard; at low press speeds it is difficult to arrange them so that they can't be "beat" by a quick "stab." It is often difficult to make them large enough or long enough to be effective without introducing a shear hazard between the guard and the die or other fixed part. They should be operated by hand control only; the sweep must be wide enough to carry the hand out of danger regardless of the position of the arm; and the guard (flag) must be of such size that the operator cannot reach around it to the danger zone during any part of the "sweep." They should be actuated by the ram, as otherwise they give no protection against a repeat due to clutch fault. 

52. The invention commonly known as the "electric eye" has been applied to press guarding. It projects a light curtain across the front of the press to a suitably placed target or targets. The interruption of the light beam by an object as large as a hand or finger prevents the tripping of the press. It does not interfere with the operator's view of the press operation and eliminates guard adjustments or changes on change of dies. 

53. The "pull-out" device consists of a harness attached to the operator's wrists and so arranged as to draw his hands forcibly out of the danger zone at each stroke of the press. Its weakness lies in the fact that its use is subject to the will of the operator. It must be accurately adjusted to the hand and finger length of each operator.

Also, any loosening of the fastening at the wrist would increase the effective reach of the hand. 

54. Small or oddly shaped pieces are sometimes advantageously handled by tongs, tweezers, suction disks or other special tools, with soft noses to prevent damage to the dies. In such case, since the operator need not reach into the danger zone, the temptation not to provide a guard may be strong. However, guards should never be omitted as sooner or later the operator will make a slip that will, if the danger zone is unguarded, result in an amputation. 

55. Take-off (Ejection). The removal of the piece without reaching into the danger zone is as necessary as it is in feeding. Automatic removal must accompany automatic feed. Strippers are usually necessary on presses to loosen the part from the die. Gravity can often be made to do the rest. Jets of compressed air are sometimes used. Often reversing the dies and providing a knock-out on the upper die, or placing the stripper at the front of the die and inclining the press, allows the piece to fall or slide into a container at the back. 

Dies can often be arranged for the punching to drop through them. 

56. Treadle Guards. Many injuries result from accidental tripping of the treadle in setting up, making adjustments, or in pauses in opera-

In all cases, there should be effective protection against accidental tripping. Usually an inverted U-shaped metal shield over the treadle is effective. If the long bar (across the machine) type of treadle is used, the entire length of the bar should be protected. 

57. Prevention of Press Repeating.-Most so-called repeats are from riding the treadle and are not actually repeats, for "repeat," when applied to a press (or other stroke type of machine), means a stroke that occurs without operation of the control mechanism. In all cases in which the operator's hands enter the danger zone and the guard is not operated by the ram itself, either a single stroke clutch (a clutch that releases with each stroke) or a treadle-disconnecting device (disconnecting the treadle at each stroke) should be used. Many advocate nonrepeat devices as standard for all presses because of the added safety they give when the press is being tried out or adjusted. 

58. Foot and Hand Presses and Punches.-These machines produce many injuries which, while mostly minor, add up to a large total of needless suffering and expense. New York State reports show a total of 1,474 compensated injuries from this source in 2 years on which an average of $83 compensation per injury was paid. Sixty of these injuries (approximately 4 percent) involved permanent disability. 

59. All of these machines can and should be safeguarded. Limitation of stroke or fixed ram enclosure with a limited (three-eighths inch or less) opening can often be used. Where neither is feasible, a basket ram enclosure arranged to raise with the lift of the ram and descend ahead of it is practical and effective. 

60. Metal Forming and Straightening Rolls. When the nature of the work permits, a fixed bar or angle should be run the length of the rolls in front of the nip line, so placed as to leave not over a threeeighths inch opening into the nip. Often the type of material is such as to allow it to be fed over an apron wide enough to leave the nip out of reach. When guarding is not possible, a quick stop should be considered. This involves the use of a dynamic brake and is expensive, and since in many cases the hazard is small, it need not always be required. When it is provided, the control bar should be so placed, so shaped, and of such length as to insure a person caught being able to operate it instantly from any position. It should stop the rolls practically instantaneously and should be tested frequently.

Many injuries occur through lack of care in sorting out hard metal which might fly at the cut or throw chips from itself or from the knife. 65. Lathes and Automatic Screw Machines. Lathe injuries are chiefly due to the operator's getting caught (or hit) by the projecting set screw on the lathe dog, by projections on chucks, face plates, or the work itself, and, in the case of turret lathes and automatic screw machines, by the projecting revolving stock. The chief hazard is in filing. The file should have a secure handle; it should be so held that if forced back it will pass the body; the left hand should stay on the near side of the work and the right hand across it. 

66. In addition to the use of safe methods of work, chucks and face plates should be free from projections, and dogs, if used, should be of the safety type only-circular in shape with no projections beyond the periphery. Chip guards to catch flying chips, particularly in the case of high speeds used in the softer metals, are often advantageous. Rotating stock on turret lathes and automatic screw machines should be completely enclosed in pipe long enough to contain the longest stock used. These latter machines are particularly likely to throw oil. Suitable shields and oil catchers should be provided to prevent floor slipperiness and aid housekeeping. 

67. Drill Presses. The rotating spindle with such projections as keys presents a considerable hazard. All projections should be eliminated. Also as much of the spindle itself should be enclosed as the work will allow. New models are so guarded by some machine manufacturers. Spindle drive belts, when in range of the operator's head or body, should be guarded against both contact and breakage.

New models are available with this hazard eliminated. 

69. Where clearance from fixed objects is inadequate men may be caught by the stroke of planer or shaper; in such cases space to allow for the maximum possible stroke should be railed off in such manner as to give protection without creating a shear hazard between the railing and the planer table or the work carried on it. 

70. Abrasive Wheels.-The majority of injuries from grinding wheel operation are to the eyes and result from flying particles. Injuries from bursting wheels are less frequent but usually serious. Also, burns and abrasions occur from contact with the wheel itself. The very wide distribution of abrasive wheels and their frequent use under conditions where safety-minded supervision is lacking magnifies the importance of full attention to user safety in their manufacture and installation. Fortunately, the excellent "Safety Code For the Use,

Care and Protection of Abrasive Wheels" (approved by the Ameri can Standards Association) has gained complete acceptance by abrasive wheel manufacturers. User acceptance is vital also because failure to follow the code in remounting and in maintenance soon produces unsafe conditions. State codes, so far as they exist, generally conform to the A. S. A. Code. The problem is one of education and adequate inspection. 

71. Eye injuries are prevented by the use of goggles and provision of nonshatter glass shields over the grinding area of the wheel. Faithful use of goggles cannot be had by merely providing goggles and telling the men to use them. The matter must be viewed from the worker's standpoint. Unless he is convinced that he should and must wear them, and unless all disadvantages, from his viewpoint are removed, satisfactory results will not be secured. Important items are: (a) Each person should have his own goggles carefully fitted and provided with prescription lenses, if necessary. Workers who should wear glasses but do not, upon attempting to wear goggles are almost certain to blame their eyestrain on the goggles. Goggles with the proper lenses would relieve these cases. (b) Provision must be made for goggle maintenance. (c) Provision must be made to enable the wearer to keep the lenses clean while on the job. 

72. Undoubtedly men working continuously on grinding wheels should wear goggles, because they give maximum protection. However, because it is so difficult to get men to put on their goggles for short grinding jobs, it is generally advisable to place nonshatter glass shields over the grinding area with a light mounted to illuminate the work clearly without shining into the operator's eyes. All grinding wheels used for tool maintenance and touch-ups (that is, in the ordinary machine shops) should be equipped with such shields. Their use in production grinding is less practical because the flying particles soon abrade the glass surface and make it cloudy. The use of inexpensive cover glasses that can readily be changed meets this difficulty fairly well.

Common Woodworking Machines. 

76. High cutting speeds are necessary in woodworking. This means that instant amputation is the normal result of contact with the cutting edge of amost any woodworking tool in operation. However, reasonably effective and entirely practical guards have been devised for each type of machine, and for nearly every operation performed. This does not mean that any of these machines, if properly guarded, are safe for the use of careless or uninstructed operators; but it does mean that, if they are properly maintained, the skill required to operate them safely can be readily acquired by any worker of ordinary intelligence. Persons who fail to learn safe working methods should not be allowed to operate these machines. 

77. The duty of the purchaser (employer) to safeguard so far as possible the operation of his machines has been discussed. It is particularly important in the case of certain woodworking machines (saws and jointers) because their use is so widespread that application of regulatory requirements by enforcement authorities is extremely difficult. Training and supervision of the operators are, therefore, especially necessary. 

78. Proper guards and their faithful use are not enough. Safe operation of woodworking machines, particularly circular saws, shapers and jointers depends so much upon proper maintenance and safe operating practices that unless these factors receive continuous I and careful attention along with guarding, the workers will continue to suffer needless injuries.

(c) safe working habits. Hazards requiring guards and the guards in question are: (1) Contact with saw above table. A hood completely enclosing all of the saw above the table at all times, self-adjusting to the thickness of the work being cut, affording a view of the point of operation sufficient to the needs of the work, not offering appreciable resistance to the insertion and passage of the work through the saw, strong and rigid enough to resist any reasonable blows and strains incident to normal operation, designed and secured to make correct adjustment as easy as possible. Where this is not feasible a separate spreader suited to the saw for which that throat piece is used may be fixed to each throat piece. (4) Antikick-back dogs (points) mounted on or in addition to the enclosing hood guards should be used on all ripsaws for further protection against kick-backs.

CHAPTER XIX 

Education, Training, Supervision,

Organization Scope and importance of promoting safe work practices. Basic procedures-education, training, supervision, and organization. Value and limitations of promotional education. Training in safe practices and elimination of dangerous habits. Supervision-supervisors and safety.

Organization-major function of regular organization, place of safety engineer and safety committees, management leadership necessary. Reference material. 

1. The preceding chapters have dealt primarily with the broad outlines of the industrial accident picture and have discussed in considerable detail the methods of finding and eliminating physical hazards. We now come to the equally important matter of promoting safe behavior-safe practices. The detail involved in securing and maintaining a good standard of safe behavior among the employees of any given establishment is very great. Yet, the basic procedures used are relatively simple. They may in briefest terms be set forth as: (a) Safety education. To develop safety consciousness—a vivid awareness of the importance of eliminating accidents and a mental alertness in recognizing and correcting conditions and practices that might lead to injury. (b) Safety training.-Developing the worker's skill in the use of safe work techniques and practices. (c) Safety supervision. Supervision that teaches, exemplifies, and practices first rate safety performance. (d) Safety organization.-Devising, maintaining (and modifying as conditions require) a specific set-up to develop plant-wide teamwork in the furtherance of safety. 

2. Safety educational work to be effective must be much more than an advertising campaign. Promotional programs employing such devices as slogans, stunts, pictures, posters, plant newspapers, safety meetings, contests, and the like play a vital part in safety, but as is true of all advertising, this type of promotion must be supplemented by intensive individual contact. Each workman must be stimulated and helped to weave safety into his own day-by-day activities. This means that the hazards of all operations must be analyzed and safe procedures established (job safety analysis). Safety educational work limited to giving workmen general cautions and safety slogans without telling each one definitely what to do, what not to do, and why, are of very limited value. Few individuals will of themselves apply general principles or the wisdom contained in a slogan to their own activities except sporadically and in limited fashion. Detailed guidance and reasonably continuous pushing is needed which brings in the matter of training. 105

SAFETY SUBJECTS Training. 

3. Training increases one's effectiveness in doing that which education points out the way to do. Training in safety must be given as it is in other arts, by instruction, demonstration, and repetition under supervision. Fortunately, most men can quite readily be "sold" on the safety idea enough for them to want to work safely and with relatively little supervision. One of the most important jobs facing safetyminded management is to discover those men who have not responded properly to the safety education and training program, find out why, and either help them to overcome the difficulty or put them on work of inherently low hazard. The always difficult matter of correcting unsafe habits is involved here as is accident proneness (Ch. XXI). 

Discharge as a means of accomplishing adherence to the safety program is not recommended. Not only is each discharge evidence of supervisory failure, but harsh penalties will foster an attitude of 'covering up" bad practices. 

4. Habit plays an important part in accident causation. Safety men and supervisors should bear in mind the fact that practically no workmen now middle aged received any safety instruction when, as young men, they were learning their trades. Even today, 30 years after the birth of the organized safey movement, the attention given to safety in training procedures is generally inadequate. Much more thorough-going and careful attention to safety in vocational training of every sort is seriously needed. 

5. Habits once well established are very difficult to change. A poor or even difficult way of doing anything becomes the easy way when well established. Any change appears difficult and involves extra effort. Through sufficient repetition the new way becomes easier, but unless the old way of doing is completely erased, reflex action is likely to cause reversion to it in moments of stress. The key to elimination of a bad habit is to fully convince its possessor that it will pay him to change it, so that he will actually work at its elimination. Habits are rarely changed "on order from the boss." Discharge for a bad habit rarely brings correction. Instead the discharged worker merely carries his bad habit to some other place of employment. Thus unsafe workmen tend to gravitate to unsafe employers. Supervision. 

6. It is evident that quality of supervision will determine effectiveness of training. The foreman is the keyman in any safety program. 

He must see that safety is given its full share of attention in all that is done. Basing their attitude on this fact, many managements have passed the entire responsibility for safety to their foremen, but the job cannot be done in that way. Management must not only require that proper attention be paid to safety by every staff member, but it must plan and direct the work, take an active interest in it, and take whatever action may be necessary to discover and correct weaknesses. 

Securing competent supervision is a major problem throughout industry. It is particularly true in the safety field. Only when management assumes its full responsibility to train its supervisory personnel adequately in safety can first rate safety performance be reached and maintained. Too many excellently planned plant safety programs fail becaues of the weakness of this vital link.

1. By safety organization is meant the definite, planned, and organized set-up whose purpose is to enlist and maintain the combined effort of the entire personnel of an establishment or undertaking in the work of preventing accidents. Some may consider this too broad a definition, preferring to limit the term "Safety Organization" to the safety committee set-up. However, since many firms whose safety performance is excellent do not make use of safety committees at all, yet do, through definite organized effort, get the necessary combined employee effort and interest, the broader interpretation seems preferable. 

2. In a well-managed industrial organization, when the chief operating executive (president, vice president, general manager) wishes to establish any change in policy or procedure for production, employment, safety, etc., he must issue the necessary orders and then follow them up to make sure that the new plan is carried out. In issuing his orders, however, he does not go directly to the workers. Instead, he works from the top down through the organization so that every person in a position of responsibility fully understands, first, what is wanted, and, second, just what he is to do about it. This constitutes the essence of successful executive direction of safety provided the "chief" makes it clear that he really wants safety, for any employee will try hardest to accomplish whatever his supervisor appears to want most. 

3. In order to reach and maintain first-rate safety performance, the whole-hearted cooperation of everyone in the organization is needed. 

A definite program should be developed to interest and educate all employees in safety and to secure their active participation in the effort to eliminate accidents. Such a program must be based on the full assumption of its responsibilities by the management. The program must supply leadership and executive drive. Plant and equipment must be made safe. Safety must be included in all phases of planning, purchasing, supervision, and operation. Once these fundamentals are understood by management, and it fully assumes the responsibilities involved, the appropriate type of organization will be evolved. Active executive interest and direction leads to an effective program, regardless of the form of safety organization. Proof of this is furnished by the fact that every known type of safety organization 108

4. We may (making allowances for variations in detail) classify safety organizations in three general types, namely: (a) Those in which the safety work is carried on wholly through the line organization. (b) Those in which the safety work is headed up under a safety director reporting only to a major executive. (c) Those in which safety work is carried on primarily by committees set up for the purpose.

Type (A) Organization-Line Organization. 

5. Obviously, if no full-time safety personnel-safety techniciansare used, the production executives must become expert in the safety problems involved in their establishment or type of business. The chief executive must also give a larger share of his personal attention to safety than would be the case if he had a highly competent safety technician on his staff. This fact has led some chief executives using this type of safety organization to add a safety specialist. In such case he functions as a technical assistant to the chief executive. 

Should he possess strong qualities of leadership, he may take over increasing responsibilities for the safety work and bring about a progressive change of the organization into the type (B) form. 

6. The strength of the type (A) set-up lies chiefly in the fact that it centers entire responsibility for all safety effort within each operating unit on the head of that unit. It is his duty to incorporate safety into each and every part of the day-by-day activities of his unit. Just as he plans and supervises the work of each of his men for adequate production and satisfactory quality, he does likewise for safety. 

He must become the best informed and most safety-minded person in his unit. The weakness of this type of organization is that supervisors, occupied as they are with other production problems, find it difficult to acquire the special knowledge needed to reach a high standard of safety performance. This means that too much of the knowledge of how to prevent accidents is gained from the accidents that occur. In other words, it is largely "after-thought" prevention, and, therefore, progress is apt to be slow. 

7. While some large firms use the type (A) set-up, it is most commonly found among firms too small to justify the employment of a full-time safety engineer. If the small plant executive will really give, and continue to give, a proper share of his executive attention to safety, steady improvement invariably results, leading ultimately to excellent performance.

Type (B) Organization-Safety Director. 

8. This is the type of organization used by most large firms. Its effectiveness depends upon two things-the attitude of the top management, and after that, the competency and ability of the safety director. While, as already pointed out, keen interest in safety by top management is essential to first-rate safety performance in any case, a large share of responsibility for safety work can be delegated to a strong and competent safety director.

9. A safety director's job is not easy. He should function both as the special assistant and adviser on safety to the major executive and as the adviser and "stimulator" on safety for the whole organization. 

He cannot interfere with or usurp any of the functions of any member of the supervisory force, but he must work closely with all of them. 

Since no man can function properly under two bosses, the safety director cannot give orders except to personnel under him, yet he must often secure prompt correction of unsafe conditions or practices. His effectiveness, therefore, will be in direct proportion to: (a) His ability to secure the confidence and respect of every foreman or other executive. (b) The amount of responsibility given him by his boss. 

10. The safety man must realize that his is a service job and must not encroach upon the authority of the various members of the supervisory force. 

11. His standing with the boss will be good if he gets the confidence of the supervisory personnel and if, in addition, he keeps his chief fully informed as to progress of safety work. To be effective the actual means of doing this must suit the personal preferences of the chief. 

Reports should be brief, definite, and factual. 

12. The safety director, then, must, first, think of the help he can give each supervisor or other staff man; second, seek to determine the viewpoint of each so that his help will be well accepted; third, be sure of his facts and careful in drawing conclusions; fourth, keep the boss informed. 

Type (C) Organization-Safety Committees. 

13. Safety work governed by committees is usually found in establishments too small to justify a full-time safety director or where management prefers the work to be directed jointly by members of his staff. It has the weakness of any set-up governed by a committee instead of by an executive. A committee is, at best, weak in execution. It has the advantage of bringing together the viewpoints of the group and its joint judgment is normally better than that of any individual in the group; but prompt, effective, and orderly execution depends upon the placing of authority and responsibility in the hands of one person and the faithful discharge by him of that responsibility, including both the giving of the orders and the follow-through necessary to their proper execution. Therefore, the committee-type organization functions best when the chief executive himself is chairman and makes use of the committee to: (a) Strengthen his judgment. (b) Keep chosen staff members fully in touch and informed. (c) Promote the interest and cooperation of the committee members.

When made use of in this manner, the type (C) organization merges into the type (A) set-up. 

14. Failure to appreciate the limitations of the committee method is responsible for frequent examples of establishments having on paper excellent committee set-ups, but whose safety performance is disappointing. Too often the safety committee idea is borrowed from an establishment making successful use of it and is installed in a more or less perfunctory manner. The chief executive in such case presum-

Safety Committees. 

15. The advisable committee set-up depends chiefly upon the size of the establishment but other factors have a bearing, such as the progress that has been made in safeguarding the plant when committee work is started, the size and relationship of the various departments or plant units, the type of business (manufacture, construction, transportation, public utilities, etc.). However, at least the following fundamentals should be met in forming the committee or committees decided upon: (a) Each committee should be so made up as to have standing appropriate to its field of work. For instance, a main or governing committee should include such key executives as master mechanic and production manager. A workers' committee should be made up of members well known to and having the respect of their fellow workmen. (b) The committee membership should encompass the maximum in knowledge of the methods, practices and conditions in the plant, undertaking, or group represented. (c) The committee should be as small as is consistent with the above requirements. A committee of three functions more effectively than one of five. The larger the committee the more the debate and the less the action. 

16. When a committee is formed, certain matters of policy and procedure should be definitely set forth in writing. Written instructions should cover at least: (a) Scope of committee activity. (b) Extent of committee authority. (c) Procedure as to: (1) Time and place of meetings. (2) Frequency of meetings. (3) Order of business. (4) Records to be kept. (5) Attendance requirements. 

17. A committee will take its work seriously in proportion to management's attitude toward it. A management that sets up a committee to accomplish a specific purpose, makes it clear that it wants results and gives effective executive supervision to its activities, will get satisfactory results therefrom. Effective committee conduct of safety work in the absence of executive leadership is possible only when committee members have unusual initiative and determination to advance the cause of safety. In such instances they often convert the non-safety-minded management. Sometimes the "salesman" of safety, failing to arouse adequate management interest, can accomplish his purpose by getting a safety committee organized and aid it to plan a program that will interest the top management, thus at the same time getting safety work started. 573065-44- -8

18. While as pointed out, scope of activity, extent of authority, and the procedure to be followed should be put in writing and records of activities kept, this should not be carried too far. Too little system leads to confusion, waste motion, duplication of effort and indirection. 

Too much yields red tape and the needless expenditure of valuable time and effort. In other words, as any other identical activity, safety committee work requires planning and orderly procedure、 under executive leadership. 

19. In summation it can be said that the major advantages of committees are: (a) They bring together varying viewpoints and generally yield sounder decisions than does the individual viewpoint. (b) They widen interest by giving active participation to a number of persons in conducting the work. 

20. Major weaknesses of committees are: (a) Group action requires deliberation and is, therefore, slow. (b) The meetings necessarily consume much time of many persons. 

21. Commonly used types of committees are: (a) Main or governing committee. (b) Workers' committee. (c) Technical committee. d) Special purpose committee. 

22. A large plant may make use of all these committees; a small plant may use only one. The point is that once a definite decision is made as to the scope of the work to be handled by committee action, the types of committees needed, and the number required are readily determined. 

23. The chief function of the main or governing committee is to determine the policy and set the standard or plane at which the safety work is to be conducted. The chief operating head of the plant or undertaking in question should be chairman. This makes it possible for the chair to issue definite orders to the various members as committee decisions are reached, thus avoiding both the delay and the possibility of overruling involved where committee decisions must be referred to a chief executive. It might appear that since the chief operating head can give orders at any time, the use of committees is of little value. 27. Fundamental to the effective functioning of a workers' safety committee is the sincere desire of management to enlist the help of the workers and obtain their faithful adherence to a course that will promote mutual confidence and respect. Workers' committees should not be set up unless at least the following essentials are accepted and established: (a) The work and place of the committee in the scheme of things is accepted as important. (b) All recommendations and suggestions made by the committee receive prompt consideration. If accepted, the action called for should be prompt. If rejected or modified by the chief executive, or a higher committee, the reason for such rejection or modification should be clearly explained. 

28. If a foreman, or other supervisor, is chairman, he should function as the chairman of a committee of equals, never as the "boss." 

29. Firms using workers' safety committees usually report their chief value to be that of promoting worker safety-mindedness. Until at least the more evident physical hazards are safeguarded, there is little use in attempting to get worker cooperation in preventing accidents. Every workman knows that safeguards cost money and efforts to get him to improve his work practices before obviously justified expenditures are made for safeguards will be taken as evidence of management's desire for safety "if it doesn't cost anything.' 

All of which means that workers' committees should not be started until at least a reasonably good standard has been reached in the safeguarding of physical hazards.

Accident proneness and accident "repeaters." Tips for handling people. 1. Psychology is "the science which treats of the mind in any of its aspects.' Industrial psychology deals with the behavior of persons at work, and safety psychology with this work behavior as it affects their safety. The possibility of injury exists in all work activities, and, therefore, it is important to understand and control, insofar as possible, all work behavior in the interest of safety. The psychologists can tell us much that will help us to understand and control this behavior. 

2. When a new man reports for work, be brings with him certain abilities and mental attitudes, either latent or more or less fully developed, which will have definite influence upon (a) the manner in which he performs the job to which he is assigned, and (b) the manner in which he will respond to suggestions for improvement in his work methods. 

3. Theoretically, the new employee should report for work only after careful selection and placement in the light of his technical, physical, and mental abilities. A number of preplacement tests have been devised to reveal such factors as his familiarity with tools and processes involved in the job, his ability to perform certain mental and physical tasks of a nature similar to those involved in the work to which he will be assigned, and other mental traits and abilities which will have a bearing upon his relationship with his superiors and coworkers. Physical examinations may be used to determine physical ability to perform certain jobs with due regard to the safety of the operator and his fellow workers. As a matter of fact, the use of preemployment examinations has been limited practically to a rather small number of large organizations, and in times when rapid expansion of personnel is essential, standards are likely to be relaxed. 

4. Once the man has been hired and reports for work, his performance, including practices affecting his own safety and that of other workers, becomes the responsibility of his supervisor. Seeing the man in actual performance and in contact with his fellow workers, the supervisor is in a good position to properly evaluate abilities and traits of character. Just as the foreman or supervisor has always been the "keyman" in production, so he is the keyman in safety workand in applying practical psychology. By intimate contact he comes to know the likes and dislikes of each one of his men; he may gain knowledge of such outside factors as domestic relations, financial troubles, background, and environment, which have a bearing on the employee's frame of mind; he learns by experience the best method of getting each employee to respond to his orders and suggestions. 115

5. First impressions are important. If a new man walks into a shop which is dirty and disorderly, if he sees unguarded hazardous machinery, or notices men working on machines with guards inoperative, he is not likely to take seriously any suggestions which he may receive as to safe-work methods. Disorderly and unsafe conditions likewise make it difficult for the foreman to convince old employees that he is serious in his campaign for safety. If he really wants to obtain his men's cooperation in the safety effort, he must do his utmost to maintain good housekeeping and to see that all machinery is guarded and that the guards are in place. He cannot hire clean-up and maintenance crews, nor can he purchase guards; but he can call these needs to the attention of the management, and he can make sure that his own men maintain order and use the safeguards provided for their protection. The effect of such measures in attacking the problem of accident prevention directly is discussed elsewhere in this series; their psychological effect is of great importance.

Although no two persons are exactly alike as regards any given physical or mental characteristic, experience has shown that approximately 20 percent of any group will be superior, 60 percent normal, and 20 percent inferior. Leaders and superior performers will be found among the top 20 percent, while the accident-prone and other problem cases will come, for the most part, from the inferior 20 percent. The remaining 60 percent, the normal-behavior group, will respond satisfactorily to reasonably well-conceived and well-administered programs of education and training.

Supervisors and foremen, interested in obtaining orderly and safe production, should learn how to detect the individual so maladjusted as to be likely to interfere with the harmonious functioning of the group.

11. The conduct of a campaign to arouse the interest of a number of persons at one time has its disadvantages. Some men respond readily to an appeal to pride, having a strong desire for approbation or material reward; others react slightly to this stimulus, if at all. Some men respond quickly to the lash of ridicule; others resent it and may even react against it. Some, with imagination and feeling, are deeply influenced by a portrayal of the harm an unsafe act might cause to themselves, or to an innocent bystander; others, unimaginative or calloused, are left untouched; still others, "overconscious" of danger, may lose self-assurance and self-confidence. 

12. The industrial supervisor has a small audience; he can address each individual separately, shaping his appeal to the traits of character and desires strongest in the person whom he is addressing at the moment. Furthermore, the good foreman, the successful supervisor, is well aware of the stimulus to use in each case. The traits and desires to which he has successfully appealed in getting quantity and quality production are those to which he will appeal in his efforts to arouse interest in safety. One man is a proud craftsman-he will not wish to injure the fingers and hands which he has so carefully trained. 

Another has worked hard and progressed for better pay he will not risk the loss of income as a result of injury. Still another is a devoted family man, always boasting about his wife and children, always planning for their future he will not wish to risk injury to himself for fear of injuring them.

1. Just as accurate cost records and reports are necessary to good management of an enterprise, adequate reporting and recording of accidents are essential to first-rate safety performance. Accident records have two primary purposes: (a) To furnish the information necessary for the compensation of the injured person. (b) To serve as a guide to preventive effort. 

2. The information required for purposes of compensation is determined by the requirements of the workmen's compensation act having jurisdiction and the terms of the insurance contract (if insurance is carried). A standard injury report form,' which, with minor modification, is acceptable to State compensation authorities, is in general use by insurance underwriters. Recently some States have shown a tendency to modify this form considerably in an effort to include more information as to accident causation. However, the extent to which such modification is practicable is limited because: (a) There is a practical limit to the detail which can be demanded in an injury report required of all employers. (b) Accurate information as to accident cause is usually obtainable only through a much more painstaking investigation of accidents than can feasibly be obtained by legislative requirement. 

3. The standard injury report form must, of course, be supplemented by a final report, giving data necessary to close the case. 

This report yields the kind of information that, in addition to its use in determining compensation awards, is valuable in keeping management informed as to the rate and seriousness of injury occurrence trends, injury sources, etc. It furnishes material usable in making comparisons which are very valuable guides to preventive policy. 

Summaries should be made at regular intervals (usually monthly, biannually, and annually) covering such items as: (a) Frequency and severity rates. (b) Comparison to show the trend and degree of change in such rates.

Value of standards. Types of safety standards voluntary and regulatory.

Development of voluntary standards-effect of first World War, American 

Standards Association, procedure in formulating standards, other standards formulating groups. Development of regulatory standards-unsatisfactory nature of early type of specific legislation, trend toward general statement of employer responsibility and grant of code-making authority to State agency, use of voluntary standards, supplementing regulatory standards with recommended material, code-drafting procedure, importance of uniformity in State standards, need for training State safety inspectors. 1. The development and general acceptance of standards is necessary if a reasonable degree of uniformity is to be secured in any line of activity. Every branch of science or engineering, every profession, every line of industry must have its own set of standards. As each new field of endeavor is developed, standards must be set up as a basis for further progress, and this ground work in turn plays its part in the development of additional standards. 

2. Industrial safety is still a new and steadily developing field. 

The standards in use have been developed through two different channels; a fact which has had important consequences. These standards fall into two groups: (a) Voluntary, self-applied standards.-The various interests, groups, and individuals engaged in the work of accident prevention have developed standards representative of good practice. 

Since the purpose is to prevent accidents, the standards amount to a crystallization of experience and are accepted and observed only by virtue of their practical value as aids to prevention. (b) Regulatory standards.--Laws, or rules, having the force and effect of law, have been adopted by governments for the purpose of securing the correction of specific hazardous conditions and of setting forth certain requirements deemed necessary to safety. 

3. The need of standards was realized very early in the organized safety movement. There was a period of trial and error experiment, and the exchange of ideas and experience-won knowledge. Standards soon began to appear, many of which were embodied in general and broad regulatory requirements. Early regulations concerned the guarding of transmission machinery, the guarding of commonly used point-of-operation machines, and requirements covering railings, stairs, ladders, etc.

The Development of Regulatory Standards and Requirements. 

12. The movement that yielded workmen's compensation legislation also brought legislation prohibiting unduly hazardous working conditions. This legislation attempted to deal with each hazard in detail and proved to be unsatisfactory in that: (a) The difficulty of amending legislative enactments makes them too inflexible to meet the changing condition of our swiftly moving industrial development. (b) Industrial hazards are so numerous and varied that legislation sufficiently detailed to be even reasonably adequate in its coverage would, because of inflexibility, work much hardship if faithfully enforced and thus might actually hinder rather than aid the cause of prevention. (c) The securing of a satisfactory degree of uniformity in such legislative enactments among the various States is difficult to the point of practical impossibility. 

13. These difficulties have led to the enactment by a steadily increasing number of States of legislation comprising essentially: (a) A definite statement that "It is the duty of every employer to provide reasonably safe and healthful working conditions for his employees and to do everything reasonably necessary to protect their life, health, and safety." (b) A provision for the drafting of rules and standards to carry out this statement of policy. 

14. Many States have developed such safety standards or codes. 

In some cases, notably Pennsylvania, these code systems are extensive. 

The sum total of experience gained in the drafting and use of such standards appears to justify the following general conclusions: (a) The majority of the States that have made provision for the drafting of safety rules either use as a guide or adopt directly the safety codes approved under American Standards Association procedure because: (1) The method of their development insures that they are representative of good practice. (2) The method of their development and the degree of voluntary acceptance resulting therefrom gives practical assurance that their provisions are in fact sound and reasonably necessary for safety and that the courts will usually so hold. (b) Continued and more widespread adoption of ASA safety standards will result in a reduction in conflicts between the requirements of the various administrative agencies, State and Federal. This will be a nationally important gain. (c) The aim of every State should be to construct by means of mandatory requirements a floor for safety and health at a level representing the minimum conditions that may be permitted in workplaces.

Common Explosion Hazards

Fired pressure vessels-steam boilers, heating boilers, miscellaneous small boilers, steam cookers, digesters, glue pots, etc. Unfired pressure vessels air compressors, gases in cylinders. Flammable liquids and gases. Dusts. Other substances. References. 

1. Explosions produce a steady stream of injury and destruction.

An occasional, spectacular explosion receives headline attention. 

The majority do not. Practically all are from well understood causes and, therefore, are preventable. Furthermore, the great majority result from a failure to safeguard a relatively small number of common hazards. This discussion deals briefly with these hazards, in an effort to present the methods of control. The student of the subject should regard the information herein as only a beginning. He should obtain and familiarize himself with all of the reference material that applies to the hazards in which he is interested. 

Fired Pressure Vessels. 

2. Steam Boilers.-The_safeguarding of steam-power boilers has been developed to such a high standard that it can be truly said that boiler explosions are entirely preventable, provided that boilers are: (a) Constructed and installed in accordance with the Power 

Boiler Code of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, or equivalent standards. (Various States, cities, and the Federal 

Government have developed codes of similar excellence which, nevertheless, vary in some details from the ASME Code. surance companies which insure steam boilers against explosion generally apply the provisions of the ASME Code.) In- (b) Operated by personnel whose competence entitles them to hold certificates granted by licensing authorities set up for the purpose. (c) Regularly inspected by similarly competent inspection personnel. 

3. The total of steam-power-boiler explosions each year is a very considerable one. The great majority of these have occurred: (a) In States or jurisdictions whose requirements are not equal to those cited above. (b) In operations not covered by code requirements. (c) In operations that escape inspection. (d) Through the operation of uninsured boilers. 

4. The great destructiveness of steam-boiler explosions is largely due to the fact that the boiling point of water rises with the pressure it is under. Therefore, water held in liquid form above its ordinary boiling point (212° F. at sea level) will tend to flash into steam if the pressure is released. The extent and violence of this change will depend upon the temperature. For instance, at 100 pounds pressure, 132

133 the boiling point of water is 337° F., or 125° above its boiling point at atmospheric pressure. If such pressure is quickly released by the rupture of the containing part of the vessel, part of the water will flash into steam with terrific violence. Often this produces a rocket effect which carries the boiler shell over distances of thousands of feet. 

5. Heating Boilers, Miscellaneous Small Boilers.-Most State boiler codes exempt boilers intended for low pressures (usually below 15 pounds). The violence with which even the smallest boiler can explode should not be underestimated. Every vessel, however small, SU in which water is heated can, if its normal outlet is closed or plugged up, become in fact a high pressure steam generator, and if its strength is exceeded will explode destructively. The recurring explosions of such low-pressure devices indicate a too widespread failure to keep 101 this simple fact in mind. Therefore, the accident preventionist should take careful account of every fired water or steam vessel and make sure that: (a) It is of safe construction for the pressures involved. (b) It has adequate means of limitation of pressure, such as: (1) A safety valve of adequate capacity. (2) A disk or diaphragm that will fracture at a predetermined pressure. (c) It is regularly inspected, competently operated, and properly maintained. 

6. Steam Cookers, Digesters, Glue Pots, Etc.-All pressure vessels to which steam is supplied from an outside source should be designed, insofar as it is practical, for the maximum line pressure. Most of them are not, and on such vessels it is vitally important to install, in addition to the necessary pressure reducing valve, a safety valve on the vessel itself or on the line between it and the pressure reducer. 

There should be no means of cutting off or bypassing this safety valve. 

Inspection and adequate maintenance of the vessel including regular testing of the safety valve are vital. 

Unfired Pressure Vessels. 

7. The frequency of unfired pressure vessel explosions is not high in view of the great number of them in use, but the actual number of explosions each year is considerable. While ordinarily less destructive than steam pressure vessel explosions, any such explosion must be regarded as a serious matter." 

8. Air Compressors. In addition to the fact that all such vessels or equipment must have sufficient strength to carry the pressure safely, air compressors are subject to a hazard from the oil required to lubricate the valves of the air cylinders. Compressing air raises its temperature. If the air temperature rises high enough, some of the oil will be vaporized and carried over into the air receiver, where it will collect on the water that condenses out of the air under pressure. 

Unless drained off frequently, and particularly if overheating occurs, this collected oil is likely to catch fire and its vapors may explode. For this reason, every air tank should have a drain at its lowest point. 

Overheating can occur from overloading or from faulty valve operation. The feeding of excess oil should be avoided, as any excess will be carried through into the receiver. Only oil manufactured for use in air compressors should be used. In practice it is hardly possible to

SAFETY SUBJECTS 

prevent some oil from being carried over in to the receiver, but the amount should not be large and if maintenance is good, if the proper amount of a good grade of oil is used, and, if the receiver is drained regularly, trouble will not occur. 

9. Gases in Cylinders.-The use of various gases distributed in cylinders under high pressures has become common. The chief of these are oxygen, hydrogen, and acetylene (used chiefly in welding and cutting), carbon dioxide, liquefied petroleum gases, chlorine, and nitrogen. Pressures used run up to 1,800 pounds per square inch.

Because of these high pressures, detailed standards for the construction, safeguarding, handling, and use of cylinders have been developed by manufacturers of the various gases and by regulatory agencies. These standards should be followed carefully. However, a few points of particular importance should be widely known and carefully observed. 

They are: (a) Oxygen, either as a pure gas or in the liquid form will combine with oil or grease (and some other organic compounds) explosively. (b) Acetylene will decompose explosively if compressed.

Dissolved in acetone, however, it is stable. In this form it is safely handled in cylinders at limited pressures. (c) Cylinders should never be allowed to fall or otherwise receive shocks. (d) Cylinders should be kept away from heat, such as the direct rays of the summer sun. (e) Cylinders should be stored away from combustible materials. (f) Chlorine gas is extremely corrosive, and special precaution must be taken if it is to be handled safely. 

Flammable Liquids and Gases. 

10. Strictly speaking, liquids, like combustible solids, do not burn; it is only their vapors that do. The temperature at which just enough vapor is given off to form (under specified test conditions) an ignitable mixture with air is termed the "flash point." The liquids commonly termed "volatiles," such as gasoline, benzol, ether, alcohol, solvent naphtha, etc., give off vapors in ignitable volume at ordinary temperatures. 

11. Every vapor or gas that will burn will also explode if mixed with air in the right proportions. The range of proportions within which the mixture is explosive is different for each substance. For some it is quite narrow; for others very wide. For instance, gasoline-air mixtures are explosive only within the range 1.3 and 6 percent of gasoline vapor by volume. Ethyl alcohol has a range of 3.5 to 19 percent, acetylene a range of 2.5 to 80 percent, while butylene has the remarkable range of 1.7 to 90 percent.¹ 

12. 13. The prevention of vapor or gas explosions (and fires) lies in: (a) Preventing rise of temperature. (b) Enclosure in underground tanks, approved safety cans, enclosed systems, etc. (c) Elimination of ignition sources by such means as: 

(1) Arrangement of process. 

(2) Vapor proof enclosures of light globes. (3) Flash-proof electrical equipment. 

(4) Control of static electricity. 

(5) Use of nonsparking, tools in hazardous locations or operations. (d) Handling methods to keep spillage and leaks to a minimum. (e) Meticulous housekeeping. (f) In some special processes the use of an inert gas, as carbon dioxide or nitrogen, to produce an incombustible atmosphere. Dusts. 

14. The dust or any substance that will burn will explode if mixed with air in the proper proportions. This fact is also true of certain metals if finely powdered-notably magnesium, aluminum, aluminum bronze, and zinc. Prevention lies chiefly in: (a) Enclosure of dust producing processes. (b) Exhaust systems to draw off and collect the dust. (c) Removal of dust by vacuum systems or wet sweeping. (d) Ventilation to keep dust concentration down. (e) Design and construction to avoid ledges or other dust catchers as far as possible. (f) Use of inert gas in grinding equipment. (g) Elimination of possible sources of ignition. (h) Provision of vents such as hinged windows or skylights or light wall sections adequate to relieve the pressure of possible explosion without structural damage. (The pressures produced by dust explosions are in general lower than those produced by volatiles, but they are still much too high to be contained by practicable building construction. They range up to about 50 pounds per square inch.)

In coal mining two methods seldom practicable in industrial establishments are very effective; namely, dusting with finely ground rock to provide an incombustible dust mixture and sprinkling to keep the dust wet.

Hazardous Substances. 

15. A considerable number of other substances in fairly common. use may yield or contribute to explosions. Chief of these are: (a) Powerful oxidizing agents, such as chlorates, nitrates and peroxides, most of which can be exploded when mixed or in contact with such organic matter as starch, sugar, gums, resins, sweepings, etc. Some of them, notably sodium peroxide, will

heat up in the presence of moisture. All of them give off oxygen if heated sufficiently and thus can contribute to fire. (b) In addition to forming explosive mixtures with air, many metals if finely ground well decompose water, liberating hydrogen which will form an explosive mixture with air. Metallic sodium and potassium react so violently with water that the heat generated will set fire to the hydrogen. These metals are kept in moisture free oil. (c) The three commonly used mineral acids-nitric, hydrochloric (muriatic), and sulphuric may cause explosion through spillage or leakage on certain other chemicals. (d) Pyroxylin plastics (commonly known as celluloid) decompose readily on heating to about 300° F., giving off heat and large volumes of highly toxic gases (chiefly carbon monoxide and oxides of nitrogen). Ignition occurs quickly, and if the amounts involved are considerable the burning will approach explosive violence. (e) Explosives, while ordinarily stored remote from industrial establishments or habitations (under specific and generally accepted standards), are nevertheless so widely used in construction work that left-over explosives and caps (exploders) are likely to be forgotten on the job or placed in toolrooms, lockers, etc. Control should be exercised through careful supervision and close check on the requisitioning, use, and disposal of all explosives.

1. The National Board of Fire Underwriters estimates the aver age direct fire loss in the United States for the decade 1930-39 at $319,500,000 annually. The all-time high was in 1926 and amounted to some $575,000,000. Since 1933 the annual losses have varied little, averaging about $260,000,000, but the war emergency turned the trend upward. Presumably this trend is due to greater industrial activity without correspondingly greater attention to protection, building up of larger stocks of materials, accompanied in many cases by relaxation of protective provisions, and increased use and processing of hazardous materials. 

2. The great reduction since 1926 undoubtedly reflects better fire prevention and protection practice. However, the fact that there are some 600,000 fires in the United States per year indicates the great distance yet to be traveled by the forces of prevention. It further indicates the great need of better general appreciation of fire hazards and the means of control. The great majority of fires are due to such common hazards as the use of matches, heating devices, etc. Unless the level of individual performance in controlling these common hazards can be raised, all that can be done in the way of better construction, better means of extinguishment, etc., can yield only moderate improvement. 

3. The above figures cover direct destruction by fire only. They do not include the numerous indirect losses that follow or are incidental to every fire, such as interruption of business, loss of wages by workmen, loss of market, public expenditures for fire fighting, etc.

Nor do they include forest fire losses. Conservative estimates indicate that the indirect losses run from two to three times the direct costs, bringing the total fire loss to about a billion dollars per year. 

4. The loss of life by fire in the United States averages about 

10,000 persons annually, with at least twice that number injured 138

5. Almost all fires are of a preventable type. The faithful application of time-tried and proven methods of prevention and control would eliminate the great majority of fires and would limit the losses from the remainder. The proof of this assertion is to be found in the fire records of numerous industrial firms. 

6. The detail involved in the prevention and limitation of fire loss is very great, but the fundamental principles on which the details are based are simple. They may be stated briefly as: (a) Prevention of start of fire (by construction, arrangement, control of operations, maintenance, housekeeping, and elimination of unsafe practices). (b) Prompt discovery and extinguishment (except for the relatively few fires initiated by the explosive combustion of dusts, vapors, or other highly hazardous substance, all fires start small). (c) Limitation of spread (provision of suitable barriers and holding to a minimum the volume and value of combustibles present in a given fire area will limit the loss. Fire area is the area within effective fire stops). (d) Provision and proper maintenance of adequate exit facilities (what constitutes adequacy will vary widely with conditions and will include the giving of suitable alarms and the control of the behavior of persons involved to prevent panic or other unsafe acts). 

Prevention of Start of Fire. 

7. Burning is essentially oxidation with the rapid evolution of heat. 

Every combustible substance will ignite and burn if raised to a suitable temperature in the presence of air. The lower this ignition temperature, the more likely it is to be reached, and, therefore, the greater the hazard. Once ignition occurs, however, other properties of the substance in question have a bearing on the hazard. Fire prevention practice starts, therefore, with the basic purpose of preventing combustibles from reaching ignition temperatures. If the process necessarily involves heating to or above ignition temperatures, air must be excluded.

Common Hazards. 

9. "Matches-Smoking," the largest single fire cause, is difficult to deal with because of the universality of the use of matches and of smoking. Control involves general education as to the seriousness of the hazard, supplemented in individual establishments by means suited to conditions. In a powder plant, for instance, absolute prohibition with rigid enforcement is an absolute necessity. In most establishments, however, limiting smoking to specific areas with good attention to enforcement will furnish reasonably good results. The safety match is strongly preferable, but the Chief Engineer of the

National Board of Fire Underwriters has authorized the statement that "the modern inert bulb, protected tip, strike-anywhere match is reasonably safe." 

10. Care in the extinguishment and disposal of matches and cigarette and cigar butts and in emptying pipes should be universal. 

In a plant, this care includes provision of an adequate number of suitable containers. 

11. Good housekeeping is perhaps the most important single element in preventing the start and spread of fire. Rubbish and waste material contribute to fire by furnishing the "tinder" for ignition from

BU 

1 small sources of heat and fuel to facilitate fire spread. Bad housekeeping promotes spontaneous combustion when substances subject to rapid oxidation are involved. Orderly storage of combustibles with spacing suited to the nature of the material are important in facilitating fire extinguishment and limiting fire spread. Piled material should not interfere with sprinkler action nor block access to fire fighting equipment. 

12. The proper and regular disposal of all combustible waste is vital. Metal receptacles, preferably covered, should be used. Collections should be removed from important areas at the end of each day's work or each shift. If stored in the building, waste should be kept in fire resistive vaults or enclosures. Baled waste is less hazardous than loose material. Baling and storage rooms should be sprinklered. 

13. Particular care should be used with materials subject to spontaneous combustion. Oily mops, rags, waste, etc., are highly hazardous. They should be kept in metal or fire resistive enclosures. 

Floor oils and cleaning compounds used should bear Underwriters' 

Laboratory approval. 

14. Outdoor housekeeping is also important. Space around buildings, storage yards, etc., should be kept clear of dry grass, weeds and rubbish. Weed killers should be used with caution as they are usually oxidizing agents and may contribute seriously to fires once started. 

15. Fires in lockers are frequent and due usually to spontaneous combustion in oily clothes or rags, to pipes or cigars not fully extinguished, or to the careless disposition of matches. Lockers should be of metal with sloping tops, solid backs and solid dividing partitions. 

16. Unbaled clean cotton waste and such packing materials as excelsior, straw and burlap are readily flammable. They should be kept in metal cans with self-closing covers. Large supplies should be kept in metal or metal-lined bins. 

17. Oily sawdust, lint, metal turnings, etc., are as hazardous as oily rags and should be treated with equal care. Cans containing oily wastes of any nature should be emptied daily. 

18. Cotton, jute and similar fibrous materials burn rapidly and their processing involves the hazards of flyings which are so highly flammable as to introduce the hazard of flash fires. Separators should be used to remove foreign material that might cause sparks; electrical equipment should be of a type similar to that approved for use where explosive vapors or dusts are present; processing machines in which fires are particularly likely, such as pickers, rag cutters, etc., should be enclosed with a sprinkler or other suitable automatic extinguishing device in the enclosure. A high standard of housekeeping is important particularly in keeping flyings from collecting and in limiting collections of combustibles to the minimum. 

19. Sparks cause many fires. Prevention of fires from sparks coming from locomotives, chimneys and outside fires is mainly a matter of using incombustible roof coverings, spark arresters, and care with fires. Inside sparks come chiefly from static electricity, furnaces and beating equipment, grinding wheels, welding and cutting torches, and from the use of steel tools. Care in the use of such equipment and segregation of spark producing operations from combustible material is necessary. Static sparks are of very short duration and produce

little heat; therefore, precautions against them are ordinarily necessary only where flammable gases, dusts, or vapors are likely to be present. 

20. Static electricity is generated by friction or by the motions relative to one another of adjacent dissimilar materials. Sparking is prevented if the static is not allowed to accumulate. Accumulation can be prevented by grounding, humidification, and neutralizing. Grounding consists of connecting to earth or bonding together through low resistance connections the surfaces on which the static would collect. 

In textile mills it is common practice to maintain approximately an 85 percent humidity in the air of the spinning rooms. Air, if ionized sufficiently, will neutralize static. Air can be ionized by special transformers that produce a brush discharge. Gas flames also ionize air and are commonly used for that purpose on printing presses. 

21. Power transmission equipment overheats if lubrication fails. 

Also, fires result from slippage of belts on wooden pulleys and rubbing of belts or shafting against wooden partitions, floors, etc. 

22. Blower systems, hot air ducts, air conditioning systems, dust and fume exhausts, while not in themselves properly fire causes, act to spread fire unless properly designed and safeguarded. Design and safeguards should be in accordance with the requirements of NFPA- 

National Board of Fire Underwriters standards. 

23. The direct rays of the sun may set fire to combustibles by shining through objects so shaped as to act as burning glasses—namely, glass paper weights, fish bowls, water bottles, laboratory flasks, bubbles in window panes; also through reflection from concave mirrors of optical instruments. 

24. The improper use, poor maintenance, or faulty construction of kerosene, or other oil lamps, lanterns, and torches produce many fires. 

Means of prevention are obvious but difficult to secure because of the widespread use of these devices. 

25. Fires in construction, alteration or demolishing operations are common, and the conditions vary sufficiently from those in completed structures to justify mention here. Means for extinguishment and limitation of spread are seldom present. Cutting and welding torches. fires, salamanders, and other heaters, gasoline, solvents (paint thinners, etc.) are used under conditions that make adequate control measures difficult. Good housekeeping is difficult to secure and maintain; values are often high and relatively small fires at key points may do great damage. The NFPA Committee on Construction Operations has developed recommended good practice requirements which should be generally accepted and applied. 

26. The uncontrolled use of fire works has been far more costly in both lives and property damage than was the war which won our independence. It has become clear that the only effective way to control this hazard is through prohibiting the public sale and use of fire works and limiting their use to public displays under close supervision. NFPA has developed a model bill to this end, which by 1941 had been made law, without substantial changes, by many of the States.

Discovery and Distinguishment. 

28. Fire Alarms.-Prompt discovery of fire is obviously vital to both extinguishment and life safety. Fire alarm systems have been developed to a high degree of reliability and effectiveness. Essentials of an effective system are briefly: (a) A reliable signal must be transmitted. (b) It must reach those trained to respond. (c) It should compel immediate attention and unmistakably mean "fire.” (d) It should indicate location of fire. (e) If transmitted by person, the means of transmission must be readily accessible and require the simplest possible performance with a minimum opportunity for delay or error. (f) To warn building occupants, the alarm must be compelling, even for sleeping persons, anywhere within the building or fire area in question. 

29. The commonly used types of fire alarms are: (a) Manual. The alarm is transmitted to a central point by pulling a lever on a fire alarm box. This is the type commonly found in city streets. (b) Automatic. Operated automatically by a heat sensitive device through such means as the fusion of an alloy, expansion of air or liquid, or by a thermocouple. These devices fall into two classes, namely: (1) Fixed-temperature type designed to operate when the temperature reaches a certain value. (2) Rate-of-rise type designed to operate when the rapidity of temperature rise exceeds a certain predetermined rate. 

The obvious advantage of automatic devices is their unsleeping vigilance and high degree of reliability of action. Their chief weakness lies in problems of proper distribution and adequate maintenance. 

30. Alert, intelligent, and responsible watchman's service is a very important factor in the prompt discovery of and attack on fires. 

However, the watchman of low mentality or whose performance is not good may be a liability. Too common examples of costly watchmanfault include: (a) Attempting to extinguish the fire before turning in the alarm. 57306544- -10

(b) Failing to make his rounds faithfully. (c) Carelessness with lanterns, smoking, etc. (d) Cutting off of sprinklers prematurely, etc.

Where values are high and hazards considerable, the watchman is vitally important, and great care should be used in his selection, training, and instruction. Supervision should be by a responsible official fully alive to the hazards involved and the importance of maintaining a high standard of performance. Suitable checks through such measures as definite written reports, portable watchman's clocks, stationary time-recording stations, central supervisory service, telephone reporting, etc., are commonly maintained. 

31. From the standpoint of extinguishment, fires are divided into three classes, namely:

Class A fires-fires in ordinary combustible materials where the quenching and cooling effects of quantities of water,or solutions containing large percentages of water, are of first importance. 

Class B fires-fires in flammable liquids, greases, etc., where a blanketing effect is essential. 

Class C fires-fires in electrical equipment, where the use of a "nonconducting" extinguishing agent is of first importance. 

Extinguishing Agents. 

32. Water. Extinguishes through cooling, blanketing, and in the case of liquids readily miscible with water, such as ethyl alcohol, through dilution. Through surface wetting it prevents or retards the reaching of ignition temperature. It is effective on oil fires only when applied as a very fine spray or heavy mist over the entire surface, or in large volume by the use of fire hose or deluge sprinklers to relatively small amount of oil, as in garage or airplane hangar fires. 

33. Foam. Various substances can be used to impart an enduring quality to foam produced by the generation of gas (carbon dioxide) through chemical action in a water solution. The foam cuts off the air through blanketing and has some cooling value as well. Its primary value is in class B fires. It should be so applied as to cover the entire surface of the liquid. However, vapors being given off will pass through the foam and if in sufficient concentration will burn above it. Foam is broken down by certain solvents (alcohols, acetone, ether, and others) and is ineffective for fires in them. 

34. Carbon Tetrachloride (commonly known as Pyrene).-Extinguishes through blanketing by rapid volatilization to a heavy vapor that will not support combustion. The liquid has a high electrical resistance and hence is of chief value for fires in electrical equipment. 

The fumes are toxic, and, therefore, in closed spaces of limited size appropriate caution should be exercised. Only a specially prepared product should be used in extinguishers. The commercial product is corrosive, is likely to be lacking in nonconductivity and its freezing point may not be low enough for safety in cold climates. 

35. Carbon Dioxide.-An inert gas approximately 50 percent heavier than air extinguishes chiefly by blanketing. It also exerts some cooling effect because of the low temperature of the stream when discharged from the cylinder, in which it is held in the liquid form by

erv a pressure of 800-900 pounds per square inch. It is valuable for class 

C fires. 

36. Steam can be used for class B fires in tanks or other enclosed spaces if the volume of steam available is sufficient to reduce the oxygen content below a figure that will support combustion (about 14 percent for most oils; about 10 percent if glowing material is to be extinguished). 

37. Dry Powdered Materials as sand, sodium bicarbonate, etc., are moderately effective for small class B fires if applied to cover the entire area of fire. The difficulties of proper application greatly limit their usefulness.

Automatic Sprinklers. 

42. The automatic sprinkler is by far the most important of all fire protection devices. The value of property protected by automatic sprinklers in this country alone is estimated at not less than 50 billion dollars. The fire loss of sprinklered property has not been more than 10 percent of what it would have been if unsprinklered.2

These figures indicate an annual saving of some 200 billion dollars in preventing direct fire loss. The saving of indirect loss is of course still greater. (See par. 3.) 

43. The value of automatic sprinklers in saving life has proved even greater than in preventing monetary loss. Loss of life in sprinklered properties occurs rarely. Failure to control fire in sprinklered buildings is usually due to: (a) Water shut off. (b) Flash fires (flammable vapors, combustible dust, lint, etc.). (c) Explosions or other violent occurrences that render the sprinkler system inoperative. (d) Obstructions or conditions that prevent the water from reaching the fire. 

44. Automatic sprinklers are particularly important to life safety for buildings where large numbers of people work or congregate, especially in such properties as hotels, schools, hospitals, sanitaria, department stores, etc. 

45. The automatic sprinkler, as its name implies, embodies the basic idea of providing a spray of water turned on by the heat of the fire itself at each point where fire might start. Since in most buildings fire might start anywhere, it is necessary to sprinkler the entire building if reasonably satisfactory protection is to be had. 

46. Water is supplied through a system of piping ordinarily attached to the ceiling with sprinkler heads spaced along the pipes. The sprinkler head consists essentially of an orifice closed by a disk held in place against the water pressure by a device that releases at a predetermined temperature by: (a) Fusion of solder. (b) Fusion of a chemical. (c) Fracture of a bulb by the expansion of contained liquid. 

47. The sprinkler head also carries a "deflector" or "distributor," the purpose of which is to distribute the water in the manner necessary to cover the area intended. The minimum flowing pressure for effective sprinkler operation is about 8 pounds. At this pressure each head will discharge about 15 gallons of water per minute and cover a floor area of about 100 square feet. Well defined rules based on long experience have been worked out for all details of sprinkler installations. The following principles are of major importance: (a) Distribution must not be obstructed by any barrier, unsprinklered roofed enclosures such as toolrooms or bins, platforms, etc.

Limitation of Fire Spread. 

52. It is obvious that if a fire is not promptly extinguished, barriers that will limit or at least retard its spread will both aid in its final extinguishment and limit the loss. Maximum advisable areas within such barriers may vary widely, depending upon values, hazards, types of construction, height of building, etc. 

53. The following list of the ways in which fires spread will indicate the detail involved in designing and protecting buildings against this hazard:3 (a) From floor to floor of the same building through: (1) Unprotected floor openings, such as those for elevators, stairs, dumb waiters, belt ways, chutes, and pipes, and through light wells. (2) Fires burning through floors. (3) Fires burning through protection provided for vertical openings. (4) External windows or other wall openings. (5) Smoke passing into upper floors and smoke explosions following. (b) From building to building (adjoining) through: (1) Unprotected wall openings. (2) Failure of fire doors or other protective coverings for wall openings. 

(3) Lack of parapets. 

(4) Flashing over or around parapets and igniting combustible walls, roofs, roof coverings, monitors, or penthouses. (5) Passing around the ends of fire walls. (6) Exposure of openings in walls at angles to each other. (7) Heat passing through walls, especially at points where timbers are let into them. (8) Smoke passing through wall openings and smoke explosions following. (9) Failure of walls through explosions, upsetting by fall of imbedded structural members, or other causes. (c) From building to building (not adjoining) through: (1) Flammable roofings. (2) Combustible cornices. (3) Overhanging roofs. (4) Unprotected wall openings. (5) Permanent or temporary openings through which sparks or brands can pass. (6) Combustible exterior walls. (7) Combustible monitors and penthouses. (8) Combustible additions, passages, or other structures.

54. Unprotected floor openings, particularly those piercing all or many floors, such as elevator shafts, are particularly serious, since they provide a chimney action greatly accelerating the fire. Heated gases from a lower floor or basement quickly rush up such a shaft and mushroom out on the top floor. Therefore, effective enclosure of all floor openings is vital. 

55. Window openings are protected by wired glass in metal frames with the added protection of shutters for extreme exposures. Door openings through fire barriers are protected by fire doors, self-closing through the operation of a fusible link placed to receive the first rush of heat. 

56. Wooden shingles have been the largest single factor in conflagrations. They are readily ignited by sparks and brands or radiated heat. Treatment with fireproofing solutions has not proved practical, because the only treatments that will not lose their value from weathering are unduly expensive. 

Exit Facilities. 

57. Life safety in all ordinary fires depends upon adequate exit facilities and their correct use. It is a relatively simple matter to provide and maintain exit facilities adequate to empty all ordinary occupancies of structures in ample time to prevent loss of life, except in flash fires or explosions. Flash fires, as the term indicates, are fires that "flash" over considerable areas too quickly for persons to escape. Even though sprinklers or other automatic devices extinguish the fire, such action cannot usually be rapid enough to save the lives of persons caught in the flashed-over area. Wherever flammable vapors or gases may be released, or wherever combustible dust may be thrown into the air or readily ignitable lint or dust is allowed to collect, flash fires may occur. Preventive measures depend in large part upon the nature of the material or process, but in general they comprise: (a) Enclosure to prevent the escape of such substances into the air. (b) Provisions for the rapid removal of any that do escape. (c) Provisions for the automatic extinguishment of fires within the enclosure as by sprinklers, carbon dioxide or foam installations, etc. (d) Provisions to prevent the start of fires within such enclosure by such means as special temperature controls, atmospheres of inert gas, etc. (e) Meticulous housekeeping to prevent hazardous accumulations of dust, lint, oil, etc.

The following principles and provisions are of major importance in determining the adequacy of exit facilities: (a) Emergency exit needs depend upon the construction of the building, the fire hazard of the materials or processes housed, the fire protection provided, and the characteristics of its occupants. 

Actually, however, it is not practicable to vary exit requirements much. In general the practical viewpoint is to provide exit facilities adequate to meet the worst probable combination of circumstances. (b) The possibility of panic should always be taken into account. Panic can occur without fire. Conditions likely to cause blocking in case of a panicky rush should be avoided (narrow exits, narrow sections or sharp corners in passageways, winders and narrow landings in stairs, inadequate spaces at foot of stair flights or at exit discharge). (c) The provision of two exits so located as to render the cutting off of both at one time extremely improbable is fundamental to safety. This principle should never be violated in areas where any considerable number of persons congregate for any purpose. (d) Free travel of able-bodied persons in single file requires a width of 22 inches, the generally used unit of exit width. A twounit width is the minimum acceptable as satisfactory practice, though building codes commonly accept narrower widths for light occupancy in existing buildings. 60. The practicable means of preventing panic is through fire exit drills. Persons properly instructed and drilled to know exactly what to do at the alarm do not become panic struck. Furthermore, the time necessary to empty a building is greatly reduced. Such drills should be frequent and varied to familiarize everyone with the use of all exits and with proper procedure under all foreseeable conditions.

Approved Devices. 

61. Early in the organized effort to reduce fire losses, the need for means of testing devices and materials on their performance in regard to fire prevention and protection was realized. This realization resulted in the establishment of two great laboratories devoted to the testing of such devices, materials, etc., and the issuance of suitable forms of approval to those materials which stand up under test in accordance with specific standards. These laboratories are Underwriters Laboratories, Inc., 207 East Ohio Street, Chicago, and Factory

Mutual Laboratories, 144 High Street, Boston. The work of the former has to do with the entire field of fire prevention and protection, while the latter specializes in the industrial field. As a vital part of this work, approvals are indicated by listing and by marking or labeling the devices themselves to show that they have such approval. 

An inspection and testing service for the approved devices and materials is maintained to insure that their quality as manufactured is kept up to the standard indicated by the approval granted. 

62. There is no need to attempt here to describe definitely the very great value of these approval systems in the field of fire prevention. 

It should suffice to say that experience justifies the admonition that only approved devices should be regarded by anybody as acceptable. 

This statement applies with particular force to all devices upon the correct functioning of which much depends, as in the case of fire extinguishers, sprinkler systems, alarms, fire doors, electrical wiring and devices, and fire hose.
