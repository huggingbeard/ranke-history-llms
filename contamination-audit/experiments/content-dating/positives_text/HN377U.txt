Foreword

This is an experimental text, designed for an elementary university course on civilian defense. It is offered in the hope that it will not be without interest and benefit to the general reader, but more particularly as a first draft upon which improvement may be built.

The literature upon this subject has appeared to be either almost wholly encyclopaedic or almost wholly functional. Rarely has a combination of the two in one running text been adequate, but by addressing these remarks to a university audience as a homogenous group, it is hoped that at least some of the hazards may be avoided in what may turn out to be an impracticable venture. 

Only enough basic material is introduced to promote intelligent comprehension of the warden's job; and even this step may be open to question since wardens do not always agree. There seems, however, little doubt but that the warden's job is the keystone in the arch, and that, all members of the civilian defense organization should possess more than a passing acquaintance with it. 

Therefore this text seeks to give basic air raid precautions data for all members of the civilian defense organization and a thorough knowledge of the warden's job. 

Most of the chapter on the warden's duties was organized by 

Mr. Leonard F. James, Chief Warden, Andover, Mass. and consultant to the Mass. Committee on Public Safety. Both Mr. James and 

Dean Henry Chauncey, member of the Harvard Committee on Civilian Defence, and Inspector for the Harvard district of the City of Cambridge, have read the text and made many suggestions. Many others, experts in their various fields, have been kind enough to read appropriate portions and to make suggestions. For all this assistance I am most grateful, yet it must be emphasized that for the defects in the text, the undersigned alone is responsible.

THE WARDEN'S DUTIES The Warden's Place in the Community 

Although he is a volunteer contributing only a part of his time, the Air Raid Warden will soon find that he is a member of one of many groups known collectively as the Air Raid Precautions Organization. Since the present purpose is to consider in some detail not only the Warden's job but also the working of that Organization from the individual civilian defense volunteer's point of view, discussion of the larger aspects of the civilian defense effort of the Commonwealth will be reserved until after the Warden's place has been discussed. 

Closely associated with the United States Army through its warning system, the Warden will also find himself side by side with the police and fire departments. The value to all our citizens of these services lies essentially in their extensive training, submission to discipline, and repeated drill. If his best efforts to protect people and property in time of peril are to bear equal fruit, the Air Raid Warden can do no less. Short of actual emergency, necessary experience can be secured, as in the other services, only by manoeuvers and drill. 

The Warden's Place in A.R.P. 

The Air Raid Warden is a civilian war officer in a war organization. 

By authority of his appointment, he represents in his neighborhood our common determination to maintain the services of the community and protect its resources with as little waste from the destruction of war as possible. He must know his job

2. well enough to do it when he may be in the grip of fear; and in doing it he will help others not to be afraid. Much has been said about morale, but the real secret is the ordinary everyday feeling which sustains us in normal life, the sense of being cared for, of belonging to the whole, and of being a real part of our community. 

Since the primary importance of the Air Raid Warden to the public is his ability to report incidents promptly, the careful geographical distribution of wardens at fixed points is fundamental. In residential areas, Posts should be established on the basis of one to every 500 inhabitants, or in cities not more than five to eight minutes walk from the Sector's most distant point.

In industrial or business areas, the distribution may be by distance with a Post every quarter of a mile. In districts wholly occupied by large plants, no Air Raid Wardens in the ordinary sense might be necessary, their place being taken by industrial wardens. For congested areas, building wardens might serve the same purpose.

The Air Raid Warden's Post should be as centrally located as possible in the Sector, both from the point of view of giving good observation to him and also for being easily found by the public. It may be in a school, hall, or garage; or it may be in a private home or place of business. An occupied building is preferred to a vacant one, since the latter would make it necessary to face the cost of maintenance, such as heat in the winter. The 

Post should be made as secure against hazards as circumstances permit; and it should be established either in a large room or in

3. quarters capable of expansion in an emergency.

All the Posts in one locality are connected by telephone to a central point known as the Report Center. The Chief Warden and his staff in this Center may be relied upon to provide whatever special help the Post may need within its Sector.

The Air. Raid Warden's Post should contain only necessary furniture, the principal requirements being at least one desk and chair and as many other comfortable chairs as there are wardens assigned. There should be at least one folding cot, and toilet facilities. Light other than by electricity must be insured. 

Candles, lanterns, pressure lamps, flashlights are all useful.

Since communication must be maintained, telephone, radio, and messenger service are necessary. 

A record book and report forms should be furnished the Post. In addition, the Warden will need in the course of his duties: first-aid belt, respirators, antigas clothing, whistles, gas alarm devices, work gloves, rope, stands, and signs for danger areas. These items will be Post equipment. The Warden's armband and steel helmet will be personal equipment. Finally, each Post might find a small supply of canned food useful, as well as some independent means of preparing hot drinks. 

Since all Wardens are required to be on duty during a raid, the maximum number of hours of regular duty at the Post should not be over four where that is possible. Such a program could be carried out on a twenty-four hour basis with six Wardens. In general the more Wardens who volunteer and are assigned to each Post, the less the amount of time and work each man will have to

4. do. With regard to Post schedules, the proper state or local authorities might at any time order the following: 1. 2. 3. 

In conditions of alert with no indication of attack or fifth column activity: minimum operation. This might require four hours out of each twenty-four for each Warden, if there were six.

In conditions of emergency with possibility of attack or fifth column activity: minimum operation, but with two additional Wardens on call if patrol should be ordered in haste. 

In conditions of probable air attack: Post may operate with one Warden on duty, and all others standing by. 

The Warden's Responsibility to His Sector 

Air Raid Wardens' most important duties are naturally those consequent upon air attack. As soon as possible after the receipt of a yellow warning, if the local authorities have not ordered otherwise, Wardens who have been standing by should begin their patrol of the Sector after checking in at their Post and receiving their equipment. Following a red warning and the sounding of the public alarm, the streets should be cleared of traffic. People should be told to retire within doors to their homes or (if they cannot get home within five minutes) to nearby shelter. Automobiles must be parked out of the way of hydrants; horse-drawn vehicles should be pulled in at the curb, horses unhitched, and tied at the back of their vehicles. The rope should be long enough to allow their heads to reach the ground.

If it is night no light must be seen in the streets, and householders must be warned of any light showing. If not obscured at once, the nearest policeman must be called. 

All shop signs or

5. other lights not within homes should be turned off or reported to the police.

Certain of the people living in the Sector will have volunteered as Fire Watchers, and they should be checked by the Warden to see that they are on duty. Sand and shovels, and other fire-fighting equipment will be available at designated points. 

Wardens are not expected to remain in the open while bombs are falling. They should either return to their Post and keep a lookout, or stand within the protection of a doorway but not within possible range of any nearby glass.

The first duty of every Warden is to report damage, even before help to individuals. The first Warden to arrive where there has been air raid damage beyond his power to deal with should make a quick estimate of such damage and get through a report to the Report Center by the quickest possible means. Neighbors can be found to take in temporarily those whose homes may have been damaged. The Warden's knowledge of first-aid will make it possible for him to help the victims before the first-aid squad arrives.

Even during the attack, individuals not in the Air Raid Precautions Organization may be pressed into service for such use as fire-fighting or directing traffic through the Sector. Wardens must not leave the Sector to which they are assigned, except upon the direction of a superior; and patrol must not be neglected if bombs are falling, since further damage may be caused at any moment. Elsewhere incendiaries may have fallen, and those Wardens on patrol may have been called in to help. Other high explosive bombs may also have fallen, in which case another Incident Officer is established, another incident reported, and additional help secured from the Report Center.

The Warden may or may not possess police power in the different communities. He would always have the right of any citizen to make

7. arrests for a felony committed in his presence. During the daytime this is the extent of his power to arrest; and it should be used with great care since the arrested person has the right to sue for false arrest. During the night, which is legally from 

9 P. M. to 6 A. M., the Warden may arrest not only for the commission of a felony in his presence; he may also arrest for an attempt to commit a felony, a power not possessed in the daytime. 

In rare instances, Wardens might have to make use of this power in cases of looting or fifth column activity.

After the raid is over, a complete and accurate report of all damage is made to the Report Center. This report is also entered in the record book in the Post, as part of the official diary of happenings.

In order that the Sector should receive as little damage as possible either to people or property, every effort at protection should be made. This demands special knowledge, and the Warden must not only exercise himself in the matter, but he must also instruct and inspire his people to do the same. 

The Warden's Responsibility to the People in the Sector First of all, the Warden himself must know the whole Sector well enough to be able to find his way about in a blackout. He must appraise the general character of the building construction and know which structures are safest and which are most vulnerable. 

He must prepare and maintain a Household Register in the Post which shall list accurately all the regular inhabitants of the Sector.

He must know their general habits of life and their special

8. abilities, if any; and he must become sufficiently familiar with their homes to know in what part of the house they sleep. 

Special attention must be paid by the Warden to all peculiarities of the Sector: location of hydrants, water cutoffs, gas cutoffs, electric switches, telephones, outside doors, alleyways, and people who are aged, infirm, or ill. Expectant mothers and children will require help, too. Places of special danger must be remembered, stores of inflammable liquids, lumber yards, or premises containing materials which will burn or explode casily. 

Places of safest shelter should be decided upon, after a study of the construction features in the Sector. AUXILIARY FIRE-FIGHTING AND INCENDIARY BOMB CONTROL War-Time Fire-Fighting and Fire-Fighting Equipment 

Fire protection in war differs from fire protection in time of peace chiefly in the much larger number of fires likely to occur at once, either from sabotage or from the action of incendiary bombs. 

Under normal conditions the organization and equipment of a municipal fire department are rightly based upon the assumption that very few fires are likely to need attention at the same time. 

To meet this situation in the Commonwealth, auxiliary fire stations are being established around each existing engine company. 

An officer or first-grade fireman will be in charge of each such auxiliary station, which may be based on a garage or at any other convenient point; and up to thirty volunteers, assigned on the platoon system, are allotted to each such station. Auxiliary stations will be in possession of fire-fighting equipment, and may function as called, either independently or with the permanent station companies.

In addition to the local arrangements for co-operation between Fire Departments, a Fire Mobilization Board is being established for each of the nine Civil Defense Regions in Massachusetts. Each of these Boards will possess complete inventories of equipment and will be responsible for control of movement within the Region. Movement of equipment from one Region to another can be ordered by the 

Board with the Governor's consent. 

The Fire-Spotters or Fire-Watchers stationed at various points on roofs or in doorways in the Sector belong to one of the Services

14. organized to help the Air Raid Warden. 

All the people, either men or women, in the Sector who are interested in helping to prevent loss from fire should be encouraged by the Warden to qualify for some part of the Auxiliary Fire Service by signing up for the instruction.

Despite these extensive additions to the regular Fire Department facilities, every building should be equipped in so far as possible to look after itself. For this purpose, a suitable number of Fire Stations should be set up, with some type of hand extinguisher and with light portable hand appliances for fighting fires. The knapsack pump will be useful. Each Fire Station should be able to communicate quickly by telephone with the nearest fire station (whether permanent or auxiliary) and with the Sector Post, both of which should be notified immediately of any fire which cannot be extinguished by the means at hand. 

The Air Raid Warden, therefore, will have the help of firefighting forces, as the occasion demands, The Fire-Watchers will be able to judge reasonably well how much help is needed, and may be relied upon to communicate directly with their fire stations. 

It must be emphasized, however, that the Warden (being in charge of the Sector) has control of the situation until the arrival of the first officer of the fire service. That officer, as in peace time, whatever his rank, assumes full authority for action upon his arrival; and the Warden thereafter resumes his place as Incident Officer.

What the Warden Should Know About Fires 

In view of the rise and ebb of highly specialized responsibilities which the Warden assumes and then hands on to those who have been trained for them, it is obvious that he cannot know too much about the various services: the Fire Service among them. But there are certain elementary points which every Warden must know. 

If he finds himself caught in a burning building or if he must enter to effect a rescue, he should remember: 1. · 2. 

In searching a burning house for occupants, to start at the top and continue downward. 

To avoid heat and smoke by lying down and crawling with head as near the floor as possible. 

3. That a wet handkerchief over the mouth and nose will keep out some smoke and heat, but is not a gas mask. 4. 5. 6. 7. 8. 9. 

To keep doors and windows closed in order to restrict the amount of oxygen the fire can take from the air.

In moving about to keep near the walls where there is greater support for the floors. 

To exercise care in opening doors by using body weight against them where possible. Thus the door is a shield and sudden blasts of pressure will not prove as harmful.

To move persons found in a burning structure by any convenient means (including a coat-collar) without attempting the complications of the so-called "firemens' drag" which firemen do not recommend.

That a person whose clothes are on fire should cover his mouth with one hand, lie down, and roll over. 

That fire on the clothes of another person may be put out by wrapping an overcoat or blanket around him and then rolling him on the floor.

In the use of extinguishers, Wardens should remember that the ordinary large soda-acid type and the Foamite type need protection against ordinary freezing temperatures. Control of this type of incendiary is not so simple a matter, unfortunately, as an account of the methods employed would lead one to think. It is unwise to employ any appliance which requires the operator to stand up or to exert himself. The possibility of an explosive charge makes it necessary either to use a jet at once or to wait for at least two or three minutes before starting to cope with the fire, and during that time combustibles within a radius of 30 to 50 feet will have caught fire from burning particles of thermit thrown off. The heat is intense and the heavy white smoke, although not harmful, is exceedingly unpleasant. Moreover, the stirrup pump which has done such excellent work consumes about 2 gallons of water per minute; and the amount of sand required to deal with one bomb will amount to some twenty quarts, or two ten quart pails full.

Even if one has plenty of sand, the use of water is generally necessary, however, if combustibles are present, for before tackling the bomb itself the surrounding fire must be extinguished. The stirrup pump is merely a suction hand pump with 30 feet of rubber hose on the end of which is a dual purpose nozzle which can produce either a spray or a 1/8 in. jet of water. The spray will carry about 15 feet and the jet about 30 feet, and from 6 to 8 gallons of water will be needed to each bomb. 

A fire-fighting team of three is best: one to operate the hose, one to pump the water, and one to keep the pail of water filled. No. 1 should lie down with his face near the floor where

20. he can breathe and see more easily. 

With the jet surrounding fires may be put out first, thus clearing a path to the bomb itself on which the spray is next directed. Six feet away is near enough to go. A flashlight and an axe should be at hand, for when the bomb has burned out, a thorough search of the area is necessary; for this purpose it may be wise to remove floor boards or to tear out panelling to be sure all smouldering remains have been put out. 

If the bomb is reached soon after landing, or has fallen in a place where surrounding fires are not easily started, sand is best used. Some form of container, preferably with a flat side, a. shovel, and a small hoe are required. The container being filled with sand, some of it is shovelled on the burning bomb, the best method being gradually to build up a wall of sand around it then to cover over the top. This is a smothering action; consequently the bomb and the sand should be pulled onto the shovel with the hoe and the whole dropped into the container (care being taken to keep the container about 1/4 full) and removed outside. Otherwise the metal will still burn beneath the sand, and even if the floor itself is fireproof, it may have combustible elements beneath it, and the heat which is driven downward may ignite them. 

Magnesium bombs on wet surfaces may react explosively with the water, and even on dry concrete may react chemically with it, throwing burning particles 10 to 15 feet. Protection may be secured by covering the attic floor 2 inches deep with dry sand, provided the sand is not scattered by impact (small mesh wire netting will prevent this). Pumice slabs 2 inches thick would be more effective and are light in weight. Roofs of gypsum slabs will afford

21. considerable protection. 

Impregnating solutions or flame-resisting paints applied to wood construction are of dubious efficacy. White wash is of some help for besides being fire-resistant it reflects heat. If sand is used, the weight would have to be kept in mind, since dry sand will add about 13 1/2 lbs. per square foot to the floor load, if applied to a depth of 2 inches.

PERSONAL AND PROPERTY PROTECTION AGAINST HIGH EXPLOSIVE BOMBS 

What the Warden should Know about High Explosives and 

Their Behavior

With the mastery of the principles involved and reasonable proficiency in translating them into prompt action, much of the apprehension with which we view fires and incendiary bombs can be removed. At first glance, it may seem inappropriate to apply the same thinking to the field of high explosive bombs. Yet, without question, much of the fear which they arouse is due to ignorance of just what they are and how they behave. 

It is true that their principles are somewhat more complex and in practice take in a wider field; but distance, weight, and cost, as well as military strategy restrict the usefulness of this form of air attack. Furthermore, although any high explosive bomb is deadly and dangerous, certain restrictions are determined by its composition.

A generous share of the Air Raid Warden's value to his Sector in the performance of his duties may depend upon how much he knows about high explosive bombs and their effects, and the precautions which should be taken in respect to this type of air attack. The number and make-up of the special services set up to help him with the damage they do cannot be expected to possess the simplicity or the co-ordination of the Fire Service.

With the exception of those aimed upon a target by dive-bombers, high explosive bombs are released from air craft in level flight; and approach the ground along an arc, striking at an angle which, up to maximum velocity, is closer to the perpendicular the higher the point at which they are dropped. Thus from an altitude of

---

27. 

1,000 feet the angle of impact is 40° at 200 miles per hour decreasing to 200 at 300 miles per hour. This angle approaches 90° until at an altitude of 25,000 feet maximum striking velocity is attained for large bombs (1,000 feet per second as against 400 feet per second for small bombs). From this height the angle of impact is about 80° at 200 miles per hour and about 75° at 300 miles per hour. The fins on the tail are for the purpose of maintaining true flight. Since nearly all modern bombers fly at a speed between these limits, these angles afford minimum and maximum limits of value.

Furthermore, since the trajectory is an arc, a bomb released from an air craft going at 200 miles per hour at 25,000 ft. will land 10,450 feet ahead of the point of release. At 300 miles per hour, the bomb will land 15,670 feet ahead of the point of release. In other words air craft directly overhead are harmless; they must release their load at a point from two to three miles away, depending upon their speed.

Altitudes of flight will vary for practical reasons also. 

The smallest bomb, the 2.2 lbs. incendiary, reaches its maximum velocity of from 350 to 400 feet per second from a height of approximately 5,000 feet. The best bombing altitudes range from that figure for the smallest bomb up to 25,000 feet or thereabouts for the largest bombs which must have time to reach their maximum velocity of about 1,000 feet per second in order to be most destructive. Bombing is apt to be done under the most favorable conditions, if they are at all possible. The time of flight, or the interval between release and impact, may represent the duration

28. of the opportunity to seek shelter.

This figure is not influenced to any appreciable extent by horizontal speed; doubling the air speed increases the time of flight by only 0.22 seconds. From an altitude of 1,000 feet, 8 seconds will elapse before impact; and this amount of time will increase to 43 seconds (about three-fourths of a minute) if the bomb is dropped from 25,000 feet. 

Every bomb gives some audible warning, although in the smaller sizes ground noise usually blots it out. When maximum air resistance has been developed, however, the bomb case has a tendency to wobble, opposing first one and then another part of its surface at a slight angle to the plane of air pressure. This tendency produces a "slapping" noise, audible under good conditions for but 

2 to 3 seconds before impact. The noise of the so-called "scream" bombs is attained by attaching several small organ-pipe whistles to their fins.

How to Judge Types of High Explosive Bombs 

The classification of high explosive bombs has come as a result of experience in their use. Since important military objectives would scarcely be unprotected, a large amount of explosive (plus a direct hit) is necessary in order to cause their destruction. Bombs for this purpose have a delayed fuse, introducing a time interval between impact and detonation. This gives the bomb time to penetrate the target before bursting. Such weapons, usually of considerable size, are called demolition bombs; and are designed for use against specific targets. Their cases are of steel, and they are often called heavy-case bombs, since less than one fourth of their weight is the explosive charge.

29. 

Technically all bombs designed to destroy by impact, penetration, and explosion are demolition bombs. The improvement of anticraft and the resulting increase in night and high altitude bombing, however, has operated to break off the lighter weights into a separate group. Less effective, of course, against specific targets, their offensive value accrues chiefly from the release of many at one point and from the laws of probability. Dropped within a general target area and without attempt at hitting anything particular, a high percentage is bound to cause a great deal of damage.

Thus, while all bombs from 100 lbs. to 4,000 lbs. may be classed as demolition bombs, present practice has divided this group. Those from 300 kg. (660 lbs.) to 1,800 kg. (4,000 lbs.) are still called demolition bombs and retain their heavy cases. Since only one 1,800 kg. (4,000 lbs.) or two 1,000 kg. (2,200 lbs.) demolition bombs can be carried, even by the largest bombers, smaller sizes are more often used, the 300 kg. (660 lbs.) and the 

500 kg. (1,100 lbs.) being most common.

The lighter end of the scale has come to include what is known as general purpose bombs. They may have either impact or delay fuses. Because there is no certainty of penetration, some saving of steel has resulted from reducing the weight of the case; and so these bombs are often called medium-case bombs. From 25 to 40 per cent of weight is explosive charge, and the most common weights run from 50 kg. (110 lbs.) to 250 kg. (550 lbs.) Some authorities include even lighter weights (down to 20 kg.), but general purpose bombs of less than 50 kg. are not commonly used at present.

30. 

Here too, since the ordinary bomber can carry ten 100 kg. (220 lbs.) or four 250 kg. (550 lbs.) general purpose bombs, as compared with two 500 kg. (1,100 lbs.) or one 1,000 kg. (2,200 lbs.) demolition bombs; and the former are effective against the great majority of targets, general purpose bombs of 100 kg. (220 lbs.) or 250 kg. (550 lbs.) are the types most used for ordinary bombing missions. Except in unusual cases, such as for the spread of a series of fires which have been started in a likely area, the heavier classes of bombs are encountered only on target work against specific objectives. Wardens who are attached to Sectors near such objectives must, of course, be especially alert for heavy attack. 

Weights of 55 pounds and under (10 kg. to 25 kg.) in high explosive bombs have been adapted for taking human life and are known professionally as anti-personnel bombs. They explode upon impact and their light cases are sometimes especially designed to shatter easily, often by being provided with deep parallel ring grooves. They are known as light-case bombs, and have slight powers of penetration. 

One startling design in this class possesses a pair of metal wings which flap open and guide it down.

How to Estimate and Report Bomb Damage 

Having considered how bombs get into our neighborhood and what they are, we may now turn our attention to what they do when they arrive. Six different kinds of destructive force are liberated almost simultaneously: 1. Impact; 2. Penetration; 

3. Blast; 4. Fragmentation; 5. Cratering; 6. Rupture or Mining.

For purposes of study the process is broken down into two parts. 

By using bombs with no explosive charge, the damage due to the shock of impact and penetration may be examined apart from the effects of explosion; and the effects of blast, fragmentation, cratering, and rupture can be determined by static tests, in which the bomb is placed in various positions with respect to the target and exploded in place.

From the results ascertained we may form a destruction pattern for each force which will include the various possibilities likely to be encountered. As a basis for this pattern, we may take a bomb hitting a flat surface in the open air.

32. 

Disruption due to impact, while often considerable, is limited to a circle of not more than fourteen feet diameter, extending laterally from the point where the bomb hit. Penetration due to impact will not exceed 40 feet in earth for the heaviest bomb.

Impact damage is swallowed up by that which results from detonation, the only surviving trace being the added depth of penetration due to impact velocity. This depth will not exceed 75 feet in earth for the heaviest bomb.

The pressure created by the explosion in the air and the flying bits of the burst bomb case are the greatest dangers to both persons and property from high explosive. The first of these forces is known as blast, and is essentially a shock wave travelling out from the point of burst at the stupendous speed of some 4,700 miles per hour. This initial pressure phase is followed by a negative suction phase, less intense but of longer duration. 

Initial blast pressure of 24 lbs. per sq. inch at 30 feet drops quickly to 0.4 lb. per sq. inch at 200 feet. The flying bits of the metal bomb case are known as fragments, and their dispersal under force of detonation as fragmentation. From 2,000 to 7,000 separate pieces of metal have been counted for general purpose bombs commonly used. At fifty feet, their velocities lie within 

2,500 to 5,000 feet per second, the higher figure being well over twice the speed of the bullet from a high-power, modern rifle. 

So great are the forces of blast and fragmentation that they must be classed with that of lightning. All three are unpredictable in their behavior. The danger zone in case of blast is less than that of fragmentation, being in the vicinity of 1,000

33. feet for the largest bomb. The danger zone from flying fragments is 3,000 feet. The effective range of fragments however is about half that, or 1,500 feet, while blast pressure falls off rapidly after 500 feet.

Cratering and Rupture, other effects of the explosion, occur in earth; and are the result of the transmission of a shock wave upward and outward, in the former case, and laterally through the earth in the latter. The limit of effective rupture force lies, for the heaviest bomb, some 87 feet from the point of detonation.

These six destructive forces which have been investigated represent the ultimate in bombing possibilities. 

The ideal, of course, would be to procure them all, at peak efficiency, in one bomb; but this is not possible. The expert must make a selective choice among them, and the means by which this is done is by their fusing. 

Considerable work is being done on fuses, and very possibly new bombing effects may result; and Air Raid Wardens during a raid should keep this fact in mind. Knowing the characteristics of bombs and the present limitations in their behaviour, he may safely put down anything radically new in their action to some new method of fus g.

These are the impact fuse, the delay fuse, and be borne in mind. the magnetic fuse. Of these the latter has been encountered only to a very limited degree; but Wardens should not approach an unexploded bomb with metal about them. The impact fuse is designed to detonate the explosive upon contact with the target; the delay fuse allows a time interval for the bomb to penetrate its target. 

It is in their relation to the destructive forces of which bombs are capable that fuses control the selection of effects. 

Impact destruction takes place regardless of the type of fuse, since its force is independent of the explosive charge. With an impact fuse, the destructive force of the explosive would simply be added to that of flight velocity; and the total effect would be all of the same general nature. With a delay fuse, however, there would be impact destruction followed by penetration below the surface, after which detonation would produce cratering and rupture. In this case two general types of destruction have taken place, one type due to flight velocity and the other due to detonation of the explosive. While it is true that the first damage tends to be swallowed up in the second, an instance might occur wherein a Warden felt quite safe with an unexploded bomb covered with debris because there had been destruction on the spot. 

Under the selective influence of fusing, therefore, two broad choices are open with the same bomb. First, use of the impact fuse, in which case there will be impact, slight penetration or none, blast, fragmentation, no cratering and no rupture. Thus the impact fuse has picked out impact, blast, and fragmentation,

35. destruction from the force of which will be the chief effects of this particular bomb. But by use of the delay fuse on the same bomb, the picture may be radically altered. We should then have impact, penetration, little blast, little fragmentation, cratering, and rupture. Thus the delay fuse has selected impact, cratering, and rupture. From a knowledge of the six destructive forces which are possible in all bombs and from an understanding of the selective function of fuses, the Warden can tell with what type he has to deal after it has disappeared from the scene in gas and small pieces.

For determining the size of the explosive, he may begin by putting the destruction from a small bomb at one end of the scale and that from a large bomb at the other; experience will suggest the gradients in between. When a typical 500 lb. medium-case bomb with a delay fuse falls at a steep angle on a many-storied building having a roof and floors of re-enforced concrete each six inches thick, it can go through the roof and five floors before exploding provided it does not break up and is not deflected by steel girders. 

The total thickness penetrated by the bomb is thus about 36 inches. 

If the bomb falls in a congested area, instead of in the open air, the different surfaces which offer resistance within the area of the explosion all reflect the pressure wave set up. 

These of course are not predictable, and a bomb explosion in a confined area will always tend to give chaotic and freakish effects. 

The various reflected waves from the impact of the shock-wave on obstacles, having both a positive and a negative component, can either neutralize or enhance themselves according to circumstances

36. when they collide with one another.

Thus the presence of an undamaged electric light in a completely wrecked house may be explained.

Since the more a high explosive charge is confined during its detonation, the greater will be the blast destruction, it follows that when the explosion occurs within a building, the resulting damage will be much greater than when the explosion takes place in the open air. In general, the more resistant the walls of the building, the greater the destruction. A building with heavy walls and small windows will suffer more damage than one with lighter walls and large window areas.

Types of Precautionary Measures and Their Value 

Having considered the nature of high explosive bombs and the destruction they cause, we turn now to inquire what may be done for the protection of persons and property against them. Since the light-case general purpose bomb does not require a direct hit to produce widespread damage, and since more of them can be carried by the average bomber, it is not only the most probably form of air attack but also the most serious threat to the civil population. 

Complete protection against the largest bombs would involve the interposition of at least 75 to 80 feet of earth or 12 to 15 feet of re-enforced concrete between the bomb and its target. Since such a provision is scarcely practical upon a universal scale, the

42. protection of a possible target must be considered: 

1. In terms of liability to air attack; 2. In terms of vulnerability to air attack; 3. In terms of value to the nation. The direct purpose of protection of property is in order that the vital services of the community may not be interrupted, such as water, power, light, transportation, communication, and the production of food. The direct purpose of protection of persons is in order that civilian morale shall be maintained, and that the members of the community shall be able to go about their ordinary duties. 

The best protection to property against impact destruction is the law of probability. In large towns about one half the area covered is open ground; and about thirty per cent moro is taken up by streets. Definition: Pressure created by explosion in the air which surrounds the explosion itself. 

Characteristics: Upon detonation the bomb case swells to 1 1/2 times its normal size and bursts. The explosion gases (including carbon monoxide and prussic acid gas) travel outward and upward for about 25 feet, at which point the shock-wave is transmitted to the surrounding air. This initial positive pressure phase is followed by a negative suction, phase of much less intensity but longer duration. The total duration of blast is not more than .030 second, of which the positive phase lasts about .005 second and the negative about .025 second.

The pressures produced are high, but fall off rapidly beyond a distance of 30 ft. PERSONAL PROTECTION AGAINST WAR GASES Behavior and Effects of War Gases 

The term "gas", when used in connection with chemical warfare, includes any chemical substance, whether solid, liquid, or gas, which is employed in war for its poisonous or irritant effects upon the human body. Several considerations serve to limit 

Its vapor pressure must be high the practical usefulness of this form of attack. In addition to producing the physiological action desired, the substance must be relatively cheap and capable of practical and economical production from available raw materials. enough to give working concentrations under ordinary conditions, and it must be comparatively stable and inactive so as not to be too rapidly destroyed by wind or moisture. substances have been examined for use as war gases; but of these only about 54 were ever actually used in the field. Of this number, moreover, only 12 proved reasonably effective. 

Some 3,000 to 4,000

Even with such a gas, the amount necessary to contaminate a large city is so great as to make it unlikely that a serious attack of this kind may be expected. Approximately 26 grams are needed per square meter for a dangerous concentration, or about 75 tons for each square mile. For a successful gas attack, therefore, upon the city of London, some 5,750 tons of gas would require at least 5,750 aircraft and some 23,000 personnel, with no allowance for reserves. Within the limits of practical use, therefore, protection against gas is easier to obtain than in the case of either high explosives or incendiary bombs.

53. 

Perhaps the greatest difficulty encountered in the search for efficient war gases has to do with their vapor pressures, which determine not only their maximum concentrations under given climatic conditions, but also the rate of evaporation of the liquid gas. 

The higher the vapor pressure the faster the evaporation (and the resulting dissipation in air); and the lower the vapor pressure the slower the rate of evaporation and the greater the persistence (and the safer the process of decontamination). The practical characteristics of gas use in war are thus opposed to the ideal, since it is impossible to have a high, rapid concentration of vapor coupled with long, continued heavy contamination of one area. If the high, rapid concentration of vapor is a characteristic, the effects of the gas (while very possibly severe) are only temporary at any one spot; and the gas itself is called non-persistent. the other hand, if the effects of the gas last a long time at any one spot, the vapor is not likely to be highly concentrated; and the gas is called persistent.

On The non-persistent war gases do not harm the skin of the human body; and their effects, while often causing some degree of permanent injury to the respiratory system, are soon diminished by dilution with air. Complete protection is afforded by any modern type of gas mask.

Steps Taken by the Warden before Reporting Gas

The presence of war gas may be detected in a number of ways: 

1. By smell; 2. By sight; 3. By physiological effects; 4. By chemical test. Tear gases are easily detected by their effect on the eyes. As some of them are persistent and consist of dark brown liquids, care must be taken not to confuse them with blister gas contaminations. Nose irritant gases are hard to detect other than by their effects, since they are all odorless. On the other hand, the lung irritants all have a characteristic odor. The persistent blister gases, and mustard gas in particular, are much more difficult to recognize and much more dangerous. The Warden must know as much as possible about gas behavior; but he will have to fall back, in time of need, upon the expert chemist, some of whom will form a Gas Identification Service.

What the Warden does in a Gas Attack

Once the presence of some gas is reasonably certain, the Warden must report to Center at once, indicating the point and what facts he may have verified by that time. It may be a gas he will have no trouble in identifying, but if he has any doubt whatever the decision as to action by the Report Center will depend on his making that doubt clear, in order that an expert may be sent if necessary. Persons residing in the Sector if they have their gas masks will have been instructed to remain indoors and to close doors and windows when the gas alarm is sounded. They would take refuge în whatever room had been prepared for that purpose. If they do not have gas masks and if in addition the refuge room is on the basement level, they will be in danger. They must either leave the area, if the gas is still at some distance; or they must retire to an upper level. If doors and windows are closed and reasonably tight, at a height of twenty-five to thirty feet persons without gas masks would be safe for some time. If evacuation becomes necessary in a heavy attack, the Warden will notify them. 

Meantime the Warden will have his gas mask on the alert as he returns to the point where the gas is discovered. Since gases and vapors tend to rise as they drift downwind, smell tests should be taken near the ground in an effort to locate the source of contamination. Broad distinctions will be possible between the nonpersistent gases, the persistent tear-gases, and the persistent blister gases. The non-persistent gases will afford little or no evidence of liquid contamination, but will be indicated by irritation of the eyes in the case of the tear-gases, by nose and throat

63. irritation in the case of the arsenical smokes, and by some characteristic irritant smell in the case of the lung irritants. 

The persistent tear-gases will be distinguished from the persistent blister gases by intense watering of the eyes, while the blister gases will present evidence of liquid contamination and some characteristic odor, but no marked eye irritation. The Warden may be able to act on the basis of his own judgment to save lives before special help comes, for if blister gas is present the area of contamination must be established and roped off at once, and any buildings downwind evacuated promptly.

It must constantly be borne in mind that well-known gases may be mixed together to mask detection, and that new gases may be present. "Swindle gas", relatively harmless but smelly, may be encountered full of nothing more poisonous than mercaptan, bone oil, or balloon dope. When in doubt, the Warden should always report promptly to Center; if left to his own judgment he should always be prepared for the worst.

EMERGENCY FIRST-AID AND THE WARDEN'S RELATION TO THE MEDICAL SERVICES The Problem of Air-Raid Casualties 

It is generally agreed that proper attention to Air Raid casualties places an unusual burden upon first aiders. Under ordinary conditions, the demand upon them for diagnosis is slight and their conditions of work at least passable if not always satisfactory. 

War has changed that. Air attack has raised the problem of large numbers of injured victims who must have attention at one time; it has compelled the first-aider to work often in the dark; and it has developed its offensive power so far that, more times than we like to think, only those powers of diagnosis and judgment which the first aider may have at his command will stand between men, women, and children and sudden, certain death. 

The adoption of mobile first aid posts which will bring the doctor nearer to the victim is, of course, a frank copy of army practice in the field. Yet this does not solve the problem. 

Military medicine may count on the high incidence, although in great variety, of a few classifications of victims. They do not have to count on women and children. 

Air raid casualties offer a very grave problem indeed to the powers of the medical profession to organize, to improvise, and to invent. Around sixty percent of the stretcher cases coming to first aid posts will require immediate operation, and in two-thirds of those cases it will be to save life or limb. Urgent surgery will be called for in respect to the arrest of hemorrhage from large

67. vessels, the amputation of hanging limbs, and the closure of an open thorax. The importance of carbon monoxide poisoning from high explosive has probably not been sufficiently realized, nor the dangers from this source to those in cellars or under-ground shelters as the result of a nearby explosion of a large mine or Experience in Spain has shown that the enormous quantity of carbon monoxide produced may find its way under pressure even through the soil. bomb. 

The record of air raid casualties indicates that victims are either very severely or quite lightly injured. The number of seriously injured, however, is far larger than that of slight cases, and their subsequent history presents a gloomy picture. In spite of the excellent work of rescue and demolition parties, 35 percent of those trapped in collapsing buildings die. Of those victims sustaining amputations made necessary by wound or other injury, 25 percent die. Of those with compound fractures 15 percent die, owing chiefly to associated lesions. 60 percent of those with operated abdominal wounds do not survive, not counting victims found thus wounded but dead. Chest wounds are very fatal; 68 percent die from hemorrhage, shock, or infection. The general mortality rate of all the wounded admitted to the hospitals of Barcelona, the slight with the serious, was approximately 45 percent, not counting those either blown into oblivion or trapped beyond recovery.

The line between success and failure is drawn very thin for first-aid service when figures run as high as these. Of the lightly, badly, or gravely wounded, what percentages will survive?

68. 

How will the figures compare with those for similar injuries in time of peace? And in the case of those who recover, what degree of delay if any, will their periods of recovery show when compared with those of peace-time patients?

The problem of seriously wounded casualties, very possibly in some numbers, can be met only by thinking of first-aid not in terms of numbers which is fairly easy, but in terms of quality which is far more difficult. When all reasonable essentials for recovery are present, and yet despite them the war casualty is not saved, experience points time and time again to a single factor as the chief cause of fatality. It is during evacuation that the damage is done, either by reason of delay or bad transportation. raid casualty's life may depend upon three processes: 

The air first-aid, to arrest immediate danger; classification, to ensure correct priority and destination; and final surgical treatment. In military medicine, the doctor has been concerned ch: efly with the last of these. Mobile first aid posts in our Air Raid Precautions

Organization now make him available for the work of classification, the second of these. He can scarcely be counted upon, however, for the first.

The elimination of any middle grade of injury in the case of air raid casualties places an unusual burden upon the first-aider. 

Since a fairly high level of medical judgment is now essential at each of the three stages, the responsibilities of the first aider or stretcher-bearer are comparable to those of the doctor. Indeed, numerically they may be greater, for a careless surgeon can hardly kill more than a dozen men in the course of the day, whereas a careless stretcher-bearer can kill a hundred.

The Warden's Responsibility for Casualties 

It will not be surprising if the Air Raid Warden finds many problems to think about in respect to his responsibility for casualties. Often he will be first on the scene, and while he may feel confident in dealing with fires and the collapse of buildings it will appear to him that in respect to casualties, only an experienced surgeon would do. 

Since this question appears to be of no little importance, it should be studied with some care. With an efficient and an ample first-aid casualty service in action, it is obvious that the first question to ask is how soon could a first-aid squad arrive and what could happen to the victim or victims in the meantime? If the 

Warden is on patrol during a raid and with a companion, the conditions are probably most favorable for a quick arrival on the scene of an incident. Under these circumstances, if it takes five minutes to reach the most distant point in the Sector, it will probably take at least two minutes on the average to reach the scene of a bombing. There must be a thorough search of the scene to determine accurately the number and situation of casualties. 

Not more than a minute for two wardens can be allowed for this purpose. At this point three minutes have been taken to reach the scene and to check on victims, and at this point also the Warden who is to remain will begin his first-aid. 

Next one man must return to the Post and telephone a report to the Report Center. If it takes five minutes to reach the most distant point, return to Post from the scene of an incident will certainly take half that on the average, or 2 1/2 minutes. Allowing 1/2 minute for the report to Center, six minutes have elapsed.

70. 

The medical representative at the Report Center must receive a copy of the report which will take say another 1/2 minute. He must call the First-Aid Depot nearest the incident, and order out a squad or a mobile post. It will certainly be another 1/2 minute before the necessary service leaves the Depot. Seven minutes have now gone by.

The distance from the Depot to the incident will vary considerably, but it will be usually longer than any distance within the Sector. Counting the hazards of travel during or after a raid, it is probable that an average driving time of two minutes will be conservative. Within that period of time a victim could bleed to death from severe hemorrhage; or, from large wounds, have passed from slight initial shock well on the road to severe shock which might be fatal. 

Furthermore, if wounded badly in the chest, the first five of those minutes might so have worsened his condition that at the end of ten the victim would be dead. Within that period of time, also, parts of buildings could collapse upon victims; or further bombing could remove any need for first-aid at all. Wind, rain, and darkness might all play an unfavorable part in the grim battle. 

It is evident that the Air Raid Warden cannot be idle, for what is or is not done may mean the difference between life and

Conditions of Work 

Additional difficulties arise. It is not unreasonable to inquire under what conditions casualties will be found and under what conditions the Warden's examination of their injuries will be made. The answer to both these questions, very often, is the worst. 

Victims may be partly buried under debris or superficially trapped; they may be hysterical or unconscious; they will usually be fully dressed; and they will be likely to be covered with the dust of the explosion. It will often be dark, and what little light can be used to help will be merely an aggravation. is Another pertinent question to ask, while we are about it, what the Warden may expect to find once an examination has been made. Again the answer in something over half the cases is the worst. Perhaps it would be a good idea to try to find out what injuries are likely to do to people, and why they are dangerous. 

Injury itself can prove fatal, for a bomb explosion may blow a person into bits. Usually, however, it is not directly a cause of death. The results of the injury are the cause of fatality, and those of primary danger are four: Hemorrhage, Asphyxiation, Poisoning, and Shock.

72. 

Causes of Fatality 

Hemorrhage means loss of blood. 

Whenever the skin is broken blood flows, and if it does not stop of its own accord someone must stop it or the victim may die from loss of blood. Attempts to classify hemorrhage as arterial, venous, and capillary are not likely to cover all instances. 

A wound of any size usually bleeds from all three at once, and the control of bleeding has to be tackled as a whole. 

Except in rare instances, bleeding from one type of vessel only is not to be seen.

Tourniquets 

A deep wound will cut through many blood vessels, but in most cases pressure upon the nearest pressure-point will allow dressing and bandaging after which the pressure may be released. should not be used unless all other means have failed except in case of wounds of sufficient extent or very serious bleeding so located that it appears at once the only means likely to be effective. Such external bleeding is not difficult to deal with, and prompt action will usually save life. Not all loss of blood, however, is external; the most dangerous type of hemorrhage is internal. In cases of this kind, there still may be visible signs. 

Bleeding of the lungs is indicated by bright red, frothy blood at the mouth; and abdominal bleeding sometimes by vomited blood from the stomach which looks like coffee grounds being dark brown in color. Bleeding from the brain in concussions may be indicated by a drop or two from the ears or nose.

However, much internal bleeding is concealed; consequently there are no visible signs at all. A very small metal splinter

73. moving at high speed may cause a lot of bleeding inside the body although the puncture in the skin is hard to find. The condition of the victim soon shows his loss of blood, and these signs of internal hemorrhage are of the greatest importance. 

He is pale, especially the lips and the lobes of the ears. Some sweating will be found, and the skin is cold and clammy. 

Breathing is quick with sighing respiration, and the pulse becomes rapid and weak, and may disappear at the wrist. He may gasp for air, become dizzy, or even unconscious. If conscious, he will be restless; his vision will become dimmer; and he will hear buzzing noises in his ears. 

The one most important point about the treatment of internal bleeding which must be remembered is to give no drinks whatsoever. 

If the chest area is involved, smoking may cause coughing and increase the bleeding. All hemorrhage cases must be watched carefully to be sure bleeding is under control where it is possible. 

Tourniquets, if used, must be loosened every 15 minutes and then re-tightened if fresh bleeding occurs. Dressings soaked through should not be removed, but a new one applied over them.

Asphyxiation means stoppage of breathing. It may be mechanical as when, in severe chest wounds which let in air, the vacuum in the lung cavity is destroyed causing it to collapse. It may be chiefly chemical as when the vapors of industrial or war gases are inhaled instead of pure air and poison the lung tissue. Or it may be chiefly physiological, as when phosgene or blister gas vapor sets up an inflammation so severe that cellular fluids collect and exclude oxygen.

Artificial respiration must not be attempted until the victim has been examined for symptoms of lung damage. It is very often

74. found in asphyxiation, and a condition originally bad will be made worse by forced breathing.

Poisoning means the introduction into the body of an agent which produces chemically an injurious or deadly effect. Closely allied to poisoning is Infection which means the introduction into the body of a disease-producing substance, such as germs or bacteria. 67 percent of deaths from femur fractures in the German 

Army, 1914-18, were from infection. Poisoning can be counteracted often by dilution or vomiting; Infection frequently calls for the surgeon. 

Shock means pronounced lowering of the rate of vital processes, notably blood pressure. It is always a most serious condition and may, by itself, cause death. It is always present to some degree in injuries. In well-established cases there is an anxious expression, thirst, vomiting, and sweating. Respiration is shallow. These symptoms are accompanied by a considerable fall in the blood pressure; the pulse rate rises to 120 to 160 per minute.

Temperature is subnormal. Some authorities regard shock onset as inaugurated by over-stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system, a loosely organized series of nerves which maintain blood pressure and control the heart rate. The chief factors associated with it are: pain, loss of blood, loss of body heat, and absorption of poisons into the blood. Anything done to reduce any one of these factors will reduce or prevent shock. After control of bleeding, heat, and hot drinks in small amounts are most important, unless hot drinks are forbidden by abdominal wounds or internal bleeding.

Types of Injuries 

Hemorrhage, Asphyxiation, Poisoning, and Shock may be produced by the following types of injuries: 

Wounds (Breaking of the skin): 

Always result in shock proportional to their size and severity; may also result in hemorrhage, asphyxiation, and infection. 

Any one of the four may be fatal. 

Fractures (Breaking of a bone): Always result in shock, frequently severe; may also result in hemorrhage, and infection. Any one of the three may be fatal.

Burns (Destruction of skin and perhaps deeper parts of the body): Always result in shock, severe to the extent of the burn, and poisoning from dead tissue; may also result in infection. Any one of the three may be fatal.

Contamination or Poisoning (Effects of war gases or industrial gases): May result in shock and poisoning; may also result in infection. Any one of the three may be fatal.

As will be understood from this analysis, it is not the injury itself which usually causes a dangerous condition or even death. 

It is rather the results to the body functions made possible by the injury. Thus hemorrhage, or other serious danger to life, is a primary cause of death if not controlled; a wound on the other hand is scarcely such a primary cause (since it often results in no hemorrhage) but rather a secondary one.

Let us now attempt to come to close quarters with air raid casualtics. We know that such forms of attack mean the use of high explosive, the collapse of buildings. We know that the use of incendiary bombs means fires and the destruction of industrial establishments. We can anticipate the possible use of war gases.

Burning Buildings 

(With or without Incendiaries) 

Fire May result in: 

Burns: This type of injury differs from wounds in that it destroys area rather than depth, and that except in very minor cases it destroys utterly. 

Even superficial burns expose the nerve endings and cause intense pain. Burns therefore are accompanied by shock in proportion to their area. Morcover, the destruction and death of tissue means the beginning of the penetration of resulting poisons into the blood. Burns are particularly liable to infection. 

If tannic acid jelly is applied, it forms a coagulated coating which hardens and affords protection to nerve endings. Poisons are also held in its texture, and escaping fluids are sealed. The treatment is dangerous to nerve endings of especial delicacy, such as those of the finger tips.

There seems little doubt but that the use of tannic acid preparations by the first aider is unwise, unless he is especially trained in their use by a qualified physician who knows the limitations of the method. Such

81. training will be provided if it becomes necessary; in the meantime prompt arrival of mobile posts will bring doctors to the scene. Small burns will not require final treatment by the first aider who may use sterile gauze pads moistened with a 2% baking soda solution. Large burns should be treated in the same way by the first aider since this method is that least likely to hinder or to slow up final treatment by medical personnel.

Scalds: This type of injury is likely to be caused by exploding boilers. It is caused by steam, and is a form of burn. 

Asphyxiation: from smoke. Victim should be kept quiet, and prevented from exerting himself. Fresh air and rest with treatment for shock will be the extent of first aid.

Acid burns: These will occur occasionally from fires in industrial plants. Any alkali which will neutralize the acid will clean out the burned area. Soap and water, or ordinary household ammonia diluted with a little warm water may be used.

Molten metal burns: These are extremely painful and may be serious. Phosphorus and magnesium burns may be found, and about all which can be done in a hurry is to immerse the burn in water. Sodium and potassium burns should be immersed in kerosene.

War Gases 

Chemical Vapors. The use of gas in warfare is less dangerous than the use of any other weapon; out of 

100 gas casualties, only 2 will be fatal; while from 100 casualties from other weapons, 24 will dic. 

The tear gases or eye irritants possess only temporary effects, and persons in contact with them should be removed to fresh air, facing the wind.

The nose irritants may cause pronounced mental depression; otherwise their effects, though often severe, are temporary. Persons in contact with them should be treated for shock and removed to fresh air. Both these gases are non-persistent types. The vapors of the lethal agents and of the blister gases both have injurious effects which may be long-continued if severe. The lethal agents are considered by some authorities to be oxidizing agents which remove oxygen from tissue; the blister gas vapors dissolve out fats and cause severe inflammation. Persons in contact with high concentrations of these gases should not be allowed to stand up, and should be transported by stretcher. They can be treated for shock. No artificial respiration should be given.

82. 

Chemical liquids. The persistent tear gases and the blister gases are liquids of similar appearances in use. Persistent tear gas liquid will injure the eyes if it comes into contact with them. Liquid blister gases are highly injurious to all parts of the body. They dissolve out fats and destroy body tissue, acting in much the same way as a bad burn. 

Persons in contact with them should apply anti-gas ointment (a paste of chloride of lime with water or white vaseline) if that can be done within five minutes of contamination. This application should be removed after one minute and care should be taken to keep it out of the eyes. Do not apply the ointment after five minutes, or if the skin has already become reddened. Instead a hot bath with plenty of soap and water is the only treatment. In any event, liquid blister gas will contaminate clothing, which should be removed as soon as possible. 

All gas casualties should be kept quiet and be reported to the medical authority.

This attempt to classify the injuries which the Air Raid Warden will be likely to encounter among air raid casualties has produced a list of some twenty-nine types of disaster which may result to the human body. Furthermore experience with air raid casualties among the civilian population wherever there has been any is unanimous in the opinion that severe injury is more likely to be met with than light injury. First aid in wartime, even among civilians, may therefore be said to differ radically from first aid in peace. Injuries are different and so are problems of transport and ultimate disposal. While a knowledge of peace-time first aid is of the greatest value, certain additions are required to bring it into line with the requirements of modern air raid conditions. 

This adaptation of first aid practice is of peculiar concern to the Warden, since he will be likely to be the first on the scene of casualty, and since he will be responsible for the measures taken within the first seven to fifteen minutes. In considering the

83. co-ordination of his activities, the fact that a first-aid squad will arrive at the end of that time should be borne in mind. 

Particular attention must be paid to those aspects of the situation which are likely to interfere with successful first-aid. The 

Warden must be responsible for deciding which victims cannot be helped at all beyond keeping them quiet and warm. The decision whether they are fatally hurt he can avoid, but he must not be so impressed with massive injury as to waste time. Large lacerations, large puncture wounds, severe crushing wounds, and extensive and complicated compound fractures are not for the first-aider; and any extensive measures he undertakes on those injuries will largely have to be done over by the medical staff. Beyond control of bleeding and the prevention of additional infection the first-aider should not go ordinarily. Finally, the conditions of work are apt to defeat common sense and to militate against sound judgment. 

Everything which has gone before makes it plain that firstaid training for the Air Raid Warden, and that of the highest possible quality, is not only necessary but imperative. The standard course of the American Red Cross is the fundamental training for all wardens to have. But until training specifically for them can be secured, special care should be taken to include as wardens those who have had wide experience in treating casualties. 

Fire Department rescue squads are one source of experienced firstaiders which must not be neglected. Those who may have been hospital orderlies or who have qualified to teach first aid for the American Red Cross are another.

Air raid casualty first-aid will have to be something of a

84. specialized field. In the training of wardens, definite firstaid instruction must be provided covering the first seven to fifteen minutes during which the warden will be left alone to deal with an unknown number of injured persons whose condition will become in some cases worse or fatal unless treated promptly. This instruction must be based upon the assumption that the first-aid squad will always arrive within a period of about ten minutes, and that nothing must be done by the warden which can be done better by the squad. Neither must he begin any measure which will interfere with or make harder the work of the doctor or surgeon. 

In planning the Warden's activities for the relief of casualties, it would be well to point out that in some instances no emergency will occur. The incidence of injury accompanying a bombing might run too light or too heavy. One occupant of a building might turn up with a cut on his thumb, for example; or twelve occupants might all be quite definitely and thoroughly dead. Then, too, some examples would be forthcoming where any number of people were injured, some perhaps seriously, but no problem arose. Aside from minor treatment of one kind or another, the severe cases were all of such a nature that time was not a factor in their relief.

Diagnosis, Classification, Disposal

The three necessities in any casualty service are first, diagnosis; second, classification; and third, initial disposal. 

These three functions put the following questions: first, what 

Second, how soon is he to be is the matter with the victim? treated? Third, where is the treatment going to be given? These three functions of casualty service bear upon medical service at every point from the site of the injury to the final arrangements made for the patient.

They must be taken into consideration by every member of the Air Raid Precautions Organization who expects to have anything to do with seriously injured persons.

Diagnosis will begin as an instinctive process whenever the first-aider finds himself with two victims. By reason of his training, he will probably be led to treat the more serious case first, provided the seriousness is obvious. In many cases it is not. Exterior wounds are misleading; extensive and visible injury is often not so serious as obscure injury not discovered; and in many cases the dangerous hazard is shock which will not develop until somewhat later. However, the first-aid squad cannot proceed to classification and disposal of casualties until a diagnosis has been made. In this, the Warden may act as their advance agent.

86. 

Some exposure of the wounded part is definitely required, but the first rule is that examination must not further disturb and injure the victim. Precautions should also be taken to keep the area as sterile as possible. Blackened wounds usually indicate bomb fragments, and commonly there are two, the entry wound and the exit wound. All wounds from the neck to the knees must be examined with care to make sure that body cavities have not been penetrated. 

To make this decision is often impossible; and when so, if the victim's condition is poor, the worst should be assumed to be the case.

The general condition of the patient will sometimes help and sometimes hinder diagnosis, and the best results probably come from a consideration of that and the definite evidences of injury together and what they may each imply. All patients who have lost blood are thirsty, but no liquids should ever be given until their internal condition has been checked if there is the least reason to anticipate injury within the body.

Classification is based upon diagnosis and determines the sorting of casualties for first treatment, first transportation, and place of disposal, whether home, first-aid post, or hospital. 

When casualties occur in any numbers not only is classification difficult, but the consequences of error are serious. Since the first judgment is apt to be acted on by those persons who treat the victim all down the line, the Warden will face a considerable responsibility. If it is one he can assume with any competence, that much of the problem faced by the first-aid squad will have been solved when they arrive, and precious time will have been saved.

87. 

As a general rule, hemorrhage requires priority of treatment, and when it cannot be controlled priority of transportation. 

Priority of transportation should always be given to: internal hemorrhage, abdominal wounds, chest wounds, pelvic injuries, and compound fractures of the thigh bone. With disposal, the first-aid squad and the doctor at the post will deal. Thus the Warden as advance agent, if he proceeds carefully and competently, can start a course of treatment which, picked up by the first-aid squad, will carry on through review by the doctor and routing to the hospital. In that happy event, the victim will have had a minimum of handling, will have secured for himself the maximum of speed, and will have, therefore, the best chance possible for recovery. 

In order to insure his ability to deal with casualty situations which present a serious problem, the Warden's training in first-aid should be directed specifically toward the period of roughly ten minutes during which he must act and act alone in most cases. It is therefore necessary to provide a framework within which his efforts may be directed, chiefly as the advance agent of the firstaid squad. He should know the fundamentals of first-aid certainly, but it is useless to teach him traction splinting which one man cannot do and to neglect internal hemorrhage which he must learn to recognize. The Warden's whole point of view about first-aid should be after the pattern in which he will be called on to use it and this will mean a generous part of his time will be allotted to diagnosis under unfavorable conditions. We must plan that initial ten minutes, emphasizing as its general purpose attention to steps necessary to save life and avert further injury, steps necessary to

1 Individuals engaged in rescue work and first aid treatment or marked physical exertion of any sort are often more severely affected by toxic gases than those who are overcome in the absence of such exertion. Chlorine, Chloropicrin, and Phosgene

In lethal concentrations chloropicrin is about four times, and phosgene practically ten times more poisonous than chlorine; and while chlorine and chloropicrin cause more damage to the respiratory passages, phosgene is more effective in the production of pulmonary edema. The essential effects are pulmonary edema, rupture of small blood vessels in the lungs, increased concentration and viscosity of the blood, with a tendency to heart failure. 

1. Respiratory tract is first to be affected, with immediate sensory irritation and catching of the breath, accompanied by a cough and a sensation of constriction and pain in the chest. Next follows gasping respirations interrupted by fits of violent coughing.

Any attempt to draw a deep breath gives rise to painful discomfort; retching and vomiting are prominent features in the early stages, and headache and a profound sense of fatigue often prostrate the victim. 

First-Aid: Treatment should begin promptly upon diagnosis of gassing by a pulmonary irritant. Rest, both physical and mental, must be complete. Warmth is essential and diet should be light. 

Except for the relief of symptoms such as cough, a good purgative of salt solution is the only active measure indicated. The doctor will have to make preparations for the administration of oxygen, and for venous bleeding as soon as signs of lung injury appear. 

These will be increase in breathlessness, increased pulse and respiration rates, commencing blueness of ears and face, copious frothy expectoration. 

2. THE WARDEN AND WAR-TIME FAMILY LIFE The Warden's Place in American Life 

In meeting the challenge of the national emergency, the Commonwealth has sought the protection of its citizens. To secure that protection the air raid Wardens organization has been established. It will be, of course, something new in American life. 

Partly for that reason it is necessary to emphasize that the Warden is a purely community officer. In no way has either the state or the national government stepped in to interfere with individual life or family freedom.

As a community officer, the Warden is appointed by the chief Warden in each locality; and all Wardens are responsible to the local Defense Committee. However, an isolated community would scarcely be able to safeguard its own security unless it could rely upon the information and help which it would have to have from its neighbors. On its administrative side, therefore, the community 

Civilian Defense organization is closely tied in with other local groups to form district, region, and state units, all of which depend on one another for common protection. 

What the air raid Warden will mean in American life cannot be foretold, but it is very likely that he will come to signify in respect to the hazards against which he seeks our protection just about what the policeman signifies in respect to crime. Like all schemes to secure protection, when they are working effectively, they never seem to be necessary at all. Most of us are so accustomed to rely upon the police and fire service that we have ceased

102. to possess any comprehension of the powerful and far-reaching organization which, alone, makes that service possible. 

The considerable extent of the Warden's powers in time of war will be an important factor in all civil defense measures. Not only will his training provide skilled assistance covering the whole network of cities and towns; but, on active duty, he is bound to be a co-ordinating force helping all the services of the community to maintain a high operating level. Furthermore the fact of his local responsibility in the sector where he is assigned will lighten the heavy load destined to fall upon the police and fire departments. 

Important as these aids will be, it is rather in service to the homes of the Commonwealth that the Warden's most lasting contribution to the national effort can be made. 

War is a disruptive force and air raids upon the civilian population are meant to bring that disruptive force to bear upon the most important element in our national economy, our homes and families. A progressive lessening of the standards of domestic existence, whether they be physical, mental, or moral, is a progressive defcat of American democracy by the enemy. Against this unhappy conclusion stands our readiness for self-defense; in that self-defense on the home front the Warden is our leader.

Types of Blackout and Their Control 

The shutting off of all light visible outside during the hours of darkness is another and very important element of self-defense. 

The regulation and control of blackout will be a very difficult problem. Under ordinary conditions of war, three different procedures may be ordered at any time: 

1. Normal lighting conditions, with immediate blackout on a yellow warning. 

2. Partial blackout, partial and reduced exterior illumination. Complete blackout on yellow warning. 

3. Complete blackout within specified hours of darkness.

It is never necessary in time of peace to turn out all the outside lights in any large area of city, town, or country quickly. 

Consequently, no provision exists in the layout of electric power transmission by which this can be done. The control of electricity at its source, while the easiest method, would rob public services of power and cut off both power and light in office buildings, hospitals, civil defense centers, fire stations, and police stations. This step is obviously not desirable. 

The alternative is electric power under strict regulation. 

Provision will have to be made to turn off all outdoor lights, and those interior lights kept burning will have to be so shaded as not to be visible from the outside. Controlled lighting is perhaps more nearly what the Commonwealth will experience, but the black nights of London have already added the word blackout to the American vocabulary. The fact is, however, that both the quantity and quality of light permitted will be subject to regulation by the civil defense authorities. 

The three procedures mentioned are

105. merely three general conditions as to light in the area; what actually would occur would depend upon the situation developing and the conditions prevailing at the time. 

The immediate blacking out of light upon receiving an air raid warning, involving as it does rapid change from normal lighting to darkness, will require widespread, simultaneous, co-ordinated action. 

In the city of Boston, for example, there are not less than 14,000 exterior circuits to be opened; and in addition about 9,000 gas streetlights.

It would be prudent for the Warden to assume the initiative where the exterior lights in his Sector are concerned. It may be that he will have certain switches to open when a warning is received. On the other hand, it may be that the control lies at some other point. If the latter is the case, the Warden should find out where; and whether alternative means exist for cutting off the electricity.

A systematic survey of all exterior lights ought to be made, and the results entered in a suitable record book, perhaps at some point in the post log-book. electricity to them should be a part of the record.

What the Warden Should Know About Shelter Protection 

Another means of self-defense for the Sector will be protection from the effects of high explosive, from the point of view of the householder with a family. Individual protection has been dealt with in a previous chapter; here we are concerned with the Warden's responsibility for shelter.

As was the case with blackout, shelter protection involves competent opinion at a considerable number of points. Sound practice is not likely to be sudden, but will emerge as the final step in a natural evolution. The Warden's part will begin with the measures he must take to spread information throughout the Sector. 

The attention of the heads of families should be called to pamphlets, newspaper articles, radio talks, and other valuable forms of publicity on the subject. 

It is desirable for the Warden to know thoroughly the general principles of shelter protection as they apply in a very practical way to the civilian population. This means of self-defense aims at a reasonable compromise between probability and the pocket-book. 

Absolute shelter against any known size of bomb is possible but costly; and if one invested the money, the result would very likely remain with a reputation for being a ghastly mistake, no bomb having fallen within a half mile of the place. Furthermore, shelter in the final analysis is more for the nourishment of civilian morale than

111. for the protection of life, since casualties on the small scale which air attack has produced would not be judged high enough to be a matter of serious concern in grand strategy. 

The Refuge Room 

Since the ordinary American family is not likely to encounter bombing on the scale suffered in London and elsewhere in England, the very much less secure refuge room is a good first step. It will seldom measure up to the minimum English standards, but it is probably going to be developed in that direction as time goes on. 

It is primarily for security and protection against blast and fragmentation from high explosive, and for that reason should be the least exposed room in the house, well away from window openings and outside doors. If that is not possible, means should be at hand for blocking up openings; and any work done on refuge rooms should conform to the English minimum standards for such work. 

Sandbagging should be 2 ft. 6 inches thick, and the exterior should be painted with tar or asphalt to prevent rotting in the weather. 

Books or heavy furniture against interior walls will help to offset adjacent exterior hazards.

The Warden should take the responsibility for examining with care every refuge room, and no such room should be allowed to continue deficient for any reason. The most convenient standard of comparison will be the shelter with walls and roof of re-enforced concrete: the walls 1 ft. thick, and the roof 4 inches thick. 

Persons in the Sector must be made to comprehend that any protective

112, measure must be of this order. In most cases, no elaborate construction should be necessary; steps taken should be designed to make use of existing facilities in so far as possible. 

The point should of course be made that retreat to the refuge room may be for an extended period, and that necessary facilities should be provided in advance so that discomfort may be avoided.

District and Regional Warning Centers and the Organization of Civil Defense in the Commonwealth 

From two to twenty or more Report Centers look to one central District Warning Center which may cover several counties, and which is in charge of a Controller with one or more Deputy Controllers.

Unlike the Report Center, it has no representatives of special services on its staff, although it is in immediate telephone contact with the large utilities, the fire and police headquarters, and some state authorities.

It maintains a map and a plotting clerk, but on the map will be indicated only the incidents reported when local facilities have been exhausted or have not been adequate. At the scene of the damage will be flagged only those services borrowed under the Controller's authority from one community to help another under heavy attack and therefore at work out of their own locality. In addition to the control of borrowing between towns and cities, assistance of the proper kind may be summoned from the utilities, the fire and police departments, or through the Regional Center from the headquarters of the Army Corps within which the incident lies. The Controller shall have the authority during an emergency to direct and order available all unused fire fighting, engineering, repair, hospital or ambulance facilities to help stricken communities within his area, or to ask help from other Warning Centers. 

He shall further have the duty of reporting to the State Committee on Public Safety on such action so that statewide or even inter-state coordination can be secured.

Civilian Defense and the Military Forces 

Variations on this scheme of organization will doubtless occur from time to time, either in the way of alteration, addition, or subtraction. On the administrative side, the nine Regional Warning 

Centers represent the highest state units of A. R. P. Twenty or so of these from a number of states look to one central U. S. Army 

Command, known as Army Information Center. Thus, on the administrative side, civilian defense ties in with active defense and with the army, naval, and aircraft forces.

The System in Operation 

The impulse to activate this entire organization is a military or tactical one to be determined by strategical considerations. It comes therefore from the Army Information Center, which is in direct telephone contact with the nine Regional Warning Centers. Within less than five minutes (or before they have gone 25 miles if they

123. are bombers flying at 300 miles per hour), presence of hostile aircraft will result in receipt of an "Alert" or yellow warning in the proper Regional Centers. From these points the signal is handed on immediately to the District Warning Centers which put all their Report Centers on the alert. Since it takes on the average less than five minutes for each Regional Center and District Center to put out its calls, the Report Centers will have their yellow warning while the enemy is still a considerable distance away. 

This fact should be borne in mind by the Warden, since it is very likely in many cases that the yellow warning will be followed by an "All Clear" or white warning. No one attack, even if made, could possibly cover all the area which will have to be alerted to safety. Thus

The blue warning is handed on by Regional and District Centers in the same way, but with this important difference, that all telephone warning lines are from that point onward kept open and manned. a typical Regional Center will be in instant contact with some twenty-five District Warning Centers and local Report Centers, and thus in touch with all points in an area covering about a hundred square miles.

This contact is maintained until the raid is ended and until any damage resulting from it is under control. Upon receipt of a white or "All Clear" signal from the Army Information 

Center, contacts no longer necessary are dropped; but any contact where there is damage may be kept until in the opinion of the Controller the situation is cleared up. At some point after the blue warning, the red warning may indicate raiders five minutes away;

Thereafter, all points in the entire network are on the alert waiting to help the Warden with his job. Upon the facts of air raid damage, as he reports them, the mobilized resources of the communities, districts, regions, and of the state or of neighboring states are called into action for the protection and relief of the citizens of the Commonwealth.

All these elements of potential civil defense must be kept in mind by the Air Raid Warden, for that, too, is part of his job.
