CHAPTER I  MEXICO, EXICO, since I have known about it, I have always loved. The tales of the Toltecs, Cortez, Montezuma, Villa, and the colorfulness of the country have made me wish to go there. The thought of Mexico kept haunting me so much that finally I started plans to see it. 

Jimmie Doolittle had just made a senior record to Mexico; why couldn't I make a junior rec- [ 13 ]

ord? My thought then turned to my plane, the

Yankee Clipper, and I felt sure that with some overhauling it would be able to wing its way southward and again prove its reliability, as it had on my record flights to Los Angeles and 

Havana, Cuba.

It wasn't long before the work was started, and while the Yankee Clipper was being overhauled, I busied myself poring over maps and planning the route. I was not going to put in any extra fuel for a longer range—I wanted to see how quickly a person could get places with a regularly equipped airplane such as the average private pilot flies. I finally decided on the following route: New York to Richmond, Virginia; Richmond to Spartanburg, South 

Carolina; then to Montgomery, Alabama; to 

New Orleans; to Houston, Texas; Brownsville, Texas; Tampico, Mexico; and finally to 

Mexico City.

I had also decided to establish a record from 

Mexico City to Los Angeles, California, since up to this time none had been made, either junior or senior. I chose the following route for this record: Mexico City to Brownsville, Texas, north to San Antonio, Texas; to Big Springs, [ 14 ]

Texas; west to El Paso, Texas; thence to Tucson, Arizona; and Los Angeles, California. The courses plotted, mileage figured, and I was set to go! I was, but the Yankee Clipper wasn't. 

Each day I impatiently watched the slow progress of her overhauling. Finally, late one afternoon, almost dark, we rolled her out of the hangar, put gas and oil into the tanks, and I got in the cockpit. Ed Purdy, head mechanic, stood by the propeller. "Gas on, switch off," he called. "Off and on," I replied.

He turned the propeller to fill the cylinders with gas, then he stepped to the side of the plane and grasping the starter crank-handle called: "Contact." I turned the switch and replied the same. Two turns of the crank and the engine caught—a few coughs, then she settled down to an even purr. Gosh! it seemed good to feel the vibration of the engine, and the wind from the propeller blowing on my face, and to see the various engine instruments at work! 

While she was warming up, and I was listening to the engine for any noise that might mar the perfect hum of the motor, Ed was looking the plane over for about the hundredth time to [ 15 ]

make sure that everything was as it should be. 

Soon he came up to me and said: "She looks good to me, Bob, whenever you're ready go ahead." per It was almost dark, but I was anxious to testhop the ship. The oil thermometer read ninety degrees. It was warm enough. I revved up the motor-one thousand, six hundred and fifty revolutions minute-swell! Both magnetos were okay. I throttled her down and motioned the mechanic to pull the blocks. I slipped my goggles down over my eyes, checked my parachute harness, fastened the safety belt, looked over the various gadgets and instruments—all okay. I looked out of the ship and slowly taxied out on the field feeling out the brakes and rudder-they seemed fine. I turned and headed into the wind. Throttle open I started to gain speed-I was off! I checked for wing heaviness -there was none-Ed Purdy had done a perfect rigging job. I climbed straight ahead; at three thousand feet I leveled off, adjusted the stabilizer and took my hand off the controlsshe kept on flying perfectly-I made a turn -fine-I leveled off, then climbed some more-made a vertical bank-one to the right and one to the left; the ship was working beau- [ 16 ]

tifully. I started to dive, one hundred-one hundred and thirty-one hundred and fiftyone hundred and eighty-two hundred miles an hour everything seemed tight. Gently I pulled her out and climbed back to three thousand, five hundred feet, a tail spin to the right and one to the left—fine! A glide—a few side slips, she seemed perfect all the way through. 

I turned on my landing lights and approached the field; a few feet off the ground I leveled off, then pulled back the stick. She nestled on the ground. Boy, what a swell job Ed Purdy did!

I flew the plane around for a few days to see that everything held and, after a final check, she was put in the hangar at the Newark airport. The wait for weather then began. When 

I left, I planned to leave at midnight so as to get as far as possible the first day.

One evening on checking the weather it looked as though the next night would be the one. I went to bed early, and spent the next day filling the plane with gas and oil, making a final check and taking last looks at the maps. 

From New York to Richmond, Virginia, was easy. I had flown it often and knew it well. The rest, however, would all be new. [ 17 ]

About four o'clock in the afternoon I went home and tried to sleep. At ten o'clock that evening I went to the field with my family and friends. The weather had turned bad, but it looked as though it might clear. After the newspaper men had finished taking pictures and asking questions, we all sat indoors and waited for the weather. Each hourly report showed no improvement until about three forty-five A.M. It then looked good. The plane was hustled out and the engine started. By four 

A.M. it was warmed up, and after saying goodby to every one I took off at four-five A.M. on 

August 9, 1932.

I climbed to about three thousand feet and settled down to watching the beacons and the towns roll by. When I had reached Wilmington, Delaware, it started to get light. The gray shadows finally became something real as the sun approached the horizon. I noticed a ground fog rolling under me, and as I passed over 

Washington, D. C., I could see only the top of the Washington Monument and the Capitol as they stuck up through the fog. As the sun rose the fog burned off. An hour out of Washington found me at Richmond. After a seven-minute [ 18 ]

stop for gasoline and oil, I was on my way to Spartanburg, South Carolina. A flight of about two and a half hours found me there. 

After another short stop I hopped off for Montgomery.

About an hour out of Spartanburg, I became very sleepy-I could hardly keep my eyes open. I tried taking my goggles off-then my helmet a swig of tea from a thermos bottlebut still I felt sleepy. I set the stabilizer so that, if I did fall asleep, the ship would automatically climb. I kept on forcing myself to stay awake a few times I did doze off to wake up in a moment with a start as the ship started to climb. As I approached Atlanta, Georgia, I decided to land and get a cup of coffee. I did so, and soon was in the air again. The sleepiness had left me. I seemed to have gotten a "second wind." I soon reached Montgomery and was quite surprised on landing to find a chicken dinner awaiting me. I ate some of it but, fearing that too much of it would make me sleepy again, I reluctantly tore myself away from my favorite dish of Southern fried chicken.

In the air once more I flew on toward the 

Gulf of Mexico. Numerous thunderstorms [ 19 ]

dotted the course. Their huge clouds piling up in inky blackness to tremendous heights-too high to fly over them, too rough to fly through -I had to chase around them. As I approached 

Mobile, Alabama, I saw a very large storm area ahead of me. I started around it and had to fly some fifty miles out of my way over swampy alligator country to avoid it. This was discouraging since I was on a record flight. Once over Mobile I decided to land and check the weather. I was told that it was okay, so continued toward New Orleans. Darkness was fast approaching, but by flying part of the way wide open, I managed to make New Orleans by night.

The next morning at five A.M. I took off for 

Houston, Texas. The country during the first part of the trip was very swampy and a forced landing would have been bad. But after reaching the Texas border the terrain flattened out into typical prairie country. I began to see numerous herds of cattle, and now and then oil wells. It was pleasant to fly along in the cool of the early morning; as the sun came up I noticed the wind was increasing, and, worse luck, it was a head wind, retarding my progress. 

By the time I reached Houston it was blowing [ 20 ]

strong, and I had to be careful when landing. 

After gassing up I took off for Brownsville, Texas. I flew along the coast line on the Gulf of Mexico. The wind was very strong and uncomfortably hot. I flew low along the beach.

It was a long hot flight, and Brownsville was a welcome sight. I landed there and soon had the customs' officer checking over my plane, baggage and papers. After an hour of fuss I crossed the border and at last was on my way to Tampico, Mexico.

The country below did not look any different at first, but thatched roofed huts told me that I was not in the States. These huts, however, were very few and far between, and it was a very desolate ride to Tampico. The weather continued to be hot, and I was perspiring freely; some chocolate bars that I had carried in my pocket were reduced to mush, and I threw them out. Three hours out of Brownsville found me over Tampico, my first real glimpse of Mexico.

Here were adobe houses, thatched roofs, and tropical vegetation. After a hasty look at the town I found the airport and landed. I checked the weather and found it bad. That meant an over-night stay in Tampico. An American [ 21 ]

pilot, flying for the Pan-American Airway, was staying there also, and he was a big help to me. 

As my knowledge of Spanish was confined to "si," he acted as my interpreter. We went to the hotel together where he ordered my dinner-no telling just what I would have eaten, if I had been obliged to order my own. Over the dinner table we talked of the hop from Tampico to Mexico City. It was to be the toughest of the whole trip, all mountains and jungles. He drew a map for me and showed me the things to check by-there were not many!

I retired early and slept under a huge fan which blew hot air on me all night. The next morning I rode out to the field and after many thanks and adios, I was off. I flew out over jungle and swamp and started a long climb-I had to get to twelve thousand feet in order to get into Mexico City. Soon the country started to rise with me. It was wild and rough with not a place to land. At about six thousand feet I hit clouds. My blind flying came in handy. I stuck the nose of the Yankee Clipper into the white billows and continued to climb and after thirtyfive minutes of blind flying, I came out on top at twelve thousand feet. Occasionally I could [ 22 ]

see the green jungle on the mountains through a hole in a cloud, where the sun's rays, hitting the green, cast an emerald-like brightness among the shadows. It was a beautiful sight, but it did not inspire a comfortable feeling thinking about motor trouble. After an hour and forty minutes I knew that I had passed the ten thousand, five hundred feet hump and was over the valley of Mexico. I came down through the clouds and was under them at nine thousand feet. Below me was the fertile Valley of Mexico, that famous valley where once the Aztecs reigned supreme. Twenty minutes more and I was over the airport at Mexico City.

It was a large one, and I was glad of that because I knew that I would land fast at the high altitude of seven thousand, four hundred and fifteen feet. I landed at seventy-five miles an hour. A welcoming committee greeted me. I had established a new record of twenty-four hours and two minutes.

As my time in Mexico City was very limited, I did not get a chance to see many of its interesting things. I did see the Air Corps and the West Point of Mexico and was surprised to find them so progressive and efficient. The [ 23 ]

glimpse that I got of Mexico, however, was enough to make me want to return and take plenty of time to see and explore it. 

Two days after my arrival, early morning found me at the airport ready to leave for Los

Angeles and a new record. After an official farewell I took off. I noticed in taking off that I ran much further than I did at sea level. Once in the air I started my long climb to get over the hump; at twelve thousand feet I leveled off, soon passed over and started my long sleigh ride down to Tampico to sea level. Two hours later found me in Tampico and thirty minutes more I was flying up the beach to Brownsville, Texas. My thoughts of Mexico City, and how

I would like to return soon, made the distance seem short, and before I realized it I was across the Rio Grande and back in the United States. 

I landed at Brownsville and the first thing in order was to clear customs. 

They not only looked over my baggage and plane, but to my amazement they ran a vacuumcleaner extension all through the plane. I later learned that this was done for stowaway Mexican beetles. After they cleaned my ship, I was permitted to go. I was off to San Antonio; after [ 24 ]

three hours' flying over mesquite and plains, I landed there. The weather report showed dirty weather, and since night was coming on I decided to lay over. The next morning found it still bad, but with daylight I thought I could get through.

As soon as I took off I knew the going would be tough. It was raining and the visibility and ceiling very poor. Fortunately, at Big Springs, my destination, it was clear, according to the reports. 

I flew under low-hanging clouds for some time but, when the country became somewhat hilly, I decided not to risk pushing one of these hills over, so I pulled up into the clouds to an elevation high enough to clear them. About forty-five minutes of this blind flying brought me out in clear weather, and thirty more, to 

Big Springs. I did not spend much time on the ground and soon was on my way to El Paso. 

After flying through the cool of the rain clouds, the flight to El Paso was hot. The dry prairie and desert and the hot sun beating down made it not only hot, but the air bumpy. It was hard work trying to keep the ship on an even keel and to fly a straight course so as not to lose time. [ 25 ]

It took three hours to reach El Paso, and I was glad when the wheels of the Yankee Clipper rolled across the sandy airport. While the ship was being gassed up I went to the stand on the field and had some ice cream. When in the air again, both the ship and myself seemed refreshed-we seemed to move along faster, and things became more interesting. Mountain ranges crossed our path, their peaks sticking far up into the sky and their sides falling abruptly into a sandy desert floor. 

When about two-thirds of the way to Tucson, my next stop, I noticed large thunder clouds piling up ahead. As I approached I saw sharp streaks of lightning darting downward. After deciding which way the storm was moving by watching the slant of the rain, I chased around the opposite end of it—this storm made me go one hundred miles out of my way before I finally got around it and ducked into Tucson. 

Due to loss of time I gassed up in a hurry, and was off for Yuma, Arizona. The weather here was better. About three-fourths of the way there darkness settled down and I picked up the airway beacons guiding me into Yuma. 

When I reached there I learned that the [ 26 ]

weather in Los Angeles was closed in and that 

I was stuck for the night.

The heat in Yuma was terrific, the thermometer read one hundred and eight. degrees, and I was happy on arriving at the hotel in town to find it air-conditioned. About ten o'clock the next morning found me on the last lap of my flight. I flew up over the desert next to the Salton Sea. About here, noticing the engine was acting up a bit, I decided to try to make the next emergency field. But when I reached there it was working fine, so I continued on. Later it missed again; and, being over Palm Springs, the last place on the desert before going into the Valley, I landed. The trouble proved to be in the magnetos; this delayed me thirty minutes. I took off and flew between the great mountains into the fertile valley in which Los Angeles lies. Then over orange groves and pretty little towns and I was over the Burbank airport in California. It didn't take long to slide down and land. A welcome by newspaper men, a check on time, and the first record flight from Mexico City to Los Angeles was made. I established it in twenty hours and forty minutes. [ 27 ]

CHAPTER II  AND OUR DREAMS COME TRUE 

SPENT about a week in Los Angeles, at the time of the Olympic Games, visiting the 

Olympic Village with Frank Kurtz, an Olympic Games contestant and a friend of mine, who is a pilot as well as an acrobatic diver. After an exhibition of beautiful fancy diving from a platform thirty-five feet high he coaxed me to the top of it and wanted me to dive off. One look into the pool convinced me that the "dive" I would make would be the less spectacular "Fireman's Dive"-I climbed down the ladder!

I flew from Los Angeles to Cleveland, a matter of two thousand miles or so, and there met my mother. We both remained in Cleveland to see the National Air Races, after which we took off for home. The trip home was not uneventful: we flew during an eclipse of the [ 28 ]

sun; and we were bounced around the sky by the shock of a bolt of lightning, and had to make a forced landing in a corn field until Dame Weather got over her tantrum. It was rather a thrilling experience, especially for mother.

After returning home I was haunted by one thought-to fly back to Mexico and really explore it. It was not long after arriving home that I met Bob Nixon, who had recently moved to Westfield, New Jersey, from Philadelphia. I learned from Bob's sister that he was an artist and a good one. Bob, being a bit too modest, had not mentioned it. I asked Nick, as I learned to call him, to 'fess up and show me his drawings.

After a lot of persuasion he did, and I was surprised to see pen and ink sketches done so beautifully that they looked like etchings. Somehow Bob suggested Mexico again-they were most unusual pictures, and here was a real artist to draw them. Why not go together! I popped the idea to Nick and he was overwhelmed by it.

We started to make plans. My new plane, a small two-place cabin monoplane, would be perfect for the trip. We planned we would go to Arizona, then down the colorful west coast [ 29 ]

of Mexico, across Mexico to Mexico City, and from there to Yucatan. Why to Yucatan? Because on the Yucatan peninsula are the jungle hidden cities of an ancient Mayan civilization. 

Here was an opportunity to do some exploring that might be of scientific value. Our proposed trip now took on this definite purpose; scientific discovery was added to romance, which made us more determined than ever to go. The problem that confronted us was where to get the funds. It would take money to explore Yucatan! 

Who could we get to back us? We thought of many people, and after much discussion decided to see Mr. Rea, of Philadelphia, a man whom I had met at Camp Wulamat, in New Hampshire.

We flew down to Philadelphia, and at the appointed hour, quaking in our boots, we presented our plans to Mr. Rea. He was immediately interested, and our enthusiasm soared when our major problem was solved by the generosity and far-sightedness of Mr. Rea.

We returned home jubilant, and immediately began preparations for the expedition. I had known that the plane would need a motor overhaul, but did not expect the wing to need a [ 30 ]

complete overhaul. It meant more money than we had figured. What to do? We did not want to ask Mr. Rea for more. A solution to this problem seemed at hand. I learned that on the following Monday there was to be a handicap race from Los Angeles to New York to be flown only at night-the first night derby.

Having flown back and forth across the States both at night and in the daytime, this was going to be a cinch for me; besides I knew the route so well I almost knew it backwards. But what was I to use for an airplane in this derby? I had sold my Yankee Clipper to Ormond Gove, a pilot friend of mine in Columbus, Ohio. Maybe he would loan it back to me if I agreed to split the prize money. Shortly I had him on the long distance telephone and, after he answered "you bet," I dashed to Newark, got aboard one of the

T.W.A. ships and flew to Columbus to pick up the plane. 

Ormond met me at the field. "How is she, Orm?" I asked. "She'll be okay as soon as we give her an engine check. She should be ready by Thursday." "Boy," I said, "that doesn't give me much [ 31 ]

time in case of bad weather, because I have to be in Los Angeles by midnight on Saturday. 

That's when the entries close." 

The ship was finished on Thursday, but I only got as far as Indianapolis where a leak in the gasoline tank held me up until Friday morning. Friday night I was in Amarillo, Texas. 

Saturday afternoon late, in Kingman, Arizona. 

When I took off from Kingman, a rocker arm broke. By sheer good luck I had a spare one.

I had it put in and was on my way again about a half an hour before dark. One hour's flight from Los Angeles it was dark. When I finally got into the Valley it was covered with fog. I flew an hour and a half above the fog trying to get down-the situation looked bad, and it was with great relief that I found an emergency field, just south of Los Angeles. I had only enough gas in the tanks for thirty minutes more of flying.

I notified the derby officials by 'phone that I was in California within shouting distance of 

Los Angeles, but would have to stay at Fullerton for the night. They said it would be okay, that

I was in the race, and to get into Mines Field the next morning so that Frank Hawks could [ 32 ]

handicap my plane. With a sigh of relief I retired for the night. It had given me plenty of concern to get here, and I had many misgivings, but now that was all over. I was in the race, the 

Yankee Clipper was in good trim and everything looked rosy. I went to sleep. 

I arrived the next morning at Mines Field, and Frank Hawks flew the ship and gave me my handicap. I spent the rest of the day and night with Tom Hynson, a friend of mine. 

Monday I tried to rest but found it impossible. 

Five o'clock Monday afternoon I flew over to the Glendale airport, the starting point of the race. When I arrived I found that the first ship had taken off. The slow planes naturally took off first, fast ones last. I was scheduled to leave at seven in the evening. I stood around talking to my friends until almost time to go, then got into my ship, started it up, and taxied to the line. At seven on the dot the starter's flag dropped, and I took off in a glare of flood-lights and headed for Kingman, Arizona.

Out on the desert I found it was a beautiful night. The moon was full and the mountains, rocks and sand barely visible, assumed weird shapes by its mellow light. Two hours out of [ 33 ]

Glendale I cut the gun and slid into Kingman,

Arizona. Gas and oil were soon put into the tanks. I found that I was gaining on the other planes, and that if I kept up at this rate, I would pass them all by the time I got to Amarillo,

Texas. 

Once more in the air I felt jubilant. Things looked promising! I flew along about thirty minutes the beacons were stretched out plainly before me. I was over a red one; ten miles ahead was another red one; ten miles beyond that one, a green one. This meant there was an emergency field at the foot of it. As I was complacently watching the beacons, the motor coughed, sputtered and died! I was just about to pull a flare, when it picked up. Whew! 

What a scare! I looked down at the rocky mountains beneath me-if I had to bail out here, I probably would break a leg or something doing so. Then too, the thought of losing the old Yankee Clipper, which no longer belonged to me, and the money it would set me back, gave me an all-gone feeling. She purred on five more minutes, then had another coughing fit. Again she picked up. Now I started right for the green beacon. The coughing fits [ 34 ]

became more and more numerous and at closer intervals. I concentrated on reaching the green beacon ahead, which seemed far, far away. 

After what seemed an eternity I was over it. 

The motor now had a real fit, gave up, and died! There was silence except for the swish of the wires as I glided downward. I looked over the field. It was marked by boundary lights but no flood-lights. I picked the wind direction, slid over what seemed to be a fence, felt my way on the ground, and stopped rolling. 

Leaving the ship, I walked over to a little house under the beacon and soon had the beaconkeeper out of bed. After I told him what happened, he offered to help me fix the motor and we worked two hours on it. Suddenly I realized that, even if we could fix the motor, the time I had now lost would, without doubt, put me out of the race! The thought was overwhelming! 

At that moment I think I was the most discouraged person in the world. Everything became blank to me and I felt perfectly rotten.

I thought of the searching parties that would be out looking for me if I did not report. I asked the beacon-keeper if he had a 'phone. He said no, and informed me that the nearest one [ 35 ]

was in Seligman, Arizona, twenty-five miles away, and offered to drive me there in his car. 

Shortly we were on our way. 

George, the light keeper, seemed to want to talk, and it bothered me. I was in no mood for conversation—I craved silence. After thinking things over, while half answering his questions with a curt "yes" or "no," I decided that better men than I had lost races, and that it was poor sportsmanship to get down in the dumps about it. I tried to perk up and answer George's questions more civilly. 

A two hours' ride over very poor roads brought us into the three (or was it five?) house town of Seligman. I aroused the sleepy telegraph operator and sent two wires announcing my safety, one to my family and the other to the field I had last left. We now drove back and went into the house where George and his wife made me comfortable for the night. 

The next morning when I first awoke, before the realization of what had happened the night before had penetrated my sleepy mind, things looked bright. Then the recollectionforced landing-out of the race-damn!

The delightful aroma of coffee and ham cook- [ 36 ]

ing snapped me out of it. I went into the next room and was informed that breakfast was ready. 

I ate it, and what is more, I enjoyed it. After breakfast I went out to the field. Great God! 

Was it possible? One look showed that it had all been plowed up except the narrow strip on which I had landed. Providence had guided my landing in the dark, for where this narrow strip had turned, so had I. I soon got hold of 

George and told him plenty about plowing the field in the first place, and much more about not having red lights to mark the plowed area. 

He was very sorry about it, but said that he wasn't expecting any planes! I soon made it clear that he was taking care of an emergency field and ought to expect a plane at any time. 

He promised that night to show red lanterns all around. I hate to think of what might have happened if I had landed on that plowed field! 

With but little enthusiasm now I again went to work on the plane's motor. After working about an hour and a half I found that the carburetor would have to come off and be taken apart. That was a long, tough job with adequate tools, and I had very few. George came over about the time I was knee-deep in work [ 37 ]

and told me that he was going to inspect a beacon about ten miles away, and asked me if I cared to go with him. "You bet!" I replied, glad for the opportunity of getting away from it all for a while.

We piled into his car and started over to the beacon. It was a rough ride and hard going to the top of the mountain; a thunder storm accompanied us on the way, giving us plenty of atmosphere. Finally we we reached the top. 

George got out of the car. I heard him shout and jumped out. "What is it?" I said. "Look out! It's a rattler!" he yelled. 

I looked down, and at the same time jumped back, for there he was coiled and ready to strike at me. I whipped out my automatic, which I always carry when flying cross-country, and fired two shots. They both found their mark, and the rattler was dead. George finished the job by chopping off its head with an ax. This was one rattler that did not rattle, although he had eleven rattles. Whew! This part of the country seemed to have a jinx on me.

After looking the beacon over, we went back [ 38 ]

to the field. It was late in the afternoon, and I did not go to work on my ship, but instead, joined a group of engineers and helped them survey a new road they were putting through from the field into town. That evening we all sat around swapping stories of the West and aviation. 

The next morning I got to work early on the motor and after a lot of trouble, found, to my amazement, a little piece of rubber blocking the jet in the carburetor. How it got there, I should love to find out. It seemed strange to have found a bit of rubber here in the carburetor after having had a check on the plane in Los 

Angeles. It might have cost me my life! 

Late that afternoon I bid the friends I had made at the field good-by and took off for 

Prescott, Arizona. I spent the night there, and the next day continued on to Columbus, Ohio, where I left the Yankee Clipper and that evening took a train for home. I had made no money out of that derby to help pay the expenses of my Mexican trip.
