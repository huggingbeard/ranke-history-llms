THE SOWDON OF BABYLON AND ITS AUTHOR 

LIKE 

BY H. M. SMYSER 

IKE nearly all of the English Charlemagne romances, the early fifteenth-century Sowdon of Babylon¹ is a translation. That its original is some poem or sequence of poems in the French Fierabras tradition is patent, and a few scholars have offered opinions as to the nature of this original and its place among the Continental redactions. 

During the nineteenth century, however, the history of the French poems was a subject of vigorous debate, and this debate absorbed the attention of Emil Hausknecht 2 and other authorities on the English Charlemagne romances to such an extent that the Sowdon was more or less neglected. Since 19033 the major problems concerning the Continental tradition have been pretty well settled, but no one has yet made a systematic study of the Sowdon with the purpose of reconstructing the original and pointing out the manner in which the translator worked. Such a study I shall undertake in the following pages.

The Saracen hero was introduced as one unknown both to the audience and to the French host before whom he delivers his challenge; a brief exposition (probably a score of lines) set forth his past achievements- viz. the conquest of Rome, the plundering of St Peter's, and so forth. He himself was said to be high ruler of Pagandom and no mention was made of Balan.¹ (4) This detached and now independent episode fell into the hands of a poet of Saint-Denis who had a two-edged ax to grind. In the first place, he wanted to explain to the merchants who came annually to the fair of the Lendit how it was that the relics of Christ's passion shown to them at fair time happened to be there. In the second place, he wanted to protest against a tax levied against all who participated in the fair. 

This poet was familiar with the Balan, but he preferred to use the Combat as the basis of his new "ax-grinding" romance.2 Making very few changes in his original, he attached to it a long "queue postiche," fabricated of stock elements, from a dozen romances, and of decidedly inferior quality. The resultant composite survives in what is essentially its original redaction in a half-dozen manuscripts, and is known as Fierabras; I shall refer to it as K Fi.³ Its action throughout takes place in Spain. The combat is extremely chivalric. After a bandying of insults, Fierabras perceives that Oliver is wounded and offers him a drink of the miraculous healing balm he carries in two kegs at his saddle. (The Frenchman refuses, but later carves the "barils" from his opponent's saddle and, after drinking deep, hurls them save the mark! - into the Tiber.) When Oliver's horse is slain, Fierabras dismounts and fights afoot; when the peer has lost his sword he proudly refuses his opponent's offer that he pick it up unmolested and instead daringly steals one of Fierabras's own swords from his saddle. At length the Saracen is overcome and cries recreant.

With the beginning of the "queue postiche" the scene of action takes on a new aspect. The French warriors who have been watching the progress of the combat and commenting upon it now recede far into the distance. Furthermore, Fierabras's scrupulous chivalry proves to have been a mere sham, for he confesses that he has that morning placed fifty thousand Saracens in ambush to ride to his assistance in case he is defeated. Now that he is recreant he really longs for Christianity and regrets that bit of foresight. He urges Oliver to make haste to carry him to the French camp to be baptized. 

Oliver siezes Fierabras's horse, and, throwing the wounded proselyte over the saddle, rides toward the pavilions of the Christians. But the Saracens have broken from their ambush and are riding across the plain like a tempest;

The peers slay him, and then, furnished with arms by Floripas, sally forth and void the castle of Balan and his courtiers. The dispossessed Sultan immediately sets about collecting an army, and within a short time is besieging Aigremore. 

The castle is strong, and it is only from shortage of food that the defenders anticipate any difficulties. For a time they are exempt even from this worry, but ultimately they are forced to make sorties for provisions. The chief episodes of the siege, beside these sorties, are Balan's furious assaults on the castle and the theological distractions of Floripas, who finally becomes a Christian.

At last Richard of Normandy breaks through the Saracen host and rides to Morimond. He finds that Charlemagne, under the influence of the eloquently treacherous Ganelon, is on the point of returning to France. When he hears Richard's message, however, the Emperor moves on to Aigremore. 

He overcomes Balan, who prefers death to baptism, and receives the relics from Floripas. Guy and Floripas are married and receive Spain to rule jointly with Fierabras. Charlemagne returns to France and deposits the relics at Saint-Denis, little dreaming that people will ever be taxed to look at them.

In the first place, stages (5) and (6) are not independent of each other. The author of the Destruction of Rome was employing a version of Fi to which the Passage had already been prefixed. Jarník rejects such a view because many details of plot at the close of HD differ from the Passage, but such difference is easily explicable when we recall that the author of the Destruction had a task which precluded too close redaction of either the Balan or the Passage, that he was bound to the latter poem only in so far as it introduced events of K Fi, and, finally, that his work survives in a badly garbled copy. On the other hand, the twenty-odd line-parallels between the latter part of HD and Passage are somewhat closer than they would be if two authors had independently drawn their materials from the expository lines at the opening of K Fi (as Jarník contends that they did). Indeed, one of these parallels cannot be explained at all by reference to K Fi, and Jarník is forced to dismiss it as a mere coincidence. After describing the anger which 

Roland feels upon hearing Charlemagne boast of his old knights, the creator of the Passage inserted an address to his audience: 

Senhors, auiatz bo sen, si'ntendre 1 voliatz.

La chanso es ben fayta: melhor no l'auziratz (Provençal, 562 f.)

There was no real necessity for this tag, as the Passage had not come to a close. On the other hand, HD ends with the anger of Roland, and very naturally appends schlussverse introducing the audience to Fi: 

Ore orres bon chanceon, sil voles escouter.

Et tant vous requere, seignurs, que ieo le puis chaunter. (Icy en finist la destructioun de Rome). (HD, 1506 ff.) 

It is precisely this parallel, however, which makes Jarník's theory quite untenable, for, as we shall see later, the Sowdon demonstrates that in the original of HD the tag did not serve as schlussverse, and its correspondence of Provençal, 562 f., therefore was not a mere accident. The 

Destruction of Rome must have been written as a preface to Passage Fi. 

In the second place, Hausknecht and Jarník do not exploit the probability that the author of D reworked his version of Fi to make of his D+ Fi a well-unified poem. True, in the Hanover MS. our version of 

D is prefixed to a raw and untouched version of K Fi, but we need not assume therefore that the proper sequel to D is not a lost version of Fi from which elements contradictory to D have been expunged and into

which traits of unity with D have been introduced. Indeed, a priori probabilities all point to such a Fi,¹ and I should accordingly hypothesize a well-unified D Fi as the redaction of Stage (6). 

As I have said, Groeber's theory of the development of the Fierabras legend cannot be coördinated with those which build up the history I have just outlined. To the learned author of Die handschriftlichen Gestaltungen der Chanson de geste Fierabras 2 and other pertinent monographs, this development took place in a radically different fashion. 

Very briefly stated, Groeber's theory is this: The Balan underwent redaction and elaboration from which it emerged as D Fi. D Fi in its turn was redacted: some poet cut out the story of the destruction of 

Rome down to the beginning of the Passage and compensated for its loss by inserting a description of Fierabras which sketched his past deeds. This poem is the Passage Fi which today survives in a Provençal translation. A last redactor summarized the matter of the Destruction in a dozen lines of exposition and gave us the most popular poem of the lot: K Fi, with its "kurze, allein kaum recht verständliche" introduction.

For example, there is the problem of Floripas's infatuation for Guy. 

According to the Prefatory theory, the author of the "queue postiche" was the creator of Floripas it was he who first introduced the lovemotif into the Fierabras tradition. As he worked away at his romantic fabric, he was faced with the necessity of explaining just why it was that the Saracen princess should feel so kindly toward the French captives. His answer was a stroke of genius: Floripas has fallen in love with Guy from seeing him overcome Lucafer at Rome. The poet, we recall, was familiar with the Balan, and he doubtless knew that his audience would likewise be familiar with it. They would remember, as he did, that Guy in the Balan was the hero of the relief of Rome it was Guy who recaptured Miraour before Charlemagne and the grand army of the French had even arrived at the scene of battle. True, the older romance may have contained no account of a combat between Guy and Lucafer, but it is perfectly plausible that such a combat could have taken place, and equally plausible that it could have been witnessed by the daughter of Balan. So far as K Fi is concerned, this motivation of Floripas's infatuation is perfectly clear. But it did not remain clear in Passage Fi. The author of this new redaction was preoccupied by the task of fabricating an introduction to K Fi which would explain more adequately the situation at the commencement of that romance, — for example, the presence of Charlemagne at Morimond, and the wrath of Roland, and he found it convenient to ignore any reference to a clash of arms between the French and the Saracens at Rome. The author of D thus fell heir to a difficulty. In redacting the Balan to make it fit Passage Fi he had to declare that the Saracens leave Rome before the arrival of the French, although Floripas in Passage Fi¹ is obstinately contending that she fell in love with Guy at Rome. Probably the author of D struck out this little speech of hers from his own finished version of D Fi; at any rate, throughout D he offers a new explanation of the manner in which the princess becomes enamored of the Burgundian: although she has never seen him, she has heard her "folles" sing of his deeds of valor!

We have noted that the interpolations are replete with "facts" as to

Thus, Laban is made to celebrate the fall of Rome by burning frankincense to his gods, while his followers blow brazen horns and feast themselves on milk and honey, the blood of wild beasts, and serpents fried in oil. Whence this erudition came, I have not been able to discover perhaps ultimately from some Crusader's fanciful memoirs, perhaps in part from the imagination of the translator himself. At any rate it is there, and its presence may suggest that more materials went into the English poem than could be found in the French Fierabras tradition and in Chaucer and Langland alone. The Sowdon of Babylon bristles with errors and gaucheries. It would be quite gratuitous to assume that any great proportion of these were due to the scribe, as some of the silliest of them make up quatrains of which the rhymes have been marred in transmission. The scribe has merely preserved the original in all its virgin imperfection. In this, he has done no more than the translator himself, for whom revision, even of patently inconsistent passages, seems to have been too onerous. As

I think that analysis will show that the Sowdon was produced under some such circumstances as the following. 

A bilingual Englishman- so far as I can see, neither priest nor minstrel — of literary aspirations set out to make a running translation of the French poem which we have designated as D Fi. He may have possessed a manuscript of this poem, but it is more likely that he was forced to rely upon his memory, to which he had committed the whole poem or large portions of it. He had no strong Continental sympathies, and was either indifferent to or ignorant of the legends of Charlemagne as they exist apart from the Fierabras story. When he had chosen his metrical form and plunged into his translation, he seized upon opportunities to insert bits of his own, as we have already seen. He also altered proper names at will, and omitted incidents which did not appeal to him, or which (perhaps) he had forgotten. As he progressed, his speed and freedom tended rather to increase.

It will not be possible to cite all variations between the Sowdon and D Fi (as it can be reconstructed, of course), for the tale is too long; but representative specimens will show upon what grounds the foregoing hypothesis rests.

Laban is sojourning at Egremour, as in D Fi, when the poem opens; the translator adds the information that Egremour is "vppon the 

Rivere of Flagote" (34). This is incorrect: we learn later that Mantrible is on the Flagot (2804). When the dromond arrives, its captain explains that his ships were driven by a gale to Rome. HD, with a better feeling for geography, adds the phrase "parmi la far sigler" (120). As I have said previously, the list of Saracen peers of D is omitted, but the translator introduces one or two new names: there are 

Laban's chancellor Oliborne and a King of Chaundre (MS.. Chaunder) who rides in the same galley (and in the same quatrain) as Ferumbras of Alisaundre. When the translator comes to describe the council of war held by the Romans, he first reveals his indifference to the genuinely cyclic traits of his original: in HD, Savaris looms out as a 

Roman Roland, a man of headstrong might, and Garin plays the part of Oliver in a vain attempt to curb the ruinous desmesure of his companion. S never mentions Garin; it is "Ifrez" (a mere name) whose cautious advice is overriden by Savaris; moreover, Savaris goes forth to fight a battle that is "wise and ware" (216).

In this change of order Hausknecht ¹ sees an improvement effected by the translator: Lucafer's claim is better justified in S than in HD, since the pagan has returned "from a victorious expedition." I doubt if the translator reasoned so carefully over the capture of defenseless virgins; it is more likely that he merely forgot to introduce the suit where it belonged, but was reminded of his omission when Lucafer came next to the fore. 

After the suit has been duly described, S leaps ahead through the events of the siege, omitting a great many, but following the original closely in those which are retained. We are told of Mavon's assault on the city (except for the naval attack), of Lucafer's capture of the outer bail, of the death of Savaris, and of the Pope's decision to send messengers to Charlemagne. In the original, when the messengers depart, the siege has all but ended only the treachery of the porter is needed to open the inner gates to the heathen. But the translator realized when he reached this point that he had omitted altogether too many episodes. 

His siege of Rome, too rapidly described, too meagre, has brought him only to line 382. Accordingly, with the statement,

The next episode is likewise a contribution of the translator: it is one of two "comic" scenes which he enjoyed enough to use twice each. A vituperative boaster, this time in the person of Laban, stands exposed to shout at the besieged. In the midst of his imprecations a dart strikes him full on the breastplate. The fourth episode is borrowed from a neglected passage of D (HD, 893 ff.): orders are passed down to Sortibrant, Brulant, and 

Mabon (S: Fortibraunce and Mavon only) to reopen the assault (412426). The translator next introduces the second of his two "comic" scenes. Estragot beats down the gates of the city, but the defenders drop the portcullis on him and cut him in two (427-439). After lingering a bit over this jest, as who should repeat the point of an anecdote two or three times, our author closes his siege with a belated use of the sortie on which the Pope is overcome (451 ff.). This episode he altered in some particulars; for example (as we have before observed), in S it is not the King of Nubia but Ferumbras who overcomes and spares the pontiff - despite the fact that Ferumbras, in both the French and the English romances, is later to murder the prelate in cold blood. The scene is full of novel names, too: Ethiopes, Assaynes, Babyloynes, the

Roman warrior Sir Hubert, the senator Gyndarde, and last, and most obviously an addition of the translator, the good steed Cassaundre, who bears Ferumbras of Alisaundre and who (for all we know) may have come from that Asiatic land mentioned in the long interpolation as the realm of Lucafer!

The siege of Rome has now been extended several folios (from 1. 383 to l. 575), and the translator runs rapidly through the story of Charlemagne's reception of the Pope's messengers and the subsequent events. 

In the story of the betrayal of the Roman citadel by the porter "Isres" (a substitution for Tabour of HD) the translator seizes an opportunity to re-introduce a favorite joke: it is not the sword of Ferumbras which decapitates the traitor, but the portcullis of the main gate. Then, per-

haps because he was eager to reach his learned description of the Saracen jubilee (673 ff.), the Englishman forgets to include the kegs of balm among the relics seized by Fierabras (665). Once this description is done, the author approaches the point at which he is going to make his longest, most pretentious interpolation, but as he does so he forgets to show that Oliver receives a wound before Morimond, and he makes Charlemagne include the douzepers among the old knights whom he is praising. When the long interpolation, with its learning and its lyric flights, is off his mind, the translator returns to his French source with more patience, to tell how Ferumbras enjoys the possession of the two kegs of holy balm (1185), how Oliver is abed "seke sore" (1108), and how Roland is sulking in wrath because the Emperor has praised the old knights (1088).

It will be recalled that in K Fi four paladins are captured by the 

Saracens with Oliver, namely l'Escot Guillemer, Berart de Mondidier, Joffroi l'Angevin, and Aubri, and that the remaining seven douzepers - Roland, Naimon, Basin, Thierri, Ogiers, Richard, and Guy—are later sent to Aigremore by Charlemagne. The author of the Sowdon strays from his original so far as to declare that Roland and Oliver alone are captured (1411 ff.). For some time this aberration causes him no difficulties: Floripas rescues the two captives as cheerfully as she could rescue five, and by the same means. But it soon becomes necessary for 

Charlemagne to dispatch the remaining douzepers to Aigremore, and in the English poem there are supposedly ten of them. If the author of S had been working from a manuscript of D Fi, he could have leafed back for the four names which he has omitted, or, if he was too indifferent to the Charlemagne traditions to do that, he at least would have had six names before him to begin on in constructing a list of ten. It is my contention that, lacking a document, the puzzled Englishman racked his brains to recall as many as he could. At any rate, he got four, Guy, Naimon, Ogiers, and Thierri,—and he was able to add a plausible fifth, Bishop Turpin. The remaining five are as strange a group of peers as ever tilted lance: "Folk Baliante," "Aleroyse," "Miron of Brabane," "Bernarde of Spruwse," and "Bryer of Mounte3," -the last two may be the result of groping for Berart de Mondidier.

Unfortunately, he forgot Richard, who is later forced to play an important rôle at Egremour despite the fact that he has never been sent there. 

The author of the Sowdon gives us his own version of the siege of

Egremour-or, rather, he gives us the episodes of his original in an arrangement that is his own, and often with additions and alterations. 

Led by associations of names and circumstances to combine events originally disparate, or to introduce the same event twice, he flounders on into one absurdity after another, and we may well imagine that he regretted not having reviewed his plot more carefully at the beginning and not having proceeded with more caution. The following comparison between S, 2067-2794, and its source (as the latter is perceived in 

K Fi, 3046–5281) indicates roughly what must have been going on in the poet's mind.

The events of K Fi which S has preserved may be described and numbered as they appear in the French romance: (1) As soon as Balan finds himself shut out of his castle, he bethinks him of a magic girdle which his daughter possesses. This girdle nourishes whosoever puts it on. Clearly the peers cannot be starved so long as they possess such a talisman. Balan calls his magician, Maubrun d'Agremolee, and bids him steal it. Maubrun enters the castle by night, finds the girdle, and puts it on. The peers awake; they seize Maubrun and slay him. Not noticing that he is wearing the girdle, they cast his body into the sea. Within a day or two the besieged are suffering from hunger. (K Fi, 3046–3144.) (2) The peers decide to make a sally for food. They arm themselves and lower the draw. Thierri and Basin remain to guard the port; the rest ride forth. In the fighting Roland slays Tempier; the peers seize nine pack-horses laden with provisions. Basin(!) is slain, and Guy is taken captive. The

French retreat to the city; they are so hard pressed that they have to abandon the pack-horses, although Oliver succeeds in preserving a few fowl and a few loaves. Once they are safely within the gates, they tell Floripas of the loss of Guy. (K Fi, 3203–3359.) (3) The Burgundian is led before Balan. He is asked his name, and gives it. After certain bickerings (which do not find a place in S), he is condemned to be hanged. The Saracens erect a gallows in full view of those within the besieged castle; they then lay an ambush about it in the hope that the peers will attempt to rescue their comrade. The hope is not vain, but the ambush proves inadequate to overcome the Frenchmen. After fierce fighting, Guy is freed. Moreover, the peers succeed in capturing a provision train of fifteen pack-horses, sent by the admiral of Corduba and intended, of course, for Balan. This time they get safely into the city with all their booty. On the way they pick up the body of Basin. Incidentally, Guy slays a Saracen named Tempier. (K Fi, 3396-3673.) (4) Balan calls Brulant de Monmire and Sortinbrant de Cornuble into council and expresses his fears that Charlemagne will arrive by way of Mautrible. Sortinbrant advises an attack with siege-engines, and a thousand trumpets to affright the defenders. Brulant ridicules this suggestion and

gives a glowing account of the valor of the peers of France, enumerating the heroic deeds of each and concluding with the observation that if there were a hundred such warriors within Aigremore, Balan would have to leave the land. The Sultan is enraged and makes for Brulant with a club. Sortinbrant, however, arrests his violence and urges him to begin the attack. (K Fi, 3681-3731.) (5) Horns are sounded and the assault begins. Mabon employs a huge siege-engine; the walls tremble. Twice Floripas saves the day: once she puts out, with a mixture of vinegar and camel's milk, the Greek fire which the enemy has cast on the battlements, and, again, she directs the defenders to hurl Balan's treasures down on the attackers. This stratagem puts an end to the assault, for the heathen warriors fall to fighting one another for the precious missiles. The Sultan is furious, and accuses Mahound of having gone to sleep when he should have been keeping watch over the treasury. 

Sortinbrant offers explanation in defense of the god, until Balan repents his hasty words. (K Fi, 3732-3839.) (6) Floripas offers maidens to the peers. (K Fi, 3915-3917.) (7) Richard of Normandy agrees to ride to Charlemagne for help. The peers plan to make a sortie from which he shall break away and set out for Paris. This sortie has to be postponed for two months, however, because of a heavy guard which Balan sets over the gates. When it takes place, it is successful. (K Fi, 3936–4039.) (8) Balan sends a messenger to the giant Agolafre, warden of the bridge of Mautrible, with a warning that he let nobody pass. (K Fi, 4265-4310.) (9) When Balan hears that Charlemagne has crossed Mautrible and is approaching to relieve Aigremore, he falls into a rage against his gods. 

Seizing a club, he runs into a temple and smashes an image of Mahound.

Sortinbrant placates him and persuades him to ask forgiveness. (K Fi, 51465172.) [This is an expansion of the latter part of No. 5. It is possible that, in D Fi, No. 5 itself contained such an appendix as this.] (10) In the course of a last assault on the city, Balan shouts vilification at 

Floripas. That lady "prinst i baston, son pere manecha.” (K Fi, 5272–5281).

The author of the Sowdon, I regret to state, begins his borrowing from these episodes with Floripas's generous offer as described in No. 

6 (2075-2094). From there, he proceeds to tell how Laban assails the walls and is repulsed (2095-2103). In chagrin at his defeat, the Sultan accuses Mahound, Termagaunt, and Appolyn of sleeping. He asks them to awake, and reminds them that his treasure is within the walls of Egremour (2104-2118). This is clearly a recollection of No. 5, although it does not include Sortinbrant in his rôle of apologist. By an association that is tacit so far as the Sowdon is concerned, the translator next gives an episode which combines Nos. 4 and 8 and which, through the former source, is tied up with the name of Sortinbrant: the Sultan

asks Brenlonde for advice, and is told to send a warning to Alagolofur at the bridge of Mantrible; he thanks Sortybraunce for this counsel and acts upon it (2119-2198). A new assault upon Egremour is distinguished by the use of No. 10, with the difference that Floripas does not merely shake a stick at her father, but answers the vilifications in kind (2199-2230).

Once again the translator borrows from the matter of No. 5: Mavon moves up his mangonel against the walls. Before much damage can be done, however, the translator changes the subject under the impulse to re-introduce one of his own favorite comic episodes: Marsedag of Barbary stands exposed to shout imprecations at the besieged and is pierced through and through by a well-directed dart. "Dame Floripe lough with loude steven," doubtless to the gratification of the translator, who, after a learned description of Marsedag's funeral rites, returns to his source for an incident of No. 7: Laban places a guard of three hundred knights over the port of the city (2231-2282).

The result of this watch is an acute shortage of provisions in the castle. As at the close of No. 1 (K Fi, 3125–3146), the maidens become faint and pale, and when one of the paladins (K Fi: Guy; S: Roland) proclaims that a sally must be made, Floripas suggests another remedy (2283-2302). In the French poem, this remedy is an appeal to the heathen gods, but the author of the Sowdon suddenly recalls the hitherto neglected magic girdle, and introduces No. 1. It is a trifle strange that Floripas should not have brought forth her sartorial talisman before the hunger of the besieged had become so acute, and it is also strange that Laban happens to bethink him of the garment just after it has been put into use, but, nevertheless, the theft comes off substantially as in the original. Once Mapyne's body has been hurled into the sea, the translator is back to the point from which he startedthe defenders are facing starvation and Roland is urging a sortie for supplies (2303-2390). 

This time the sortie takes place. It is successful. Very briefly described, it takes its only distinctive trait from No. 2: a pair of paladins are left behind to guard the gate (2390-2416). Laban is furious at the success of the enemy and flies into a tantrum in the presence of Brenlonde and Sortybraunce (cf. No. 4). He curses his gods, but is induced to apologize to them (cf. Nos. 5 and 9). He orders a new assault. Floripas causes the treasures to be hurled over the walls (No. 5), and Laban

recalls his warriors. A new tirade against the gods is followed by rites of atonement (Nos. 5 and 9) (2417–2526).

Richard is now appointed to ride for help. The sortie which is devised to get him through the Saracen ring is made up of elements from the sorties described in Nos. 2 and 3, with a single addition from No. 7 - an untimely reference to the three hundred pagans who long before were said to have been put on guard over the gates of Egremour. On this sortie Guy is captured (No. 2) and led before the Sultan (No. 3). 

The paladins are then barred from the city by the three hundred Saracens (who "kepte the pace at the brigge-ende"). Sir Bryer (= Basin) is slain (No. 2); the peers convey his body into the city (No. 3). Floripas laments the fate of Guy (No. 2) (2526–2638). The rescue of the

Burgundian on the following day is described as in the French poem, with the incidents of the slaying of Sir Tamper and of the seizure of a great supply of provisions sent to Laban by the Admiral "Costroye" (No. 3) (2639–2746). Once again Floripas offers maidens to the knights (No. 6); this time, however, Roland objects that the ladies are not 

Christian. Finally, the Sultan flies into a rage against his gods and is forced to make atonement (Nos. 5 and 9). With this familiar motif the siege of Egremour comes to an end (2747-2794).

Throughout the rest of his poem the translator follows his original with his customary freedom in detail and his customary carelessness. 

Two examples of his divergence from the French will suffice. In the 

Continental texts, when Richard of Normandy arrives at the Flagot he finds that the river runs between steep banks. He prays for help in crossing; the waters rise to the top of the banks, and a hart leads him in safety to the opposite shore. The translator says nothing about the steep banks, but when the hart appears Richard swims after it "over the cliffe" (2810). Again, at the end of K Fi (6207-6216) the poet announces that within three years after the close of the wars which have just been described Ganelon is to betray the peers at Roncesvalles, and to suffer a just and terrible punishment. The author of the Sowdon omits the allusion to Roncesvalles, but declares (O! abysmal ignorance of the gestes of France!) that Ganelon is executed for his treasonable acts at Morimond and Mantrible!

My two principal observations concerning the Sowdon of Babylon may be summarized in a sentence: The source of the English poem is the single unified D Fi, which the translator knew by heart but did not

possess in manuscript. If the former of these observations is incorrect, we must imagine that the translator was at once skilful enough to read subtle traits of unity into the two distinct poems of HD and K Fi and clumsy enough to mar the effect of those traits by a dozen inconsistencies and one long and irrelevant interpolation. If the second of these observations is incorrect, we must suppose that the translator on some occasions felt a grave reluctance to employ his manuscript and on others a willingness to leaf back and forth through it in quest of episodes to be preserved in distinctive detail. I can conceive of a literary toiler who might fit the second description, but I think it unlikely that the author of the Sowdon fits either that or the first.
