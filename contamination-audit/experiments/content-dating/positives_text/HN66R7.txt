Foreword

AMERICA'S great machine tool industry has been developed to a large degree through the efforts of men of strong character, who have had to learn the business from the bottom up "the hard way," but whose eventual success never has broken the close bonds existing between them and the skilled workmen who have worked with them. 

Typical of these men, to whom modern American industry owes so much, was the late Frank 

Lyman Cone, inventor of the Cone Multiple 

Spindle Automatic and founder of the Cone 

Automatic Machine Co., Inc., Windsor, Vermont. Within a period of twenty-five years this company has become one of the leading builders of automatics, and today is employing more than a thousand people. 

Cleveland, Ohio, 1941

1868-1936 CONE  was born and educated, and he learned his trade, developed his inventions and built up his company, up there in the Vermont hills, where his family had lived for several generations. Son of Lyman H. and Lucia 

Judd (Fardd) Cone, he was born on December 24, 1868, on a typical hill farm in the township of Weathersfield, about halfway between the villages of Springfield and Windsor-both of which today are known the world over as machine tool centers. 

Mr. Cone's father had returned to Vermont only three years before, after a long period of active service in the Union Army during the Civil War. He was one of those typical Yankee farmers who was also known up there as a "handy mechanic." As a young lad at his side,

Frank picked up the rudiments of carpentry,

blacksmithing and practical mechanics in general, for which he had a decided natural bent. 

Frank attended only a district school, but he was particularly fortunate in one of the teachers. 

This teacher was Justus Dartt, a distinguished man in Weathersfield who had served as an artillery officer during the Civil War and who was a more than ordinarily able mathematician. 

Frank Cone's training in "figuring" under

Justus Dartt was to be of inestimable value to him in his subsequent career, and was far better than ordinarily gained in a district school. 

It was this farm and district school foundation of technical training that inspired Frank Cone-at an early age when boys today are about ready to enter high school-to leave the home farm and go to work for Hira W. Beckwith. In those days he was the leading architect and builder in that section of New England, and his office was in the near-by town of Claremont, New Hampshire. Mr. Beckwith recognized real ability in the boy from the hills across the Connecticut River, and he took pains to train him, not only in first class carpentry and cabinet- 10

making, but also in the drafting and reading of plans for buildings.

In those days railways held a peculiar fascination for Vermont country boys, and Frank

Cone was not exempt as far as the attractiveness of their mechanical phases was concerned. 

Thus it came about that in 1891, just after he had married and established a home of his own on a farm in the so-called "Sixth District" in the rolling hills northwest of Windsor village, he found himself a job as general repair man in a little branch repair shop of the Connecticut 

River Railroad in that village.

The writer remembers this shop well. It was capable only of housing one car or one locomotive at a time. It was equipped only with the crudest mechanical facilities-and when I say crude, I do mean crude. 

For example, one of the best machine tools was an ancient planer so decrepit that the operator often had to shift the drive belt with a broom handle at the end of the stroke. The nature of the work and the nature of this equipment were such as to tax the ingenuity of any mechanic to the I I

utmost. Here it was that Frank Cone began to pick up his thorough knowledge of pattern making as well as of the machinists' trade. His teachers were two heavily bearded veteran railway mechanics of the old school. There were no wage and hour laws in those days, but what he learned was worth far more than short hours and big wages. Long hours never did mean anything to him.

In a foregoing paragraph I mentioned in passing that Mr. Cone was married in 1891. That event in Frank Cone's life deserves much more than casual mention. The young lady was Lena 

Marcy, daughter of Harrison and Elizabeth 

Marcy of Windsor's "Sixth District." Like Mr. 

Cone's father, Mr. Marcy had served in the

Union Army during the Civil War. He and 

Mrs. Marcy were known far and wide as "good neighbors" in the best sense of that term, as understood by old-fashioned New England people. They were religious, but they allowed themselves to enjoy life. They worked hard, but were always ready to help out others who were in trouble. They were thrifty, but they were 12

always ready and willing to share what they had with others less fortunate than themselves. 

That was the way Mrs. Cone was brought up.

That is the way she was throughout the lean years of the 1890's and early 1900's when she and 

Mr. Cone had to watch every penny to make both ends meet and to bring up their three children-two daughters and one son. She remained unchanged through subsequent periods of prosperity. All who know Mrs. Cone, know her as a "good neighbor." I have known her myself for something like forty-five years and that implies my own high regard for her about as well as anything possibly could.

It was on that date that his career as a machine tool builder actually began, for on that day he went to work for the Windsor Machine Company as shop carpenter and general handy man.

Mr. Cone continued to work in that primitive railroad shop until February 5, 1895. It was on that date that his career as a machine tool builder actually began, for on that day he went to work for the Windsor Machine Company as shop carpenter and general handy man.

The Windsor Machine Company had been 13

organized by a group of workmen and Windsor businessmen in 1889, when the Jones & Lamson Machine Company was moved from Windsor to Springfield, Vermont. It occupied the heterogeneous group of water power shops on Mill

Brook, which had been abandoned by Jones & Lamson, and which in the 1840's and 1850's had been occupied by the Robbins & Lawrence Company, pioneer manufacturers of machine tools and original developers of the turret lathe. 

A number of Robbins & Lawrence veterans were among the skilled workmen of the newly organized company, and it was from them that young Frank Cone learned of the great traditions of that old concern-traditions which he himself was destined eventually to perpetuate most brilliantly in Windsor.

At that period the Windsor Machine Company built engine lathes and turret lathes. It was being operated by two efficiency experts, Messrs. Wilson and Wheeler, when Frank Cone joined the organization. Wheeler was a promoter, and Wilson was a brilliant, though somewhat erratic, mechanical genius and designer. 14

After a hectic period under the slogan “A machine a day," this promotion bubble burst. 

Wheeler decamped leaving the faithful Wilson "holding the bag," and the company dropped back into the laps of its original owners. Then, indeed, the sledding was tough, and it is a miracle that the company survived. 

In the meantime, however, Frank Cone had gained some very substantial knowledge of mechanical drafting and machine design under chief engineer Herbert S. Wilson. The young man had become convinced that he should make machine tool building in general, and screw machines in particular, his lifework. Turret lathe drawings made by him at that time are still in existence. They reflect the combination of common sense and talent which always marked his work. The drawings are not "pretty," but they tell a straightforward mechanical story so that shopmen could understand it. 

During the uncertain days following the 

Wilson-Wheeler collapse, Frank Cone managed to hang on with the Windsor Machine Company, doing anything that had to be done, from repair- 15

ing the water wheel to pattern making and drawing. Not only did he work in the machine shop, he also worked in the foundry. No formal apprenticeship could possibly have been more effective, because "his heart really was in it."

In 1898 George O. Gridley hooked up with the tottering Windsor Machine Company, ostensibly to manage the shop but actually, though the directors did not know it, to develop an automatic which he had "under his hat." The fact that Mr. Gridley went there for $50 a month will give some idea as to how things were in those days at Windsor.

The actual beginnings of the building of the first Gridley Single Spindle Automatic were made under the guise of "repairs to water wheel," but by the time the directors finally discovered what really was going on, it had gone so far that they decided to see it through. It was very fortunate that they did, because that decision turned a one-horse country machine shop into a big industry. 

Charles Tuxbury, one of the really progressive directors and treasurer of the company, took 16

keen interest in the new automatic and, through his enthusiastic description of its possibilities, he managed to interest in the project the late Maxwell Evarts, his summertime neighbor. 

Mr. Evarts, well-known New York lawyer, general counsel of the Southern Pacific Railway and associate of industrial leaders, bought into the company, became its president and interested 

Charles A. Moore and "Diamond Jim" Brady in the proposition. Manning, Maxwell and

Moore began to push the new automatic. Evarts began to push Frank Cone along in the organization at Windsor, and the Windsor Machine 

Company really began to "go to town." 

Mr. Cone became general superintendent of the rapidly growing company on January 1, 1905, and held that position until he went into business for himself in 1916. In the meantime, he was active in perfecting the single and multiple spindle Gridley Automatics, and in the building of a new and modern shop. He developed the organization to more than one thousand men, and "sold himself" thoroughly to the late James C. Potter of the Potter & Johnston 17

Machine Company. In 1911 that company purchased control of the Windsor Machine Company from Messrs. Evarts and Moore. 

The National Acme Manufacturing Com pany of Cleveland, Ohio, in turn bought out this “big dividend maker of Vermont" in December, 1915, for $3,575,000. The Windsor Machine 

Company had paid dividends amounting to 125 per cent during that year and Frank Cone decided to establish himself in the machine tool industry as an independent manufacturer. 

Resigning as general superintendent of the 

National Acme subsidiary at Windsor in April of 1916, he hired an office in a Windsor business block, organized the Cone Automatic Machine

Co., Inc., and began drawings for a new automatic. With him at the beginning were one draftsman and his capable secretary, the late 

Miss Celia Gullivan. Like the late Miss Kate 

Gleason she was a capable "machine tool expert" in her own right. Into this undertaking went every cent of Mr. Cone's personal fortune, accumulated throughout thirty years of hard work, and every ounce of his unusual inventive ability. 18

His driving energy and business capacity-characteristics often lacking in men of inventive type -assured success of this enterprise right from the start. 

On April 20, 1916, just nineteen days after leaving the older company which he had done so much to build up, Mr. Cone guided the plow which broke ground for his own plant. It was to stand just north of the old railroad shop in Windsor village where his mechanical career really had begun some twenty-six years before. In the presence of a large gathering of village people, Mr. Cone made one of the few speeches of his career. All he said was: "Some day I am going to have this whole river meadow covered with shops!" Characteristic of him was the brevity of that speech. Proof that his faith in himself was thoroughly justified is given by the fact that within less than twenty years his prediction had come true.

Organization of the Cone Automatic Machine 

Co., Inc., was completed on August 5, 1916, with 

Mr. Cone as President and General Manager.

Actually he was much more than that. As Max- 19

well Evarts had said of him many years before in the days of the Windsor Machine Company: "Who is Cone, did you ask? Why that man is It!" 

Truly he was, being chief engineer, superintendent, head pattern maker, office manager, sales manager and various other things-and doing all of them well.

In the beginning the Cone Automatic was a relatively simple, single spindle machine, but in 

May, 1917, Mr. Cone launched the design of a four spindle machine which in its essential principles was like the Conomatics of today. With characteristic "push," he had this machine perfected and on the market by October, 1917.

The design of the Cone Automatic is so well known today that it hardly needs a description here. Suffice it to say that Mr. Cone broke away from all multiple spindle traditions in that he actually "turned the design upside down." Up to that time conventional automatics showed their turret lathe ancestry by having their operating cams either below or beside the working part of the machine. 

Mr. Cone got away from all that he put all 20

his cams up on top on one long shaft, out of the way of chips, coolant, work and operator. By this simple but highly ingenious expedient he was able to build multiples of extraordinarily large sizes, whose operating positions were right down at convenient working level-no higher than in the case of small size machines. That was one of the features which made Cone Automatics so popular on large diameter bar and tubing work -a field which previously had been confined to single spindle machines almost exclusively.

The fact that Mr. Cone had become "his own boss" did not mean to him that at the age of fortyeight he was going to take it easy. At a time when most men do begin to have ideas along that line, he began to drive himself harder than ever. Not only did he have his own and his family's interests at heart, but even more than that he had at heart the interests of his stockholders, his workmen, his "home town" of Windsor and, most of all, his customers.

In the beginning he picked out about twelve big prospects primarily in the automotive, electrical and anti-friction bearing fields. These he 21

handled personally-finding out what they needed most in the way of automatics; then designing, building and tooling to suit those needs. Customers soon grew to admire his persistence, his ability and, most of all, his integrity. 

His machines were delivered when and as he had promised them. Their prices were reasonable, and if they did not perform as expected, Mr. 

Cone either went out and made them perform or, as he used to say, he "yanked them right out and made everything right with the customer."

It was through the practical value of his engineering methods, plus these straightforward, honest methods of doing business, that this quiet -almost bashful-machine tool genius from the hills of his native Vermont succeeded, within a few short years, in getting hundreds of his multiple spindle automatics into great manufacturing establishments, such as those of Ford, Timken, General Motors, Westinghouse, Chrysler and Packard. 

If ever one man could take all the credit for the conception, founding and entire upbuilding of a large and highly successful machine tool build- 22

ing enterprise, that man was Frank Cone. He never took a lot of credit for it, however; he thought it to be merely something in keeping with the fine machine tool traditions of Windsor, carried out by a lot of hard work and the help of a loyal group of native Vermont mechanics-all of whom continued to address him as "Frank," just as they always had.

He had an office but he seldom used it, spending most of his time in the drafting room or in the shop. If he had time he would make a drawing or have one made. If it were a rush job, he would go right out into the pattern shop and start the patterns "out of his head," letting the draftsmen catch up with him if and when they could. 

If it were a complicated mechanism which the draftsmen had difficulty in working out on paper, he would go into the pattern shop and "beat them to it" by working it out himself in the form of a wooden model.

Mr. Cone never lost the skill-of-hand which he had developed under his father's teaching and under that of Hira Beckwith; in the little railway repair shop; and under the veteran workmen at 23

the old Windsor Machine Company. In spite of all the hard work they had done, his hands always remained the fine hands of a craftsman. Whenever he personally made a model or a pattern at the shop, or laid a hardwood floor, or inlaid a piece of fine furniture at home, the job invariably was a real masterpiece. 

He never appeared to be in a hurry, yet he accomplished a prodigious amount of work. 

When things were "rolling smoothly," he used to walk through the shop with his thumbs in his suspenders and his hat on the back of his head. 

When things were not going so well, he pulled his hat down over his eyes, shoved his hands into his pockets, went after the trouble and cleared it up in short order, with few but forceful words. 

Once, hearing a lot of hilarity in the drafting room when a rush job was on, he put his head in the door and said: "It looks to me as though I had just about six too many draftsmen in here!" The entire engineering staff-all six of them-got down to business in a hurry and nothing more came out of the incident except a permanent increase in the per capita output of drawings. 24

Mr. Cone seldom, if ever, took a vacation, and it was not uncommon for him to spend as many as twenty nights in a month on sleeping cars.

Many of them were between Windsor, Vermont, and Detroit, where he worked closely with the late Jerry Austerberry, a long-time associate from the Windsor Machine Company days who represented the Cone company in the Michigan territory. He never came back from a trip without orders or new ideas.

As a matter of fact, he would pick up ideas in the most unexpected places. The writer once accompanied him on a Saturday afternoon automobile ride into the "back country" northwest of Windsor. There we came across a relic of the past—an old “up-and-down" sawmill. Studying over its primitive driving and feeding mechanism with keen interest, Mr. Cone emerged from the cobwebs with a new idea for a link motion.

The strength of character possessed by Mr. 

Cone was brought out clearly by the great depression. He constantly had two things in mind: one was to keep his men working, the other was to keep his company solvent. Upon this dual ef- 25

fort he staked everything that he had—and again he won out over tremendous difficulties. When domestic business in automatics became almost entirely dormant, he ignored the warnings of many skeptics and accepted big Russian orders on long terms. Thus he kept his key men at work, kept his company alive, and eventually received every last dollar that was due on these supposedly dubious orders which others had not had the courage to accept.

At the very depth of the depression-in the spring of 1933-the National Acme Company gave up its plant in Windsor and moved all the equipment and a part of the working force to 

Cleveland. Thereupon the Cone Automatic Machine Co., Inc. became Windsor's only large industry. 

Then, when things looked their very blackest, the machine tool business began to pick up. Mr. 

Cone began to have work for the able mechanics who had remained in Windsor, and within three years he had most of them working. In the meantime, he had built another large addition to his plant. 26

The trials of the great depression were a heavy load for any man to carry at Mr. Cone's age, and finally they struck him down. For about a month he was seriously ill-then to the amazement of everyone, including his physicians, he rose from his sickbed and went to work again harder than ever. He told friends that he had many things to do and that inasmuch as he might not have much time left in which to do them, he had to "get busy." 

Like another great American engineer, James

Buchanan Eads, whose bust in the Hall of Fame has carved on it, “So many things to do, and so little time to do them in!" Mr. Cone proceeded to accomplish some of his most notable work when he knew that he was "living on borrowed time." He perfected his eight spindle automatics, he added to his already large shop, he installed much new equipment, and when the National 

Machine Tool Show was held in Cleveland in the fall of 1935, he was on hand at his booth every day from morning till night.

He made two extended trips to Europe and established manufacture of certain sizes of his 27

machines with Charles Churchill & Company in England. This quiet Yankee machine tool builder made a favorable and lasting impression upon English industrialists.

He became an active member of the National 

Machine Tool Builders' Association, attended their meetings regularly and rounded out a wide circle of acquaintances among the leading machine tool men of the United States. And in spite of it all, he remained the same quiet, unassuming figure, and all the men in his shop continued to call him "Frank," just as their fathers had before them. 

Then, finally, one bright morning-it was pud August 14, 1936-Frank Cone drove down to the office at his usual time (he invariably appeared there before eight o'clock), opened his mail as usual, disposed of some important matters, then told Henry Chaplin, his son-in-law, that he was tired and was going home. He drove home, went into the house, lay down on the davenport, went to sleep-and did not awaken.

He died just as he had wanted to die-"with his boots on." 99 28

Frank Cone's funeral was the largest ever held in Windsor. It overflowed the house and it overflowed the grounds around the house. Leading machine tool builders and other industrialists came from all over the United States, and plain ordinary folks of Windsor and the surrounding farms joined with them, to pay tribute to this unassuming mechanical and manufacturing genius who came from a Vermont hill farm down to their village to develop and build machines which not only have "made more jobs for more people" in Windsor, but have helped to make available more things—as well as more jobs-to more people all over the world. 

Certainly Frank Lyman Cone was a "rugged individualist." Of course, he believed in the traditional "American way of life."

Had he not been such a rugged individualist, he never would have been able to "pull himself up by his own bootstraps" as he did. Had he not believed in the traditional American way of life, he never would have laid plans so far ahead, nor would he have built up his engineering and manufacturing organization for the future as he did. 29

Frank Lyman Cone established and stuck to sound principles in design, construction, tooling, marketing and servicing of machine tools.

Those principles have lived on after him because, throughout his many years of industrial activity, he instilled them into the minds of a large number of younger people who worked for him and with him. These are his successors, and they are successfully applying those same sound basic principles to present-day technical and business conditions-just as Mr. Cone himself would be doing if he were still in the land of the living.
