The Commission has had assigned to it from time to time four members of the State Police and three members of the Boston police. The Commission has held fifty formal executive meetings and hearings, and four public hearings, in addition to numerous informal conferences. Its counsel and investigators have talked to hundreds of people, and 102 witnesses have appeared before the Commission, most of them in response to subpoena. Except for informal conferences, all testimony has been taken under oath and a stenographic record has been made of all testimony. 

The Commission was anxious to insure maximum protection to witnesses who appeared before it and to avoid any injury to the reputation of innocent persons.

After consultation with eminent legal authorities, the following rules of procedure were adopted. We believe that these rules, together with those set forth in the resolve, may stand as a pattern for future investigating commissions. 

1. Any person whose name is mentioned or who is specifically identified, and who believes that testimony given in a public hearing before the Commission or any sub-committee thereof, or comment made by any member of the Commission or its counsel tends to defame him or otherwise adversely affects his reputation, shall have the right to be furnished with a copy of the transcript of such testimony at his own expense. 

2. No release or public information concerning adverse statements made in executive session concerning any witness, person or organization shall be issued by the Commission, or any member thereof, unless and until a reasonable effort is made to afford such witness, person or organization an opportunity to be heard. 

3. All decisions shall be made by a majority vote of the members of the Commission. Hearings shall be held only after due notice to all members of the Commission. 

4. No photographs, moving pictures, television or radio broadcasting of the proceedings shall be permitted while any witness is testifying, except by a majority vote of all the members of the Commission.

The Commission has as its chief responsibility the collecting of information concerning the extent, character, objects and methods of Communism and subversive activities within the Commonwealth, with a view to recommending any necessary remedial legislation. It is not a prosecuting body and never was intended by the Legislature to be such. However, it was clear that information secured by the Commission might be of value to the various law enforcement agencies of the Commonwealth. An early conference was held by the Commission, with the Attorney General and all of the District Attorneys or their representatives, in order that we might consider ways of co-ordinating our activities. Since this conference, testimony of witnesses before the Commission has been furnished to the various law enforcement agencies when it has concerned possible criminal activities; and district attorneys have, in turn, co-operated in furnishing information to the Commission. 

In order to consider the effectiveness of the present statutes concerning subversive activities, a conference was held with a committee of the Massachusetts Police Chiefs Association, to our mutual advantage. It was learned that police chiefs have not been officially informed of the passage of some present statutes. They have now been advised, through their Association Bulletin, of the present laws, but the Commission strongly recommends that procedures be set up whereby law enforcement agencies are officially informed of the passage of laws which they are required to enforce. (Appendix A.)

We have been informed of certain enforcement problems with respect to chapter 264 of the General Laws, and recommend certain amendments thereto. (Appendix B.)

We also recommend that the Judicial Council consider amending the law relative to the issuance of search warrants, so as to include certain evidence sought in connection with subversive activities. (Appendix C.)

PRELIMINARY FINDINGS. 

Investigation of Communism and subversive activities has become increasingly difficult during recent years. 

This point has been repeatedly stressed by J. Edgar 

Hoover and need not be labored here. It must, however, be kept in mind.

During the period of the Commission's existence, we have been able to uncover a good deal of information, containing many promising leads. To the extent that such leads may enable us to reach and expose the well concealed underground policy-makers of the Communist movement, it is not deemed desirable to discuss them in any detail at the present time. It is easy enough to find the fringes of Communist activity, but it is far more important to reach the center of such activities and to strike at their source.

These preliminary findings do not in any way purport to give a complete picture of the Commission's activities and findings. Some of the evidence developed by the 

Commission is already known. We discovered that until recently the Communist Party of Massachusetts maintained an active bank account under that name, and has been renting office space at 2 Park Square. Legally, the continued existence of the Communist Party, subsequent to the passage of chapter 805 of the Acts of 1951, which outlawed it, can be assumed on the basis of these facts. 

The activities of the Communist Party, and of its former members, are by no means so open. The unmistakeable evidence of Communist activity which has come to the attention of the Commission shows that Communist Party work is being carried on through a number of subsidiary groups or fronts. Among such groups is, of course, the "Freedom of the Press Club," an organization of many years' standing in Boston, whose chief function is the collection of money for the Communist Party newspaper, the "Daily Worker." There are other groups which are still under investigation by the Commission.

EDUCATION. 

There is unmistakable evidence, made clear by the testimony of numerous persons, both before Congressional Committees and before this Commission, that during the late 1930's and the early 1940's a number of teachers were attracted to and joined the Communist Party. This was the period during which Earl Browder was the leader of American Communism, and also the period during which the United Front was the policy and the tactic of the American Communist Party. Teachers in colleges and universities were considered valuable for the following reasons: as sources of funds; as instructors and administration of Marxist or "labor" schools; as prestige people who might attract others to join; as a good source of names, sponsors, and so forth, to promote activity in and support of innumerable Communist fronts.

After the expulsion of Browder from the party, it appears that the emphasis of the Communist Party changed. Those professional people who remained within the party assumed more subtle and concealed rôles. 

The Commission has sought to follow up every lead presented to us in the field of education. Six college presidents and 19 college professors and public school teachers have appeared before the Commission, and a number of others have been questioned by counsel or our investigators.

All of the college presidents have made clear that as a matter of policy they would not knowingly employ or retain Communists on their teaching staffs.

Of the college professors and public school teachers there are some who admit that they were Communists

[May 

in the past, but who have turned their backs on Communism and have co-operated fully with the Commission. 

Others who have been questioned on the basis of reported Communist front activity have stated that they were never members of the Communist Party, and some of them have given the Commission valuable information about the groups with which they were associated. 

The Commission examined two college teachers who have declined to answer certain questions, using their constitutional privilege under the XIIth Article of the Massachusetts Constitution. These persons are not in permanent positions, and in one instance the teacher's contract expires in June, 1954. The Commission is considering the constitutionality as well as the wisdom of a statute which would forbid teaching by persons availing themselves of the constitutional privilege. This complex question is still being studied, and the Commission does not make a recommendation at this time.

Five professors and teachers have declined to answer some of the questions put to them in hearings, but have not invoked their constitutional privilege. They answer many, and in some cases, all questions relative to their own activities, but have refused to answer all or some questions relative to the identifying of persons known to them. The Commission is giving close scrutiny to the problems raised by these people.

Two persons who appeared before the Commission were subsequently suspended by their local school committees. 

The investigation of the educational field is still in progress.

In addition to the work of investigating reports of past or present subversive activities in schools or colleges, the subcommittee on education of the Commission has been studying the problem of curriculum and teaching procedures which will strengthen, in an affirmative manner, the understanding and adherence of students to our government, our society and our democratic processes.

GOVERNMENT SERVICE. 

The Commission has been able to give only brief attention to the investigation of subversive activities in the public service of the Commonwealth. One state employee, on being questioned by his department head concerning Communist Party activities, profferred his resignation, which was accepted. The Commission discussed the case with the department head involved and is assured that proper action was taken.

LABOR AND INDUSTRY. 

Although this area was not the primary one which the Commission sought to study, circumstances brought forceably to the attention of the Commission the problem of the infiltration of Communists or other subversive persons into the industrial field. The vast majority of persons in both labor and management are strongly opposed to the Communist influence. There is evidence however, that an effort has been made to infiltrate and attempt control of a few unions. The Commission is in possession of evidence of Communist activity in six unions within the Commonwealth. The industrial field is now being studied intensively, for the purposes of identifying the source and methods of this sort of subversion, and of recognizing the efforts of those many unions who have successfully combatted subversive influences from within their ranks. 

There is some evidence that during the last few years the chief effort of the leadership of the Communist Party has been directed to the infiltration of the ranks of labor. To the credit of union members, this effort seems to have met with little success. Nevertheless, it is in this area that the Communist effort has, in recent years, been most noticeable. With the exception of propaganda through a few "front" groups, the chief propaganda outlet in Massachusetts appears to be found in two or three questionable union publications.

POLITICAL ACTIVITY. 

Instances of direct political activity on the part of Communists or other subversive persons appear to have been very limited in the past two or three years. This field is under investigation at the present time. 

FURTHER RECOMMENDATIONS. 

At the beginning of the Commission's work we were disturbed to learn that the files, records and data of previous commissions were not readily available. We consider it important that information secured on behalf of the Commonwealth should not be dissipated, but should be kept in usable form. 

Further, we were informed by representatives of the Massachusetts Police Chiefs Association that police departments throughout the Commonwealth have, since 1949, been reporting evidence of subversive activities only to the Federal Bureau of Investigation, and that they have not known what state agencies, if any, should receive such evidence.

We consider that the enforcement of laws relating to subversive activities is and should be a police matter, and we believe it should be handled by trained people, sufficiently expert to investigate, and, equally important, to assess such information and reports as may come to their attention. We therefore recommend the creation within the Department of Public Safety of a small division charged with the maintenance of records, the receipt and investigation of reports, and the duty of co-operating with law enforcement agencies concerning subversive activities within the Commonwealth. (Appendix D.)

A complete account of the methods and findings of the Commission will appear in a final report to the Legislature. Much of our work is still in progress. Some of it would affect the adjudication of cases now in actual preparation by law enforcement agencies. We have discovered and are still in process of discovering valuable
