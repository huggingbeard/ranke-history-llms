CHAPTER I UNITED STATES DEPARTMENT OF LABOR RADIATION CASES PART A DIGEST OF BUREAU EMPLOYEES' COMPENSATION CASES (NOS. 1-24) Type of Injury: Skin Cancer. 

CASE NO. 1 BEC's Decision: Compensation Denied-No Appeal Taken. 

Claimant's Allegation: That an injury diagnosed as skin cancer present in 1956 on certain upper areas of his body were the result of exposure to gamma radiation incurred during an earlier period of government employment.

Facts: Claimant is a 66 year old male Inspector-Radiographic Technician, employed by a government installation for 24 years, who was engaged in inspection techniques used in studying foundry products and steel forgings using radium "pills" and photosensitive film. For a period of about five years, from 1935 to 1940 and intermittently thereafter until about 1945, he handled radium "pills" varying from 250 to 500 milligrams each. He arranged the articles to be x-rayed and removed the radium "pill" from its lead container by means of a fish line attached to an eyelet in the pill. He then nested the pill on a tripod where it remained during exposure. For short periods the claimant's hands were approximately one foot from the pill. He reversed this procedure after exposure and replaced the pill in its lead box. 

Sometimes the line attached to the pill would wear out or break and to replace it the claimant would place a metal beam, or other heavy material, upright between him and the pill, reach around the sides and tie a new line in the eyelet. He carried the lead box containing the pill from one location to another repeatedly in a motor vehicle. Trips var ied in distance up to 100 miles and the box was always placed in a far away part of the vehicle.

Film badge readings were not located. Numerous medical tests were made in 1960. Radon breath samples showed approximately 32 × 10-2 equivalent curies per liter activity and did not indicate any abnormal body burden of radium present. Bone marrow was normal and other findings and tests were not remarkable. 

Medical Evidence: BEC submitted the case file to a radiologist for his opinion. Lacking quantitative data on the actual amount of exposure, 11

he based his opinion on the medical probability of injury. He noted that the evidence indicated that the average distances from the source of exposure involved 15 to 20 feet up to 5 hours at a time. Basing his calculations on a 40-hour week, 280 day working year, 20 foot average distance, the radiologist was of the opinion that the claimant could have received .4520 r/week and 18.08 r/year from a 500 mg. source (intensity 0.0113 r/hr. at 20 feet). The radiologist stated: "It is doubtful that this type of exposure amounted to even half the maximal amount calculated." He pointed out that present exposure limits are far more liberal for the skin than for the total body radiation exposure and that present limit for the skin is 1.5 rems or 1500 mr per week and applies to the hands and forearms etc., provided that gonads, blood forming organs and lens of the eyes can be shielded so that their long time average limit of 5 rem per year is not exceeded. Observing that the claimant's hands were exposed to the gamma more than any other part of his body and that his eyes were also facing directly toward the radium during most of the operations, the radiologist pointed out that the hands and eyes presented no signs of radiation effects. The radiologist also stated that while it is well known that gamma rays of radium, in "sufficient quantity" can cause skin cancer, he concluded that the epitheliomas on claimant's trunk were not due to the radiation encountered because: 

1. There was no likelihood that the skin dosage exceeded permissible limits especially in the areas involved by lesions. 

2. No noted damage to the most heavily exposed areas (the hands). 

3. No damage to eyes was noted and he considered cataracts most likely sequel to exposure of this type (if excessive). 12

Type of Injury: Lymphosarcoma. 

BEC's Decision: Compensation Granted. 

Claimant's Allegation: That lymphosarcoma was contracted as a result of possible exposure to radiation in the performance of his duties between 1941 and 1956. 

Facts: Claimant is the widow of a welding inspector for a government installation. The significant period of employee's exposure covers many months under different conditions.

Between 1942 and 1944 the employee spent about 60 percent of his working time in a production area where a 250 KVP x-ray machine was being used to check equipment. Exposures varied from 30 seconds to 30 minutes. He supervised the placement of specimens and was usually in the building when x-rays were being made. In this same type of operation x-rays were often made using a 300 mg. radium source. 

The employee worked in and around this "pill” or “capsule” repeatedly and frequently. Many times it lay unshielded while he and others. worked only a few feet away for extended periods. Film badges and dosimeters were not used.

Between March 1954 and July 1955 the employee worked closely and directly with an x-ray machine of the 220 KVP type doing industrial testing. There was another x-ray machine in use about the same area. 

It was a portable unit 250 KVP. The shielding around the first machine did not overlap properly and the second machine was poorly shielded. The employee worked regularly about this x-ray testing area several hours per day and the x-ray machines were often operated several hours each day. He placed specimens, supervised the x-ray exposures and otherwise participated in the activity.

Development of the facts in the case revealed that the 1941-1942 period, prior to above, involved exposure to magnaflux testing machines rather than to ionizing radiation. 

No film badge or dosimeter data were available to cover this employee during any of the periods in question. No protective garments were used; no periodic blood counts were given to the decedent and his training included very little reference to radiation hazards and precautions.

On January 26, 1956, employee had sudden onset of extreme dizziness, perspiration and upper abdominal pain. He rested for about one hour and resumed work. Four hours later his complaints recurred accompanied by diarrhea. He was admitted to a hospital on January 31, 1956, where inpatient examination disclosed multiple lymphosarcoma. 13

He did not associate the disability with ionizing radiation until about January 1959 at which time he filed claim for compensation alleging that while inspecting industrial equipment by means of radiography between 1941 and 1955 he incurred this disability. He alleged use of both x-ray machines and radium containing "pills."

Medical Evidence: The radiologist selected by BEC to review the case and furnish an opinion was compelled to base his reasoning on statistical probability without the benefit of specific quantitative data on the exact amount of exposure. He opinion is in part as follows: 

Possible exposure has been reviewed and the uncertainties of dosage received by the decedent noted. It appears, however, that he may have received amounts substantially in excess of standard limits. Quantitative relationship to leukemogenesis is not well established except for rather high dosage. As has been noted in other cases, an increased incidence averaging something like two cases per million per year per rad for an exposed population has been calculated. This, however, involves a linear extrapolation to low dosage, not generally accepted. Radiation effects also appear to be lessened when dosage is spread out. The general incidence of leukemia is given as about 6 per 100,000 population per year, or 60 per million. These figures suggest that with 30 rad general body exposure, a person so exposed, who developed leukemia, would have a 50-50 possibility that ionizing radiation was responsible. As already noted, such a linear extrapolation does not probably hold good. However, at what point the incidence would be doubled is uncertain. My own estimate, which is no more than what might be called an educated guess, is somewhere between 60 to 120 r, especially if concentrated.

Present day standards permit an average of 50 r per decade, provided no one year's exposure exceeds 15 r, which would be about 0.3 r per week for that period. The general average, however, is about 100 mr per week. În 30 years this would permit of 150 rads of general exposure, an amount deemed relatively safe even with one additional accidental exposure of 25 r. In the nature of things, absolute safety is not guaranteed. In the matter of leukemogenesis, a dosage relatively safe for the individual . . . may nevertheless not be without significance . . . if leukemia should develop. If sufficient radiation is received by a group to quadruple the rate, the probability of radiation being the cause becomes 3 to 1. Yet the incidence would still only be 24 per 100,000 and the individual chances of developing the disease only I out of about 4167. 

At all events, the zone of 6-120 r could well be of significance in leukemogenesis, in my opinion, if received in a 21 month period. 

Such dosage would be beyond the average of 100 mr per week and the exceptional limit of 15 r for one year. It thus becomes of interest to calculate how much time a person would have to spend close to a radium source to receive a 100 r at an average of 700 mr per hour. This amounts to about 143 hours and on the basis of 21 months or about 90 weeks, would call for 1.6 hour per week and 0.277 hours, or about 17 minutes per day (6 day work week).

This poses the question as to whether or not the decedent might 14

have spent so much time close to the source-roughly a quarter hour per day. Without detailed knowledge of his working habits one cannot say and, of course, we all know that such exposure could have and should have been avoided. However, a busy man, very poorly indoctrinated as to radiation hazards and not subject to rules or supervision, could readily spend that much time close to the source. Added to such exposure there would likely be some from x-ray. This would, in my estimation, not be beyond usual limits but would, of course, add to the total. 

Reasoning that the exposures were in fact considerably in excess of the maximum permissible dose, the radiologist concluded that the industrial exposure described above probably caused the death from "lymphosarcoma with a marked leukemia reaction." He expressed the opinion that he favored “chronic lymphatic leukemia" as the primary entity and that from the standpoint of possible etiological effects from ionizing radiation, the relationship should be considered that of leukemia. 15

CASE NO. 3 Type of Injury: Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia. 

BEC's Decision: Compensation Granted. 

Claimant's Allegation: That cumulative radiation exposure received during employment covering a period of about six years, resulted in chronic lymphocytic leukemia. 

Facts: Claimant had some potential radiation exposure in April 1952 involving nuclear testing at the time he was in military service. No film badge readings or other data were available to disclose the extent of this exposure. As a civilian employee, 1952-1958, claimant's next phase of radiation exposure involved a period of nine months from June 1953 to early 1954 during which period claimant issued and received radioactive sources used in courses in radiological safety. Most sources, about ten in number, contained cobalt 60 with intensities ranging from 7.6 to 58.7 mr. per hour at 1 meter. Included in this number was also a 100 mg. radium source and a 25-50 mg. radiumberyllium combination. This source was incorrectly identified as radium and ineffectively shielded in a lead container or "pig" until May 1956. This source was used on all field trips and treated as a radium source. There is evidence that this source was stored in the vicinity of the instructors for six months before it was realized that it was dangerous. The next episode involved about 15 months of teaching, April 

1954-July 1955, in which out of 390 teaching hours only 27 involved radiation exposure. Personnel dosimetry data are lacking but there was evidence of a class member's pocket dosimeter readings of from 30 to 60 mr. in field trips averaging about three hours of actual exposure. 

The next phase of exposure extended over about two years, August 1955 to July 1957 and probably involved much more exposure. Work called for 21 hours of instruction in which radiation exposure was present for each four weeks course; occasionally 42 hours exposure would be involved. The number of courses given is not listed but ordinarily there would not be much time between courses. There are no records of the claimant's exposures listed during this period except for one film badge reading of 50 mr and four negative readings over the period November 24, 1956 to February 1, 1957, a period when testimony indicates that readings were not reliable. Neutron exposure doubtless occurred and until May 1956, such exposure would be subject to increase by reason of inadequate shielding of the source. The final teaching assignment of about 8 months, August 1957 to April 1958, involved only about 15 hours of exposure per four weeks course and no extra exposure due to inadequate shielding of the neutron source. A number 16

of negative and three positive film badge readings, apparently reliable, are noted in this period, with values 100, 90, and 80 mr. 

Medical Evidence: A board certified specialist in radiology and internal medicine was selected by BEC to review the case file. He reported to the Bureau as follows: 

1. Over a 6-year period there were certain instances of exposure which could well have been over permissible limits. Quantitative estimates are quite conjectural owing to the lack of precise data. It may be possible to obtain dosimetry figures for the nuclear tests, but there seems little possibility of establishing adequate dosage levels for the years of work with radioactive sources including an unidentified and improperly shielded neutron source. In regard to this last, dosimetry calls for special devices. Neutron exposures would not be properly recorded at all on ordinary film badges or by usual radiac meters. 

2. The main question is whether or not these exposures, conjectural as to amount, should be held accountable for the leukemia in this case. This involves an inprecise and controversial field for low and moderate dosages. It is well known that dosages probably totaling some hundreds of r units have been responsible for about a tenfold increase in the incidence of leukemia in the older group of radiologists. However, a linear relationship permitting of extrapolation to low dosages is much to be doubted although assumed by some scientists and used as the basis for very disturbing predictions. As noted in another case, a medical specialist in radiology has calculated an increase of about two cases per million population per rad per year for acute or chronic radiation. On such a basis, since the normal incidence is about 60 cases per million per year, about 30 rad should double the rate. Thus, according to these figures, among people so exposed, there would be an even chance that a given case of leukemia would be due to the radiation received. Such figures as already indicated are not considered valid by other workers and "I believe" that the incidence in the lower dosage ranges will prove notably less. The problem remains as to precisely what level a leukemogenic effect will become significant. 

As yet it is in a twilight zone. In this particular case, we find the door open to overexposure to radioactive sources as already noted and radiological safety practices appear to have been very lax. In addition, there were two incidents of probably exposure in the course of nuclear tests. In the last which occurred. at about the time of, or just prior to the initial manifestation of his illness, he appears to have monitored a very “hot” area. 

In general, degrees of exposure are conjectural but may have been substantial. Thus in view of all circumstances, "I feel" that the claimant should be given the benefit of a doubt and his disease considered the probable result of his occupational exposure, notably that during the years he handled the radioactive sources. His final monitoring exposure could well be considered as an aggravating factor. 

3. The medical records have been reviewed and, in “my opinion," the diagnosis of chronic lymphatic leukemia is justified. 17

4. The records and circumstances attending the exposures of the claimant to ionizing radiation have been reviewed and analyzed in the light of present quantitative data on leukemogenesis. The amount of radiation received is highly conjectural as noted before, but "in my opinion" there appears to be reasonable probability that it may have been sufficient to be the cause of leukemia. 

5. Favorable action on the compensation claim is recommended. 18

CASE NO. 4 Type of Injury: Brain Tumor Resulting In Death During the Month Of December 1957. 

Claimant's Allegation: Widow filed death benefits claim alleging that her husband had been in excellent health prior to the time that he worked on experimental projects with various radioisotopes starting in the year 1951 and was exposed in July 1955 to a considerable amount of air-borne iridium 192, in the course of his work. The widow particularly emphasized that the radioisotopes he used in a variety of research activity which included tantalum, iodine, scandium, phosphorous, and iridium 192, were all highly potent gamma emitters and that also some of them were volatile.

Facts: A radiation safety specialist reviewed the safety practices of the agency with respect to the particular work performed by the deceased. 

He noted that the decedent had been fully indoctrinated by his agency in radiological safe practices; that his work involving radioisotopes was sporadic or seasonal including field trips (that is, he did not employ such materials in his daily routine). Dosimetry and film badge readings indicated, according to the specialist, that the decedent's exposures were well below those considered maximal permissible, except for a few exceptions which the specialist considered "readings rather than actual exposure." The specialist stated that he reached this conclusion in view of explanation by decedent's July 16, 1954 letter to the extent that the two most substantial readings, 350 mr and 490 mr in the period June 28-July 12, 1954, were due to accidental exposure of film badges when they were not being worn. The decedent had explained this situation in his July 16, 1954 letter to the agency wherein he indicated that he expected these two badge readings "to turn out bad” because they were packed in close proximity to the isotopes for about 18 hours during a field trip of the deceased. 19

Medical Evidence: The case record was reviewed for BEC by a medical expert in the field of the effects of radiation on the body. He was a physician not previously connected with the matter and was not employed by the agency. His opinion is in part as follows: 

1. Deceased death was diagnosed as caused by glioma of the midbrain, and the question of possible etiological relationship to exposures to ionizing radiation has been brought up.... 

2. As regards the cause of death, clinical and pathological data appear conclusive. The symptoms that caused the deceased to seek medical care began in June 1957 and are noted by a physician, as spells in which he experienced a peculiar strong organic odor followed by dizziness, weakness and sweaty feelings. However, for some months previous, intimate associates had noticed deterioration in his abilities. Special studies suggested subcortical atrophy but tumor was not ruled out. His condition rapidly deteriorated, resulting in two hospital admissions. He showed weakness of the extremities, partial paralysis, incoherence and confusion. It appears that a convulsive seizure occurred and also an instance of bedwetting. He died in a hospital. 

3. Autopsy showed a large infiltrating tumor involving the left frontal, parietal and temporal lobes with extension across the midline to the right frontal lobe. Pathological diagnosis indicated a rapidly expanding glioma of astrocytoma variety. Blocks of tissue were eventually sent to a medical center where the diagnosis was verified as glioma of primarily diffuse growth. From clinical and pathological aspects it appears that there can be no substantial doubt as to the correctness of the diagnosis of glioma as the cause of death. 

4. This brings us to the matter of radiation exposures received by the decedent and possible relationship of such to his death from brain tumor. It can be said at once that any etiological relationship of brain tumor to radiation appears an unlikely sequel to those of us familiar with radiation pathology, but nevertheless it is only proper to review the details fairly and judiciously. 

5. Beginning with the exposures received, we find that these are well documented and do not appear excessive. The work of the decedent called for use of radioactive tracers and was largely limited to summer seasons during the period 1953-57. The decedent was well trained and an expert in his field. He appears to have used Iodine 131, Scandium, Rubidium86, Iridium192, and Silver¹10. Dosimetry appears to have been reliable and film badge and monitoring reports indicated that the decedent's exposures were well below those considered maximal permissible. A few exceptions were noted which, however, appear to have involved readings rather than actual exposure. According to a statement of deceased himself, the two most substantial, 350 mr and 490 mr, were due to accidental exposure of film badges when they were not being worn. The incident accorded greatest importance in the matter of the brain tumor was the use of Ir192 in the summer of 1955. It does not appear that external dosages were excessive, but the possibility of inhalation has been brought up. Circumstances indicate possibility of such, involving, however, minimal amounts. Nevertheless, it is appropriate to note the radiobiological potentialities 20

involved. Ir192 of the type involved is a 74 day half-life beta-gamma emitter with gamma energies extending up to 1.16 mev. Biologically we find the effective half-life in the body is listed as 17 days for the kidneys and 45 days for the spleen, these being the critical organs, according to Table 1, page 246, Atomic Medicine 3rd Ed., 

1959, Chap. 12 by Robertson and Cohn. The brain is not a critical organ for Ir192; nor for the other isotopes deceased used. This is in keeping with the fact that experience indicates that the prerequisite for the induction of malignancies by radioactive isotopes is long term lodgement as in the instance of bone sarcoma from fixation of radium in skeletal structures over many years. Thus no basis is found for incriminating Ir192 or the other radioactive isotopes involved in this case, as causing or aggravating the brain tumor considered to be the cause of death. 

6. The clinical and pathological features of this case have been reviewed and the opinion that the death of the decedent resulted from an extensive glioma of the brain, is considered completely justified. 

7. The exposures of the decedent to ionizing radiation have been reviewed with particular regard for the possible role of Ir192. It appears that no significant overexposures occurred. 

8. The relationship of radioactive isotopes, especially Ir192 to the development of brain tumors has been reviewed with negative results. 21

Furthermore, the dose of radiation that will produce small, stationary opacities in the lens has never produced any retinal changes, to the best of our knowledge. Therefore it is difficult for us to conceive any cause and effect relationship between radiation and the patient's macular lesions.

Examination of the lenses alone in this patient would not enable one to say definitely whether or not the opacities resulted from radiation. They are compatible with observed radiation changes.

Ionizing radiation has been assumed to be an etiological factor in cancer, and there are two idfferent types of situations. One is the long protracted chronic type of exposures, such as . . in the hands of the radiologists, as well as leukemia appearing in radiologists. In this situation, a portion of the body or the entire body is exposed to relatively low doses of radiation at very frequent intervals over a long period of time. While it is impossible to assess the dose of radiation of this type of exposure that may cause a malignancy in later years, it would be assumed that a dose in the order of one-half to one roentgen daily, approximately five days a week, over a period of several years, would increase a likelihood of the exposed individual to develop some form of malignancy. 

The other type of exposure would be the acute exposure, in which instance the individual would be exposed to ionizing radiation to a portion of his body or his entire body over a short period of time, a few days to a minute or less. It has been proven experimentally that the longer the exposure of an acute dose of radiation, the greater the dose required to produce the detrimental result. A dose in the order of fifty to one hundred roentgen to the entire body in one day or less would probably increase the chances of that individual developing a malignancy or leukemia later in life.

In either situation, there is a latent time following the chronic or the acute exposure before one would expect the malignancy to develop. This latent period is usually considered to be approximately ten to twenty years following exposure, of the acute type, or ten to twenty years of exposure to the chronic type. In other words, a radiologist who exposes his hands or his body to ionizing radiation during his daily work would not be expected to develop the malignancy due to such exposure for approximately fifteen years. 

On the other hand, an individual who has been exposed to an acute dose of radiation in a matter of a day or two or even a minute or less would not be expected to develop a malignancy due to this type of radiation exposure in a period of less than approximately ten to fifteen years, in my experience. 55

In referring to an eye examination of the claimant performed by another physician in 1961, the radiologist stated: 

At that time, it was recognized that "radiation" could produce the type of tissue changes seen but the entity of radiofrequency or microwave cataract and the pathognomonic relationship of posterior capsular opacification to this entity had not yet been elucidated.

Thus, it can be stated that the etiology of [claimant's] cataracts was exposure to radiofrequency radiation. 

(2) The Radiations. 

The radiations are divided into two types-the non-ionizing and the ionizing.

Non-ionizing radiation is recognized as that portion of the electromagnetic spectrum extending from the short ultraviolet region to long radiowaves. Insofar as cataractogenesis is concerned, non-ionizing radiation produces this effect by thermal injury to the capsule surrounding the lens and not by direct injury to the lens substance. Such thermal injury can originate in two different loci of the lens capsule, either the anterior or the posterior regions. 

The infra-red portion of the spectrum affects the anterior capsule 60

ད་ A more than the posterior capsule for two reasons: (1) the temperature gradient is higher close to the surface; and, (2) the iris, in front of the lens, is a good absorber of infra-red, and as the iridial temperature rises, the iris serves as a secondary thermal source which is in contact with the anterior capsule of the lens. The radiofrequency (particularly microwave or radar) portion of the spectrum tends to raise the temperature of the eye uniformly; however, the anterior surface of the eye is bordered by a circulatory system (iris blod flow and aqueous) which acts a coolant; therefore in this case, the temperature is higher at the posterior capsule.

Ionizing (gamma or x-ray) radiation, on the other hand, affects the substance of the lens. Although the late effects of ionizing radiation may ultimately be seen at the posterior pole, even when they are limited to the subcapsular region of the lens, such effects are physically internal to the capsule and the capsule itself remains transparent. This is true of a healthy lens and, in such an event, the formation of the lens opacification occasionally occurs in a pathognomonic manner proceeding through the stage of posterior doughnut to posterior polar subcapsular cataract. 

Some types of particulate radiation (beta rays or neutrons) may also produce cataracts. Here, we shall limit our discussion to beta radiation (highly ionizing) which is recognized to be a stream of electrons with certain similarities to that produced by the electron gun of the suspect microscope. Beta radiation can produce changes in both the capsule and the substance of the lens. The secondary x-rays produced by beta radiation act in the same manner as the ionizing radiation discussed above. 

The cataractogenicity of ionizing radiation (whether purely x-ray or combined x-ray and beta ray) may be considered in two different situations, first-in relationship to an otherwise healthy lens, and second-in relationship to a diseased lens.

For the healthy lens, the cataractogenic dosage is within an order of magnitude of 500 roentgen (50 to 5000 roentgen depending on individual sensitivity, fractionation of the radiation, etc.) However, if a lens exhibiting any stage of cataractous changes whether metabolic or thermal is irradiated by any form of ionizing radiation, the cataractogenesis will proceed at an accelerated rate. If the cataractogenesis were quiescent or latent prior to irradiation, it would become active and progressive. At least one order of magnitude less irradiation would activate or accelerate such a previously sensitized lens. The nature of the original type of cataractogensis would not be altered. In this case, it remains a microwave cataract. The net effect of exposure to even extremely low levels of ionizing radiation is to exacerbate the latency and accelerate the process. 

(3) The Inter-relationships. 

Claimant exhibited the typical findings and history of radiofrequency cataracts. He had a history of working in a microwave environment (radar) during the period of 1942 to 1945. After a latent period of many years, he worked intimately with an ionizing radiation source, and for purposes of this discussion we will assume it to be a weak source of radiation. However, at that time, 61

he worked close to the source in order to make fine adjustments. 

Vision was affected primarily by means of his dominant left eye so that for any spurious exposures to ionizing radiation, the left eye received more radiation than the right eye. These exposures resulted in the re-activation and/or acceleration of the dormant cataractous changes which subsequently led to mature cataracts in the left eye prior to the right eye. The pathognomonic finding of posterior capsular cataract was documented in the right eye.

CASE NO. 40 Anders v. Sandia Corp. 

CLAIM NO. 63 LA 247-180 Type of Injury: Leukemia. 

CIAC's Decision: Compensation Denied. 

Date of Decision: 1964. 

Claimant's Allegation: That the decedent's death was caused by job-incurred exposure to radiation.

Facts: Decedent was employed by the defendant corporation as a cameraman and laboratory technician from June 1953 to August 1959. His duties consisted of photo-lab work and the operation of cameras in photographing atomic tests at certain test sites. He was hospitalized in 

March 1962 for extreme fatigue, dizziness and nausea. He died on July 

13, 1962. The death certificate gave "lumbar pneumonia" as the immediate cause of death and also stated that the decedent had “hyperplastic anemia" and "acute leukemia."

The widow testified that decedent had been sent to one test site for the testing of atomic detonations; that he slept in a sleeping bag more than once at his camera site out on the range; and that on one occasion he was asked to move "a few feet" because he was in a radiation area that was being posted as a radioactive area.

Records showed that decedent was never issued a film badge and, accordingly, there was no record of radiation exposure during his trips to the test sites. At one of the sites, the testing in which decedent participated was of "inert vehicles", devices dropped from planes, shaped like bombs and carrying only a small spotting charge. While evidence indicated that atomic tests were performed at one of the sites, there was no evidence that decedent ever received substantial exposure; any that on no occasion was decedent ever in a radiation area, required to participate in full-scale detonations or to observe these detonations. Accordingly, his supervisors testified there was no occasion for issuance of film badge or other radiation measuring devices. A health physicist, employed by the defendant corporation, testified that the records of those employees issued beadges indicated "the lowest recorded exposure was 

10 milliroentgen ... one-tenth of the permissible weekly dose." He had no idea of the extent of radiation of anyone not issued a film badge or dosimeter.

nearest the only detonation which would have delivered a measureable dose. Basing his figures on the assumption that decedent was at this photo station, he estimated decedent's dose as 7/100ths of 1 mr. He stated that since he had no knowledge of where the decedent had allegedly camped out, he was unable to calculate the radiation at that point. He further testified that while there are different levels of sensitivity in cells and in individuals, and while the absolute amount of dosage required to cause leukemia is not known, evidence has shown that in no case has leukemia been observed which can be said to have been caused with certainty by radiation where there has been a dosage of less than 350 rads. Based on an assumption that decedent was at that photo station nearest the detonation for 18 months and for 56 hours per week, he also calculated that decedent would have received approximately 130 to 150 mr per year.

CASE NO. 55 

Johnson v. Robert D. Johnson Mining Co.

Type of Injury: Squamous-Cell Carcinoma of Left Lung with Metas tasis, Resulting in Death.

Claimant's Allegation: That carcinoma of the left lung was contracted as the result of exposure to radon gas in uranium mining during the performance of his duties for a period of 9 years prior to disablement on August 22, 1958.

Facts: In the summer of 1957, a sputum examination of the decedent was made. The sputum contained cells, grade III—suspicious of malignancy. A repeat sputum examination and bronchoscopy were done, and they were reported as negative at that time. On August 22, 1958, a chest X-ray was taken which showed very marked hilar enlargement on the left side, indicative of either a lung tumor or very marked infectious reaction. It was also noted that due to the bilateral increased lung markings, a diagnosis of silicosis could be made. 

The decedent was treated for the next five days with antibiotic therapy but showed no improvement. A repeat X-ray was taken which continued to show the presence of a probable tumor mass. The patient was then referred to a physician who bronchoscoped the patient and found malignant cells. As a result of this examination, it was suggested that the patient go to a clinic for possible surgical resection of the left lung, X-ray therapy, or further workup. While at the clinic, he was diagnosed as having lung cancer, left lung inoperable due to involvement of the recurrent laryngeal nerve (causing the patient to be hoarse), and also due to silicotic changes whereby it was believed that if the left lung were removed, the right lung would not be able to support the patient. He died November 15, 1958, before the case was determined. The pathologist reported that the type of cell was squamous carcinoma. Thereafter, the decedent's widow filed a claim and the case was heard on the basis of her claim for death benefits. 

A state industrial hygienist testified that after extensive research into the problems of uranium mining, a maximum safe working level of 300 micromicrocuries of radon daughters per liter of air had been established for Colorado. He further testified that tests made in 1952 and 1953 on the mine where the decedent worked showed a concentration 10 to 20 times higher than the above stated maximum working level. 124

A public health official testified that the primary problem in uranium mining from a health viewpoint is radon gas; that this radon is always being produced and is constantly transforming itself into a succession of other elements, the radon daughters; and that radio-lead is the final product of radon daughters and tends to concentrate in human bones. Air samples taken at the decendent's primary work site in 1951 were measured for radioactivity. One of the two samples showed 16 times the recommended working level and the other approximately 300 times the working level. In 1952 two samples were taken and these were 42 and 72 times the recommended working level. 

Medical Evidence: Examinations of the decedent, revealed conflicting evidence regarding the presence of pulmonary silicosis. Testimony was centered upon the possible role of uranium mining and lung cancer. A specialist in chest diseases expressed the opinion that, while studies in the Colorado area of uranium miners had not produced any specific evidence, the radon daughter products have been indicated as the cause of bronchial irritation so that a sufficiently long exposure to high concentration of radon gas is thought to be responsible for the cause of malignancy of the lungs in most cases. He stated that exposure to radon gas, in this particular case, should definitely be "ruled in” as a causative factor. A specialist in internal medicine, said flatly, that in his opinion the deceased's exposure to radon gas in uranium mining was the cause of the development of his lung cancer. After examining the decedent, he wrote the State Compensation Insurance Fund: 

The silicosis is definitely considered to be occupational, but the question of radiation (radon gas) as to the cause of lung cancer has not been definitely proven. However, in this particular case, I believe that radiation had a very definite influence on the development of this man's cancer, because he is much younger than the usual age when lung cancer develops, and he has also had very heavy exposure to radon gas in his various mine leases, particularly in the last two years in [a particular mine.] There are several theories as to the cause of lung cancer-radioactive gas is definitely an irritant and cell stimulator and could cause lung cancer per se.

An epidemiologist answered the question as to causal connection between lung cancer and prolonged exposure to high concentrations of radon and radon daughters as follows:

Yes, I think that our data together with the data from European mines combined with our basic radiobiological knowledge makes this a very strong case that exposure at high levels in such an atmosphere would be very likely to produce or accelerate cancer. .

Also, the excessive radio-lead (4212 times the non-miner group) found in decedent's body was a direct result of his environmental exposure. 125

While the majority of medical experts testified that the decedent's lung cancer was definitely related to his occupation as a uranium miner, a radiologist, expressed the opinion that, while it would be impossible to entirely exclude the possibility that long exposure over the years to radon gas might be an etiological factor, it is "extremely unlikely” that either radon gas or the inhalation of extremely finely powdered uranium was the cause of the deceased's carcinoma. 

Findings of the CIC: 

From the evidence the Referee finds that there was a direct casual [sic] connection between the conditions under which the decedent worked and the carcinoma of the lung caused by exposure to radioactive materials, and this disease appears to have followed as a natural incident of his work. 126

No radon daughter measurements had been reported to the Field Office on any of the mines in which decedent worked prior to January 1957. The exposure levels for all these uranium mines were estimated at between one and two times the Working Level. Many of the readings for the period 1957–64, during which decedent worked in defendant's mine, were high, with one reading of 86 W.L. recorded.

In view of the fact that decedent had worked in more than one uranium mine, a Public Health Service epidemiologist was asked for a general opinion on the problem of "last injurious exposure." He replied:

The National Committee on Radiation Protection, the Inter national Committee on Radiation Protection, Federal Radiation

Council, and the Division of Radiological Health of the U.S. Public Health Service all operate on the premise that all radiation is harmful, that there is no threshold below which no harm to body cells is done. When initially used, this premise was questioned by many workers in the field, but as our knowledge of radiobiology has increased, more and more workers in the field have come to 142

agree with this premise-even though absolute proof is still lacking. 

One consequence of this premise is that in setting maximum permissible exposure values, these groups have generally felt that any exposure value they set might be slightly harmful to somebody so they have developed the philosophy that they should try to balance the amount of potential harm against the benefits to society from allowing these exposures. Consequently, the permissible levels they have put out have never been officially referred to as “safe levels." This includes the "Recommended Working Level" which has been used for uranium mines. A statement which sometimes accompanies maximum permissible exposures is to the effect that these are not necessarily safe levels, that exposure should be kept as far below these levels as practicable, but that it is considered unlikely that a person exposed continually at his work at these levels would suffer overt harmful effects during an average lifetime.

With this background, there are two ways one can go in attempting to answer your question as to whether or not a recent minimal exposure to radiation in a mine (following earlier large exposure) can be considered as a "harmful exposure" in the legal sense. 

1. One can work on the premise used by official radiation protection groups and flatly declare that all radiation exposure is harmful. This might take care of the situation satisfactorily except for one item: There is some radon and radon daughters in cigarette smoke, in the atmosphere of all homes, even in all outdoor air. 

To rigorously apply this concept, the "last harmful exposure" would obviously be the hearing room where the compensation claim is being heard! From the legal standpoint, this is obviously ridiculous, even though it may have some merit from the purely biological standpoint. Using this approach, one could back off a bit from the "pure" concept and take the position that the last exposure which was appreciably higher then that received in “usual breathing air" was the "last harmful exposure" to the last uranium mine in which a man worked. But would this be equitable? 

2. In the case of a uranium miner with lung cancer, it might seem more equitable to phrase the "last harmful exposure" question in this way: Which was the last workplace at which this man received an exposure which might reasonably be expected to contribute to his lung cancer?

This would be a question which would require quantitative cumulative exposure estimates and would invoke the use of information from unpublished studies relating the total amount of radiation received in mines to incidence of lung cancer. In addition, it would require a high degree of judgment in relating incidence or frequency figures to what might be a "reasonable expectation". 

Using this approach I would be inclined to look for the most recent workplace at which a man had received somewhere between 100 and 300 Working Level Months of exposure, and say this is the one which most nearly fits this concept of “last harmful exposure". 143

CIC Decision: Claim Against Company "B" Denied; Claim Against

Claimant's Allegation: That the lung cancer from which decedent died was caused by exposure to radon daughters in uranium mining. 

Facts: Claimant, a uranium miner, had a 12 year work history of mining in various mines from October 1952 until November 1964. He worked for Company "A" from June 1953 until April 1964. He commenced working for Company "B" in August 1964 and left in November 1964.

Records show that decedent was admitted to a hospital on January 6, 1967; that he was discharged on May 4, 1967, and transferred to another hospital where he remained until his death on June 16, 1967. He was first admitted to the hospital complaining of cough and right-chest pain. He was given deep x-ray therapy, 2000 R. in air over the right lower chest posteriorly. Bronchoscopy was done on January 10, 1967. The biopsy showed undifferentiated carcinoma. The malignancy was considered inoperable. 

Medical Evidence: Measurements on several of the mines in which decedent had worked were on file with the State Compensation Insur ance Fund in connection with a U.S. Public Health Service study of uranium miners, of which decedent was a group member. The U.S.

Public Health Service reported: 

Our estimate of his cumulative radiation exposure in uranium mines is 3718 Working Level Months. . . .

He was 64 years old at the time of diagnosis of his lung cancer. 

The time from start of mining to lung cancer was 12 years. He smoked about 20 cigarettes per day for about 15 years, 30 per day for about 12 years, and 40 per day for about 10 years. [Decedent's] radiation exposure in uranium mines is quite high -high enough to put him in a rather high risk group. His age is rather high, but he started mining uranium rather late in life, so that in this use the age should not be considered a significant factor. The time from start of uranium mining to lung cancer is consistent with an occupationally caused cancer. His excessive ciga- 159

rette smoking is the only factor which points away from occupational cancer. 

Additional information on the lead-210 in bone and the cell type of cancer will be helpful in reaching a decision in this case, but with the information at hand, it appears to me that the odds favor considering this an occupationally caused cancer. 

Records of claimant's exposure while working in Company "B's" mine showed 392.2 working level days or 12.9 working level months of exposure. With respect to this estimated exposure, a specialist in occupational medicine testified:

The type of tumor is the most likely to be the result of exposure to alpha emitting radiation. Likewise the history of early exposure to large concentration of alpha particles associated with the high concentration of 210 polonium and 210 lead would indicate a moderately long latent period. For these reasons and the fact that this patient had only 12 working level months late in his exposure as compared to the 3712 working level, I still feel that there can be no relation between this low exposure and the patient developing lung cancer.

State Compensation Insurance Fund decided to admit liability on the question of causation. The Commission approved the admission of liability for the reason that the amount of the last exposure established injurious exposure within the meaning of the Colorado Occupational
