Preface 

TH 'HE purpose of this book is not to awe the reader with the of stiff traditions ordinarily associated with the formal use of fine wines and rare liqueurs. 

Rather, it is intended as an authoritative reference guide, which in simple, uncomplicated form, may bring to you new and interesting facts concerning the origin, use and classification of the world's choicest vintages and spirits. 

Quite often, it has been observed that the proper use of a particular spirit, wine or liqueur will impart a new and favorable taste. On the other hand, there are certain wines, liqueurs and spirits which, if incorrectly used, unjustly are robbed of both flavor and favor as well.

Cocktail Snack occasions.

Fifty pages have been devoted to the USE-SERVICE— 

STORING-PURCHASING and GROWING of wines. While combined with such information you will find liberal comment regarding the types and definition of practically every known wine, liquor and cordial.

This Book represents the work of many months and the services of more than 80 experts who assisted both here and abroad in its compilation.

It is our hope that this reference guide will serve you in good stead, by aiding you in properly inventoring your liquor requirements prior to the time of purchase. May it afford you as much pleasure in consulting it, as it gave us in preparing it.

TR Champagne

RUE Champagne is made only from grapes grown in the old Province of Champagne in the northeast corner of France, just north of Burgundy and west of Alsace. It is today known as the Department of the Marne. Wines produced in other wine districts of the world and even in the United States have been labeled Champagne, but in the true sense of the name, only wine made in the French district is Champagne and should be called Champagne. The French 

Society of Winegrowers has been very strict as to the authenticity of Champagne. If the wine is produced in France and is labeled Champagne, the cork will also have the word, Champagne, imprinted on it; then the purchaser can be sure he is getting an authentic wine. 

The three most important cities in the Champagne district are Riems, Epernay, and Ay. The principal vineyards which produce the grapes from which Champagnes are made lie along the banks of the Marne river.

Champagne is not a cheap wine because it takes a long time to prepare it for the market, the high duties and taxes, and it is and has always been the wine of luxury.

Grape vines have been cultivated in Champagne since time immemorial, but up to the close of the 17th century only red and white still wines had been produced. Although the discovery of sparkling wine has been credited to Dom Perignon, Head Cellarer of the Abbey of Hautvillers, sparkling wines had been made before he was born. 

However, Dom Perignon does deserve credit for first blending wines of different vineyards and for being the first to use the bark of the cork tree as a stopper for the bottle instead of the bits of tow dipped in oil or tallow.

Since Champagne is always a blend of wines from various vineyards, and since some of the wines are made from black (Pinot Noir) and some from white (Chardonnay) grapes, the most important thing. to know about Champagne is the reputation of the blender or shipper whose name appears on the label as your gurantee of quality. After all, it is his ability, proven by the quality of wines he has shipped over a period of years that counts. The blends of each shipper are closely guarded secrets of the house so that particular blends or cuvees of certain shippers become well known for a high grade of quality Champagne. A reliable shipper will not bottle a wine of a poor vintage year 13

The How and When but will blend a quantity of fine wine with it and bottle the blend as a non-vintage wine. This wine, although a non-vintage product, will be superior to a vintage wine of a poor year. The finest Champagnes produced by the leading shippers will be crops of fine vintage years and will be labeled Brut or Nature. The best wines will be between 

7 and 15 years old, although a wine is really ready for market from 

5 to 7 years after the time of vintage.

The wine is made in the fall at each vineyard. As soon as fermentation is completed and the wine falls bright, the wine is racked in the clear casks and shipped from the vineyard to the cellars of the great Champagne firms where the wines of each district are blended together.

In the following spring before the sap begins to run in the vines, the wines from the different districts are blended one with the other to make the cuvee or blend. 

As soon as the Cellar Master is satisfied that the cuvee has reached the pitch of perfection, the wines are ready for bottling. At this time a small dosage of sugar in the form of a syrup is added to each bottle. 

This dosage will vary in quantity depending upon the natural sugar which remains in the wine itself and may run as high as 5 to 6 per cent of the contents of the bottle. The dosage of sugar causes a secondary fermentation to take place and since the bottle is tightly stoppered, the gases become a component part of the wine itself. The bottled wine is then stored in deep cellars where it is allowed to mature for the next 4 to 5 years. After this period, the bottles are subjected to a period of daily agitation, the object being to shake down into the neck of the bottle and onto the cork any sediment that may be floating about in the wine. This process takes about four months. When all of the sediment has been precipitated onto the cork, the neck of the bottle is frozen and the wine is ready for disgorging. The cork is unwired and released from the bottle carrying with it the sediment which has frozen to it. The wine is then examined carefully by an expert. If the wine passes this inspection, it is ready for labeling, packing, and shipping to market.

B Burgundy URGUNDY has long been considered the wine of Kings and at the present time many a lover of fine wines has pledged his allegiance to the wines grown in the Province of Burgundy. 

Burgundies are red or white, still or sparkling. They are fuller to the taste, being heavier in body and flavor than the wines of the Bordeaux.

The Burgundy district is divided into the Cote d'Or, Outer Burgundy (Chablis), Maconnais, and Beaujolais. Wine growing in this district is different from that in the Bordeaux. In Burgundy, the vine yards are owned by large numbers of small proprietors who grow and prepare the wine. An example of this is in the Clos De Vougeot. 

This entire vineyard has roughly 127 acres and it is owned in part by 52 different shippers. This is an average of less than 3 acres per shipper. After the wine has been prepared, the wine merchants purchase the output of a number of the small proprietors for blending and bottling under the shipper's own label. This blending permits the shipper to market a product of uniform quality year after year. 

The selection of the quality of the wine desired is based primarily on the integrity of the wine shipper and the type and vintage of the wine. The district where the wine is produced is also of major importance.

The most famous of the named Burgundies are: 

RED WINES Chambertin, Clos De Vougeot, Pommard, Clos De Beze, Les Grand Eschezeaux, Romanee Conti, La Romanee, La Tache, Richebourg, Romanee Saint-Vivant, Saint Georges, Les Feves, Les Greves, Clos De Roi, Cailles Le Corton. 

WHITE WINES-Montrachet, Chablis Moutonne, Meursault Pei nere, Chablis Valmar, Meursault Charmes, Le Chevalier Montrachet, Chassagne Montrachet.

The district wines most commonly met are: 

Volnay, Beaune, Pommard, Macon, Beaujolais, Nuit St. Georges, Gevrey Chambertin, Chablis, Pouilly.

The heavy red Burgundies are excellent when served with roasts or wild game. The white wines are ideal with fish of all kinds and 17

The How and When with shellfish. Chablis alone is excellent when served with oysters or clams.

There is very little difference between the wines of the Cote De Nuits and the Cote De Beaune, although the former are a little bigger. 

Any wine of the Cote d'Or in a good vintage will develop into a full bodied manly wine. The Maconais and Beaujolais give us lighter, more delicate wine suitable for every day consumption when Burgundies are preferred. Even these wines are fuller bodied than the 

Bordeaux wines.

Clarets

HE name Claret applies to the red wines produced in the de- 

THE 

partment of the Gironde. Bordeaux is the main city and seaport of the district. Therefore while Claret is a type of wine, it also has a very definite geographical origin in Bordeaux. 

The vintage in Bordeaux begins in the last two weeks in September. The grapes are gathered and brought to the pressing house of each individual vineyard, where they are pressed by mechanical presses. The resultant juice known as "Must" is allowed to ferment naturally in large vats. The skins and pips are allowed to remain in the must during the fermentation to give the wine its color and body. 

The first or violent fermentation takes from 3 to 4 days, then the wine is put into fresh, clear casks. However, the wine continues to ferment until all of the natural grape sugar is fermented out. This process takes 3 to 4 weeks or more depending on the climatic conditions. 

Two or three months later when the wine falls bright, it is racked off into clean, sulphured casks. The wine is carefully watched for the next two or three years, during which time it will be racked into clean casks 4 or 5 times. In case of the finer wines, they will be kept as long as four years in the wood before being bottled.

There are three main types of labels in the Bordeaux wine trade. 

The first is the "Parish" or district label. The second is the "Monopole" or private brand label, and the third is the "Chateau" or estate bottling label.

THE Sauternes 

HE Sauternes district which is just south of the Graves district produces most fine white wines of world. The celebrated of these wines is Chateau D'Yquem—a rich liquorous wine which occupies first place among the white wines of the world. 

The Sauternes district is comprised of five communes: Barsac, Preignac, Bommes, Sauternes, and Fargues. Barsac is the only one of these communes whose wines will bear its own name rather than the generic name of Sauternes. 

The Sauternes are always rich, sweet, luscious wines. The wines though of the unfortified type become sweet and fruity to bouquet because the grapes are left on the vines so long that the skins begin to crust with grape sugar. The Haut Sauternes are better and fuller than the Sauternes.

When the great wines such as Chateau D'Yquem or Chateau La Tour Blanche are being produced, the grapes are left on the vine until fall or until they become over ripe, shrunken, and sugary. This process evaporates the water in the grape and concentrates the sugar above normal, making a richer, fuller tasting wine.

The Graves district lies immediately South of the city of Bordeaux and also produces some very palatable dinner wines. 

The district gets its name from the gravelly quality of the soil. 

The Graves district produces considerably more red wine than white, but because it has become customary to bottle white wines under the simple appelation of "Graves" or "Graves Superieur," a great many people are under the impression that only white wines are produced in this district. The white wine of the Graves is a dry fresh wine. 

There are a great many famous chateaux in this district, some producing Claret and some white wines. The most famous Chateaux producing white wines are Chateau Olivier, Chateau Laville, and 

Chateau Rochmorin.

There are many more Chateaux in the Sauternes district that produce white wines. The most famous of these are: Chateau D'Yquem, Chateau La Tour Blanche, Chateau Guiraud, and Chateau Climens. 

All of these Chateaux practice what is known as "Chateau Bottling."

Under this practice the wines grown on the vineyards of each individual Chateau are matured, bottled, and labeled by the proprietor of 23

The How and When each Chateau. The wines which are of the finest quality are identified by the French phrase, on both the label and the cork, “Mis En Bouteille Au Chateau." This inscription is a guarantee that the wines were grown, matured, and bottled by the proprietor of the Chateau. 

The finest vintage years in White Bordeaux are: 1904, 1906, 1914, 1921, 1923, 1926, 1928, 1929, 1933, 1934.

Rhine and Moselle Wines

HE Rhine and Moselle vineyards are the most Northerly in the Tword, consequently great care and scientific culture the necessary to produce the fine wines for which this district is noted. The wines are more hardy and dry than the French wines, since they must develop, mature and ripen in a very short space of time. 

The Moselle section consists of 135 districts of which some of the better known are, Berncastler, Zeltinger, Brauneberg, Traben-Trabach, Erden, Piesport, and Uerzig. The Moselles are extremely dry and have an excellent delicate freshness, concentrated bouquet, and taste. They are easily digested and wholesome. Moselle wines are at their best when they are young and if kept over 5 to 7 years will grow tired and lose their freshness which is its most charming characteristic.

Many of the better known Rhine wines are grown in the Rhiengau district. From the section comes the famous Johannisberger, the 

Steinberg, and the Marcobrunner. These are the great Rhine wines. 

Lesser wines, still of excellent quality, are the Rudesheim, Nierstein, Liebfraumilch, Oppenheimer, Nackenheimer, Deidesheim, Durkheim, and Wachenheim. Rhine wines are fuller to the taste than the Moselles. They are bottled after the third or fourth year and mature in the bottle from ten to 15 years before reaching their final maturity. 

All of the wines are grown, bottled, and shipped under strict governmental supervision in order to protect the consumer. The regulations govern the vintage, the name of the particular vineyard, and the type of wine.

Port Wine 

ENUINE Port wine comes from the Douro valley near the City

GE of Oporto in Northern Portugal. Portuguese law in which the 

English law concurs has forbidden any wine to be called Port unless it comes from the Douro Valley.

Port wine belongs to the sweet, fortified group of wines. It is a rich, robust wine fortified with Portuguese brandy during fermentation.

When the grapes are fully ripened in the month of September, they are gathered and brought to the pressing house of the vineyard. 

The grapes are dumped into a large circular or oblong stone or cement trough called a lager. Here barefoot men and women, to the accompaniment of gay music, tread the mass of grapes until they are fully trodden out. The whole scene is a happy one since those engaged in this work are singing and dancing.

After the grapes are thoroughly trodden, they are left in the lager to ferment. The must is carefully watched during the fermentation and when just the requisite amount of sugar remains unfermented, 24 to 28 hours later, depending on the amount of sugar in the must, brandy distilled from wines of the district of a previous vintage is added, raising the alcoholic content of the mass up to 20 per cent. This addition of brandy stops all fermentation. The unfermented sugar remains as sweetening in the wine itself. The wine is then drained off into large casks and stored away for two years. During this period, the wine is carefully cared for, and finally shipped down to Villa Nova Do Jaia, a small town across the Bay from Oporto, where all the various shippers have their warehouses.

Sherry N the Spanish speaking countries, the wine we know as Sherry IN is known by the name of a small town, Jerez, from which it comes. 

The word Sherry is the English corruption of the manner in which the early London wine merchants pronounced and wrote Jerez. 

Jerez de La Frontera is in the Southern part of Spain near the city of Cadiz in the Province of Andalusia. This district produces the only genuine Sherry in the world. All other wines though they may be excellent in taste and quality are not Sherry.

The vintage of Sherry begins in the early part of September. 

The grapes are brought to the main building of the vineyard, where they are first spread out on straw mats in the courtyard for a few hours in the sun. They are then placed in an oblong pressing trough known as a Lager. With special rakes, the grapes are leveled off into a layer a few inches thick and then they are sprinkled with a few shovelfuls of chalky earth dust of the vineyard. Another layer of grapes is raked out and more dust sprinkled over them, and so this continues until the lager is half full. Men wearing special shoes, with protruding pegs from the sides, then tread the grapes out thoroughly. This is done to the accompaniment of chanting and singing. 

As soon as the treading commences, the juice begins running off through a connecting trough into fresh butts (casks of 132 gal.). 

The butts are only partially filled. As soon as the first fermentation is completed and the wine falls bright, the casks are filled / full and are ready to go to the Sherry shipper's bodegas in Jerez.

Upon arriving at the bodegas, the newly made wine is left in the courtyard for 12 to 18 months. During this period the bungs are not driven home, and the wine is allowed to be in direct contact with the air and exposed to the elements. This is done to encourage the flowering of the wine. The flowering is a special development which is peculiar to Sherry. A thin film of yeast develops upon the surface of the new wine. This is a secondary fermentation that converts the wine into Sherry. Before the flowering, the wine is identi cal but by the time that the flowering is completed, mysterious changes take place in each butt, so that 12 or 18 months after vintage some of the wine will be found to be the light delicate Fino type, some medium in color and taste, some will be golden rich 33

Italian and French Vermouth 

VERA ERMOUTH is a wine which has had herbs, roots, and other flavoring ingredients steeped in it so as to flavor the wine. Although vermouth is made in many parts of the world, the two most important types are the dry, French vermouths and the sweet Italian vermouth.

In the process of making the French vermouth, white wines from the choicest vineyards are selected. To these white wines are added the finest spirits to fortify the wine. The blend is allowed to stand in thick Oak casks called Demi-Muids. After the wines are thus conditioned for about two years, they are then embodied with the infusions obtained by macerations of numerous special plants in ac cordance with a duly tested formula. This brings out the characteristic vermouth "Bouquet."

There are about 40 different herbs, plants, roots, leaves, peels, seeds, and flowers used in the process of making French vermouth. 

Some of these are nutmeg, coriander seeds, cloves, cinnamon, hyssop, majorcam, angelica root, gentian, flowers of elder, etc. 

It takes about 4 years to properly mature, age, and prepare true vermouth. This means that enormous reserves supplies must be kept on hand at all times, as well as involving a tremendous investment. 

Therefore only a few houses produce a really fine quality produce. 

French vermouth of a fine quality can be drunk straight as an aperitif. This practice is very popular in France. In the United 

States vermouth has its greatest popularity as an ingredient for numerous cocktails.

Italian vermouths are sweet when compared with French vermouth. This is due to the fact that the basic wine used for the mak ing of the vermouth is sweeter and fuller than the thin white wine used in making French vermouth. The wine used in making Italian vermouth is called Marsalla and the wine used in making French vermouth is called Herault. In addition to this the herbs, roots, seeds, flowers, and plants used are different. A certain amount of bitters is also employed to impart a very slight bitter flavor under the sweetness.

Italian vermouth can be matured in a much shorter time than the French. For this reason the reserve stocks do not have to be so large 37

On the Care of Wines in the Cellar

HE ideal wine cellar should be under ground, dark, dry, and with TH a constant temperature as near as possible to 55 degrees Fahrenheit. The cellar should be well ventilated and free from draughts and vibration. In country homes a corner of the cellar may be walled off and the above conditions maintained. The corner should be devoid of hotwater or heating pipes. Town apartments present greater difficulties. The solution advised is to select a closet, without heating pipes in the walls, and use it as a working cellar, keeping your principal stocks stored with your wine merchant.

Wine bins may be built like a honeycomb and fixed around the walls. Metal racks may be purchased for the purpose. In this manner the wine should not have to be disturbed until ready for use. All wines should be placed horizontally immediately on their receipt and remain unmoved until required. The wines are kept in this position so that the cork will be moist. Sparkling wines will go flat quickly if allowed to stand vertical. When a wine stands upright for a period, the cork will dry and then shrink. This shrinkage allows the air to enter the bottle and deteriorate the wine. Champagne and other white wines should be kept in the lower or cooler bins. The Clarets and other red wines should occupy the next tier while the Ports, Sherries, and Madeiras can be placed higher still. The latter need not be stored horizontally, since exposure to air does not harm a sweet fortified wine.

All wines, especially red wines, should remain tranquil for at least ten days before serving. The wines should be inspected regularly for leaking or ailing corks. These faulty bottles should be removed immediately since the damage often spreads. The leaking bottles must be recorked and if necessary filtered to remove bits of cork or sediment.

Many wine lovers usually find it interesting to keep a cellar book, noting entries, dates, costs and comments on the wine when used. 

This book will be a record of the wines used by the host and the comments entered will enable more advantageous purchasing in the future, since the wines most enjoyed will have the most favorable entries. 40

On the Service of Wines 

HE voyage of a bottle of wine from the cellar to the wine glass, if conducted correctly, will take on the semblance of a ritual. 

The procedure may seem a little complicated, but the pleasure derived from serving a bottle of fine wine is enhanced by the care taken during the service.

The amount of care observed in serving a white or red wine will reduce the disturbance to the wine itself. Shaking or turning a wine, especially a red wine, causes the sediment to cloud the wine and distort the color and brilliance. The use of a wine basket facilitates the voyage and reduces the amount of shaking and jarring that the bottle must undergo before it is served. If a wine basket is not used, slowly turn the bottle upright and keep it in that position until ready for service. 

DECANTING-Only ancient wines containing sediment in quantity need be decanted. The bottle should remain in an upright position for at least 24 hours before decanting. While decanting a wine, use scrupulously clean instruments, pour evenly without halting, and stop at the first sign of sediment. It is unwise to decant more than one bottle at a time, since each bottle may have different characteristics.

These may be lost when the two wines are decanted together. 

TEMPERATURES The full flavor of a wine is more easily brought out if it is served at the correct temperature. Clarets and still red bur gundy should be served at room temperature. This is best accomplished by placing the wine in the dining room several hours before serving. Never warm a wine by artificial means, even if you are hurried. Leave the wine in the kitchen until it is required. White wines should be served chilled or iced depending on the sweetness of the wine. A great sweet white wine like Chateau Yquem should be thoroughly iced, while a great dry white wine like Montrachet Burgundy should only be chilled. Icing serves to conceal the failings of an inferior wine. Ice should never be put into any wine, only around the bottle. Champagne and other sparkling wines should be thoroughly iced, but not iced too long. If a wine is chilled too long, it will lose most of its flavor and bouquet. Usually chilling champagne in an ice bucket for about 20 minutes or on the bottom of a refrig- 43

The How and When erator for about an hour will bring the wine to the correct temperature.

OPENING THE BOTTLE-First remove the foil from the neck of the bottle and also the wire and any other material that may come in contact with the wine during the pouring. Then wipe the lip of the bottle with a clean cloth. It is correct to wrap a napkin around the bottle to prevent any injury in case there is any flaw in the bottle which would cause it to break. Then insert the cork screw and slowly withdraw the cork. Be careful not to disturb the wine or break the cork. The best type of corkscrew to use is one with a long, sturdy shank operated preferably by leverage. Then taste the wine to make sure it is worthy.

GLASSES The true lover of wine will avoid the use of fancy, colored, or eccentric shaped glasses. A proper wine glass is clear, thin, and very delicate with a tulip shape type of bowl of generous propor tions, which is set on a graceful, slender stem. A modest but adequate glassware supply would include about six types and shapes of wine glasses. The wine glass should never be more than half full, permitting exposure of the wine to the air and the gathering of the bouquet. Connoisseurs often prefer a very large paper thin balloon glass to display a noble burgundy or claret.

ENJOYING WINE-Fine wines are enjoyed for their color, their fragrance, their taste, and the appreciation that follows drinking. Sip a fine wine, slowly, admiring its taste and other charms. Never drink a fine wine to quench the thirst. Use water for this purpose. It is not only proper but suggested that the host serve his own fine wines. The host should pour the first few drops into his own glass, so that any particles of cork in the wine will not be served to the guests. 44

On Wines With Your Food 

HERE are several customs which should be observed when wines are served with food. They are not set rules, but if observed the enjoyment of good wines will be enhanced. These usages can be altered to meet your own taste if those suggested do not agree. 

The host and his guests should always regard wines as an accompaniment to a certain type of food. Many people who have decided that they do not like a sweet wine such as Chateau Yquem, simply have neglected to serve this great wine with the right type of des sert. Chateau Yquem is best enjoyed when served with a not too sweet dessert. This linking with another type of food is true of all other wines. The host should not reserve the use of wines only to his formal dinners. The use of wine should be increased to a com mon occurrence. When wine is served there need not be five or six wines over the course of the dinner. If there is only one bottle of wine in the house, serve it. It will be correct and very enjoyable. 

When serving several wines with a dinner, the effect to strive for is one of stepping through the lesser wines to the great wine usually served with the game or roast. Red wines should never be served before white wines, nor a red burgundy before a claret, or a sweet wine before a dry wine. Sweet wines should not be served through the course of the whole meal. Dry wines are reserved for this purpose, while the sweet wines should be served with the dessert or with fruit.

Vinegar and jelly are unfriendly when served with wines. Salads and dressings containing vinegar should be omitted from the dinner when wines are used. Red wines should be served with game, roasts, pork and other red meats. White wines are more enjoyable with fish, white meats, vegetables, oysters and shell fish.

A light white wine like Graves may be served throughout a simple meal and Champagne may be served throughout any meal. Rhine, Moselle, Champagne, and white Burgundies are ideal as summer luncheon wines.

The Different Types of Brandy BRAND 

RANDY is a distillation of fruit or grape wines. The finer products are usually blends of several different types of brandy, with each type donating its distinctive characteristics of body, aroma, flavor, quality, and age. Brandies are made from many different kinds of grapes and fruits, and each may have a great quality all its own, but still be entirely different from the other. When the word brandy is used unqualified by the additional name of a fruit, grape brandy is always understood.

The several types of brandy commonly known are: 

COGNAC BRANDY 

Brandy may be made from any wine produced in any part of the world. However, the finest grape brandy is Cognac, which is distilled from wine made from grapes grown in that section of France known as the Charante District. The principal city is the ancient town of Cognac. The by-word for grape brandy all over the world is Cognac. Only brandy made in the Charante District may be called Cognac. Therefore all Cognac is brandy, but not all brandy is Cognac.

The large shippers of Cognac brandies do not grow or distill all of the brandy they sell, on the contrary, the major part of the dis tilling is done by individual proprietors of vineyards who later sell the distilled product to the shippers. The grapes are picked in 

October, but distillation takes place usually in December under the supervision of the French Government. In fact, the stills are kept under seal by the Government and the process of distillation at any vineyard cannot begin until the Government inspector comes to measure the quantity of wine the vineyard owner has for distillation. He then breaks the seals on the stills and gives the proprietor a government permit. 

The new brandy is placed in barrels of Limousin Oak. It is at that time a colorless liquid of very high proof. The oak imparts to the brandy a particular aroma and the beautiful golden hue which all Cognac has. Liqueurs 

ENTURIES ago, liqueurs were originated by alchemists seeking the "Elixir of Long Life" and by Monks who prepared potions for weakened travelers who came to them for aid. From both we have obtained many of the fine liqueurs we now enjoy. 

In the making of liqueurs, man has a very wide field wherein to exercise his ingenuity to give his product practically any shade or color he thinks best to attract attention and raise curiosity. He has at his command countless fruits, seeds, nuts, herbs, and spices with which he can please even the most fastidious taste. 

There are two main methods by which liqueurs may be made, namely, the infusion method and the maceration method. Almost all of the fruit liqueurs such as peach, apricot, cherry, etc., are made by the infusion method. According to this process a certain proportion of the fresh fruit is steeped in a barrel or vat of fine brandy for about one year. The brandy will then have absorbed all of the flavor, bouquet, and color of the fruit that was infused in it. 

When the spirit it drawn off, it is then filtered and sweetened with syrup. The liqueur is then ready for bottling. 

The plant liqueurs are made by the maceration method. The ingredients of the liqueur are steeped in fine brandy for two days. 

Then the whole content of the vat is distilled. The resulting distillation has all the necessary properties of taste and bouquet of the spices, herbs, and seeds first used. Since all distillations are colorless, most of the plant liqueurs are brilliantly colored, artifically. 

Examples of these liqueurs are Creme De Menthe, Creme De Cocoa, and Creme De Roses.

Liqueurs are mainly used as after dinner drinks, since they are sweet and potent. However, during recent years, there were invented numberless cocktails and mixtures calling for various liqueurs as ingredients. Notable among these cocktails are the Side Car calling for Triple Sec, Alexander calling for Creme De Cocoa, and the 

Stinger calling for Creme De Menthe.

Whiskies SCOTCH WHISKY 

THE whiskies produced in Scotland are made primarily from TH States are made from corn, rye, and wheat. Scotch whiskies are aged in casks which have contained Sherry, whereas American whiskies are aged in charred oak casks.

The processes entered into during the production of Scotch whiskey are divided into malting, mashing, fermenting, distilling, maturing, and blending. 

MALTING-In starting the malting process, the barley is first pressed and when required for use it is steeped in water and spread out on the malting floor for about three weeks. During this time the barley is sprinkled with water, the moisture causing the barley to sprout. When the sprouts are about 3/4 of an inch long, the water is turned off and the grain becomes known as malt. The malt is then dried over a peat fire with the pungent smoke impregnating the malt with the peat flavor and aroma that is even evident after fermentation and distillation.

MASHING The peat dried malt is ground, after which it is transferred to the mash vat and soaked in hot water. After soaking, the ground malt is removed, the liqueur having absorbed all the goodness and flavor.

FERMENTATION The liquid known as the "Wort" is run into fermenting vats, after which a small quantity of very fine yeast is added and the fermentation takes place. The result is known as "Beer."

DISTILLATION-The beer is distilled and the resultant distillate is redistilled. The choice part of this second distillation is Scotch whisky.

MATURING AND BLENDING The new whisky is placed in 

Sherry casks and then left to mature for about three years. After this period it is old enough to be blended or married with other 71

The How and When 

Scotch whiskies. After blending, the whisky is again placed in the 

Sherry casks for another four or five years or more before bottling. 

Practically all Scotch whisky is blended, since most malt whiskies are too heavy to be palatable without tempering with neutral spirits. 

The finer the whisky, the smaller the quantity of grain whisky in the product. This is also true of the quantity of Highland malt whiskies in comparison to Lowland and Campbelltown malt whiskies in the blend. However, there are on the market, though few in number, some straight unblended malt whiskies. Most of the straight Scotches are produced at the famous Glenlivet Distillery.

The straight malt whiskies have a very distinctive smoky flavor and heavy smooth body.

Irish whisky is a fine smooth liquor produced in the same manner as Scotch whisky, but does not have the smoky flavor. It is said. that the connoisseur will not miss the smoky flavor in the least, since a good Irish whisky over 6 years old is one of the finest and most satisfying liquors made. Of all these distilled liquors, Bourbon and Rye predominate in the demand. 

The whiskies produced in this country are classified as "Rye," "Bourbon," or "Corn" whiskies. Bourbon is made from a mixture of corn, rye, wheat, oats, and barley, with corn making up at least 51% of the total. Rye whiskey is made from rye and other grains, with rye making up at least 51% of the total. Corn whiskey is made from corn with just enough barley malt added to aid in conversion. 

Barley malt is used in all of the above whiskies to aid in the conversion during fermentation.

The production of whiskey is made up of six different operations: preliminary treatment of raw materials, mashing, yeasting, fermentation, distillation, and maturing. 

MATERIALS The materials from which whiskey is produced are grains such as: rye, corn, oats, barley malt, and wheat. Rye and corn predominate in the preparation of the grain mash. On account of the firm structure of the kernels, the raw grain must first be ground into meal and then cooked to make them readily soluble to the enzymes during mashing.

MASHING In the making of corn or rye whiskey a certain series of processes are required by which the starchy raw materials are prepared for yeast production or fermentation. For every bushel of grain twenty to twenty-five gallons of water are added to the mash. 

The mash is then thoroughly stirred and allowed to stand over night, during which time a small amount of lactic acid forms which gives the mash a sour or acid characteristic. The mash is then brought to the boiling point, and maintained for about fifteen minutes. YEASTING-Yeast is a single cell vegetable organism which reproduces itself under favorable conditions by budding. The four important essentials needed for the development and reproduction of yeast cells are: a supply of fermentable material, a supply of yeast food in the form of minerals, an ample supply of oxygen, and proper temperature conditions. Pure yeast is first cultured under favorable conditions at the distiller's laboratory. Yeasting is the process of adding the yeast to the prepared mash to induce fermentation.

FERMENTATION-During the mashing process, the starch has been converted into sugar which is readily fermentable. The fermentation process decomposes the sugar by means of certain enzymes, resulting in the transformation of the sugars into alcohol and carbonic acid gases. Under the Sweet Mash method of fermentation the period of fermentation is limited by Federal Statute to 72 hours and not more than 45 gallons of beer to each bushel of grain. The 

Sour Mash process in which the time of fermentation must not exceed 96 and not more than 60 gallons of beer per bushel of grain, produces a whisky heavier in body and richer in flavor than the Sweet Mash method. In the early stages of the fermentation, there is a rapid development of the yeast. Carbonic acid gas bubbles break the surface of the mass, forming a thickening cap of foam. 

The whole mass then comes into vigorous motion. The temperature rises and the main fermentation takes place, the sugar being transferred into alcohol.

DISTILLATION—The fermented mash then enters into the distilling process for the purpose of separating the high volatile liquid from the low volatile liquid and a non-volatile substance. The fermented mash is put through the still where the alcohol is separated from the slop and beer.

MATURING-The aging of whiskey depends upon time and temperature. Newly distilled whisky is practically colorless and has a crude unpleasant flavor. By storing it in charred oak barrels certain changes are brought about which transform the whiskey into an amber, mellow liquid higher in proof.

Prolonged storage results in a loss in bulk and an increase in the alcoholic content. The loss in volume is a result of evaporation in part and part through the pores of the oak cask in an action called "Breathing." There is no noticeable loss in the esters, aldehydes, furfural, acids, or fusel oil during the storage. During the aging process, there is an increase in the acids and esters which reach equilibrium after the fourth year of aging.

Rye whiskies were as a rule aged in heated warehouses where the changes taking place were aided by the higher temperature. 

This higher temperature increased the loss in volume. Bourbon whiskies on the other hand were as a rule aged in unheated warehouses so that there was less loss in volume, less increase in proof, and less chemical change.

Whiskey aged in uncharred casks lacks the aromatic flavor peculiar to American whiskey, resembling more the flavor of Scotch whiskey, but lacking the smoky flavor. 79

The Types of Gin 

LONDON DRY GIN-This gin is dry, bland, and moderately aromatic. It is made from distilled grain and redistilled with juniper, angelica, and other spices and herbs depending upon the maker's formula. All spirits no matter how pure, will differ slightly due to the mineral content of the water used in the mash. Even though the identical procedure is used in the distillation, a spirit distilled in London will still differ in taste from a spirit distilled in America. An English made gin has been proven on many occasions to have more finesse, a more delicate flavor, and mellower taste. 

OLD TOM GIN—Old Tom Gin is highly aromatic and made in the same manner as London dry gin, but with sweetening added. It is usually darker in color than dry gin. Sugar is less necessary in drinks made with Old Tom Gin.

PLYMOUTH GIN-Plymouth gin is a combination of dry and Old Tom Gin, being slightly sweet, but not as sweet as Old Tom Gin.

HOLLAND GIN-Holland gin is a rich, aromatic, highly flavored spirit. The mash is made from grain with a certain amount of rye grain and other spices added during distillation. Holland gin has its own distinctive flavor and is seldom used in cocktails. 

GERMAN GIN-German gin is produced from grain mash with a certain percentage of rye grain along with other spices and herbs. 

FRUIT FLAVORED GINS-This gin is made from a regular gin base with the fruit flavor added during the distillation process. This gin is made in various fruit flavors.

SLOE GIN-Sloe gin is not a gin but a liqueur. It is made from sloe berries, has a red brown color, and is quite sweet.

MATURED GINS Matured gin is a London dry gin which has the distinction of being the only gin aged in Sherry casks. The gin is aged about four years and as a result has an amber color and fine flavor. 80

Syrups and Bitters

GRENADINE Grenadine is a syrup made from fresh Pomegranates. It is usually non-alcoholic and mainly used in cocktails to give them color and to take the place of sugar. Grenadine is a light red color. 

GOMME SYRUP-Gomme syrup is a simple syrup or simply sugar syrup. It is used in the making of drinks in place of sugar. 

ORGEAT SYRUP-Orgeat syrup is a combination of simple syrup and bitter almonds. Recipes for many cocktails call for this syrup. 

FRAMBOISE Framboise is a raspberry syrup; Fraise is a strawberry syrup, both being used as flavoring in cooking and in the making of cocktails. Blackberry syrup is also in this class.

FALERNUM--A flavoring used in the preparation of rum cocktails.

ORANGE BITTERS -Made from dried orange peels mixed with various flavoring and other aromatic agents. It is used extensively as a flavoring in making cocktails.

Correct Bar Procedure 

1. Use good ingredients at all times. 

2. Use correct glassware as noted in recipe. 

3. Glassware should be clean and dry. 

4. Cocktail glasses should be chilled with shaved ice before serving. 

5. Shake drinks vigorously and serve very cold. 

6. Do not use the ice a second time. 

7. Super fine sugar will make clearer drinks. 

8. Follow recipes given carefully. 

9. Mixed drinks should not stand too long before serving. 

10. In order to frost a glass, first chill it with ice, then wet the inside rim with a small piece of lemon, and dip the glass into powdered sugar. 

11. Measuring devices to be used are as follows: (A) Jigger-1½ ounces. (B) Pony-34 ounce. (C) Bar Spoon-equal to ½ teaspoon. (D) Dash equal to 20 drops or 1/4 teaspoon. (E) Gill-equal to 4 ounces. 

12. Sugar syrup may be used in the same proportions as dry sugar. 

A spoon of a dry ingredient should be level, not heaping. 

13. Lemons may be substituted for limes, but avoid the use of the peel of the lime in a drink, since it has an unpleasant taste. 

14. Cut peels for garnishment as thinly as possible and never place in the drink. Hang it over the edge of the glass. 

15. When stirring a drink, use a bar glass one-half full of shaved ice. 

16. If stronger drinks are desired stir them. Shaking makes drinks weaker. 

17. The three forms of ice to be used in mixing are: (A) SHAVED ICE-cut from cakes of ice with shaver. (B) CRACKED ICE-placed in towel and cracked with hammer. (C) CUBED ICE-made in electric refrigerator. 

18. Never add more bitters, liqueurs or syrups than are called for. 

A drink may be easily spoiled by too little or too much bitters. 87

MUTTON WITH CURRANT JELLY SAUCE 

2 tablespoons butter 

1/3 cup currant jelly 

1/2 tablespoons sherry 

2 tablespoons flour 

1 cup brown stock 

1/4 teaspoon salt pepper 

6 slices cold mutton

Brown the butter and add flour, salt and pepper, and stock gradually. Then add the jelly, and when melted, add the mutton. When the meat is heated add the wine. If there is some mutton gravy on hand, use instead of making the brown sauce.

CHICKEN ALA MARCO 

Take the breasts of large broilers and cut close to the breast bone, removing them whole. Keep the small filets with the breasts.

Allow one side breast for each person. Season with a dash of cayenne pepper and a bit of salt and dredge lightly with flour. Brown very delicately in fat. Remove the breasts from the pan. Then put a piece of fat into the pan and let it melt but not brown. 

Add a cup of cream and a 1/4 cup of sherry. Lay in the breasts and cover. 

Cook very slowly until the breasts are tender. Add ½ cup cream to the remaining sauce and about 3 mushrooms for each portion. Let these cook slowly for about 10 minutes or until done. Put breasts and mushrooms on a hot platter, cover with the sauce and garnish with rounds of cold hominy which have been fried until crisp and brown. Serve with lettuce and drained canned cherries, dressed with fruit, French dressing.

LOBSTER SPECIAL 

Open two small cans of lobster or have ready meat from a large boiled lobster, cut into small pieces. Put into a chafing dash 3 tablespoons butter, add the lobster, and saute a few minutes. Add salt and pepper to taste, a tea spoon of dry mustard, a dash of cayenne, and a glass of sherry. Turn the lobster until juices are reduced to about 1/2 cup. Serve the lobster on toast points and provide an accompaniment of chutney or sweet pickle. 178

Into some melted butter put large pieces of broiled lobster, season with salt and paprika and let cook for a few minutes. Add sherry and allow to simmer. Add heavy cream and cook for 10 minutes. LOBSTER SPUMANTI 

1 onion 1 carrot ounces butter 

3 small lobsters 

1 bottle Asti Spumanti 

1 stalk celery 

1 bay leaf salt 

Chop the onion, carrot, and celery and fry in butter until golden brown. 

Add the salt and the bay leaf.

Wash and split the lobsters very carefully and place in a small steam boiler. 

Pour the fried mixture over them and add 1 bottle of asti spumanti and seal the pot hermetically. Place over a slow fire and when the indicator shows the maximum pressure, remove from the fire and allow it to stand for 20 minutes. 

Then place it on ice until chilled and serve. 

SOLE-SAUTE 

Hash a tablespoon of carrots and another of truffles. Add 2 tablespoons butter in a covered frying pan for a few minutes. Add a wine glass of sherry and mix well. Pour this sauce over the sole and season. Poach in the oven. 

Remove the sole and reduce the sauce. Add a few tablespoons Bechamelle and a little heavy cream. Serve the sole dressed with the sauce, and garnished by 

1/2 tomato sauted in butter on each fillet. The tomato can in turn be garnished with mushrooms or truffles and parsley.

SHRIMP IN MADEIRA JELLY 

This is an excellent dish for a summer luncheon. Prepare the Madeira wine jelly fairly stiff. Half fill individual moulds with it; chill until firm. Arrange dry packed shrimp on the jelly, with a few sliced olives or thin strips of pimiento. Pour the remaining jelly over the whole and chill thoroughly. Serve with salty bisquits.

BREASTS OF GUINEA CHICKEN-TRUFFLES A LA GERALD 

Have chickens dressed for roasting and steam until very tender-about an hour. Cool, then separate the breasts from the bones, keeping halves of breast meat whole.

Saute the breasts in butter, very slowly, until delicately browned. Meanwhile stew a small can of truffles in 112 cups of dry white wine for 20 minutes. 

Peel and slice the truffles and return to the sauce. Add this to the chicken and simmer very slowly until the juices are pretty well reduced-about 20 minutes.

Spread bread sauce on the serving platter, lay the breasts on it, and cover with the truffles and juices remaining in the pan. Fill the spaces in between with browned crumbs. Garnish with persimmons cut in quarters.

FROG LEGS A LA POULETTE 

Take a dozen frog legs and place in a sauce pan with 2 chopped shallots, 3 sliced fresh mushrooms, salt and pepper, 1 glass white wine and a piece of sweet butter. Cover the pan and place it on the fire for 10 minutes. Remove the frog legs from the sauce pan and place them in a chafing dish. Cook the gravy on a swift fire for 3 minutes, adding a spoon of Hollandaise sauce, a little lemon juice and chopped chives. Pour the sauce over the frog legs in the chafing dish and serve very hot. Serve melba toast separate.

BREAST OF CHICKEN A LA BANQUO 

Fry in olive oil or butter a minced carrot, some celery and parsley. When done, remove the vegetables and add breast meat of chicken and some bacon. 

When meat has browned on both sides, add chopped liver, giblets and fresh mushrooms and a little water or broth. Thicken the sauce with a little flour and add 1 cupful of good white wine. Simmer slowly until gravy is reduced to one-half. Place meat on platter and strain gravy over it. 

RUMP STEAK BORDEAUX

SOLE AU VIN BLANC 

Put a medium sized English sole on a fish plate. Add some chopped shallots, butter, white wine and fish stock, until the sole is covered. Cook in the oven for 15 minutes. Pour the liquid off the sole and let the liquid simmer.

Add heavy cream and when the sauce is thickened, strain it and add the yellows of 2 eggs mixed with butter. Pour over the sole and put a slice of truffle over the top.

EGGS AU VIN 

Put 3 cups burgundry in casserole, add 3 tablespoons sugar, rind of ½ lemon, raisins, and chopped almonds. When liquid boils break eggs into it and poach. When eggs are done put them into a hot serving dish. Add flour to the wine and cook to a cream, then pour over the eggs. 

BEEF POT ROAST IN BURGUNDY WINE 

Place a piece of beef into a double heavy roast pan. Add a piece of fresh fat pork, cover the roast with salt, pepper, and some pure lard, brown it in an open pan in oven. Add a few clove stuck onions, carrots, celery, garlic, and some aromatic herbs. Pour a bottle of good burgundy red wine, and add stock or water to cover the roast. Bring to a boil on the top of the oven. Cover hermetically, set back into the oven and cook for five hours. By that time the vegetables will be cooked to puree and there will be sufficient gravy for the meat left. Take off the fat on the top of the gravy and then strain. Serve the meat in rather thick slices covered with gravy.

Arrange on circular pieces of toast, over each of which has been poured one teaspoon sherry from the mushroom caps. Pile mushroom caps 5 or 6 high on each piece of sweetbread; add 2 tablespoons heavy cream, and bake in moderate oven 8 minutes. Cook in individual glass-covered dishes. 181

CREPES SUZETTE 

3 eggs ½ teaspoon salt 1 cup milk 

1 cup flour 

6 teaspoons sweet butter

Beat eggs slightly, add milk, then stir into sifted flour and salt. The batter should be very thin in consistency. Put a teaspoon of sweet butter into a very hot frying pan about 6 inches in diameter. Pour in enough batter to cover the bottom of the pan. The mixture should flow freely, the thinner the crepes the better they are. Brown, turn and brown the other side. Repeat until all of the batter is used. This makes 6 crepe suzettes.

SAUCE FOR CREPES SUZETTE 

1/2 pound butter juice of 1 orange 

6 lumps sugar (rubbed with orange peel) 

Mix butter with sugar and orange juice. Add 4 jiggers cognac, curacao, and maraschino. Put 1 teaspoon in pan, soaking 1 pancake at a time. Turn over, roll, and sprinkle granulated sugar over rolled pancake. Put finished pancake in pan with brandy or rum and ignite.

NESSELRODE PUDDING 

11½ cups preserved marrons 

1 teaspoon lemon juice 

2 tablespoons chopped almonds 

1/2 cup water 

3 cups cream yolks of 5 eggs 1/2 cup sugar 

3 tablespoons sherry wine 

2 tablespoons chopped crystallized cherries

Make a syrup by mixing the water with the sugar and bring slowly to a boil. The How and When GLACE BENEDICTINE 

3/4 cup sugar 

4 egg yolks 2 cups milk 

2 cups whipped cream 

1/2 cup benedictine

Combine the sugar and the egg yolks. Add the milk and cook slowly in double boiler, stirring constantly until mixture coats the spoon. Cool and add benedictine and fold in the whipped cream. Pour into trays, set into the refrigerator and freeze until firm. This makes 12 servings.

CREPES FLAMBEES BENEDICTINE 

1 cup all-purpose flour 

1/4 cup granulated sugar 

1 pinch salt 3 eggs 11/2 cups milk 1/2 cup benedictine 1/4 cup butter 1/3 cup cognac brandy Mix and sift the dry ingredients. Combine the beaten eggs with the milk and add to the first mixture to form a batter. This batter should be very thin. 

Add 2 teaspoons of the benedictine. For frying each crepe place about a teaspoon of the butter into the small frying pan and add about 2 tablespoons of the batter. Tip the pan so as to spread the batter in a very thin layer. 

As soon as one side is brown, turn over and brown the other side. When done remove and roll up. Repeat the operation until all of the batter is used up. 

Combine the rest of the benedictine with the cognac, warm, pour over the crepes, and ignite. Serve while lit. This recipe makes 16 servings.
