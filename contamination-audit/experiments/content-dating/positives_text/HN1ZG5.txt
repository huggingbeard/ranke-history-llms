CHAPTER ONE 

AS DORIS BLAKELY let herself into the rather cheerless apartment that was her home, she could not escape a slight shiver, despite the soft warmth of the early September air. This apartment that was her home, in one of New York's once elegant but now deteriorated neighborhoods, was characterized by that which is more depressing than frank and honest poverty; it bore the touches of the faded gentility which is poverty ashamed of itself, poverty trying to cover itself by pale pretenses, poverty which notwithstanding its pathetic efforts is poverty and nothing else. 

Her day at the office had been tiring. She was not yet eighteen, and not long out of the business college that had given her the training to earn her bread; and an eagerness to make good on this first job of her career made her overdo it a little. 

Not that she minded, though. It was really very exciting to be in the busy, bustling office, with people going in and out all day, and an atmosphere full of intimations of large affairs. The clerks in the office were pleasant young men; and the head of the firm, 9

Jasper Richards, was the sort of man to set up a flutter in the romantic young feminine breast. Tall and distinguished-looking, amazingly young in appearance, with touches of gray at the temples the only indication of his forty-four years, he shared with Rudy Valentino and Thomas Meighan the enthusiastic encomiums of the girls in his office, during their lunch hour conversations.

Doris was glad she wasn't staying home this evening. 

She and Ruth Anders, the girl at the desk next to hers at the office, were to meet right after dinner and go down to Coney Island. CHAPTER TWO 

A SPACIOUS room, elaborately furnished in rose and silver, was dominated by a large and elaborate bed, which stood out from the wall, toward the center. At its foot was a chaise longue, heaped with pillows. 

Half a dozen chairs, beautifully upholstered, were scattered about. All the other appurtenances-the thick carpet, the dressing-table, with its rows of expensive jars and bottles, the exquisite inlaid French commodes, the hangings of rich silk, were redolent of luxury and the subtleties of feminine appeal. 

A full-length gilt mirror filled the space between the two windows.

Before this mirror stood a woman. She was in her early thirties, although an uninstructed onlooker would have given her no more than twenty-two or three. Her slim, straight, beautifully molded body was hidden in the folds of an intricately designed negligée of shaded rose chiffon and cream-colored lace; and on her tiny unstockinged feet were mules of rose-colored satin.

Her face was delicately lovely, its natural charm en- 23

His face was strong, and at the same time sensitive; and his thick hair was crisply bronze.
