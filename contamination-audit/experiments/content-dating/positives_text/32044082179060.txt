THE AMERICAN GEOGRAPHICAL SOCIETY 

is the only organization of its kind in the United States-a scientific research institution devoted solely to the collection and dissemination of geographical knowledge. Through its library and map room, its book, map and serial publications, and its expert research staff, the Society has rendered unparalleled service to the public, the profession and the government for the past 98 years. Now, as we approach our centenary, we have undertaken to assess the role of the Society- and, concomitantly, the role of geography-in our modern world. 

That the role of geography is important, and becoming ever more so, none will deny. At the end of the war, the American people found themselves irrevocably committed to an active position in international affairs since, whether for better or for worse, the leadership of the western powers became its responsibility. With this growth in world stature came the necessity of being alert and perceptive in handling the problems of land, life and livelihood both within and beyond our own borders, in short, the necessity of being geographically aware. 

But it is equally undeniable that, as a people, we could lay claim to no such awareness: for most of us formal education in the subject ceased at the sixth grade and all too often consisted in the stultifying practice of learning the location of the more important capes and bays. 

Accordingly, early in 1947, the Council of the American Geographical Society initiated a series of inquiries to see how the activities of the Society might be reoriented, and perhaps enlarged, to meet the challenge of the

vastly changed national scene and, more specifically, how it might help to raise the level of geographical literacy among the post-war generation. 

The statement that follows presents the findings of this extended inquiry and poses the problem of how the Society can secure the ends which it now has in view. 

A RETROSPECT 

Forty years ago the Society, aided by generous donations, set itself the task of assembling the men and materials with which to fashion a geographical science worthy of the name. A brilliant leader was chosen in the person of Isaiah Bowman, and around him was gathered an effective corps of experts who entered as a team upon this impressive task. They revised the Society's Bulletin and made of it a scientific journal of topmost rank, which they called the Geographical Review. They undertook, or directed, investigations in a dozen different directions to bring new and basic literature to the shelves of geography. They went to the root of map-making, sorted out the good from the bad, assembled draftsmen, and undertook one of the hardest assignments ever to be accomplished under public or private direction- the mapping on the scale of approximately 16 miles to the inch of the entire area of Latin America from Mexico southward. They worked with the new medium, the aerial photograph, and were pioneers in developing methods and instruments for translating the aerial picture into the contour map. They studied the problem of flight navigation in the polar regions, and devised for the benefit of airplane pilots rapid graphical methods of position finding from astronomical sights. They ventured upon a study of the problems of pioneer settlement which has guided the development of the fringe regions the world over. They took over the management of the great "Atlas of the Historical 

Geography of the United States," scaled it to practical dimensions and saw it through publication. They persisted in the selective acquisition of geographical material for the library, which by their efforts has become the finest collection of its kind in the Western Hemisphere- and, to speed the use of this collection, invented an index fitted to the peculiar nature of geographical fact-finding. They became masters in the art of 2

selection for the map library, to the end that the items held would be comprehensive without growing to overwhelming proportions. They invited other sciences to utilize this fount of geographical knowledge, and placed in residence a distinguished medical scientist who is now well along with a program of studies in the distribution of the world's major diseases and their environmental correlatives.

In pushing forward, by these diverse means, the frontiers of geographical knowledge, the Society has, we contend, been making a fundamental contribution to a science that can do much to increase the sum of human well-being.

Financially, however, the picture has grown dark. Never wealthy in terms of endowment, the Society gained support for its achievements by the contributions of individuals, principally from those who from time to time served as members of the Council. In the earlier years annual contributions, tendered on an almost contractual basis, supported the programs, and built up a reserve of some proportions. This dependence upon the loyalty of a few individuals, however, was certain to end at some point, and it was in fact quickly terminated by the events of 1929 and after. Programs then underway were completed by securing emergency aid from the foundations, and new ones, out of necessity, were for the most part held in abeyance. While the period of the war brought temporary revenues from government contracts which offset the impending losses (and satisfied the scientific conscience by yielding a certain number of publishable works such as the 5-sheet Map of the Americas), both before the war and after, the maintenance of the institution was accomplished increasingly by the consumption of its capital reserves. At present these reserves total $600,000, and are being spent out at the rate of approximately $100,000 per year.

THE INQUIRY AND ITS FINDINGS 

It was in this challenging, yet sobering, context that the Society embarked upon its fact-finding investigations in 1947. In all five major conferences were held. The first one was general in nature, and the rest were concerned with special viewpoints, namely those of the research scientist, 3

the teacher, the curator, and the younger professional geographer. Men and women were drawn from all parts of the nation and given ample time to talk, while we listened. The result was one of the most searching analyses ever submitted to by any scientific organization. The records were preserved in detail and are available in an organized and indexed report: they may be borrowed upon request.

Many stimulating compliments were paid to the institution, which convinced us of its leadership in the domain of geographical thought and standards, and of its indispensability to the profession at large. "Without the American Geographical Society," said the head of one of the large graduate schools, "geography in America could not function." We also received many equally stimulating criticisms from persons who could look upon the institution with disinterest. These criticisms ranged from the trivial to the fundamental and concerned methods, topics, emphasis, end-result, and even personnel. Occasionally the Society came under fire for being out of touch with current needs, but in general the comments concerned activities which were good as far as they went, but which fell short of satisfaction in extent and scope; undertakings which the Society had pioneered with enthusiasm, but had failed to follow up satisfactorily; books not kept in revision, or in print; research facilities wasted because of the non-existence of scholarships - all failures which could be traced to inadequate financial resources. 

The Need of a Bridge between Scholarship and Teaching The most striking disclosure of the conferences, however, was the contention, supported at every turn, that the Society's main works of scholarship do not get through to the grade and high school teacher and hence to the school child. Teachers on these levels, we learned, have neither the time nor the training to read and understand the Geographical Review, with the result that its contents, the top record of geographical thought, are largely passed by in the preparation for classroom teaching. The same criticism was tendered concerning the monograph series, which occupies so basic a position in geographical literature. What is needed, we were told, is a series of books of lesser stature, interpretive writings and semi-

popular readers, prepared particularly to fill the wide gap that exists between the technical or scholarly treatise and the school textbook. In addition to books, moreover, the educators want a bright, readable, illustrated magazine which will make available without undue delay the results of basic geographical research and exploration, and will present their practical uses and applications in language that is readily understandable.

Nor is this all. A wide range of aids to the teaching of geography require to be created if the teacher is to put across the essential elements of the subject. Were these aids in existence today, said one authority in education, not only would the teachers themselves be infinitely better trained, but the time taken by them to instruct students would be reduced four-fifths.

Startling comments, these, to a nation that prides itself on its educational standards! The inference is clear: the gap between the geography of the scholarly world and the geography of the teaching world is too wide for the teachers themselves to bridge. The approach must be made from the other direction, or better still, from both sides. 

It was generally agreed that these educational objectives could best be attained by the following means: 

1. Crystallization of opinion among the authorities in geography on the one hand, and in education on the other, on what to teach and how to teach it. 

2. Establishment of a central agency where the needful classroom materials (including globes, atlases, maps, teaching packets, film strips, a representative periodical, a news sheet, etc.) can be developed and tested. This agency must command the recognition and respect of government, industry and the professions, since their cooperation will be needed to gain an effective result. It must have permanent quarters and an assured future, in order to sustain the work once it has been started. 

3. Instruction of teachers from elementary schools, high schools and colleges throughout the country in the use of these aids, through attendance at regionally placed summer schools.

The Need for Accelerated Research The discussions which we arranged with research geographers-chiefly the heads of the graduate schools in this country-ranged far and wide, and produced a quite embarrassing crop of ideas and advice. But on one point at least there was absolute unanimity, namely, that there is not enough geographical research in progress to satisfy the needs of the nation. And for a good reason: there are not enough highly trained geographers. The setting up of university departments of geography and the absorption of graduates into government agencies has proceeded so rapidly during the past quarter century that the profession has found it impossible to expand its numbers in pace with the demand. There recently existed, at one time, more than two dozen unfilled chairs in geography. A direct consequence of this situation is that most highly qualified geographers pass their time in teaching and administration, and have neither time nor zest for sustained research. Yet, never was there such a demand for expert research workers as there is today. 

The Need for Leadership 

The point will not have escaped the reader that the tremendous responsibility of satisfying these needs was being placed upon the American Geographical Society not simply from choice, but from compulsion: no other organization possesses the basic physical resources as represented by the library and map collections, a comparable group of experienced scholars, technicians who administer the collections, expert cartographers and editors, and a reputation for sound pioneering and scientific integrity. What it did not possess was the viewpoint of the teacher. 

Most of 1949 was spent sifting the personnel of the geographical profession and that of closely allied branches in searching for a man whose training, abilities and breadth of vision would qualify him for the tasks outlined above. In November, George H. T. Kimble, Professor and Chairman of the Department of Geography at McGill University, was named as the new Director and assumed active charge in June 1950. Dr. Kimble, who is 41 years of age, stands in the top rank of geographers. Important to

our purposes, he has demonstrated not only excellent scientific and administrative ability, but also the ability both in lecture and in print to convey his subject to the public. His appreciation of the importance of close liaison with the teaching group was shown by the effective work of the geography summer school in Canada (Stanstead), which he originated. 

We believe him to be the man to carry out the enlarged program. 

At this point it may well be asked: "What is geography and what does it do that is not being done by other sciences?" There is much wisdom in the question, for geography is a parent authority which overlaps to a confusing degree the provinces of a large number of other disciplines, many of which may seem to take its place. It is this very overlap, however, which supplies its thrust as well as its meaning, for geography is an integrative science, and the greater the "splintering" the more imperative does the need for integration become. 

In the following section Dr. Kimble presents briefly his conception of the relevance of geography for the people of today, and shows by reference to specific examples the kind of contribution geographers can make to the cause of government, education and international understanding.

THE ROLE OF GEOGRAPHY IN THE MODERN WORLD By George H. T. Kimble 

It used to be said that the world was wide and made for wandering, but no longer. Now it is small and crowded, nobody can wander far without trespassing and every man is near-neighbor to two and a quarter billions of his fellows.

To learn the art of living in such a world is going to call for the exercise of many practical skills and much physical effort; but it is also going to call for new philosophical insights and educational policies. What is needed is schooling in understanding as much as in techniques: less emphasis on esoteric specialisms low in community worth and more on values which are independent of time and place. To be truly effective of its high purpose, such a philosophy must find a place, inter alia, for two kinds of learning. Firstly, for learning about the earth environment so as to enable us to make successful adjustments to its forces and resources. 

Education can no longer be predicated on the assumption that there is more land than man can plough; more fertility than he can exploit; more minerals, fuels and fish than he can consume-on the assumption that there is nothing in the wide world except his own folly or ignorance (or somebody else's) to prevent him from being a resounding success. There are too many man-made deserts in Africa and in the Americas, too many abandoned mineshafts in Europe, and too many scarred hillsides and rampaging rivers in every continent for that philosophy to sit comfortably upon our consciences.

Secondly, it must foster knowledge of the peoples of the earth so as to enable us to understand their modes of thought, cultural habits and, indeed, their entire way of life. Only so may we hope to see what is behind the Russian attitude, the Jewish viewpoint, the Japanese argument-not to mention all the other burning issues of our time. 

To succeed in this school of learning, it is necessary to study the peoples of the world, not merely as so many figures in a statistician's tables, but

as flesh and blood actors, working out their destiny in a unique environmental setting, and tackling their problems, be they social, economic or political, in accordance with the urges and constraints of that environment, and the accumulated experience with which history and circumstance have endowed them.

If the validity of this point of view be accepted, then a place must be found in every educational program for the systematic study of the relations of peoples to their physical environment and a consideration of the ways in which those relations may be stabilized so as to ensure that the earth abides and that man does not perish from its face - at least from starvation or other preventable "natural" causes. In other words, room must be found for Geography. And for at least three reasons.

In the first place, the teaching of geography promotes an awareness of the true nature of man's environment. For want of this, far too many of our fellows (even in America) are still treating the environment as if it were a bank to be burgled. Until the gospel of resource conservation gains a very much wider acceptance, we shall make little progress with the task of bringing the whole of the habitable earth into a state of enduring fruitfulness. And certainly the world never needed to bring forth fruit more abundantly than it does today, with its population increasing at the rate of approximately 20,000,000 per annum. Planning, we are told, is the answer, and it must be admitted that the contemporary scene can provide some impressive illustrations of its accomplishments. 

But land planning, whether it is on a grand scale, like the Tennessee Valley Authority, or on a small scale like the Ganaraska Watershed scheme of southern Ontario, costs millions of dollars, and sooner or later the tax-payers of the world are going to get very tired of paying out for such projects. Would it not be simpler- and far cheaper - to spend money educating people in the business of environmental appreciation, whereby they would be able to weigh objectively the pros and cons of their traditional land economy, to discern cause and effect relationships and so to adjust that economy from time to time that it could ultimately provide them with a continuing livelihood and all the physical ingredients of a good life? At least it is worth trying, especially in the backward areas

of the world such as Puerto Rico, intertropical Africa and Amazonia, where capital for major planning projects is never likely to be overabundant. We readily concede that the geographer is not the only man who could assist in such a campaign, but with his understanding of the “interconnectedness" of land and life, he would appear to be peculiarly fitted for the task. 

In the second place, and no less important, geography helps us to comprehend the significance of differences between one environment and another. It is not for nothing that the world was endowed with such diversity. Environmental differences from place to place are real, and of incalculable economic and social importance, for "place" influences human endeavour, whether that place is large or small. Some people are more advantaged by their physical environment than others: there can be no doubt, for instance, that the Canadians, with two-thirds of their territory unsuited to field crop production, are much less well served by their environment than the Americans are by theirs. Others are only able to realize the advantages of their environment and surmount its disadvantages with greater effort or more time. Thus, it has taken Denmark a thousand years to bring its dairy farms to the productivity levels reached by the people of Wisconsin within the space of a generation. In the Dutch polders, the price of a crop is ceaseless vigilance against tide and storm, and the hard-won wisdom of many generations of engineers. In many parts of the Paris Basin all that is needed to garner harvests of comparable magnitude is a wooden plough and a modest spring rainfall. The recognition of these inequalities is not as general or as perceptive as we could wish, but it is absolutely necessary if we are ever to understand the diversities of human economies and cultures, and the uneven timing of developments that lie at the root of so many of our international difficulties. Indeed, we might go further and say that in as far as the characteristic attitudes and actions of human communities have a rational origin, the chances are that the rationality is more likely to be disclosed by viewing these attitudes and actions against the background of their unique environment (because no two nations, states or even tribes face identical sets of physical circumstances) than by any other line of ap- ΙΟ

proach. It could be argued, for instance, that some part of Russia's traditional aggressiveness towards her neighbours in the West stems from the shortcomings of her climate, thanks to which few sections of her vast grain-growing lands experience more than two good harvests in a row. 

Over much of her steppe-lands farmers annually face the hazard of too little rain, or too much sun. Eastward, northward and southward of the steppes conditions worsen perceptibly: only westward do they improve. 

Thirdly, geography supplies us with the techniques of mapping these differences, and of assessing realistically the adequacy, or inadequacy, of the environmental conditions of a given locality for the maintenance of a satisfactory life. In this age of growing social unrest and economic dislocation, a comprehensive spatial analysis of the circumstances of a people's way of living (which includes such things as the distribution of soil types, climates, land use, mineral and power resources, means of transportation and other public services) is as essential to the furtherance of that people's way of living as accurate maps of terrain, lines of communication, military installations, airfields, ports and so on are to the furtherance of an army campaign. In no small measure, it was for lack of such maps that one farm in every three in the dry belt of southern Alberta was abandoned between 1921 and 1926. The early attempts at group settlement in the timbered lands of Western Australia were thwarted for a similar reason. Not even the expenditure by the government of £10,000,000 in the settlement of a mere 1,700 families (i.e., approximately £6,000 per family) could compensate for the deficiencies of knowledge of the region's soils, water resources and vegetative cover. 

On the other hand, it was the existence of the highly detailed and exact land classification maps produced by the Land Utilisation Survey of Great Britain that enabled that country to expand its ploughland acreage in the early days of World War II by 60% and to double its output of essential human foodstuffs. But the geographer is able to do more than make such maps: he is also able to interpret the significance of the spatial differences which these maps reveal. In so doing, he is often able to identify areas of maladjusted economy and stress-points which threaten the cultural stability of a locality-perhaps even a nation- and to indi- 

II

cate the existence of other areas offering greater possibilities for commercial enterprise and land settlement. The Canadian Prairies have provided examples in plenty of such maladjustments and stress-points. 

To the early settler the prairies of southern Saskatchewan looked much the same as those of the Red River of southern Manitoba, and for a while they yielded so well that even the farmer saw no reason to doubt the evidence of his eyes. But by the mid-thirties his children knew differently, for whereas the Red River Valley was still yielding tolerably well, even in the face of subnormal rainfalls, southern Saskatchewan was now the scene of a mass emigration. Today the area is better understood: more ample data are available concerning its water table levels, its soil profiles and its precipitation regime, as a result of which much of the erstwhile derelict land is now being put to some productive use-as cattle pastures, irrigation farms, wood lots and shelter belts. And there is even a chance that some of the reclaimed land will eventually sustain a denser rural population at a high standard of living than it ever did in the "wheatmining" era.

In wartime no nation can afford not to think geographically. For wars, while they may start in the world of the mind, are fought out in the world of the material—of jungle and marsh, wind and sand, where a storm of rain can do more to turn the fortunes of a battle than a dozen impassioned speeches, and where a foreknowledge of the beach conditions along the enemy's coast can be a lot more comforting and rewarding to an invading army than the promise of a supporting naval bombardment. 

The price of erroneous geographical intelligence comes very high in war, as Napoleon discovered on his ill-fated Russian campaign, and in more recent times, as the Allies learned on more than one commando raid. 

However, it has to be admitted that, by and large, modern military strategists are seldom slow to appreciate the weight of the geographical factor, or to grasp the opportunity of having their men briefed regarding the environmental circumstances they will be called upon to face. For a while during the past war, the Army's insistence upon such briefing was only equalled by the inability of colleges and universities to provide it! And it can be said without fear of contradiction that more first class geo- 12

graphical research was accomplished by governmental agencies between 1939-1945 than had been carried out previously in any comparable period. The popular literature of the same epoch gave an equally gratifying emphasis to the geographical background of the various campaigns. 

The need for sound geographical education is, however, no less imperative in peacetime, for it would not be difficult to show that many of our problems in the inter-war years were aggravated by the fact (even if they did not actually arise from it) that we were grossly ignorant of the basic facts of world geography. Can it be denied, for example, that the 

Hungary of the Trianon Treaty was a territorial oddity, or that Budapest, with its population of nearly one and one half million people and large industrial establishments, was vastly too big for the much shrunken territory (essentially agricultural at that) which it served? After all, treatymaking is more than a matter of drawing new lines on an old map. Nations need food as well as frontiers; economic resources as well as tariff blocs and restrictive immigration laws. To curtail a nation's power to wage war is one thing—and a very good thing-but to deny her the environmental basis of a workable economy, that is, agricultural as well as mineral resources, and commercial opportunities commensurate with her population's needs, is a very different thing-one not at all calculated to heighten the prospects of a durable peace. Unfortunately there are not wanting signs that the lessons of 1919 to 1939 were ill-taught and that the high purposes of the signatories of the Atlantic Charter have been forgotten. Thus, it is difficult to see how Japan, shorn of all the possessions she has come by since 1894 when she had a population of less than 30,000,000, is going to satisfy the legitimate needs of a population now nearing 80,000,000, and increasing by a round million a year. It is equally difficult to see how India and Pakistan can continue to exist apart in proud defiance of the many physical and economic ties that bind them. In few parts of the world are the geographical arguments for political fusion so clearly drawn as in the Indo-Gangetic plain with its complete absence of natural barriers to communication, its master streams demanding unified control if their power and irrigation potentialities are to be properly exploited, and its unequal distribution of fertile soils, mineral resources and rainfall. 13

Nor is it any extenuation to say that these are matters solely for the statesman and the politician, for in the last analysis it is the common folk who put the politician and the statesman in their chairs of office. 

And, anyway, is there any assurance that, in this respect, the "experts" are always so much better informed than the laity? Even in so uncomplicated a matter as geographical location, there would seem to be some reason for supposing that the answer is in the negative. Some time ago an enterprising investigator, Rouben Gavoor, printed the word "Azerbaijan" on a card, showed it to 500 people with a high school and college education, and asked them what it meant. Of the 500, 484 confessed that they had never seen the word before, 16 of them (about 3%) gave partially correct, but vague answers, and not one gave a completely correct answer - and this notwithstanding the fact that only 4 years ago we narrowly missed being plunged into a third world war over a clash of Anglo- American and Russian policies in Azerbaijan! If ignorance of mere place names is so widespread, then it is obvious that ignorance of the geopolitical significance of such places and the merits of conflicting claims to their resources must be almost universal. At times, we are sorely tempted to wonder how much the members of our parliaments and congresses, and the men who formulate our international policy, know about the dozens of places over the earth where, without warning, world peace might be jeopardized-even lost-by reason of irrational behaviour springing from such ignorance!

Fortunately, in this particular instance, our ignorance did not prove expensive. But we may not always be so lucky. The British were not so lucky with their peanut scheme in Tanganyika, which has already cost £24,000,000 and will cost many more before the British housewife derives an ounce of margarine from it. And why? Because the scheme was 

Utopian? poorly administered? inadequately staffed? No! The scheme could not have been more timely or manned by better trained and more enthusiastic men. The real trouble is that not enough was known about the climatic vagaries of the region, or the character of the terrain to be cultivated, the constitution of the soil, or even the working habits of the natives. In other words, not enough was known of its essential geography. 14

Nor do we need to look as far afield as Africa, for there are indications that some of our own spectacular social engineering projects may founder, or at least run aground, on the shoals of geographical reality. Is it certain, for instance, that the multimillion dollar scheme (forming a part of the multibillion Missouri Valley Authority program) to build a nine-foot channel for barge traffic upstream as far as Sioux City will produce results commensurate with the magnitude of the undertaking? Bulky downriver cargoes, other than grain, are practically nonexistent, while the prospect of finding profitable return payloads is clouded by the fact that there are next to no large urban centers anywhere along the middle and upper reaches of the Missouri River. Furthermore, is it certain that the watershed region of the Missouri could be made to provide enough water for the maintenance of such a navigation channel into which would flow the silt-laden waters of some of the worst soil-eroded country in the Union? If the history of water storage projects elsewhere in the West is any guide, then there is much more to the question than the building of a dam. Through siltation the Sweetwater Reservoir in California lost 

48% of its storage capacity in less than seven years. Near Stillwater, Oklahoma, a flood-control reservoir covered a 1,000 acres in 1937, and in 1944 nearly 3,000 acres - silt had backed up the waters, at the same time seriously curtailing the flood-control value of the reservoir.

But perhaps we do not need to press our point any further. The fact is that, no matter where we look, we are constantly faced with problems involving "the strategy of men, space and resources," problems that have their roots in the earth and man's use (or abuse) of it, and can only be solved by men and women who have learned how to assess the importance in time and place of those facts of environment that are operative in human affairs, and who are then prepared to use the knowledge acquired by their studies.

Such knowledge, however, is hardly won. It is not to be had by an overnight stay in a foreign capital, or by an exchange of letters, the reading of official releases, or the occasional viewing of travelogues "in glorious technicolor." It is only to be attained by a long continued educational program, wisely administered and supported by all the agencies of popu- 15

lar communication, such as the press, the radio and television, currently at our command. Here, assuredly, is a task which the Society with its unsurpassed reputation for disinterested research along the frontiers of geography is uniquely fitted to assume. It is also a task that needs to be embarked upon without delay and for no less a purpose than the fashioning of a more decent world for our children. 16

PLANS AND PROSPECTS 

The plan evolved by the Society upon the basis of its investigations not only is ambitious, but probably revolutionary in the relationship between scholars and teachers. Yet, to us, it seems natural and inevitable, and, it may be said, long overdue.

We propose, first, to strengthen and secure the central functions of the Society, that is to say, to remove the danger of early financial collapse; second, to develop immediately a greatly accelerated program of geographical research, utilizing existing personnel in the profession; third, to bridge, as completely as possible, and at all points, the gap between the sources of geographical knowledge and the facts that reach the public at large, particularly the school child. 

So large an order is best broken down into parts, and the following sequence of steps constitutes our program: 

The Basic Operations of the American Geographical Society Chiefly, the problem here is one of finance, the need for an endowment or reserve which will ensure the continuation of the central functions of the organization upon which all of the rest of the program is dependent. 

These functions are costly, undramatic, routine in character - but fundamental. They comprise for the most part activities without sales value: administration of a library and map collection; operation of a map department, maintenance of the editorial and publication staff; a consultation department; and a half dozen or more specialists who provide the expert counsel on which judgments are based, affecting the work not only of the Society, but of numerous outside agencies, governmental, business and academic.

The flow of donations to the Society which maintained this work during the past 40 years totalled $2,500,000. We know from recent estimates that the projection of that work onward for a reasonable period of time, by which we mean 25 or 30 years, will now require the sum of $10,000,000. 

This we term the Basic Endowment. 

If it proves to be impossible to secure the money with which to operate 17

these behind-the-scenes activities, the rest of the program cannot be attempted.

The Research Program 

In order to swell the output of mature geographical research we suggest offering fellowships to senior graduate students. The location of the study would of course hinge on the type of problem undertaken. Some geographical researches can be completed in a library, but more often they require travel to the site itself. The tenure of the appointment would range from one to three years, depending upon the problem and the time available to the candidate.

Between 1946 and 1949, doctor's degrees in geography were awarded in the United States at the rate of about ten per year. Perhaps no more than half of these men would be available, or qualified, for the fellowships, but to this number we would expect to add approximately as many from other fields, such as medicine, statistics, demography, etc., so that we are speaking of about ten starting fellowships per year, or, on the average, about twenty fellows holding office at one time. Estimates of the cost of maintaining this program, based upon a salary of $3,500, plus travel, equipment, publication and other expenses, amount to approximately $8,000 per man-year, or $160,000 annually. 

The tremendous effect possible from putting this group of men at work on geographical research would be still further augmented, we believe, by the supervision which we propose to render. We have long felt that the 

Society's workshop offers an ideal interneship to scholars and scientists in perfecting their skills. We have in steady progress the routines by which research is initiated, reported, checked and published, and a short tour of duty under the experts who manage the several departments would speed the candidates' work, reduce errors and false starts, and evoke an interest which would persist throughout life. Furthermore, and this we have checked by extensive inquiry, the prestige accruing to a holder of an American Geographical Society Fellowship would substantially enhance his position in academic circles. 

We hold this portion of the program to be of enormous significance, 18

not simply for scholarship but as a principal source of vitality for the educational link. We urge that this be supported by a Research Endowment of approximately $4,000,000, with the provision that principal as well as income may be gradually expended as opportunities or need arise. 

The Educational Link 

Our proposal here is to act as an integrating agency bringing together the best minds of the research field and the best talent in education, in order that subject and method may be joined. “What is it that shall be taught?" is the question that must be kept constantly before the graduate school authorities, for they and they alone can estimate the relative values of the subject's parts, and sense the trends of its movement. But let it be understood that the choosing of the content is their principal duty to education. "How to teach it" is the other question. Almost anything can be taught, as was proved during the recent war, when the techniques of getting a subject across were successfully explored for a wide range of ages and mental levels. Here the Society can play a multiple role: first, by acting as a clearing house for the experience already accumulated; second, by producing in its own workshop and elsewhere prototypes of the special aids needed to convey the subject to teachers and students; and, third, by stimulating and coordinating the development of regional training centers and summer schools, where teachers under training and those already in practice explore the subject matter and learn how to use the aids. We may expect that such centers and schools will be heavily attended in the first years during which the graduates of an earlier period catch up, but that attendance will decline to a relatively stable "refresher course" level thereafter.

The task of quickly achieving the educational aims of this plan is a very large one. In the United States there are some 25,500,000 pupils attending the elementary and secondary schools, instructed by nearly 900,000 teachers. A portion of these teachers must be trained to know the subject and its methods, and this portion may involve 100,000 to 150,000 persons. Colleges constitute a smaller though highly important problem, 19

since the teaching of geography at the college level still leaves much to be desired.

It is more difficult to estimate the cost of this portion of the program than of any other. For example: 

1. Sums must be spent to cover the development costs of certain basic aids which will stimulate commercial producers to improve the quality of teaching tools in this field and to make them available at prices within the reach of the market-e.g., the schools and the libraries. These aids include globes, maps, and relief models. 

2. The development of first class photographic material—perhaps the most wanted item on the geography teachers' list-likewise requires subsidization of the experts who secure the pictures. One of the most immediate objectives is a representative collection of colored stills out of which film strips can be constructed. These can be obtained in part by culling the files of the professional agencies or the illustrated periodicals, but in many cases it requires sending geographers, geologists and others whose training fits them to recognize the scene, into the field. 

Once the master films are produced, the rest of the process (reproduction and distribution) should become self-sustaining at a cost to the schools compatible with their budgets, and therefore widely accepted. 

3. The cost of running the projected summer schools cannot yet be determined. Perhaps in the later years the courses would be self-sustaining, but the initial effort of introducing the program would almost certainly call for supporting funds.

These three examples, while not permitting us to name a firm figure, indicate that the Educational Endowment should consist of substantial expendable funds with which to launch the program, and a residuum with which to maintain the smaller activities of after years. 

CONCLUSION 

It is our considered belief that the program herein described represents a sane and necessary preparation of the American people for their future. 20

While it breaks with many of the established conventions of academic procedure, our advisors, a large and representative group, believe it to be entirely feasible, and urge that it be put in motion. 

We of the Council of the American Geographical Society view the magnitude of the task proposed with the utmost respect. Our membership is composed mostly of lawyers, bankers and businessmen who serve voluntarily and without pay. Many of us also sit on the boards of other institutions such as hospitals, churches, universities, foundations and special assistance groups, and we therefore harbor no delusion whatever about the perils of institutions which depend upon private giving as the means of continued existence, nor any deception regarding the shrinking meaning of the word "perpetuity" as applied to endowments. We realize that there is a limit to long-range planning, and that from the financial standpoint the span of a generation is perhaps the longest unit of time with which we can deal, just as from the personnel standpoint, it is the shortest span of time within which the tasks envisaged for the Society might be consummated.

Above all, the Council appreciates the fact that there is in this country not enough philanthropic money to go around, and that the bed of surplus funds in private hands is shrinking rapidly and surely. In proposing the continuance of the American Geographical Society and the enlargement of its scope, the board is not unmindful of the needs of the thousands of other organizations seeking funds, and the possibility that many may eventually be compelled to close their doors. This grim fact has been squarely faced, and we are prepared to close this institution if its worth is judged to be subordinate to that of others in the arena. 

The commanding and stimulating fact, however, is the solitary position of the American Geographical Society in the field of scholarship in geography today. Its journal is the foremost authority in the world; its library and map holdings stand first among the working collections in the western hemisphere; its aggregation of authorities in the techniques of geographical investigations is not equalled by any other group in this country or abroad.

The question which we lay before private donors, therefore, is one not 21

of competition within a class of institutions, but of the continuation and further development of the only existing institution of its kind in this country. In the last analysis it is all a matter of what the nation needs most, and of whether the plans developed during our three years of investigation are capable of developing such a heightened appreciation among the American people of the role of geography in modern life that they acquire competence to deal with the vast problems of "men, space and resources" which the events of recent years have thrust upon them. 

In submitting this statement to possible donors, we invite critical inquiry, and the opportunity for discussion in detail of special portions of the case.
