THE OLD CHURCH ON RIVER DRIVE, PASSAIC, N. J. 

On this site the congregation worshiped for two hundred years.

Through The Years HIS grand old Protestant Reformed Dutch Church at Acquacka nonk, familiarly known as "The Old First Church of Passaic," the oldest church in Passaic County, is now celebrating 250 years of continuous existence. The early history of the church is contemporaneous with the early life of our city as for 132 years it was the only church in Acquackanonk and served a radius of 30 square miles. 

In 1686 fourteen God-fearing men from Holland received the Acquackanonk Patent (deed) from the English crown. This deed, recorded on Page 164 of Book A of Deeds in the Secretary of State's Office, gave them title to a tract of 5,520 acres. This included the present cities of Passaic and Clifton, also a large part of Paterson. The deed confirmed the Patentees' honorable purchase of the fertile tract from the original holders, the Lenni-Lenape Indians, in 1679 at their own price of “several coats, blankets, muskets, handfuls of powder and strings of wampum."

Urian Tomason (Urian son of Thomas) or Jurian Thomassen, of Rypen, North Jutland, was one of the Patentees, sailing to America from Holland. 

His descendants, the Van Ripers and Yeareances, have been affiliated with the Acquackanonk Church throughout its existence and served on the Consistory as early as 1715. The most recent representatives on the governing board were John T. Van Riper, as Elder and Dr. A. Ward Van Riper as Deacon. 

The names of Post, Speare (Speer), Van Winkle, Van Houten and Vreeland also appear frequently on the early consistorial records. 

At the same time that the Patentees laid out their own farms they set aside land for a church and a home for a pastor. This original church property was a triangular piece comprising 13 acres, bounded by the present County 

Bridge at Gregory Avenue, Park Place and Prospect Street. 

Elias Michielsen (Vreeland) a Patentee, built the first house in Passaic County at the corner of the present First and South Streets. This home was used for church services and a secular day school for several years until a small log church was built, probably about 1702, on the River Drive site (now lower Main Avenue) where the congregation worshiped for 200 years. 

The oldest written record, 12 sheets of unruled paper, folded over and stitched in the middle to make 48 pages, now yellow and tender from age, dates back to June 12, 1693. On that day "the sum of 18 stivers ($1.80) was collected."

There have been 14 pastors during the 250 years. The Rev. Henricus

Schoonmaker, the Revolutionary War Pastor, gave the longest service, 42 years. 

The Rev. Guillaume (William) Bertholf, the first pastor, was second with 30 years, and the Rev. Edward Dawson, Pastor Emeritus, was third, serving for 

29 years.

The congregation was first known as "The Low (Nether) Dutch Reformed Church at Achquechnonk" (80 spellings of the original Indian name are noted). The present name, The Protestant Reformed Dutch Church at 

Acquackanonk, was recorded in the Clerk's Office in the County of Essex on March 23, 1795, though the record was later "consumed by fire." 

The Dutch language was used exclusively for the first 123 years. Then the services became bi-lingual, until gradually the English supplanted the 

Dutch entirely.

Scott's History tells us: "In those early days the church was a power in secular as well as religious affairs. It owned every foot of land devoted to business and for a century refused to sell, preferring to lease the land which was divided into lots upon many of which the tenants erected stores, taverns and other business places which extended along the river." 

The church built the first school house and the minister was the first teacher. Children were taught to read from the Heidelberg Catechism in the original Dutch.

The church not only comforted the sick but buried the dead (at a fee!) in the graveyard adjoining the church. Its voorleser (lay reader) wrote most legal documents and was official auctioneer.

MINISTERS UILLAUME BERTHOLF, the first pastor, 1694-1724 had been voorleser and the first school teacher at Acquackanonk. His spirituality and magnetic personality, also his ability as a public reader, leader of singing and scrivener, commended him strongly to the congregations here and in Hackensack ("The Old Church on the Green", organized in 1686). They jointly paid Voorleser Bertholf's expenses via sailing vessel to Holland that he might be licensed and ordained to the ministry by the Classis of Middleburg (Middleberg) to serve their churches. 

Mr. Bertholf assumed the joint pastorate in 1694 when 37 years old. He thus became the first Reformed minister in the State of New Jersey and was the only one of that faith for fifteen years. "His parish reached from Tappan on the North to Belleville on the South and from Pompton on the West to the Hackensack River and even to the Hudson on the East." Before Dominie 

Bertholf's time those living in "the wilderness of Acquiggenonck and Ackinsack" had known only the infrequent visitations of the Rev. Petrus Tesschenmaeker, “first messenger of Christ and minister to these people," who was killed in the massacre of Schenectady in 1690.

Mr. Bertholf was paid 1,000 guilders ($125.00) a year by each church and "this was usually in arrears." Acquackanonk raised its share of the salary by renting sittings on the hard benches. It cost $15.50 a year for a man and wife with a son and daughter. The pews passed from father to son and from mother to daughter. Neglect to pay the seat rent for a year and six weeks forfeited the seat. Pew rentals remained a chief source of church income until the congregation voted to have free pews during Dr. Dawson's pastorate. 

Mr. Bertholf's death after 30 years of service ended the association with the Hackensack Church. In that period the Dominie also found time to organize the "Reformed (Dutch) Church at the Ponds" at Oakland, and that at Pompton, N. J.; likewise the churches at Tappan, Tarrytown and Port Richmond, N. Y., as well as those at Raritan, (now Somerville) Belleville and 

Schraalenberg, (Dumont) N. J. In all Mr. Bertholf added 1,000 souls to the kingdom. In addition to his regular charges, Mr. Bertholf supervised the new churches despite the difficulties of travel those days. It is not strange that the health of this domestic missionary at large failed when he was only 67 years old. He died shortly after his retirement to his farm near Hackensack and is supposed to be interred under the pulpit of the Hackensack Church. Descendants of Dominie Bertholf's eight children may still be found in Passaic and Bergen Counties.

While the Acquackanonk Church waited for the mother church to send "a young preacher from Holland," Dominie Gualterus Du Bois, celebrated 

New York preacher, “exercised a fatherly care over the shepherdless flock." (Quoted from The History of the Classis of Paramus compiled by our own 

Drs. John Gaston and Ame Vennema). Corwin's Manual of the Reformed 

Church in America mistakenly lists the Rev. Gideon Du Bois as pastor here from 1724-1726, evidently confusing him with the Rev. Gualterus Du Bois who only occupied the pulpit occasionally. 

In 1725 the Classis in Holland sent us our second pastor, the Rev. Henricus 

Coens, ("The Blessed") 1725-1735. Dominie Coens also served Pompton and for a time the church at Second River (Belleville). The latter connection proved an unhappy one and was discontinued in 1729. Dominie Coens was buried beneath the pulpit of the first church. 

His successor, Johannes Van Driessen, 1735-1748, gave less time to the Pompton Church, styling himself "Pastor at Acquackanonk and Extraordinary at Pompton." He was the first Dutch preacher in America who had not been regularly licensed and ordained by the Classis in Holland. Instead he was licensed and ordained by a Congregational Council of 11 ministers at Yale College, New Haven, Connecticut. Dominie Van Driessen buried his wife in our churchyard at the church's expense, the bill listing such items as "baking for the obsequies, 8 shillings; barrel of beer, 10 shillings; sugar, rum and butter for the funeral obsequies of Dominie Van Driessen's wife, f2. “15s" 4d. The receipts for the same are preserved. 

Dominie David Marinus, 1752-1773, served both Acquackanonk and Pompton. His quaint call specified: "Twice a year you will have family visitation at Acquackanonk and twice a year at Pompton." It promised him “a good dwelling, a little barn for horses and cattle, a well, a garden and 6 acres of land. We will keep all this in repair. We will take firewood to the Dominie's house. Pompton will find you free provender for your horse every year, and some of the Acquackanonk Church will also bring you something for your horse but will not be obliged to do so as are the Pomptoneers. All this you will enjoy as long as Your Reverence continues to be our pastor," the call concludes. 

Dominie Marinus organized and served the church at Totowa, (now 

Second Reformed Church in Paterson) near the 100-acre farm he owned there.

Unusually brilliant, but erratic, he was suspended by Synod in 1773, “because he sought relief from the distressing contentions of his time in a too free use of the convivial bowl" (quoted from The Church Tablet, edited by Dr. Vennema).

Henricus Schoonmaker, 1774-1816, also served the Totowa Church for a time. He was the last minister to preach in Dutch, "being unwilling to submit to the awkwardness of attempting to minister in English, a language in which he was little at home." It was Dominie Schoonmaker who welcomed 

General George Washington to Acquackanonk on November 21, 1776. He assisted the church women in giving food and warm clothing to the poorly clad and half-sick soldiers encamped in the graveyard.

Dominie Schoonmaker was the first minister to be given a vacation. The consistorial record states: "We agree to allow our beloved pastor four Sundays in the year for his own use in order to spend them somewhere else." He was also the first to receive a pension, being paid $205.00 yearly until his death. 

During his 42-year pastorate he married 896 couples and baptized 2300 infants. 

The Rev. Peter Froeligh, 1816-1825, was called to “preach in low Dutch or the English language as the consistory might direct." During Dominie Froeligh's pastorate the church was split by his refusal to baptize infants unless one parent was a professed Christian. The Classis of Paramus decided against Mr. 

Froeligh. Thereupon he seceded from the denomination in 1825 and with a substantial number of his parishioners organized the True Dutch Reformed Church of Acquackanonk. This organization still exists under the same name though familiarly known as the Prospect Street Christian Reformed Church. 

The Rev. Benjamin C. Taylor, 1825-1828, is remembered for organizing the first Sunday School in 1825. The 100th anniversary of its founding was appropriately observed in 1925 during Dr. Dawson's pastorate. At the close of the Civil War this Sunday School was the largest in the County and “acted as a magnet to draw the boys and girls from a distance of several miles around." 

(Scott's History). 

During the Rev. Christian Z. Paulison's short ministry, 1829-1831, the first beautiful church was built on the original River Drive site. During the Taylor and Paulison pastorates the Secessionists outnumbered the parishioners of the parent church. 

The quarter-century ministry of the Rev. William Bogardus, 1831-1856, (grandfather of Mr. Stephen Merselis of Passaic) "brought better days spiritually and temporally," Dr. Gaston tells us in his beautifully handwritten, Spencerian-shaded, series of historical sermons. Dominie Bogardus healed the breach caused by the secession. He was the first minister to urge temperance in the use of liquor. Drinking among church men was very prevalent in those days and not considered inconsistent. Mr. Bogardus, his wife and son were buried in the old church yard. The Rev. J. Paschal Strong, 1856-1869, served the church for 13 years. 

During the pastorate, in 1859, the church was enlarged to nearly double its length.

In 1867 a group left to form "The Presbyterian Society", the beginning of the First Presbyterian Church. In 1868 Mr. Strong organized the North Reformed Church (now Temple Emanuel) on the corner of Jefferson Street and Hamilton Avenue, with 36 members. The following year he resigned to build up the new church, which eventually erected a beautiful stone building and Church House adjoining. The North Church enjoyed five prosperous decades. 

It was responsible for the organization of the Lakeview Heights Reformed. 

Church, Clifton, of which an "Old First" boy, the Rev. Peter G. Baker, was the first pastor. North Church also played a prominent role in establishing the First Reformed Church of Garfield 52 years ago. There were six pastors during its existence of 55 years which was terminated of necessity because of changing conditions which had led the parishioners to move to other sections. 

On November 4, 1923, Dr. Dawson received 170 North Church members into the fellowship of the mother church by letter. Others affiliated themselves with churches nearer their homes.

Under Dr. John Gaston, 1869-1895, Mr. Strong's successor, the church prospered greatly. In 1884 the sum of $6,411 was collected to renovate and refurnish the church building. In 1887 the historic stone parsonage on Lexington 

Avenue at Sherman Street, for 129 years the home of successive pastors, and 12 acres of ground around it, was sold for $32,000. It cost L370 ($1,850) with 

44 acres in 1758. Dr. Gaston then built and occupied his own home at 180 

Lexington Avenue, where his daughter, the much beloved Miss Anna E. 

Gaston, still resides.

Dr. Gaston organized the first women's societies, namely: The Woman's 

Missionary Auxiliary, the Helen E. Aycrigg Mission Band, (afterward the 

Young Women's Church League for Service, no longer in existence) the Ladies

Aid Society (which merged with The Circle), also the Young People's Society of Christian Endeavor. Dr. Gaston was elected President of the denominational 

Board of Education and held the position 21 years, until his death in 1901. 

The last two years of his 26-year pastorate, because of failing health, Dr. Gaston

had as assistant pastor, the Rev. Arthur Spaulding. The Rev. Mr. Spaulding died on Easter Monday, April 26th, 1943, aged 77 years, having served successfully as pastor of the Presbyterian churches at Bainbridge and Salem, N. Y. after leaving Passaic. In 1895 Dr. Gaston retired and was declared Pastor 

Emeritus by the Classis of Paramus. Dr. Gaston is the only pastor remembered with a memorial window in the present church. The beautiful Sermon on the Mount Window on the Passaic Avenue side was the gift of the congregation. He had laid the cornerstone of the building six months before his death. 

Like his successors, Dr. Vennema and Dr. Dawson, the degree of Doctor of Divinity was conferred on Dr. Gaston by Rutgers University, his Alma 

Mater, during his local pastorate. Dr. Vennema's Alma Mater, Hope College, also honored him in like manner. Dr. Dawson received the degree from Central College as well. Dr. Gaston was a member of the Board of Foreign Missions as was Dr. Vennema, while Dr. Dawson, the Pastor Emeritus, is president of that Board. 

The present beautiful building on the corner of Passaic and Paulison Avenues, is a monument to the untiring efforts of Dr. Ame Vennema (18951911), who succeeded Dr. Gaston. Dr. Vennema was the last pastor to preach in the old Church on River Drive. After 200 years of service on the original site the congregation followed the growing community to the "Hill" section. 

The cornerstone of the new church was laid on June 1, 1901. The church was dedicated on June 22, 1902, entirely free from debt. The church, built of marble from the granite quarries of West Grove, Pa., and its furnishings, cost $70,000 and the land $14,000.

Later, the River Drive church was sold to SS. Peter and Paul Polish National Catholic Congregation. On March 17, 1930, this church, with the tall spire which had been a landmark for 101 years, was destroyed by fire. A church of similar Dutch Colonial architecture has replaced it. 

Dr. Vennema's election to the presidency of General Synod, the denomination's highest office, on June 1, 1901, also marked the twenty-fifth anniversary of his ordination to the ministry and of his marriage to Miss Henrietta Le Febre, of Holland, Michigan. During the Vennema pastorate the congregation assumed the support of its first foreign missionary, Dr. Harmon V. S. 

Peake, in Japan. Dr. Vennema organized The Circle, still a strong arm of the church, and performed a valuable service by his translation of the early records of the church from Dutch into English. The church still has many visitors wishing to consult our records for authentic genealogical information. Dr. 

Vennema edited The Church Tablet, a valuable source of information on

earlier days of the church, bound volumes of which are in the Astor Library, New York City. He was a frequent and valued contributor to the denominational magazines. Dr. Vennema resigned after 16 years to assume the presidency of Hope College, Holland, Michigan, where he served seven years, relinquishing it because of ill health. After a short pastorate at the Mahwah, N. J. Church, he returned to Passaic in 1920 and became Pastor Emeritus. He fell asleep in the Lord on the afternoon of April 26, 1925, after attending the morning service and greeting his friends.

The Rev. Edward Dawson, 1912-1941, succeeded Dr. Vennema. The commodious Church House was added in 1917 at a cost of $25,000. The present parsonage was acquired in 1923. At one time during Dr. Dawson's pastorate the church supported four foreign missionaries: one in China, (Miss Edith C. Boynton, an "Old First" girl) in India, in Arabia and in Japan. Japan is no longer represented. A North Church legacy supports Dr. W. Harold Storm in Arabia.

Mrs. Galen R. Scudder is the Sunday School Missionary in India, and Mrs. 

William Vander Meer the Church Missionary in China. Old records tell us that "as far back as 1709 and even earlier the church raised considerable contributions for missions." During this pastorate four young men entered the ministry: the Rev. Peter G. Baker, missionary at Bahia, Brazil; the Rev. Dr.

Wallace W. Anderson, pastor of the State Street Congregational Church, Portland, Me.; the Rev. Leonard Sibley, pastor of the Central Avenue Reformed 

Church, Jersey City, and the Rev. Frederick R. Hellegers, Ph.D., former assistant professor of English Bible at Princeton University, now pastor of Bethel 

Presbyterian Church, East Orange, N. J. Mr. Blase Levai, Jr., entered Hope

College, Holland, Michigan, to prepare for the ministry and is now a member of the middler class at New Brunswick Theological Seminary. Ten other young people entered various kinds of Christian work. The Round Table, women's evening group, was organized in 1934. 

Dr. Dawson represented the denomination as English preacher at The 

Hague, Holland, in 1924. He served two years as president of General Synod, 1932-34. He is listed in Who's Who in America. In 1938 the 245th anniversary of the church was celebrated with a pageant. The episodes for this were written from the Church records, and were directed by Mrs. Rosina F. Morey, church helper for 14 years, from 1922-1936. On Dr. Dawson's retirement in 1941 the church recognized his long faithful service with appropriate ceremonies. He is Pastor Emeritus and resides in Nutley, N. J. He is serving as supply pastor of 

Grove Reformed Church of North Bergen, N. J., for the duration while the 

Rev. Dr. Miner Stegenga is in the Service.

The Rev. Millard M. Gifford, S.T.M.

, became the 14th pastor in January, 1942. 

During this brief ministry a Couples Club for young married people and a Choral Group for those who love to sing have been organized. Monthly communion services have succeeded quarterly communion services and a children's confirmation service has been instituted. A note burning ceremony on 

June 20, 1942, wiped out a church debt of $4,300.

CHURCH BUILDINGS 

HERE have been but four church buildings during the 250 years. 

A log church, lined with clay, 20 feet wide, 36 feet deep, one story high, was built on River Drive (lower Main Avenue) in 1702 after the congregation outgrew services in the Elias Michielson home. 

The building's steep roof was thatched with straw and there was a tall cupola. 

The floor was bare earth trodden hard and the benches were slabs from trees. 

An upright post set in the ground with a slab laid upon the top of it was the pastor's pulpit on Sunday and the master's desk weekdays before a school was built. The charcoal-filled foot stoves the women carried from home furnished the only heat. The materials and labor were largely contributed by the people. 

The record of June 25, 1702 shows $3.37 expended for a pewter tankard, the sole communion service "and doubtless considered an elegant piece of plate," Dr. Gaston comments.

The bell for this first church was cast in The Netherlands in 1702. It was used until 1760 when the log church was razed to erect a larger one of rough field stone. A larger bell being needed, the old bell was loaned to the 

First Reformed Church of Little Falls, N. J. When no longer needed there, it was returned at our request. It hung in the entry of the last church on River 

Drive until the present building was occupied. Now, cracked and discolored, its original metal clapper replaced with a wooden one, it is suspended in the vestibule.

Although Acquackanonk was never molested by the Indians, for a generation after the Bergen Massacre, Acquackanonk men carried their muskets to church. A guard of armed men sat near the door while others did sentry duty outside. Men sat along the aisles and preceded the women and children to the door. Church services began at 8 or 9 A. M. and lasted until noon. After eating the lunches brought from home, services were resumed until time to leave for the nightly care of farm animals.

The second church (1760) had a sloping roof and a belfry in the center. 

In 1799 it was voted "to form the church into pews and install galleries on three sides." Bench sittings now became pew rentals. "Sixty-four pews were offered for sale at public vendue." Part of the money to renovate the church was raised by selling tickets for "A Lottery for Repairing the Low (Nether)

Reformed Church at Achquechnonk". The sale of lottery tickets was authorized by the State of New Jersey at that time and was considered a proper way to supplement church funds.

When this building was destroyed by fire the first really beautiful church

was erected on the old site in 1829 when the Rev. Mr. Paulison was pastor. 

It was built of rough brick with a frontage of 50 feet, a depth of 60 feet and a height of 28 feet. The steeple was 50 feet high. There were galleries on three sides, one corner being reserved for the negro slaves. This church cost $7,148 complete with painting inside and out and a two-rail banister fence with three gates and an urn topping each post. The written contract stipulated: "This does not include the bell rope which the Trustees must furnish." A long line of sheds in the rear accommodated the horses of the largely rural congregation which came from miles around. "The pews of the new church were offered at Public Sale on the 7th day of December, 1829", the record states. 

This building was enlarged to nearly double its length in 1859. A Sunday 

School building adjoining was erected in 1860. This "lecture room" as it was called, is still used by the present church owners. In 1942 they made the repairs necessary to preserve the original walls. In 1884, $6,411 was spent in renovation of the last church on that site. Families still active in the church have happy recollections of this church dating back for generations. 

The present beautiful and well-equipped church, much discussed during Dr. Gaston's pastorate, became a reality during Dr. Vennema's regime. Costing $84,000 for building, furnishings and land, Dr. Vennema frequently remarked later that it could not be duplicated for half a million dollars. Its usefulness for Bible School and recreational activities was much increased by the building of a commodious Church House during Dr. Dawson's pastorate. 

Three large stained glass picture windows greatly enhance the beauty of the Church. The Resurrection Window, covering much of the South-western wall, was erected by Annie Aycrigg Fuller in loving memory of her parents, Dr. Benjamin B. Aycrigg and Catherine E. Anderson. The Sermon on the

Mount Window, the congregation's memorial to Dr. Gaston, is on the Passaic 

Avenue side and the De Mott family memorial facing Paulison Avenue, pictures The Ascension. Smaller colorful symbolic windows memorialize the Van Dien, Terhune, Jelleme and Post families.

PARSONAGES 

UR first parsonage, built in 1713, stood almost opposite the old church site on River Drive. Records tell us this house was 44 feet by 40 feet and the height from the ground to the upper edge of the top beam was 26 feet. In 1754, after alterations, it became "The Tap 

House on the Hill" where Washington spent the night of November 21, 1776. 

This historic building was destroyed by fire in 1877. 

The second parsonage was the perfectly preserved brown stone house on Lexington Avenue at Jackson Street (now 125 Lexington Avenue), which section was then called "Gotam." The house was built in 1758 and was little changed during the 129 years the church owned it. The Rev. David Marinus, the fourth pastor, was the first to occupy it and the Rev. Dr. John Gaston, the eleventh, the last to live in it.

Dr. Vennema first lived in a rented house at 125 Park Place, until he built his own home at 76 Lafayette Avenue. Then the McGibbons' home which stood on the new church site was moved from the Passaic Avenue corner to 185 Paulison Avenue, next to the present church. This was the parsonage until the Hale residence, which adjoined the church on Passaic Avenue, was purchased in 1923. Dr. Dawson and family occupied it after extensive repairs. 

Dr. George Fitzsimmons purchased the Paulison Avenue property and erected a modern residence there.

THE CEMETERY 

HE cemetery beside the church was the only one in this part of the State for 50 years after the first settlement. Families from 20 miles away buried there in spite of the travel difficulties of those days.

It was there that Washington's army encamped the night of November 21, 1776. After the church moved to its new location in 1902 there were fewer burials. By 1919 they had ceased completely, and vandalism made the deserted graveyard an eyesore to the community. At the suggestion of a group of prominent citizens the church deeded the cemetery property to the city for a public park in October, 1920. Now called Armory Park, both the 

World War Cenotaph and the Armory are located there.

CHURCH EXTENSION 

N 1891 the "Old First" assisted the North Church in organizing the First Reformed Church in Garfield. Both shared equally in financing the first chapel and the enlargement into an attractive church building later. The late Elder Thomas A. R. Goodlatte of the North Church served eight years as superintendent of the Garfield Sunday School. 

After the return of the North Church congregation to the mother church the "Old First" consistory administered the endowment fund which the North Church had set aside for the Lakeview Heights Reformed Church which it had founded.

The Passaic Bridge (now Passaic Park) Christian Union, which included a number of "Old First" members, on June 5, 1879 opened the Passaic Bridge

Union Chapel on Franklin Avenue (this site is the present 276 Main Avenue). 

Here for 33 years an afternoon Sunday School was held and preaching services at night in charge of students from Union Theological Seminary. The Church, Clemons, Morse, Grear, Van Winkle, Aycrigg, Hawkins, Turner and Nickerson families were among the most active supporters. 

The First Congregational Church was organized March 15, 1885 by a group of Chapel attendants. With a church in the vicinity, the Chapel was no longer necessary. Therefore, on December 17, 1912 it was decided to deed the property over to the "Old First" with the understanding that the money realized from its sale should be used to help another church program. 

The Chapel was sold to a private party and later purchased by First 

Church of Christ, Scientist.

The money received was used to help establish the Allwood (Clifton) Community Church. The late Elder Cornelius W. Hellegers gave four years of faithful supervision to this young church.

So endeth the history of 250 years' continuous existence of The Protestant Reformed Dutch Church at Acquackanonk. The pastor and the people give thanks to God for the share our beloved "Old First" had in the development of Passaic from a tiny village to a modern city. We humbly pray it may be an even greater blessing to the community for centuries to come.
