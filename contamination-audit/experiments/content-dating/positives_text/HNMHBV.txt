The plain fact is that science has landed us back into the face of the mysteries, and unless we accept the explanation offered by a belief in God and a belief in man, they are mysteries which confound. Accept belief in God and belief in man becomes possible. 

Accept belief in man and the universe becomes the expressive word of a Creator. Reject either and man becomes an insignificant detail, tortured by the sense of his own futility. Hold to both, and you are roused to a sense of greatness which must drive you to key up life to an ever-increasing pitch of effectiveness. Hold to both and you become conscious of a Mind in all things to which we are cognate, with whom we can hold friendship, and towards whom we can tend. 

Hold to both and the Universe is a home; reject either and it is a furious alternation of terrific heat and appalling cold, on which we gaze with terror, while we cling desperately to a chance-cooled cinder from which we can scarce wring our sustenance for the brief hours in which we can exist.

The modern astronomer finds himself compelled to use a scale beside which all the dimensions of earth are trivial. Let us take his measure, as he constructs it, stage by stage. He starts from the fact that light travels at the rate of 186,000 miles a second. That is to say that if you turned on an electric light, the light 13

would reach a person 186,000 miles away one second after you lit it. I repeat this, not simply for the sake of making the meaning clear, but in order to emphasise the large number of miles, 186,000, traversed in one second. One might have thought this a sufficiently big unit of measurement. But the astronomer finds that it is far too small for his purpose. He uses as his measure a light-year, that is to say the distance that a ray of light travels in a year. It is worth the trouble to consider the seconds in an hour, the hours in a day, the days in a year, and then again that enormous number of miles, 186,000 for every second. That is the astronomer's unit of measurement 186,000 × 60 × 

60 × 24 × 365 miles. That is his foot-rule, surely a sufficiently large one. Yet it is not long in comparison with what he has to measure. For there are telescopes through which he discerns objects distant 140 million light-years from us. Turn that statement round and consider it again. 

We know of stars so far distant from our earth that light travelling at this enormous rate of 186,000 miles a second takes 140 million years to reach us.

Let us look at a corner of the Universe in another way. Suppose we make an imaginary model of our own little system, with a point to represent the sun, and another an inch away to represent the earth. (This inch will stand for 92,000,000 miles.) We shall have to put our dot to represent the next planet Jupiter at 5 inches, that for Saturn at 10 inches, then one for

Uranus about 20 inches, and for Neptune at 30 inches.

We have now got to the end of our Solar System and the next thing to put in will be the nearest fixed star. But we will, on the same scale, have to go three and a half miles to get the right position for the dot to represent it. And then we have only reached the inner fringe of the Universe which surrounds us. 

We are accustomed to think of God as a Being who is present everywhere. "Everywhere" has always meant a good deal. "Everywhere meant a great deal when Europe was the "World" to us. to us. It meant a great deal more as discovery added continent to continent. But now when we have to envisage these terrific distances, what is left to say? Nothing, of course, until we have so exercised our minds on these new materials that they have become used to working on this scale, and have learnt to charge our language with this new content. Man is faced with the challenge of new knowledge and must put himself to school again, and the effort to enlarge his mind must be painful as well as exhilarating, and perhaps as tedious as it is gigantic. And, as in all such efforts, we can only succeed a little at a time. The road to success, as usual, must be through failures, and failure our goad to drive us towards success.

Let us try another way. An attempt has been made, by a method that seems reasonable, to estimate the number of stars in the universe. The answer can be put on paper by writing down the figure 2 with twenty-

four ciphers after it. That carries us far beyond the impression made on our minds by the sky as it appears to the naked eye, and, indeed, may be said to set the mind moving in a particular direction, rather than showing it a goal to attain-which is very good for the mind. 

Now these stars are not mere points of light. Try to judge them by the standard of the earth. The sun is as big as a million earths put together. In this case, where we are dealing with bulk instead of distance, a million is not quite so difficult to grasp. A squared block of stone which is 8 feet 4 inches each way contains a million cubic inches. So if we make a model in our minds, and represent the earth by a little cube I inch thick, and compare it with a cube of stone a couple of feet higher than a very tall man, we shall get some idea of the comparative size of the sun. Yet there are many thousands of millions of stars of the same class as the sun, and this is only one class. But without attempting to define this further, we can use it to set us on the way to forming a conception of the extent of the universe. We must stick to the method of thinking in pictures. Suppose we reduce the stars in our mind's eye to the size of the motes of dust which drift about in the air. Think of a space as large as Waterloo Station with half-dozen of these specks floating about in it, and you have a diagram which gives an idea of the abysses of space which separate star from star. 

So we get the glimmerings of an idea of the vast field

over which the Creator works. But we must introduce another consideration unless we are to fall into a complete misapprehension. Half a dozen specks of dust, sharing an otherwise empty railway terminus, would not appear to have much to do with one another. But the stars have a great deal to do with one another. 

Every one attracts all the others, and attracts its neighbours powerfully enough to affect them. Even though they are at such vast distances, they are bound into a system, they hold one another to their courses. The universe does not consist of an enormous number of stars just thrown anyhow, and left lying about. Everything is held in its place, or at least has its path determined by great forces outside itself. The stars are suspended and supported in their places. However we may phrase it, the whole universe is a great meshwork of forces. So we can leave the idea of the extensiveness of the universe, suggestive as it is, and go on to the thought of the greatness of the Mind that conceived this enormous system and devised the rules which combine it into stability. This conception of the greatness of the Mind of God will arise so often in what follows that it would be monotonous to put it into words every time. So we must leave the reader to carry it in his mind, except where important additions have to be made to it. Meantime the extent, and, if we may put it so, the completeness and thoroughness of this great work gives us an indication of the ability of the Mind behind it. The work is always less than the mind of

which it is a product. Here is an incredibly great work, so great that we have to stretch and strain our minds to conceive of it. We must, consequently, exert an even greater strain on our minds to begin to conceive the Mind which thought it out and produced it. 

This thought of greatness has to be carried further still. For we have to add a greatness of time to the greatness of space. Astronomers have materials enough to enable them to compute the age of the stars. Speaking broadly, this works out at millions of millions of years. The variation is not great, and there is sufficient closeness in the numbers to suggest that there was something like a definite starting-point. But the point to be considered at the time is the immense period over which God carries on His processes. For processes they are. Great changes have taken place, and are taking place. The brilliant stars—and they are so manyare the seat of intense activities working along definite lines. I know that others have thought differently, but to me, at least, there is something awe-inspiring and yet congenial in the thought of operations carried out over such an enormous range both of time and space. 

Added to these is the vast amount of power employed. 

To take a mere detail: think of the power necessary to set the earth rushing at its present rate round the sun, and of the incredible force which holds it to its orbit and prevents it from flying off into space. Think of some stone you have whirled on a string, of the force you had to exert if the stone was at all heavy, and of

how it tugged and strained at the string when you had got it well going. And then think of our whole globewith no string to hold it, but with what a pull to keep it to its circuit !

This opens up an amazing train of thought, as we proceed to let our minds travel over the constellations, for regulated motion is the rule of all the universe. But even this only leads us along one avenue. There are others, no less amazing. Begin from an obvious consideration, the way in which each star is held together, the force binding its constituents into one whole. Take the familiar earth, for example. We have examples here continually before our eyes. Lay hold of a stone and lift it upwards away from its contact with the rest of the earth. It is hard work to do so, and the moment you let go, it rushes back and strikes the ground with a thud, drawn by the force which holds it to the rest. 

Try to break a piece of stone, and you find that its tiniest particles are held together by a cohesion which has never once relaxed since the remote age when the stone was formed, and offers a violent resistance to your efforts to break it down.

We do not reflect on this astounding display of force, because we are so used to it, and it does its work so unremittingly that we take it for granted. But look at it where it does its work under greater difficulties, and indeed with an obvious struggle. The particles which compose a solid are all holding on to one another-to use a loose phrase which may serve to represent the 82

facts in a figurative sort of way. But the particles which compose a gas are pushing one another away, are trying hard to get away from one another. Yet there are stars consisting of masses of gas each immensely greater than our earth both in size and weight which are held together and confined to a particular shape and boundary just as satisfactorily. They are bodies charged with forces which are always compelling them to strain at their limits, and vet they are held inexorably within those limits. 

And as night comes after night, bringing back the same spectacle of the stars set in their immemorial places, we recollect again that all these bodies are surrounded by and are imbedded in a universal web of force which maintains each in its station, giving each star its part in the whole effect, and enabling each to play its part in steadying the whole. The unchanging aspect of the fixed stars reminds us that they are not like so many huge stones scattered over a solid floor, but are hung in space, that without any solid framework to guide or check them, they are made to move in exact and undeviating courses. Even those which seem to disturb the system only display the same forces in a more startling manner and combination. There is a sense of grasp about it all, of power restraining power, which is at once an elucidation and a correction of our ideas of the Source of all Power. We feel that such a scheme involves a Mind so great as almost to shatter the very word. We are impressed by the way in which

that Mind shackles the forces under its control, or, if you will, bends them to shackle one another. Incredible force is wedded to unbelievable restraint, even as far as we have thought it out, and there is more to come.

Even at this stage we have a valuable correction of our ideas. It is often said that if God were omnipotent, nothing could stop Him. What is meant is that Omnipotence is bound to exert itself, and achieve its purposes on the instant. There is, in any case, a bad flaw in such reasoning. A God who had not power over himself to restrain himself would not be all-powerful. Nor would He provide a high ideal. Bursts of fury are neither so admirable nor so effective as the self-controlled power which adapts itself to the circumstances on which it works. But in any case, Nature presents us continually with the spectacle of enormous power under firm control. It affords freedom because it is marked both by action and restraint. Even apart from the gradualness of its processes, which, nevertheless, are the product of terrific forces, we are provided at every instant with a clear indication of a Nature which has unending power at its disposal, but always holds that power with an unyielding bridle.

Even the apparent exceptions not only reinforce this idea, but also cast a light on a vexed problem with regard to humanity. For were a star to break from its orbit and proceed on a road determined by itself, not only would it derange those whom it had left, but it

would be almost bound to terminate its career in a crash. Only those who work together can retain their status and powers. Any other must hurry on alone unless and until it nears another star and thenunless in such very unusual circumstances as those which probably produced the earth-its own weight would drag it into full and shattering collision. The price of liberty to be oneself to have that self-expression which so many people seek in isolation and caprice -is co-operation with others. Indeed, the price of survival is co-operation, and the price of retaining all your powers, submission to restraint.

But our picture of power is still deficient, and we must at least suggest other factors which have to be remembered. The stars are not just huge lumps of powerless matter, like great stones. Many of them are seething with internal energy. The light of the stars is no mere reflection of the sun, it is a sign of prodigious activity and unimaginable heat. Nor is it the glare of furnaces which are burning themselves out, but of perennial fountains of blazing, penetrating energy. 66 

The sun is, of course, only a small specimen of what we may term active stars. But since it is so near to us, it allows us to gain at least an inkling of what larger stars do in the way of sending out force. It is a commonplace that all or practically all the energy which man has had at his disposal, so far, is the direct product of the sun's power, transmitted to us in the

shape of light and heat. It is to the sun's light and warmth that we owe all our food, for without them nothing could grow. And it is to vegetable growth that we owe the beds of coal, which have been and are still the main source from which we derive the power which impels all our machinery of manufacture and locomotion. The whole of the activities of all the men in the world are borrowed from the Sun, and have their source in the radiation from it which catches the surface of the Earth. Yet, of course, we only trap a fraction of the Sun's light and heat. The earth is only a comparatively small body at a comparatively large distance. The light and heat pour out of the sun on every side, and the earth only forms a tiny island in the great sphere as it swells out past us. 

And this is only what our senses tell us. Light and heat are by no means the only forms in which power flows out in an incessant torrent. There are other rays of which we have only just become cognisant. The total is immense. Every square inch of the sun's surface discharges radiation into space at the rate of fifty horsepower.¹ Even the light and heat, as we know them, are pale and diluted. Light is difficult to measure, but we are all accustomed to the measurement of heat in degrees. On the scale used by scientists, freezing point is zero (i.e., no degrees) and boiling water a hundred degrees. Using this scale, the heat at the centre of the sun is found to be in the neighbourhood of fifty

are compelled, then, to reconstruct our conceptions of real physical greatness on an enormous scale. And, though the greatness of God is of a different order, we must reconstruct our ideas of Him in the same sort of way. As far as I am concerned, it comes home to me in this way. All these things are numbered among the effects of God. And however cautious we have to be about arguing from the nature of effects to the nature of causes, it is true of God, as it is true of any other being or of anything, that we only know them by their effects on us. However baffling, then, may be the problem of God's nature as it is in itself, when we come to consider, we find God to be an eminently knowable God. The effect of all this display of power is not to confound or baffle us. It has had the contrary effect. Every gain of knowledge has led on to more, and science has been more than justified in its belief in full and pressing enquiry. We are long past the day when it was deemed impious to search into the nature of things. The history of modern science is one continuous encouragement of the mind that wishes 25

to push its knowledge further. Nature has not given any contemptuous refusal to those who wish to know her more thoroughly. And it seems to me that we are entitled to assume that the character of Creation reflects the character of its Creator. 

This is not to say that He is easily or casually known. 

Nature teaches no such lesson as that. On the contrary, she teaches us the necessity of careful, patient investigation and indeed the necessity for training in how to investigate. A scientific observer is not made in a moment. He has to learn to see and to guard against errors in his own sight. He has to follow the facts and accept all the facts, and let his mind be moulded by the facts. The additions made to our knowledge as the reward of this diligence and adaptiveness have been most remarkable. And the effects on the mind of the scientist, and on the minds of those who learn from him, have been most remarkable-and will, I believe, be yet more remarkable.

Even our opinion of man has been raised, as the result of this pursuit and gain of knowledge. We have often been told that the effect of the discovery that the earth was not the centre of the universe, but a mere satellite of the sun, was to lower man's idea of his own importance. This statement has always seemed to me to be quite contrary to the facts. The individual of to-day has no less an idea of his own importance than his mediæval predecessor. Rather I should say that the main feature of the post-Copernican period

was the increasing importance of the human race in its own sight, and the emergence, at the same time, of a sense of the value and capabilities of the individual man. The human race has not been humiliated, but rather the contrary.

However that may be, the human mind has shown, in these matters, an extraordinary power of responding to the new demands made upon it and of accepting and assimilating new classes of facts. Confronted with a new universe-new at least to all his concepts-man is bracing himself to the task of grasping it mentally. The step forward which is being asked of the human mind is enormous. Before we can comprehend what a million, let alone a million millions means, we shall have to make as great an advance as that which has already brought us from the mental condition of those aborigines who were found to be unable to count any number greater than three. From their level to ours has been a terrific ascent, and may well have looked as impossible in its earlier stages. But it was achieved, and it is a remarkable proof of the innate capabilities of the human mind that it should have been achieved. 

I confidently believe that the further step will be achieved.

Indeed, I think that too much has been made of the difficulty. It may seem to us--or most of us—at the moment that we shall never grasp these huge numbers in detail. Having regard to the power of grasping an enormous number of details which seems characteristic

of the most highly-developed minds, it does not seem impossible. Such minds are a standing proof of the capability of human nature, though, as yet, they are exceptional. I should like to point out in passing that the very existence of these persons provides a corrective for a common assumption about God. It is assumed that because He is so great, details, such as we are, are beneath His notice, that because He, presumably, surveys the whole universe, He cannot be expected to trouble about its details. The whole assumption is really a marked apology for our own laziness. And it is contradicted even within the limits of human capacities. Most of us, I suppose, have known or known of men who were accustomed to deal with large masses of men, almost, you might say, mankind in the lump, and yet were able to recognise and name any person whom they had met for many years back. I may be pardoned for recounting a story of Napoleon which is pertinent to this subject. It is said that when he was at the height of his power, commanding vast armies and conducting a great campaign, he required one of his officers to prepare a list of all the pieces of ordnance in France. The list was prepared and submitted to him. 

On reading it through, he said, "You have omitted to mention a cannon of such a weight in such a village in the north of France and another of such a weight in such a village in the west of France." He was carrying in his mind the position and capacity of every cannon in that great military country.

Indeed, most of our knowledge is of this rough and provisory nature. We can comprehend, for example, what is meant by " a field of grass," though few, if any, would be able to guess at the number of blades which it contained, and I fancy that if the number in a good-sized field was computed and written down, it would appear to be beyond our comprehension. At all events we can relate it to lesser wholes, and lead our minds from lesser groups to greater. We can, at least, form a conception of very large wholes, even though it may require practice and time. 

Now, this capacity of the mind to extend its range seems to me to be of importance in assessing its value in relation to the world it surveys and the God to Whom it looks up. I must confess that a mind which can face and assimilate such a store of knowledge as it has already acquired, which can widen its scope to the enormous range of modern science, can treat these universes as objects of study, and devise methods for investigating what lies so far beyond our physical reach and grasp, takes its stand on a far higher level than physical nature, and naturally assumes a dignity higher than any mere bulk or extension. It is intrinsically

greater than many million tons of matter. On the other hand, the bare fact of its understanding so much, and the swiftness with which it has extended its understanding, seem to point to an essential likeness in kind to the Mind which conceived Creation.

There is at present so much disparagement of man and his position in the universe that the point is worth amplifying. Man is self-conscious, but there is nothing to suggest that the Universe is aware of its own existence. Man can have an idea of the Galaxy, but can the Galaxy have any idea of man? Even where there is life and intelligence the contrast remains. Can one imagine even his favourite dog being so much as aware of the stars, let alone of the stars as separate objects?

Animals do not show the least trace of that esteem of knowledge for the sake of knowledge, which is characteristic of the more developed type of human mind. 

For, were it only because the human mind has reached its present state through a process of development which is still going on, we must judge the human mind by its highest examples-perhaps even by its highest hopes. Men may talk as they will, but it remains true that they treat the universe as something laid before them for inspection, and regard it as something on which they look down. To come to what concerns us more nearly, though man, in his earlier days, was at the mercy of the conditions of the world which he inhabits, and contrived to survive only where he could escape the world's rigours and cataclysms, he has made

himself able to defy storm and cold by means of an apparatus of housing and heating and clothing to which there is no parallel. In the confidence which success has begotten, he is taking heart to contemplate greater difficulties, such as those resulting from over-population and the failure of our present sources of fuel, which have not yet thrust themselves on his notice, but which he foresees. Notice this, for we cannot imagine a star, or for that matter an animal, foreseeing and providing for circumstances which have not yet arisen. 

Judged by their comparative sizes, man may appear to be an inconsiderable parasite, too small and even too few in numbers to make any serious difference to the earth on which he dwells. However, the attempt, lately begun, to reckon up the extent to which man has altered the face of the earth, by his irrigation, cultivations, and so forth, has produced rather an astonishing total. For better and worse, the earth is a different place-sometimes with a different climate-because of man's activities. So, whether the facts be of merely intellectual interest and of vast range, or of practical importance and close at hand, in one way or another man masters them. Consider the difficulties which the astronomer has to overcome, and the ordinary citizen has had to overcome, and you have some measure of the mind whose agility and power are of an order far above physical nature. Man's mind conceives its purposes and searches out ways to secure them, and that is far more than the greatest star can do. The influences

of the stars are by-products; the influence of man is of his own selection and under his own direction. 

We are beginning to see that the God who is reflected in Nature is a knowable God. At one time the Universe seemed beyond the reach of our investigations. When its real size began to dawn on us, it seemed impossible that we should find out what went on at the other side of those vast abysses. The great Comte, who based his whole system on science, and believed that science, and science only, gave positive knowledge, declared that it was impossible that the composition of the fixed stars should ever be known to us. But it has become known, and known to a degree of detailed fulness of which he never dreamt. The size and weight of whole stars and constellations, and the character and constitution of the atoms which compose them, have been revealed to the patient study of astronomers. Homage ought to be paid to the ingenuity and persistence of the observers. 

But the success which has rewarded these virtues demonstrates the essential knowability of the Universe and implies a Creator who delights in comprehensibility. Even comprehensibility demands observation, has its difficulties, and needs-where such as we are concerned-time. He and he alone possesses the mind which can analyse the forces of nature and re-combine them to do his work and extend his knowledge. It has been argued that some animals show signs of being able to use tools. And there are probably insects which use forces, like the invisible rays, which our senses cannot perceive. Now we must turn again to the consideration of spiritual laws. As I have already shown, I feel bound to infer from the ideas expressed in the physical world, that a thorough survey of the spiritual world will reveal laws as regular and reliable as those of the physical world. But, in man's handling of both worlds, there comes in another set of laws, namely, the moral laws. 

These, I believe, have as fixed a sequence of cause and effect as the others. The result of a given action will always be the same. But there is a difference at the start, and a difference which is most significant. 

Physical laws say "Must," moral laws say "Ought."

The star is bound to keep the right track in space, the man ought to keep the right track in morals. The constellation must continue in its ordained course, the man must choose whether he will or not. The suns are under a compelling obligation, but man is not. That is why the Solar System cannot do wrong, but man can. And truly the Solar System cannot do right—it does just what it has to, and there is neither merit nor demerit about its motions. 

But man can, may, ought to choose an upward path ; man can deliberately choose to co-operate with his

fellows.

Thereby man is set in a superior position. 

Do what you will, you cannot explain away the moral sense. I have known men who have persuaded themselves that they had no free will, and that all their actions were ordained by iron necessity. But they were just as sensitive to praise and blame as anyone else. Now, he who has no choice cannot be praised or blamed. He who is not responsible cannot be condemned or applauded. Their anxiety for praise, their complaints of the injustice of blame, betrayed the fact that they knew better than their creed-or, shall say, brains?—that they could not but regard themselves as capable of deciding moral issues, as doing what they ought or ought not according as they chose.

Facts will come out, and the fact is that man has to do with a higher range of law, which is distinguished by the substitution of moral choice for simple necessity. 

This is not a characteristic of all life. It is not a plane on which animals move. The only exception--and that is a doubtful one-is the animal which has become partly humanised by constant association with man. That may afford ground for speculation and even hope as to what man may be able to do in the future to assist animal life to rise higher. But at present it is a rare and borrowed gift. If it becomes more widespread, it will be the gift of man, or at least the result of man. 

As things are, it is the peculiar prerogative of man, and puts him in a place by himself. It gives him a character like God, for even those who have trained themselves

We all know the difference between the law of gravitation under which the foot is drawn to the ground and the law which bids us restrain or avert the foot's descent where it would hurt the foot of another. We do not impute moral blame to the stone that crushes or the animal which bites, much as we may resent it. physical nature. Men can be found who believe that the inorganic forces are too strong for man, but none to believe that he is inferior to them. In short, our critics have given up deriding the Christian belief in man's elevation above the rest of creation, and quietly adopted it as their own.

Having regard to man's enormous advance in the control of physical force, I cannot believe that his battle for existence is bound to end in defeat. On the contrary, I feel an optimism which is becoming the attitude of most scientists. Science, in the present-day sense, has only been in existence for a very short time. 

It has certainly shown that where there is a will, there is a way. Though it foresees the exhaustion, within ten generations perhaps, of our present sources of power and heat, it knows that there are other sources, and believes that it can get at them. And though conditions may become such that the earth can no longer produce food, the chemist can enable us to produce it for ourselves when the need comes. We are thus led to believe not in any transient superiority of man, but in his permanent supremacy.

It had long been generally believed that all the world needed was the removal of certain defects. This earth was a temporary stage, it was true, but its character and the character of life upon it were fixed. It would be a more advantageous stage if sin were got rid of, and such objectionable persons as tyrants were removed, and liberty were secured, but all this was only needed for the purpose of realising existing advantages. And now to speak of Darwinism itself. A rumour has gone abroad that Darwinism is dead. Nothing could be further from the truth. What has happened is that Darwin's ideas about evolution are being restricted to their proper sphere, and being combined with other generalisations. It is quite wrong to believe and unfair to represent that Darwin professed to give a complete account of evolution or to account for its origin. What he did was to make clear part of its mechanism. The briefest consideration will show this. 

He starts from what are called "variations." A newly hatched brood does not always conform to pattern.

One member of the brood may be slightly different, say in colouring or length of limb, from its predecessors and its contemporaries. If this different colour or length of limb is an advantage, if it enables it to evade its enemies or get at its food better, the animal will fare better and be able to raise a more numerous progeny. 

Those of its children which inherit the variation will share the advantage, and those who have it in a more marked form will increase their advantage. So, in

the long run, there will arise a new class of animal or bird which will be able to oust its competitors. They will remain, while the others are starved out. That is the process, according to Darwin, by which new species are formed in consequence of the occurrence of variations. But he does not even profess to explain what causes the variations. He certainly does not provide us with a machinery automatically producing life, and does not pretend to do so.

Even, however, with Darwin's theory, a certain caution is necessary. It seems obvious that animals having a better protection from the observation of their enemies should gradually usurp the place of those who were less well protected, and that, consequently, features which made concealment difficult should disappear. In the field of protective colouring, in which Russell Wallace was Darwin's distinguished coadjutor, this seems to be true. But, we are puzzled to know, why does not the same rule apply to smell as to colour? One would have thought that the scent which an animal gives out and leaves behind it was so great a danger that it would have been eliminated or reduced to a very slight thing. But it is not slight, and it is a constant source of danger to the weak and frustration to the strong. So here is a case where what seems an inevitable process does not take place. This, as I say, should teach us to be cautious.

ARWIN'S view of evolution was that it proceeded slowly and gradually, by way of slight modifications, each leading into the next. New characteristics took a long time to develop, and evolution was, strictly, the drawing-out of qualities already present. It was a system without surprises. 

It must be said, once again, that his generalisation includes most of the facts. But not all. There are the "sports" of which we have spoken, features which make a sudden and unexpected appearance, as for example an entirely new colour in a flower. Such sports" are rare, but they have been observed and must be taken into account, A new thing, and, in its way, a complete feature, does occasionally arise without any steps leading up to it, or, at least, any preliminary appearances which would account for it. So there is added to Darwin's theory a new and special theory to bring these cases together. This is the theory of 

Emergent Evolution. It is suggested that breaks occur in the chain of cause and effect, or that effects appear which are due to something else than the precedent 149

causes. There are features which emerge, which have not been produced by what has gone before, and are not entirely the result of what has gone before. They could not have been foreseen, but are new in the strictest sense.

The great example of this would be life, which is something more than the chemical substances which paved the way for it. The change from plant life to animal life is suggested as another. By way of fortifying this an appeal is made to another science altogether, Chemistry. It is pointed out that when hydrogen and oxygen are combined together they form water, a substance which is quite unlike either of them. The properties of water are not a blend of the properties of hydrogen and oxygen, nor can we see how these new properties come to be there. The new features emerge, they are not produced by combining the properties of the old. I cannot quite make out whether this example is quoted as a loose illustration or as a case in point. But whichever it is, it seems dangerous. 

For it is really an argument from ignorance. We do not know the connection between the make-up of an atom and the properties of the substance which it forms. No doubt there is a connection, but we do not know it. So we cannot say what a combination of atoms can be or cannot be expected to produce in the way of properties. This leads me to consider the character of Christianity as a historical religion. It differs from its competitors, in this country at least, in being expressed not simply as a programme, but also in a series of historical events. 

Its creeds, indeed, consist mainly in historical statements. Once again it is in line with the ideas which

SUGGESTIONS 

157 we have been considering. Science deals with the manifestations which appear in the world of matter. 

It has learnt to detect and value what does not appear through what appears. It has come to regard the visible world as the revelation of underlying forces, not as a world in itself. It is at great pains to observe how life makes itself evident and effective in matter. If, then, religion has to deal, as I believe, with a spiritual world and spiritual forces, and it is at all in line with the character of creation, and of God's action as seen in nature, we should expect to find that it has had a manifestation and a growing manifestation on the material plane-in short, has expressed itself in history. 

And when one considers the character of its history, its congeniality to the expectations aroused by science becomes more marked. For it is the record of something which emerged slowly and with difficulty, something that rose from stage to stage, held back by its environment, struggling with its environment, dominating its environment. Moreover, it is not represented as attaining to finality and coming to a standstill. The New Testament avowedly looks forward to a more complete manifestation. Christ 

Himself is to become more evident in an organism whose cells are human beings, which should give Him a scope of which His manifestation in one human body was but a foreshadowing.

Other points might as well have been chosen, but I select this because it brings into view the analogy which

goes far to justify a much-criticised feature of Christianity, its much-abused Institutionalism. The whole tendency of physical science has been to increase our appreciation of the way in which all the Universe tends to group itself. Combinations are the order of its being. When we come to organic nature and its evolution we find the same principle at work. Evolution is the history of rise by cohesion, by the building up of cells into organisms. The lowest form of life is the single cell, which is its own all-in-all. The first advantage is gained when a sort of loose confederacy of equals sticking together, called a raft, is formed.

This is still a very low type of life, though it seems to be the religious ideal of some people. The important step is taken when combinations become definite, and work is divided up, so that one set of cells takes up one part of the work and another another. To leap to the end, the bodies of the higher forms of life, plants and animals, are vast corporations of cells, joined into groups and highly specialised for the work of each group.

On the other hand, while the building-up of communities has created advanced types of life impossible to the single cell, the individual cell has developed enormously in the process. Contrast the earliest cell, a speck of jelly just able to seize on what food meets it, and a cell in the human brain, the very vehicle and instrument of thought. It is true that the cell in the brain is tied irrevocably to its partners, and is depen-

SUGGESTIONS 

159 dent on others for fuel, food and protection, that it has become highly specialised; yet it has a capability and a freedom which make the other cell seem almost utterly helpless and ineffective in comparison. This, then, is the object-lesson of Nature, that the finer qualities are developed by combination, by a combination so close that it becomes a unity. 

Indeed science has given us a fresh idea of unity. 

The idea which held men's minds and was pushed forward in the discussions about God was that unity meant singleness and utter simplicity. Its symbol was, say, a marble cube uniform in composition. 

Bergson voices a reaction against this. The cube, he says, is not a unity. It is a fragment, which can be cut up into other cubical fragments. But a bird is a unity. It is an irreducible unit. Take away any one of its parts and it ceases to be. The bird is destroyed and you have a lump of dead meat on your hands which will rapidly dissolve under disintegrating forces. His contention is just. Even the cube of stone is a unity because its innumerable parts bind it together. It looks as though we should have to adopt his saying that a unity is essentially a thing united. At all events the real unities of life are differentiated in their internal structure they consist of parts (limbs and organs and nerves and so on), but the parts lose their character if they are separated. They are real units and act as such, following a single aim because they contain elements which can be differently adjusted. It is not

astonishing that the flood of all this concrete fact should have quietly submerged the supposed necessity for a simple God Whose chief feature is a featureless uniformity. Though an appeal is still made to the "mathematical" unity, the figure One, regardless of the existence of fractions, it carries little weight; and few are now found, even among the veterans, to borrow the Mohammedan gibe and call the doctrine of the 

Trinity "polytheism.”

The terms in which that doctrine are stated are avowedly drawn from the nature of man. Like all terms, they have to be used with care, as suggestions rather than delimitations. "Person," for example, is a description or rather name transferred from man to God. It is meant to suggest that there is something of the same character in God, though not with the same limits. To refer to something in actual existence is a safer way than to define our words by etymologies. Nature is a safer standard in these matters than dictionaries. Now that we have settled down, as St. Paul did, to study human nature, we find the advantage of going by facts instead of words. For personality is a far richer thing than we imagined. It branches out into society, it focuses itself, it arises out of unfathomed depths. So by using it as a figure our ideas are expanded, and our words grow richer in content, so as to be more valuable in our attempts to understand our glimpses of God and to piece together our fragmentary experiences of Him.

Meanwhile we are grateful that "personality" has never been defined. Definition has usually been what the word implies-a marking off of borders. We do not know its borders. We know some of its characteristics. 

But we will not say these are all. On the contrary, we believe that there are more to be discovered. Meantime, personality is the highest form of mind known to us. We prize it above all we have. As the highest point we know, in drawing out the path to God, we trace it up through personality. As the highest thing we know, we have a right to say that it is more like to God than anything that we know. 

I do not wish to pursue this subject further here, as I believe that the trouble is passing. But I want to rebut the charge that in using the word Person, we are laying down limits for God. Personality is curiously impatient of limitations. The human person cannot be detached from society. It is only as a member of a society that he is a person. And, as a matter of practice, if you shut a man completely off from society, not only does he cease to be moral-since morality is a relationship to others, but he goes mad-and madness is the anarchy of personality.

To the clues given him by man's mental and moral nature, St. Paul added one which has lain like a seed waiting to be developed. He drew attention to the human body as the diagram which would best illustrate that aggregation of many lives into one which he saw 

Christ beginning before his eyes. I have tried else-

where to work this out to some small degree.¹ But I would like to mention here one suggestion which derives a special interest from present-day knowledge. 

St. Paul, in trying to explain what goes on, makes a good deal of use of the symbolism of blood. We need only to remember the main functions of the blood-to cleanse, to feed, to convey air-and we begin to understand and to recognise. Among the many valuable medical operations of our day, none has caught the attention of the public more than transfusion, the operation in which the blood of a healthy person is passed from his arteries into the veins of a weakly and exhausted one. Here is an exact analogy, a close illustration of that very language about our receiving Christ's blood into us which so puzzled and, indeed, estranged men but lately. "Look through Nature up to Nature's God" applies not only to the surface of the landscape, but to all that is revealed by observation and experiment. Science has enriched our understanding of what the apostles, evangelists and prophets observed. And it has re-taught us the importance of observation.

In so doing it has made us look on our Bibles with added interest. For these are books written by men who wished to record what they had observed, and how what they had observed had taken a definite and intelligible state. We find, to a greater extent than might have been anticipated, that we can use them as we use scientific text-books, to observe the facts for ourselves and to gain our own understanding of them. One reason why we can still use them, despite their distance in time, is that their terminology was so largely derived from the materials of common life, and not from the language of scholars or the phrases of philosophers. Fashions in literature and thought pass, but humanity remains, and the creation out of which it emerges remains. 

We need not, therefore, take too seriously the warning against harnessing Christianity to passing scientific theory. The modern scientists' theories are groupings of facts, and are held in estimation only in proportion as they succeed in grouping large sets of facts. The groupings may have to be set into larger groups or subdivided into smaller. But they are not likely to prove unsound. In any case, they are different from the older theories, which were conjectural explanations. 

Your modern scientist is very shy of explanations. He waits for the facts to become lucid, and he is always collecting facts and more facts. His idea of a theory is a way of looking at things. The way may alter, but in the meantime he has noticed more and more things. 

Even those alterations do not condemn him as they are sometimes supposed to do. For example, he has had to abandon the idea that the atom is the fundamental factor in the universe, individual and irreducible. He

has been forced to regard it as a system of electrons. 

Nevertheless, the atom is still the atom, and all the information regarding it which he had collected remains true. And

It is still sometimes thought that science gives a depressing idea of man's past and future. I cannot agree. The age-long process by which his body was. shaped ere the human mind emerged does him honour. And after all it is only another version of the statement that God created man out of the dust. as to the future, the scientist sounds the note of hope and expectation. He has learnt that the sun will not burn out, but remain without noticeable change for ages yet to come. He looks on the using up of our stores of fuel without dismay, as he is working forward on the path which will give us far greater sources than these ever were. He is learning the secrets of the laboratory in which Nature manufactures food. So he regards the future not as a doom but a challenge. 

That challenge may and will grow more imperative.

But man's rise in the past has been in consequence of just such challenges, and the principle of advance is in him. Evolution is an expectation based on past experience, and there is every reason why man, having grasped its existence and exploring its principles, should climb up on every new obstacle, raising himself steadily to greater heights above what he is at this day. It is so little a time since he truly learnt that mind was capable of making matter his servant, that

And, even already, he is moulding creation and creating new creations. Science has performed and is performing the feat of making man more god-like, and in doing so has not only exalted the capacities of man, but our conception of God.
