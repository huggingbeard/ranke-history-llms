CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION The Eleventh Semiannual Report of the Small Business 

Administration provides information on the progress of the Agency in carrying out these responsibilities assigned to it by Congress. 

1. Helping small business gain access to adequate capital and credit through financial counseling, bank-Small 

Business Administration participation loans, direct Government loans, and assistance to privately owned small business investment companies; 

2. Helping small business obtain a fair share of Government purchases and sales; 

3. Helping small business obtain competent management, technical, and production counsel; and 

4. Making disaster loans to persons whose homes or businesses have been damaged by storms, floods, and other disasters, and to small business concerns which have suffered substantial economic injury because of excessive rainfall or drought conditions in their areas. 

In this 6-months period, the Small Business Administration received a record number of business loan applications 4, 981 requesting a total of $282. 8 million. The Agency also approved a record number of business loans 2, 628 for $119. 7 

1

million. These totals compare with 3, 200 applications for $190.7 million and 1, 501 loan approvals for $73.4 million in the same 

6 months of 1957.

Government purchases set aside for small business in this 6-months period by the Small Business Administration, the Department of Defense, and co-operating Federal civilian agencies totaled $428. 5 million. This was the greatest dollar amount of small business set-asides in the first half of any fiscal year since start of the Agency, and was almost $50 million more than the setasides in the first half of fiscal year 1958. The set-aside program also resulted in 10, 521 contract awards, totaling $316 million, to small business concerns in this reporting period, as compared with 5, 705 contract awards for $222 million in the same period of 1957.

Many activities of this Agency during the past 6 months were conducted in co-operation with other Government agencies, with the Small Business Administration serving as spokesman and advocate of small business.

ECONOMICS OF OF SMALL BUSINESS CHAPTER 

This chapter presents the most pertinent recent data concerning the economic position of small business. 

New Business Incorporations 

Business Failures 

The number of new business incorporations reached a record high of 150, 268 in 1958. The previous record was 140, 775 in 1956. (See page 8.) Although not all incorporations are of newly formed businesses some are of established businesses which formerly were operated as proprietorships or partnerships the number of incorporations is a significant indicator of the rate of formation of new businesses, particularly small ones. The record number of incorporations in 1958 indicates that despite a temporary slack in economic activity, businessmen have remained confident of the opportunities in small business. 

Business failures in 1958 totaled 14, 964, according to

Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. The 1957 total was 13, 739. After reaching a monthly peak of 1, 495 in March 1958, business failures began to decrease.

The number in the second half of the year was approximately the same as in the last half of 1957. 

The increase in failures in the past 2 years, and particularly after the decrease in economic activity in the second half of 1957, was concentrated in the retail trades (the automotive group, eating and drinking places, and furniture and home furnishings dealers) and the residential construction industry. In the manufacturing industries there were increases in the lumber and mill products field and in some metalworking industries. 

The improvement in residential construction activity in 1958, together with the improved outlook for residential construction and automobile sales in 1959, should result in a further decrease in failures in these lines.

Earnings Before and After Taxes

As was to be expected in view of the element of fixed costs in manufacturing operations, the decline in economic activity in the first three quarters of 1958 was reflected more in manufacturers' earnings than in their sales. Although the decline in sales in this period was relatively small, the decline in earnings of both small and large corporations was quite sharp. 

In the third quarter of the year, earnings before taxes of the smaller corporations totaled $409 million, compared with $472 million in the third quarter of 1957. In the first three quarters of 

1958, earnings before taxes of the smaller corporations dropped to $820 million from $1, 292 million in the same period of 1957. This was a drop of 37 per cent. By comparison, earnings of the larger corporations in the first three quarters decreased from $20, 817 million to $14, 786 million, a drop of 29 per cent.

The decrease in earnings after taxes was even sharper. 

Smaller corporations' earnings decreased from $678 million in the first three quarters of 1957 to $373 million in the same period of 1958, a decline of 45 per cent, while earnings of larger corporations decreased from $11, 230 million to $8, 249 million, a decrease of 27 per cent.

In the table on page 14, figures are presented for quarterly earnings, before and after taxes, by asset size of corporations, beginning with the first quarter of 1957. Annual earnings are shown

for 1951 through 1957. The chart on page 15 shows the trend of quarterly earnings by asset size before and after taxes from the first quarter of 1957 through the third quarter of 1958, and the annual trend for the years 1951 through 1957. 

Rate of Return on Stockholders' Equity 

Stockholders' equity in a corporation is comprised of capital stock, minority interest, earned surplus, surplus reserves, and that portion of other reserves which is considered surplus.

Earnings on stockholders' equity by asset size of corporations are presented in the table on page 16 for selected years during the period 1947 through 1957, and quarterly beginning with the first quarter of 1957. 

Before taxes, earnings on stockholders' equity in smaller manufacturing corporations in the first three quarters of 1958 averaged 11.1 per cent, compared with 18. 2 per cent in the same period of 1957. The rate of return for the larger manufacturing corporations decreased to an average of 14.5 per cent from 21. 3 per cent in 1957.

After taxes, earnings on stockholders' equity in smaller manufacturing corporations in the first three quarters of 1958 averaged 5 per cent, compared with an average of 9.5 per cent in the same period of 1957. In the case of the larger manufacturing corporations there was a decline from 11.5 per cent to 8. 1 per cent. 

As in the case of dollar earnings, the decline in economic activity has had a more pronounced effect on the rate of return on stockholders' equity in smaller manufacturing corporations than for the larger ones.

As shown by the chart on page 17, the annual rate of earnings on stockholders' equity in the period 1947 through 1958, was lower for the smaller manufacturing corporations than for the larger ones. This is due partly to the fact that the industries in which small manufacturing corporations predominate, such as the textile, apparel, lumber and furniture industry groups, are characterized by relatively lower earnings. On the other hand,

industry groups noted for large corporations, such as the aircraft, electrical machinery, instruments and drug fields, are characterized by relatively higher earnings. 

Military Procurement 

In the first 5 months of fiscal year 1959, military prime contract awards to small business were approximately $180 million higher than in the comparable period of fiscal year 1958, increasing to $1, 203 million from $1, 023 million. Although the dollar volume increased, the small business percentage was 16.8 compared to 17.2 per cent in the same period a year earlier and 

17.1 per cent for the entire fiscal year 1958.

This lower small business percentage was due to the increased awards for guided missiles systems and research and development and to the emergency procurement of petroleum in the Lebanon crisis.

As shown in the tables on pages 22 and 23, in fiscal year 1958 the net value of military procurement actions with small business was $3, 729 million, compared with $3, 783 million in fiscal year 1957. This is a decrease of $54 million. At the same time, the total net value of military procurement actions with all business firms for work in the United States increased to $21, 827 million from $19, 133 million. As a result, the small business percentage of overall military procurement declined to 17.1 per cent from 19.8 per "Net procurement" refers to "new procurement less contracts or modifications that decrease the amounts of obligations by $10,000 cent. 11 or more.

This fiscal 1958 decrease in the small business percentage was due almost wholly to a sharp increase in the procurement of aircraft and guided missiles systems. Procurement of aircraft increased from $5, 018 million in fiscal 1957 to $7, 416 million in fiscal 1958, or from 28. 1 to 36. 2 per cent of total military procurement actions of $10, 000 or more. Procurement of guided missiles systems increased from $1, 864 million to $2, 837 million, or from 

10. 4 to 13.8 per cent. However, the small business percentage of total aircraft procurement decreased to 1.7 per cent from 2. 2 per cent, and of guided missiles systems to 1.5 per cent from 1.9 per cent.

Military procurement of services last fiscal year decreased slightly to $2, 720 million from $2, 771 million in fiscal 1957, and the small business percentage of these purchases decreased to 8.3 per cent from 9.7 per cent. Experimental, development and research contracts account for about one-half of the procurement of services.

ADMINISTRATION OF LOAN PROGRAM The Small Business Administration is now servicing approximately 17,000 business and disaster loans, including loans which were made by the Reconstruction Finance Corporation and which have been transferred to this Agency for servicing. The rapid increase in the Agency's loan portfolio has added to the loan servicing problems. To meet these problems, the Agency's regional offices are giving increased attention to streamlining of procedures and training of personnel. Greater stress also is being placed on financial counseling and preventive measures to forestall loan delinquencies.

Agency financial specialists assigned to loan servicing are giving particular attention to counseling borrowers in matters affecting the improvement of the financial condition of their businesses; obtaining more frequent financial and operating statements from borrowers for careful examination and analysis; and visiting the borrowers' places of business or plants to inspect their facilities, equipment and operations. 

Since the Small Business Administration appropriation for the fiscal year 1959 was not enacted until August 27, 1958, the Agency was delayed in its efforts to recruit qualified loan servicing specialists as rapidly as they were needed to perform the necessary loan servicing functions. Every effort is being made to obtain the necessary staff as quickly as possible. 

BUSINESS LOANS PROGRAM Financial Counseling An essential part of the Small Business Administration 

Business Loans Program is counseling of small business owners and managers on their financial problems. This service is provided through financial specialists who have had long experience in credit and financial matters. 

When a small business concern asks the Agency for financial assistance, its problem is explored carefully by the Agency's financial specialists. Frequently, they are able to suggest alternatives to borrowing, such as changes in capitalization of the business, liquidation of outdated or surplus inventory, or modernization of sales methods. Where borrowing apparently is the solution, the specialists often are able to help the business obtain the loan from private lending sources. 

On some occasions, after the Small Business Administration processes a loan to the stage of closing and disbursement, a bank which originally declined the small concern's request agrees to make the loan. The Agency therefore cancels the loan authorization. This result, while producing no interest income to the Agency to offset the cost of processing the application, is considered in keeping with the mission of the Small Business Administration.

Bank Participation 

As shown in the chart on page 35, 58.3 per cent of the business loans approved by the Small Business Administration in the last 6-months period were in participation with banks. Although the ratio of participation loan approvals to total loan approvals decreased slightly, the percentage of deferred participation loans increased, reversing a downward trend which had prevailed since 1955.

Bank participation loans are mutually advantageous to the small business owner, the participating institution and the Government. A bank participation loan gives the small business borrower the opportunity to establish and maintain a satisfactory credit record with the bank a record which will be of prime importance to him in years to come. From the standpoint of the bank, the participation plan affords an opportunity to make a longer term -and sometimes a larger loan than otherwise would be possible. 

Further, it permits the bank to broaden its lending activity while maintaining liquidity, obtaining a fair return on money loaned, and providing increased service to its customers. From the standpoint of the Small Business Administration, the plan reduces loan servicing costs, requires fewer Government employees, lessens the drain on lending funds, and may make possible sale of an entire loan, when seasoned, to the participating bank. 

The legislation governing the Small Business Administration Financial Assistance Program does not fix the rate of interest on the bank's share of participation loans, and the bank's rate on its share of the loan may exceed the Agency's statutory rate of 5-1/2 per cent per annum, provided the rate requested by the bank is legal and reasonable.

CHAPTER IV ASSISTANCE UNDER THE SMALL BUSINESS INVESTMENT ACT 

On August 21, 1958, the Small Business Investment Act of 1958 (Public Law 85-699) was signed by the President. This 

Act provides a new avenue of financing for small business concerns. Under its terms the Small Business Administration will license small business investment companies, which will be privately owned and privately operated, and these companies, in turn, will provide equity-type capital and long-term loans to small business concerns. In addition, the Small Business Administration under the Act is granted new authority to assist State development companies and local development companies in the financing of small businesses in their areas. 

Under the provisions of the Act the Small Business Administration is authorized to buy subordinated debentures from the small business investment companies in order to assist in the creation of their initial operating capital. In addition, the Agency is authorized to provide further funds to encourage the formation and growth of the small business investment companies. The Agency's functions will include the regulation and supervision of such companies. Licensed small business investment companies are entitled to certain tax advantages under the 

Technical Amendments Act of 1958. 

Although the companies will be privately owned and operated, the Federal Government will have a considerable stake in them since the Small Business Administration will bear a responsibility in licensing them and also may make a financial contribution to their operations, whether by purchase of the subordinated debentures for the initial operating capital or by further funds. The Small Business Administration must see to it that this money in the hands of these private credit institutions is channeled into small business enterprises and that it is not diverted into uses inconsistent with the purposes of the Act. 61

With these objectives in mind, proposed regulations were framed and published in the Federal Register. Since this is a pioneering effort in the field, comments were invited from all concerned. Agency officials consulted with or obtained comments from professional societies, such as the American Bar Association and the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants, associations of small businessmen, members of Congress, State officials and others. Final regulations were issued on December 4, 1958, slightly over 3 months after enactment of the law. 

It was then announced that applications for licenses would be accepted.

The regulations issued by the Small Business Administration emphasize the great importance of sound management of investment companies to insure accomplishment of the aims of the Act. In the short time remaining in 1958, after the regulations were published, a number of applications were received by the Small Business Administration and given careful review. 

The first of a series of public meetings was held in New 

York City on December 1 and 2, at which Senator John J. Sparkman,

Chairman of the Senate Small Business Committee, staff members of Congressional committees, representatives of private investment companies and commercial banks, and officials of the Securities and Exchange Commission, Internal Revenue Service and the Small 

Business Administration discussed the Small Business Investment 

Act and the regulations. The proceedings of this meeting have been published by the Senate Banking and Currency Committee as a 

Senate document.

Under Title V of the Act, a number of applications for loans have been received from local development companies. One loan was approved prior to the end of 1958 and other applications are being processed. Preliminary negotiations were in progress with officials of several State development companies with regard to loans under Section 501.

For the purposes of the Act, a $250 million addition to the Small Business Administration Revolving Fund was authorized by the Congress. Of this amount, $50 million was appropriated for the first fiscal year of operations under the Act.

How the Program Works 

As the basis for the Joint Set-Aside Program, the Small Business Administration has assigned representatives to many of the major Government purchasing offices. There, in co-operation with procurement officials, they screen proposed purchases, determine whether small firms can supply the needed items or services, and evaluate the extent of small business competition for such contracts in the past. When an Agency representative finds that small concerns can and will bid on a purchase, he recommends that all or part of it be set aside for competitive award to small business concerns. 

In some instances, as discussed later in this chapter, setasides are made possible through action of the Small Business Administration in locating additional competent small firms interested in bidding on the proposed purchases.

The decision to set aside a purchase for award to small business must be made jointly by the Small Business Administration and the purchasing agency involved the Small Business Administration cannot act alone in the matter. 

However, as a result of a change in the Small Business Act made by the last Congress, the Small Business Administration may appeal rejection of a proposed set-aside to the Secretary or head of the Government agency concerned. SMALL BUSINESS ADMINISTRATION

When an entire purchase is set aside for exclusive award to small business, bidding for the contract is limited to firms which qualify as small. If a large concern submits a bid, the bid is considered "nonresponsive," as not meeting the stipulated requirements.

Where only part of a proposed purchase is set aside for award to small business, a small firm, to be considered in negotiation of a contract for the set-aside share, must also bid on the unreserved portion of the purchase, that is, the part that is open to all bidders, large and small. Furthermore, its bid on the unreserved portion must be within 120 per cent of the highest unit price at which an award is made on that portion. 

In negotiating with small firms for the set-aside share of the purchase, the purchasing office starts with the one which submitted the lowest bid on the unreserved portion. 

The Small Business Administration's set-aside program is designed to assure that suitable purchases will be earmarked in whole or in part for exclusive award to small business. The pro- gram broadens the procurement base for the Government by encouraging small business participation in Government contracts. 

Legislative Changes to Strengthen Program 

Three changes in the Small Business Act made by the Congress this period should increase the effectiveness of the Joint 

Set-Aside Program. 

The first of these established an additional basis for small business set-asides, that they can be made "in the interest of assuring that a fair proportion of the total purchases and contracts for property and services for the Government are placed with small-business concerns." Formerly, small business setasides could be made only "in the interest of maintaining or mobi. lizing the Nation's full productive capacity," or "in the interest of war or national defense programs."

The second legislative change authorized the setting aside for small business of classes of awards or contracts. Formerly, set-asides could be made only on individual procurement actions.

The third change, referred to previously, provides that whenever the Small Business Administration and a purchasing agency fail to agree on a proposed set-aside, the matter shall be submitted for determination to the Secretary or head of the Department or agency by the Administrator of the Small Business 

Administration. 

The Small Business Administration has acted promptly to place these legislative changes in effect. 

The first change, broadening the basis for small business set-asides, was put in force by the Small Business Administration and co-operating purchasing agencies immediately after the President signed the amended Small Business Act. 

The provision for class set-asides has been placed in effect recently. For instance, the first class set-aside was made by the Post Office Department and the Small Business Administration on October 7, 1958. Since then class set-asides also have been made with the other co-operating purchasing agencies. 

The provision for submittal of disagreements to the Secretary or head of a procuring agency also has been made effective by the military services and the Federal civilian agencies which co-operate in the Joint Set-Aside Program. 

Guiding Small Firms to Areas Where Competition Is Needed 

A Small Business Administration program of great benefit to small business is that of increasing small business competition on Government purchases that in the past have involved few or no bids from small business concerns. 

When a Small Business Administration representative at a purchasing office screens a procurement for possible setaside action, he considers carefully the record of previous purchases, including the extent of small business competition. When he finds that an item or service can be supplied by small business firms, but that there has been little or no competition from them, he arranges for Small Business Administration field offices to locate competent small business suppliers that want to bid on the purchase.

This action increases the number of small business bidders, broadens the procurement base, and frequently results in a better price for the Government because of greater competition. 

It also adds to the variety of items and services on which small business set-asides may be made. 

In screening a recent proposed purchase of warehouse refrigeration units, the Small Business Administration representative at a military purchasing installation found that small firms could supply the units, but that small firms had never bid on previous purchases. Instead, the item always had been supplied in the past by large firms. 

In order to find small business suppliers for the immediate purchase and to establish a basis for future joint set-asides for small business, the Small Business Administration representative asked the Agency's regional offices to supply names of small businesses capable of producing refrigeration units of the type desired. Copies of the bid form were sent to each small business firm located by this survey. One of these, a small California firm, was the low bidder and received a contract for $205, 224. Thus, the co-ordinated effort of the Small Business Administration field office enabled a small firm to obtain a sizable contract and at the same time established a basis for future set-asides of the item. 

As an example, here are some items on which the Agency was able to obtain set-asides during this period by locating competent small firms to bid on the purchases: Roller conveyors; transformers; production of motion picture film; battery chargers; fluorescent light fixtures; steam and hot water generators; engine lathes; safety toe lasts and patterns; machete sheaths; vacuum cleaners; sprinkler system; plastic correlation tables; guard services; engine hoists; gas heated steam tables; window shades; halyards; steam whistles; air cylinders; torpedo loading trays; plastic sheets; pumping assembly; dynamotors; fire trucks; soldering irons; repair and overhaul of civilian aircraft; architectural engineering services; electrical construction; and rope.

Certificates of Competency The Small Business Administration is authorized by law to certify that a small business concern, small business production pool, or group of small firms jointly engaged in research and development, has the necessary financial and technical ability to carry out the terms of a particular Government contract. Through this authority to issue "certificates of competency," the Agency offers what is in effect an appeals procedure for the small firm, pool, or group of small firms, whose low bid on a Government purchase, or high bid on a Government sale, is rejected for reasons of financial ability or productive capability. 

By law, a Government contracting officer must accept a certificate of competency issued by the Agency as conclusive without requiring the small business concern, pool, or group of firms to meet any other requirements as to financial ability and productive capacity.

Certificates of competency issued in this period related to Government purchases amounting to $8, 483, 347; those issued in the last half of 1957 related to purchases valued at $5, 452, 601. The 

Agency received 86 applications for certificates and issued 49 certificates, as shown in the table on page 81. By comparison, in the same period of 1957 the Agency received 92 applications and issued 40 certificates of competency.

many instances, spade work accomplished by Small Business Adminis tration field offices has resulted in contract awards without the necessity of the issuance of certificates.

Production Assistance 

Small manufacturers, and especially job shops, which specialize in certain lines, often find that their products or services have become weak in competitive fields or are not producing a profit. In these cases, Agency technical specialists are available to assist with the problems and to help the firms adopt sound business and operating practices. A few typical cases are cited: 

1. A small midwestern manufacturer was seeking a chemical in powder or crystal form, to be soluble in water, to substitute for hydrochloric acid in a water closet cleaner. Small Business

Administration field personnel located a suitable material -- a granular sodium bisulfate. 

2. A small manufacturer received, through a Small Business Administration certificate of competency, a contract for floor lamps with shades having a brass-strip edging. His original method of applying the strip required vertical cutting to permit bending, then crimping to the shade, thus involving two separate handlings. A simple die, suggested by a Small Business Administration production specialist, reduced this work to a single operation and thereby reduced production time and handling time by half. Prior to the suggested change, it appeared that the manufacturer would fail to meet delivery schedules. Operational time saved by the Agency's suggested change returned the contract to schedule. 

3. A New York manufacturer was not meeting delivery schedules on Government contracts. Small Business Administration production specialists checked the contractor's plant and operations with a view to helping him overcome his difficulties. They found

that the plant was "cluttered," one reason being that various parts needed in production were stored in several places, instead of being readily available to the assembly line. The Agency specialists suggested improved methods of inventory storage and an inventory control system. These suggestions were accepted by the contractor, and subsequent progress checks showed that production had improved. 

4. A northwestern inventor was considering making the body of a new tool from forgings. He discussed this with a Small 

Business Administration field engineer, who suggested a design based on the use of steel stampings. This resulted in a reduced tooling and production cost. 

5. A small manufacturer asked the Agency's advice on ways to mass produce, at a reduced cost, a wire clip for attaching wire fencing to steel posts. A Small Business Administration field engineer suggested several possibilities. As a result a die arrangement for attachment to a punch press was worked out. It included a wire straightener, feed attachment, a shear and a forming die, and proved very efficient.

Inventory of Small Plant Facilities 

The Small Business Administration maintains an inventory of small business productive facilities on a decentralized basis in its field offices. Companies which have registered in the inventory are carefully classified as to capacity, machine tools, number of employees, types of products, and other essential production factors. 

Through this information the Agency can readily evaluate a manufacturer's industrial potential, including the kinds of production for which his facilities are now suited, and the types to which the facilities can be converted in case of full mobilization for defense. 

The inventory is now used principally in assisting the registered companies to obtain prime contracts and subcontracts, in locating new suppliers for the military services, in providing needed data for the Damage Assessment Program directed by the

Over 39,000 small plants are registered with the Small Business Administration, and the Agency is seeking to enlarge this

The completed manuscript is printed by the Government Printing Office and sold by the Superintendent of Documents at a nominal price. 

The most recent volume in this series is Profitable Small Plant Layout, which is 48 pages in length and sells for 25 cents.

Starting and Managing Series 

This, the newest of the series, has these distinctive features: The books are designed chiefly for prospective enterprisers, and individual lines of business are discussed. Starting Two books are currently in preparation and Managing a Small Business of Your Own and Starting and Managing a Small Credit Bureau and Collection Service and are scheduled for publication during 1959.

Management Development 

Like the Small Business Management Series, these booklets will be printed by the Government Printing Office and sold by the Superintendent of Documents.

The Small Business Administration's Management 

Development Program is largely one of cosponsorship, by educational institutions and the Agency, of administrative management courses, seminars, institutes and clinics for small business owners and managers. 

The educational institutions assume educational and financial responsibility for the courses, with the Small Business Administration furnishing administrative guidance and published materials.

In the 6 months which ended December 31, 1958, the Agency and 45 educational institutions cosponsored 58 courses attended by nearly 1,500 owners and managers of small business firms. On a cumulative basis, 577 administrative management courses have been given since they were first started 4-1/2 years ago and about 15, 000 small businessmen have attended the courses.

In October 1958 a new edition of Administrative Manage ment Courses for Small Business Owners was issued. This publication is a guide for educational institutions in establishing and conducting small business management development programs.

Management Counseling and Conferences 

This program, carried on by both the Washington and field offices of the Small Business Administration, is designed to provide assistance to prospective small business operators in need of information and guidance and to small businessmen who have a particular management problem. 

Each month hundreds of businessmen make use of this free service.

The Small Business Administration also seeks to improve the management of small firms by participating in conferences which cover some phase of business management and which afford an opportunity for the Agency to make known its services to additional small business concerns. Typical sponsoring groups include educational institutions, banks, chambers of commerce, and trade associations. 

The publication of Small Business Bulletins is another part of the Agency's counseling service. These are 2 to 16-page leaflets, usually of a bibliographical nature, which deal with some particular kind of business or business activity. Two bulletins have been issued, one is now being printed, and 12 are in preparation.
