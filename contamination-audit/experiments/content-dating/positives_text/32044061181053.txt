The President's Letter of Transmittal To the Congress of the United States: 

Transmitted herewith is the Seventeenth Semiannual Report on the operation of the Mutual Security Program for the period ending December 31, 1959. The report was prepared under the direction of the Coordinator for the Mutual Security Program by the Department of State (including the International Cooperation Administration), the Department of Defense, and the Development Loan Fund. 

In the decade of the 1960's our country will face new and somewhat different problems in the mutual security area. For example, the divergence in living standards between the Western democracies and the underdeveloped countries poses a growing problem to our purpose of maintaining and extending free political and economic institutions. 

It also presents an increasingly acute need for development assistance. 

Our friends and allies in Europe are also aware of this need, and as their ability to provide development assistance grows, we are increasingly consulting with them to determine how the burden can most efficiently be shared by all.

The growing need for development assistance demands of our Mutual Security Program the utmost prudence and foresight in the management of its resources for such purposes. I am accordingly convinced that our program must be flexible, as well as strong, so that we may be able to deal with this and other problems that arise quickly, wisely and successfully.

THE WHITE HOUSE, 

May 2, 1960. 

Dung Lisenhow

PART I 

Chapter 1 Operations of the Program Operations of the Mutual Security Program (MSP) during the first half of Fiscal Year 1960 were carried out in an atmosphere of relative world calm; the last 6 months of 1959 was one of the least troubled periods in the decade of the 1950's. In terms of international "incidents," the world appeared almost quiet, except for the final, overt act of aggression by the Chinese Communists against Tibet and the Chinese Communist usurpation of Indian territory and maltreatment of Indian personnel in the Northeast frontier area. 

Barely under the surface calm, however, large areas of the world were far from quiet; in the emergent nations of Africa and Asia, and 

Latin America, pressure continued to mount for a better life. In the new nations of Africa, and those territories about to become nations, the primary emphasis now is on independence. At the same time there is deep preoccupation with economic and social development. 

In other countries the pressure may be for land ownership or better housing, more food or other material things, or possibly a restless, unfocused drive toward "something better." This growing realization of hundreds of millions of people that hunger, poverty, and lisease need not be their inevitable lot represents an emerging force which will probably become the most significant single social factor of the decade of the 60's.

This mighty surging movement in the underdeveloped countries seldom makes for headlines and so far rarely for international "incidents." Yet its implications for the decade we are now in are tremendous; not only in the case of the awakening giants-India and 

Communist China-but the whole continents of Africa, South Amerca, the vast and diverse areas of the Near East and South Asia, and he Far East.

The people of these vast reaches of the earth are caught up in the 'revolution of rising expectations"; they are determined to have social nd economic progress, and they want it without delay. They want o compress into a few years the hard and sometimes painful economic nd social evolution that took the industrialized nations more than a entury to achieve.

It is evident that there is much to be gained by coordinating the growing aid and development efforts of the Western European nations and Japan-of which the preceding paragraphs give only examples with the efforts of the United States. Eight members of the Organization for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC), as capital exporters in a position to provide significant bilateral assistance to less developed areas over and above their contributions to multilateral organizations, have formed themselves into the "Development Assistance Group" which met at Washington in March to discuss problems in the field of capital lending. Japan has also become a member of the group.

All of these efforts and others now taking place-are badly needed if we are to help the less developed countries to achieve a rate of social and economic progress that will inspire in them hope for the fulfillment of their aspirations.

It has become increasingly clear that the Western industrial nations must make available to the less developed countries the knowledge, advice, and capital to allow economic development within the framework of a free society. If they do not do so, the political and economic influence of the Communist system will be firmly established over whole continents, where the fateful first steps of political and economic development are now being taken. 

Where those steps lead-whether it will be development within the framework of free institutions or development under political and economic tyranny-will affect our society for generations. Some time igo, Mr. Khrushchev threatened "to bury the United States" in what he called "friendly" economic competition between capitalism and socialism. One of the principal battlefields will be the underdeveloped nations of the world; the Soviet offensive is already well underway o persuade these countries that communism is a short and easy path

to economic advancement. It behooves the Western democracies to demonstrate by their aid and encouragement that economic advance ment can be achieved without putting on the straitjacket of political and economic tyranny.

Program Development 

Determining the need of a foreign country for U.S. military and economic aid, the appropriate dimension, and the most effective form of such aid is a difficult and complex matter at best and was no easier for the FY 1960 programs than in other years. For FY 1960 programs, the determination of need started with a study of U.S. interests in the country or the area. It progressed to an analysis of the total resources available to the country in relation to its requirements, a determination as to whether providing assistance would advance U.S. interests, and thereafter, of the nature and extent of the need for outside aid. Thorough consideration was given to possibilities of aid from third countries and from international institutions. If, after allow ing for aid from other sources, a need for U.S. aid was found to exist, the next step was to evaluate how best to deal with the needwhether by one or a combination of the instruments available under MSP or through other instruments of U.S. foreign economic policy, such as local currency sales of surplus agricultural commodities under P.L. 480 or loans by the Export-Import Bank (Eximbank). Wherever it was determined that U.S. private investment could and would do the job, the mutual security programs were confined to providing advice, plus support in the form of investment guaranties for investors found willing to take capital into underdeveloped areas. 

Of particular importance to the overall Mutual Security Program is the relationship of military assistance to economic and political development. In the FY 1960 programs attempts have been made to balance these factors in a way which would not overtax the economies

of underdeveloped nations and at the same time insure the existence of forces necessary to maintain internal security and to cope with limited external aggression until help could arrive. This is realistic acknowledgment that there is no more sense in a militarily strong but economically and politically bankrupt nation than there is to a prosperous but defenseless democratic state which would fall easy prey to subversion and military threats. 

An important byproduct of military assistance, which was recognized for the first time in new amendments to the Mutual Security Act and emphasized in the FY 1960 programs, has been the local forces supported and trained with military assistance which can be and are being used in construction of public works and local development projects when not required for purely military missions. Many of the 

I skills involved in modern military technology are for the most part I readily adaptable to civilian needs, and the military personnel trained under the Military Assistance Program (MAP) auspices contribute these skills to the local economy upon their return to civilian life. In many underdeveloped countries this increment of training and knowledge is an important addition to the painfully meager pool of citizens possessing skills useful in a developing society. 

These were the basic steps taken in arriving at country plans, each element complementing and reinforcing every other element to the greatest degree possible. A plan was made for every country where 

U.S. interests required that economic or military aid be provided. 

I Those countries where the need for aid was most pressing were in many cases the most primitive; the problem of planning programs of t economic aid in the absence of even reasonably accurate basic data about a country was an immense one. This lack of reliable information sometimes extended even to population figures, basic resources, 1 and rudimentary geographic data. In this light, it is easy to see why the report of the Draper Committee on the Mutual Security Program said that there is no more difficult administrative undertaking in the U.S. Government than the management of the various economic assistance programs.

The implementation of these programs will be described in detail in the semiannual report to the Congress at the end of the fiscal year. 

This report is confined to the operation of the Mutual Security Program for the first half of FY 1960. In addition a new section has been added to this report on an experimental basis (chapter 17) covering certain major problems that have now, or will have in the fairly near future, a significant effect on MSP, which the Congress will have to consider in dealing with MSP in future years. This report is designed to define the nature of these problems and suggest their possible future dimensions. No attempt has been made to be comprehensive or detailed in presenting them nor has any attempt been made to provide solutions for them.

Chapter New Provisions of the Mutual Security Legislation 

Most significant of the new amendments to the Mutual Security Act of 1954, as amended, is the revised statement of policy which puts greater emphasis on the mutuality of interest between the people of the United States and other peoples, on the dignity and interdependence of men, on expanded freedom, and on economic, political, and social development. Further, it favors the use of assistance to promote the emergence of political units which are economically viable, either alone or in cooperation with neighboring units. 

The military assistance provisions of the act were modified in some respects. Section 103 (a) was amended to provide that beginning in FY 1961, the military assistance programs be budgeted to compete with other Defense Department programs for financial support in the defense budget. This means in effect that Military Assistance Program (MAP) requests will have to establish their importance to the security of the United States in direct competition with U.S. Army, Navy, and Air Force budget requests. The authorization for military assistance appropriations for Fiscal Years 1961 and 1962 was provided at the same time. Section 105 (b) (4) was amended to place new restrictions on the furnishing of military equipment and materials to 

Latin America. 

• In significant new legislation dealing with the Development Loan Fund (DLF), section 202(b) of the act provides guidance in the assignment of loan priorities. By section 202(b), DLF was directed to recognize that its assistance would be most effective in certain countries. In effect, priority was assigned for loans to countries which are responsive to long-range economic, political, and social aspirations of their peoples, which demonstrate willingness to take self-help measures and which demonstrate consistency of such assistance with their long-term development objectives. • The fractional reserve which DLF may maintain for any guar anty which it may issue was limited to not less than 50 percent of the DLF contractual liability under such guaranty, and the total contractual liability of DLF under all such guaranties at any one time was limited to no more than $100 million. Section 517 of the act, which sets up certain planning requirements as prerequisites to obligations in amounts over $100,000 for assistance authorized under defense support, technical cooperation, and special assistance, was extended to 

DLF operations for the first time.

• The Investment Guaranty Program, as its name implies, is designed to insure American private investment abroad against what can be termed noneconomic eventualities-expropriation, etc. Section 

413(b) (4) was amended to limit investment guaranties to underdeveloped areas only. • An important new section, 503 (c), was added to the act, requiring that during the FY 1961 mutual security presentation there shall be included a specific plan for each country receiving grant special assistance or defense support under which, wherever practicable, the grant assistance shall be progressively reduced and terminated. • A number of provisions in both the authorization and appropriation acts were in response to various criticisms of the administration of the program. One of these provisions created the Office of the 

Inspector General and Comptroller, Mutual Security (section 533A). 

The Office of Inspector General and Comptroller was assigned the functions formerly carried out by the International Cooperation Administration's (ICA) Office of Evaluation and certain functions formerly carried out by ICA's Office of Personnel, Security, and Integrity. In addition, the Office was charged with other specific "duties related to financial management and adequacy of program performance. They include financial and statistical reporting, internal audit, and inspections with respect to end-item use. • Three provisions in the Mutual Security Act and one in the appropriation act require fuller disclosure of information concerning the 

Mutual Security Program by the executive branch. These sections are numbers 533A (d), 534 (b), and 550 of the Mutual Security Act of 1954, as amended, and section 111(d) of the Mutual Security Appropriation Act, 1960. In signing the Mutual Security Act of 1959, the President stated that such provisions would not be considered to Talter and could not alter the recognized constitutional duty and power of the Executive with respect to the disclosure of information, documents, and other materials. The President made it perfectly plain, however, that the principle of nondisclosure did not apply to factual information but primarily to expressions of opinion within the executive branch. In the case of the fourth provision, the President is expressly authorized to certify that he has forbidden the furnishing of particular material to Congress or the General Accounting Office. • The Mutual Security Appropriation Act, 1960, among other things, requires that the respective appropriations committees of the Senate and House shall receive within 60 days following enactment a report containing a full and complete revision of the data presented to those committees in justification of the FY 1960 Mutual Security 

Program appropriation request (section 111(a)). Section 111(b) requires followup reports within 30 days following approval of significant program changes.

Chapter Office of the Inspector General and 

Comptroller

By new legislation dated July 24, 1959, there was established within the Department of State an Office of Inspector General and Comptroller reporting directly to the Under Secretary of State. 

The purpose of the new section 533A as stated in a report to the House Committee on Foreign Affairs was "to give a single individual with the necessary supporting personnel, a sufficiently high place in the executive hierarchy, sufficient independence from control by operating officials, together with sufficient authority and money to assure that he will be able to detect shortcomings in the mutual security program and to make recommendations for correction which cannot be disregarded by those administering the program . . ." 

The House Conference Report had the following to say concerning this new office: "The most serious questions in the minds of the 

Congress and the public today concerning the mutual security program no longer relate to whether or not the program should exist, but to whether it is being or can be effectively administered. The 

Inspector General and Comptroller can and should do much to provide reassurance on this subject. He can do his job only if he is fully informed of the facts and if the various operating officials do what they can to facilitate his work." 

The act, as amended, transferred to the Inspector General and Comptroller all functions, powers, and duties of the Office of Evaluations of the International Cooperation Administration and so much of the functions, powers, and duties of the Office of Personnel Security and Integrity as relate to investigations of improper activities in connection with programs under the International Cooperation Administration. In addition, the law provides broad responsibility for establishing systems of financial controls, reports, and audits relating to all mutual security programs. 

The Committee of Conference gave particular consideration to the relationship between the Office of Inspector General and Comptroller, the Department of State, and the Department of Defense with regard to the Military Assistance Program. The Committee of Conference emphasized that the Inspector General and Comptroller should not set up an organization to duplicate the work being done within the Department of Defense. At the same time, however, the Committee clearly desired an independent review and approval of military assistance administration and control through the Office of the Inspector

PART II 

Since the tenth anniversary meeting of the North Atlantic Council in ministerial session in early April 1959, two major developments have taken place in the free world's relations with the Soviet bloc. 

At the Foreign Ministers meeting at Geneva during the spring and summer, France, the United Kingdom, and the United States advanced the comprehensive Western peace plan designed to achieve an equitable and just solution to the problem of Germany, whose unnatural division continues to be a source of tension in central Europe. The Soviet 

Union refused to enter into constructive discussions on this proposal. 

The Soviet Union also refused to agree to an interim arrangement on Berlin which would safeguard the freedom and security of the people of West Berlin and their links with the free world.

Immediately after his visit to the United States in September, Mr. 

Khrushchev withdrew the time limitation which he had previously imposed for negotiations on Berlin. Although thus retreating from the threatening position he had taken in November 1958, he continued to make it clear on every occasion that the aim of communism continues to be the achievement of world domination by all feasible means. 

The assumption that the world is to remain divided into opposing blocs was clearly implicit in his call for "peaceful co-existence." 

Secretary Herter summed up the meaning of these developments for

ANATO and the free world when he said on his arrival at Paris for the NATO Council meeting on December 13:

In considering the international situation, our assessment should be a realistic one.

The sense of crisis appears to have lessened of late. We welcome this, but it remains to be seen whether the Soviet Union is prepared to negotiate seriously to reach equitable settlements of major issues. It would be a grave error to base our plans for the future on a more optimistic appraisal than is warranted by the facts.

The Council's semiannual review of the international situation revealed wide and general agreement with the U.S. view that member governments must be most vigilant lest public opinion develop exaggerated hopes as the Western nations pursue their negotiations with the Soviets. On the other hand, the Soviet Union should not be given the opportunity to place the onus of any failure of these negotiations on the Western Powers.

NATO's defense posture in terms of manpower, training, equipment, organization, and weapons has improved consistently over the years, 11

particularly as regards the availability of modern weapons in which very substantial progress has been made in the past 2 years. 

The collective strength of the North Atlantic Alliance and its basic unity of purpose are not only the means of safeguarding the security and freedom of over 470 million people but also the foundation on which the free world can base its future efforts to reach agreements with the Soviet Union on major international issues. 

Economic aid to Europe has virtually terminated, with only Spain, Yugoslavia, Iceland, and Berlin left as recipients. The Greek aid program is described in the chapter on the Near East and South Asia. 

Much of Europe has turned to the role of donor of aid to underdeveloped countries. As was suggested in the introductory chapter of this report, Western Europe is taking an increasingly vigorous role in seeking a solution to the problem of development aid. 

There has been a significant increase in the defense expenditures of our NATO partners, reflecting the increasing economic stability and prosperity of Europe. The total defense expenditures of European 

NATO countries rose from $12.2 billion in 1958 to $13.6 billion in 

1959, an increase of more than 11 percent. The Netherlands and Italy have announced increases in their defense budget for next year of 9 percent and 4 percent respectively. The United Kingdom will increase its defense budget for 1960-61 by 9 percent, or $322 million. 

German defense expenditures increased from $1.6 billion in 1958 to $2.7 billion in 1959 and are expected to be higher in 1960. However, despite this concrete commitment to do more, many of our allies in the developed countries will be unable to meet entirely modernization requirements in FY 1961 which have been determined in common to be essential to maintain the capacity of the NATO alliance to resist aggression.

During the last 6 months of calendar year 1959, with the assistance of the Military Assistance Program (MAP), the NATO countries made further progress in incorporating modern weapons into the forces of NATO's European shield; advanced weapons accounted for an increasing proportion of materiel programed and delivered under MAP, with a corresponding decrease in the amount of conventional equipment provided.

In the United Kingdom, satisfactory progress has been made in the Thor program. Delivery of missiles to the Royal Air Force (RAF) has been on schedule, and earlier delays in missile site construction and installation of ground equipment have been made up for. During this period the British Government announced that the Thor “is able to take its place as the operational front-line of the Royal Air Force." 122

In Italy, construction to meet the intermediate range ballistic missile (IRBM) operational dates is on schedule, and operational missiles to strengthen the Italian forces committed to NATO are being provided according to plan. The Italian Government which has increased its defense budget, will concentrate its primary effort on meeting its NATO commitments, the fulfillment of which will provide a substantial force for the protection of NATO's southern flank. Italian construction of new naval vessels is proceeding on schedule. 

The Federal Republic of Germany continues to pay its own way in meeting its requirements for arms and materiel. German armed forces are receiving modern equipment in an increasing amount through the Mutual Security Military Sales Program, including advanced weapons such as Honest John and Nike missiles. Germany has assisted the NATO common defense effort by purchasing conventional arms from neighboring European countries, thus strengthening the European production and mobilization base. Germany also will produce modern F-104 aircraft to meet its own and possibly other 

European requirements.

Honest John tactical missiles for NATO ground forces were delivered to many of our European allies, and Nike missile battalions were installed during the last half of 1959 in Norway, Denmark, and Belgium, thus strengthening the air defense capabilities of NATO on its northern flank. Denmark and Norway are participants in the joint 

NATO program for the coordinated production in Europe of the Sidewinder missile system, and Italy and Belgium are participating in the Hawk coordinated production program. 

Military assistance provided to Spain has strengthened the capability of the Spanish armed forces to contribute to the defense of U.S. bases located in that country and has added to the defensive strength of Spain and Western Europe as a whole. 

The Military Assistance Program for Portugal has supported the antisubmarine warfare mission to which the Portuguese Government is giving primary emphasis. 

Pursuant to congressional authority granted in the 1958 Ship Loan Bill, a number of destroyers and submarines were delivered to NATO countries.

In addition to the Hawk and Sidewinder coordinated production programs already mentioned, arrangements have been made for production in Europe of maritime patrol aircraft for antisubmarine warfare. A cost-sharing shipbuilding program is underway in Denmark which will add 23 vessels to the Danish NATO fleet. Discussions are being held with the Norwegians on a cost-sharing shipbuilding program for the modernization of the Norwegian Navy and the bolstering of the Norwegian shipbuilding potential. Africa As Africa becomes increasingly a continent of newly independent d nations, the magnitude of the political and economic problems these nations face is brought more and more forcefully, to mind. Seven i new African nations have come into being since the end of World War II, and by October of 1960, there will be five more-Cameroun, Togo, Somalia, Congo, and Nigeria. The Federation of Mali (Senegal and the French Soudan) and the Malagasy Republic (Madagascar) may also become independent during 1960. By the end of 1960 more than 100 million Africans will be members of self-governing nations, free to act in their own interests. Yet the limitations that presently inhibit African development are immense. The average rate of literacy throughout Africa is about 10 percent. Basic technological advances such as the wheel are not used in many parts of Africa. In effect, the bulk of the population of Africa is not acquainted with modern economic life. 

Mutual Security Activities 

It is readily apparent that the people of Africa need to be able to find support from the free world in their drive for political expression and economic betterment. The most pressing needs of the new and emerging nations are first, administrative, managerial, and technical skills and second, capital for development projects. The United 

States plays an important role, through training, guidance, and aid to educational institutions, in meeting the first need. Capital for investment is available from a variety of sources, including the former colonial powers, the World Bank, and private investors, supplemented in appropriate instances by loans from the Export-Import Bank 

(Eximbank) and the Development Loan Fund (DLF). 

In the technical cooperation programs there has been an increasing emphasis on assistance to the countries of tropical Africa. There will be an increase of some $5.4 million in funds available for technical cooperation programs throughout Africa-from $15.5 million in 

FY 1959 to $20.9 million in FY 1960. All but $.9 million of this increase will be used to expand project activities in countries South of the Sahara. A small program will be initiated in Guinea and increases will occur in Ethiopia and Nigeria. 

Throughout West Africa there is preoccupation with the coming of political independence. But there is also concern with economic and social development, and it is in this context that the expansion of the Technical Cooperation Program (TC) is planned. In Nigeria, 15

the TC Program in the current year centers upon education and agriculture and in terms of money will be approximately 50 percent larger than the program of last year. In Ghana and Liberia the training of personnel and the development of educational and agricultural institutions continues. The pattern of the American contribution to these countries varies, of course, in response to local conditions, but generally support is provided for the establishment and expansion of local educational or agricultural institutions. This is done by providing advisers, support in training the personnel required, and on occasion equipment and materials incidental to demonstration and training. In Nigeria, for example, over half the program will support educational programs; and one-quarter, agricultural projects. 

With the establishment in July 1959 of a small liaison office in Nairobi, a broadening of activity, again with emphasis upon agriculture and education, is planned in Uganda, Tanganyika, and Kenya.

Near East and South Asia

This extensive area is not a clear-cut geographic area like the North or South American continents. It extends from Greece through the tier of free countries on the Asian land mass and ends in the east with East Pakistan, including on the way the Arabian Peninsula and Ceylon. Its problems and its potential strengths are as diverse as its area is vast.

The Near East 

Most of the underlying factors making for instability in the Near East remained unresolved during the first half of FY 1960, although the area continued to enjoy a period of relative calm. There were fewer overt evidences of the tension between the Arab States and Israel than has frequently been the case in the past even though the basic issues between them moved no closer to settlement. Reestablishment of diplomatic relations between Jordan and the United Arab Republic and an agreement on division of Nile waters between Sudan and the United Arab Republic were encouraging steps forward, indicative of the improved atmosphere among most of the Arab countries. Continued misunderstanding prevailed between Iraq and its Arab neighbors. Progress was made toward more normal relations between the 

United Arab Republic and the Western countries, aided by the increased interest of the U.A.R. in greater support of its developmental activities by the United States. The reestablishment of diplomatic relations between the United Arab Republic and the United Kingdom also marked an improvement in relations with the West. 

Soviet-bloc efforts to expand its influence in the area continued. No large new programs of military or economic assistance were begun by the Soviet bloc, but fulfillment of existing commitments to Yemen, Iraq, and the United Arab Republic went forward. In January 1960 work on the Aswan Dam was formally begun, as a result of Soviet commitments to provide the necessary aid. The Soviet Union has now agreed to provide credits totaling $387 million for the two construction stages of this dam, around which centers so much of Egypt's aspirations. 

Programs Underway

Mutual Security Program efforts during the last 6 months of 1959 were concentrated principally on carrying on programs undertaken earlier. Technical assistance missions continued in Israel, Lebanon, Jordan, and Sudan, and, on a limited basis, in Iraq. 19

A request by the Government of the United Arab Republic, for assistance in arranging educational opportunities in this country for about 200 students and the dispatch of a number of university teachers from the United States to teach in U.A.R. institutions was handled promptly and successfully.

Sudan's foreign exchange position, which had deteriorated to a dangerously low level in 1958, was improved because of increased sales of its cotton and recovery of world cotton prices. It was therefore possible to reduce the special assistance aid provided below the FY 1959 level. Significant economic development projects were continued, including projects aimed at crop diversification. The Sudan has a one-crop economy (cotton); therefore, diversification is highly important for its future economic health.

The Mutual Security Program for Greece, Turkey, and Iran during 

FY 1960, as in past years, is directed primarily toward enabling the three Governments concerned to meet their defense commitments. 

Owing to the complexity of the politico-economic and social structures of the three countries, virtually all of the various types of aid have been employed, including technical cooperation, direct military aid, defense support, and loans. Very real progress has been made, although in Greece a widening gap is appearing between the resources required to support the desired level of defense effort and those available, and in Iran there is danger of inflation.

From a position, in 1958, of financial stringency which was both dangerous and disheartening, Turkey has begun to refill her empty pipelines with essential goods, to improve her export position, and to come to grips with the problem of investment coordination which has long been a stumbling block in efforts to expand the Turkish economy enough to enable it simultaneously to improve the living standards of the Turkish people and to support the Turkish defense effort. This has been achieved by a program of economic reform centering around the stabilization program, which has had the close and active support of the United States, the International Monetary Fund (IMF), and member nations of the Organization for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC). In the period under review, it can fairly be said that 

Turkey has made more real progress toward these economic goals, and hence toward an important mutual security objective, than in any similar period in her history.

South Asia

The five countries of South Asia-Afghanistan, Ceylon, India, Nepal, and Pakistan-have a combined population of 525 million. 

This is 18 percent of the total world population and about 28 percent of the population of the free world. 

Of major significance to relations between countries of South Asia were increasing evidences of a rapprochement between India and Pakistan. In August 1959 it was announced that the two Governments had reached agreement in principle on a plan to resolve the division of waters of the Indus River system, a dispute which arose out of the partition of India in 1947. It is expected that a treaty governing the settlement may be signed in 1960. Agreements were also reached on border settlements between East and West Bengal, and meetings were held to adjust certain financial claims arising out of partition.

In Pakistan the government of General Ayub, which came to power in October 1958, has succeeded in arresting inflationary trends and in stopping the drain on the country's foreign reserves. The first stage of a program of land reform has been completed. 

In Nepal the country's first parliamentary government took office in July 1959, following countrywide elections. This new government has expressed a determination to proceed as fast as possible with a broad program of development. 

All the countries of the area are beset by poverty; per capita incomes in South Asia are among the lowest in the world. Among other handicaps, the literacy rate averages about 20 percent. There are shortages of trained administrators and technicians and in some cases even the basic institutions of a modern economic society. As the usual accompaniment of such a situation, there is a great shortage of investment capital and foreign exchange reserves. All these countries are embarked on economic development programs of various kinds, yet population in the area is growing at a rate that absorbs much of the gains that are achieved. India has entered on the fourth year of its 

Second Five Year Plan which emphasizes industrial development.

Far East

The Mutual Security Program provides the United States with effective means with which to assist its friends and allies in the Far East to maintain their independence and security against Communist aggression and subversion and make progress in meeting the needs of their people for social and economic progress. 

Communist intentions with respect to the Far East remain patently unchanged. Recent pronouncements by the Soviets regarding their peaceful intentions have not been accompanied by a lessening of the Communist threat to countries of the Far East region. 

In the face of these actions by the Communists-the attacks on the Quemoy group of offshore islands, the suppression of freedom and local autonomy in Tibet, the encroachment on the borders of India, and the incitement of Chinese nationals in Indonesia-our friends and allies in the Far East have continued to keep up their defenses. 

To do so, they are obliged to maintain military forces at levels which are generally much higher than they are capable of supporting without outside assistance.

Within this context, the largest allocations of FY 1960 appropriations were made, as in the past, to the three divided countries— Korea, Viet-Nam, and the Republic of China-which must maintain large defense forces and are consequently in the greatest need of 

U.S. assistance.

In the fall of 1959, a series of great natural disasters struck the Far East. In August and September disastrous typhoons and floods struck Taiwan, Korea, and Japan, inflicting great losses in lives and property. Assistance from the United States to the stricken peoples included the provision of emergency assistance financed through the Mutual Security Program. In addition, certain economic aid programs were readjusted in order to provide help with rehabilitation efforts.

The flood in Taiwan, its worst of this century, resulted from a 14hour torrential downpour of almost 40 inches. The number of persons affected was estimated at 305,000 and material damage was calculated at more than $100 million. Large areas of farmland were inundated and crops destroyed, irrigation systems were wrecked, rail and highway transportation was paralyzed, and an important power station was submerged.

Typhoon Vera, which struck Japan in September, has been described as the most extensive Japanese natural disaster since the great Tokyo earthquake of 1923. Over 5,000 persons lost their lives and 1,500,000 were made homeless; in addition, there were extensive economic losses. 25

The United States contributed medical and other supplies for emergency distribution to needy flood victims and provided substantial transport and rescue services with military aircraft. In addition to this assistance, 4 million pounds of wheat flour, valued at about $320,000, were provided by the International Cooperation Adminis tration (ICA) under title II of P.L. 480. 

In September Typhoon Sarah dealt a severe blow to Korea. About 

10,300 fishing craft, 100,000 homes, and more than 90,000 metric tons of rice and other grains were destroyed. Major harbor installations were also extensively damaged. The preliminary estimate of these losses is nearly $175 million. The Korean Government immediately took steps to provide resources for reconstruction and relief. To the extent possible, ICA activities and funds have been reprogramed to support Korean Government reconstruction efforts.

Many of the nations of the Far East have only recently gained political independence and are in the earlier stages of political and economic development. In addition to its regular lending operations, the Development Loan Fund (DLF) placed major emphasis on certain other activities during the 6-month period ending December 31, 1959. These were: 

The early initiation and implementation of projects under signed loan agreements. • An analysis and review of the backlog of $1.5 billion in proposed projects. 

The introduction and application of a new policy governing the source of procurement with loan proceeds. 

Major Developments 

• Administering the initiation and implementation of projects covered by the $753.3 million in signed loans comprised a much larger share of DLF's activities than in earlier periods of operation. Staff energies were extensively devoted to implementation procedures to loans previously approved. These procedures ranged from determining the scope of responsibilities of an engineering firm in overseeing construction activities to selecting the reporting and accounting procedures to be followed on a project. • DLF also undertook an analysis and review of the large number of loan requests on hand in order to put its backlog on a more current basis. On June 30, 1959, the value of the proposals under consideration or on hand stood at $1.5 billion. The comprehensive analysis underway is expected to result in a substantial reduction in this backlog. In most cases proposals are being returned to applicants because of their lower economic priority relative to other applications; the 

DLF's limited capital availabilities make it unlikely that they can be acted upon favorably within 9 months to a year. • Another major development during the period was the announcement on October 20 of a new policy governing the source of procurement with the proceeds of DLF loans. The new policy provides that "particularly in financing the foreign exchange costs of development projects and programs DLF will place primary emphasis on the financing of goods and services of United States origin." Earlier, DLF had permitted the use of its funds for procurement from the lowest bidder without restriction as to free-world source. The new policy was initiated in the knowledge that other industrial nations which supply capital goods to the newly developing countries are 35

now in a position to provide long-term financing for such exports By assuring that the flow of U.S. funds is accompanied by a flow of U.S.goods and services, this policy will also contribute to reducing the deficit in our balance of payments.

The new policy became applicable to loans approved by the DLF Board of Directors after October 20, 1959. (Prior to that date the 

Board had, out of $1.397 billion of appropriated resources available. for dollar loans, approved $858.6 million in loans and commitments.)

Statutory Requirements Governing Operations of DLF 

The Mutual Security Act requires DLF to take into account the following criteria in considering a proposal (i.e., request for a loan): Whether financing could be obtained in whole or in part from other free-world sources on reasonable terms; • The economic and technical soundness of the activity to be. financed; • Whether the activity gives reasonable promise of contributing to the development of economic resources or to the increase of productive capacities of a less developed country; • The possible adverse effects upon the economy of the United States, with special reference to areas of substantial labor surplus, of the project or program and the financing operation or transaction involved. 

The statute also requires that: • DLF in its operations recognizes that development loan assistance will be most effective in those countries which show a responsiveness to the vital long-term economic, political, and social concerns of their people, demonstrate a clear willingness to take effective self-help measures, and effectively demonstrate that such assistance is consistent with and makes a contribution to workable long-term economic development objectives; • Loans be made only on the basis of firm commitments by borrowers to make repayment and upon a finding that there are reasonable prospects of such repayment; • DLF be administered so as to support and encourage private investment and other private participation; • DLF be administered so as not to compete with private investment capital, the Export-Import Bank or the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development; • DLF, before entering an agreement which obligates funds, ascertain that (a) overall plans to carry out the assistance is completed and the cost estimate is reasonably firm and (b) any necessary legis lative action in the borrowing country may reasonably be anticipated to be completed within 1 year. 36

Chapter 11 Technical Cooperation 

For FY 1960 the Congress appropriated $181 million including $31.2 million for multilateral technical assistance programs. This is an increase of $9 million over that appropriated for FY 1959. The bi lateral Technical Cooperation Program of the United States attempts to aid the peoples of economically underdeveloped countries to raise their standards of living, increase their productivity, and assist them to exploit more effectively their potential resources through exchange of technical knowledge and skills. Multilateral programs similar in purpose are carried on by other nations and international organizations, to several of which the United States contributes. Among them are the technical assistance programs of the United Nations (UNTA) and the Organization of American States (OAS). Other such programs are carried on by, among others, Israel, Germany, and Norway and by the Colombo Plan countries.

Study of Technical Assistance 

Ten years ago, following limited operations in Latin America, the 

United States initiated a worldwide Technical Assistance Program. 

In the ensuing decade, the size of this program was greatly increased, its geographic coverage progressively extended, and the scope of the fields covered materially broadened. Today this program affects some 

60 countries and territories and concerns such diverse fields as agriculture, education, public administration, labor, communications, and community development. During this same period the United Nations and its several specialized agencies, the Organization of American States, and other multilateral institutions have also embarked on major programs of technical assistance to which the U.S. Government has made substantial contributions. In addition a number of other governments, several foundations, many voluntary agencies, and some business organizations have either newly entered the technical assistance field or increased the scope of programs which they had commenced at an earlier date. The technical assistance provided from all these sources has represented an impressive effort to help less developed peoples to train the human resources and to develop the institutions necessary for their economic, social, and political progress. 

It is probable that the general problems of underdevelopment to which technical assistance programs are now largely addressed will 41

remain with us, though in changing forms, for at least the remainder of the 20th century. It is also probable that technical assistance, properly conceived and effectively administered, not only can contribute importantly to the solution of these critical problems but also will represent an indispensable element in any solution of them. In view of these probabilities and of the presently growing magnitude of technical assistance programs, it now appears timely to undertake a searching analysis of the experience which has been derived from past technical assistance activities and, based upon such experience, to determine whether and, if so, how, the purposes, emphasis, approach, techniques, and administration of technical assistance programs should be redirected or revised in order to maximize their usefulness in the several decades ahead.

Should technical assistance programs receive relatively greater emphasis and be significantly increased in size and, if so, how can the resources necessary for the effective implementation of enlarged programs be developed and mobilized? Should more stress be placed on a few selected fields, such as health, education, industry, or transportation? Or is technical assistance essential in a great variety of fields, either simultaneously or in some logical order of sequence, in any developing country? Should there be, for example, a massive attack on the problem of illiteracy, more concentrated efforts on the creation of local institutions, or a greater emphasis on the establishment of indigenous credit organizations and habits of saving? What is the best approach to the problems of village level growth, and how can we most effectively help in the solution of developmental difficulties confronting populous urban areas? What kinds of organizational relationships and administrative mechanisms are best adapted to concerting and coordinating the efforts of the many different governments, international agencies, and private organizations engaged in providing technical assistance? How can the revolutionary advances in communications media and techniques be utilized in solving developmental problems? Better answers are urgently needed to these and many other similar questions, and a careful study of the lessons of prior operations and a full use of our accumulating knowledge in the social sciences should help us to find them. 

For the foregoing reasons, the International Cooperation Administration has been requested to initiate a major study of the entire subject of technical assistance. The study will be in depth, will extend over a period of some 18 months, and will be handled as a research project. It will attempt to identify and to assess the reasons for successful and unsuccessful technical assistance activities in our own bilateral program and, insofar as practicable, and on a cooperative basis, to examine the comparable experience of other governments, multilateral organizations, and private institutions. Based 42

upon this research and similar research which has been or is being undertaken by universities, foundations, and other private groups, the study will then endeavor to isolate and evaluate the major factors which should govern the size, character, methods, and administration of technical assistance programs over the next several decades and to describe areas in which further research may be required. 

Among the kinds of issues which will be considered in the study are the following: 

1. The purposes, capabilities, and limitations of technical assistance. 

What purposes can technical assistance further? How effective can it be as an instrument for helping less developed peoples in their process of growth? To what extent can it be useful in helping other countries solve various problems that are not primarily developmental in nature, such as a pressing refugee situation, an epidemic, the consequences of a natural disaster, or a monetary crisis? Is it an effective means for increasing international understanding and cooperation? Do our bilateral programs help to convey a better image of the United States to other peoples and to strengthen bonds of friendship between us and them? What are the capabilities and the limitations of technical assistance in promoting these and other el like purposes? Is there one single or predominant purpose which should be emphasized or are there a variety of separate purposes for which technical assistance can usefully be employed? 

2. The character of the problems which technical assistance can help to solve. What are the most basic problems confronting less developed countries which technical assistance can help to solve? 

What, if any factors, are common to the situation existing in all less developed nations at comparable stages in their development? 

Are there major elements affecting development that are unique in each society and which create radically different sets of problems? 

What kinds of social, economic, and political changes must take place in the processes of transition to successively more advanced stages of development? To what extent are these changes ones in technology, institutions, motivation, behavioral patterns, or attitudes?

What local factors, such as an awakened desire to improve one's way of life, favor those indigenous changes which are a prerequisite to sustained orderly growth; and what comparable local factors, such as negative attitudes toward work or saving or the absence of inducement, inhibit such changes? How can the former be encouraged and the latter discouraged? 

3. Methods, techniques, and channels of technical assistance. What general approaches and methods of operation are best adapted to helping other countries to solve the variety of problems which they face? How is the answer to this question affected by a country's stage of development, its cultural background, and other unique fac- 43

tors? For example, are the forms and methods of providing technical assistance which are suitable in a newly emerging, predominantly rural nation significantly different from those which are most helpful to a country which has long been independent and is already in the early stages of industrialization? In what ways can a provider of technical assistance best work with the governments and peoples of other countries in furnishing such assistance? As an illustration, when is a mechanism for joint operations, such as the servicio arrangements in South America, a useful device? What are its strengths and weaknesses as a means of providing technical assistance for different purposes? Under what circumstances is it preferable for the desired technical help to be given, (a) under the U.S. bilateral program, (b) through multilateral channels, (c) under the bilateral programs of other governments, or (d) by private institutions? What technique or combination of techniques is most suited for the solution of each of the great variety of problems involving the development of human resources and institutions which confront less developed countries at various stages in their growth? 

Should there be more or less emphasis on providing technical training for foreign students in the United States, on facilitating the training of such students in third countries, and on establishing regional or local institutions which can increasingly provide such training? When are demonstration, formal instruction, or advice to government officials the most effective means of achieving technical assistance objectives? What role can be played by audiovisual devices? Under what circumstances do university contracts represent the best method for giving technical assistance? 

4. Implementation of technical assistance programs. In the case of each of the several available techniques for extending technical assistance, how can its effectiveness be improved through revisions in organization, better administration, improved programing, and other similar measures? 

5. Relation of technical assistance to other forms of assistance. To what extent and in what ways does the effectiveness of technical assistance depend upon the extension of other forms of assistance or upon the initiation by the donor or recipient government of other measures not involving assistance? Under what circumstances is it enough merely to provide information, skills, knowledge, or advice, and when must such technical assistance, in order to have its desired effect, be accompanied by physical or financial resources? For example, when we demonstrate the use of better seed, the application of fertilizer, or the method of establishing a health center, to what extent does the effectiveness of such demonstration depend upon the simultaneous or subsequent provision of enough seed, fertilizer, or medical equipment to permit the widespread practical use of the knowledge 44

so conveyed? What kinds of organizational, administrative, financial, and other complementary steps must a recipient government take if the technical assistance which it receives is to have a lasting effect? 

6. Nature of future technical assistance programs. In the light of the answers to such questions as the foregoing, what, in general terms, should be the size, trend, character, and emphasis of the technical assistance programs conducted in the years immediately ahead, and what should be the priority accorded to these programs in relation to programs involving other types of assistance? 

7. Measures to assure the feasibility of implementing required technical assistance programs. Taking into account the optimum size and character of future technical assistance programs, what measures should and can be taken to make the effective implementation of these programs a reality? For example, what measures are necessary in order (a) to assure the availability on a continuing basis of the number and quality of persons who may be needed to execute these programs; (b) to provide for the effective coordination of the technical assistance furnished through different governmental, multilateral, and private channels; (c) to improve the capacity of American and foreign universities and other institutions to offer meaningful and useful training for the volume of students from less developed countries who will require such training; and (d) to develop and maintain the kind of organizational and administrative framework and procedures and to provide the leadership, requisite for the most efficient and effective conduct of these programs? 

8. Measures for the continuing future improvement of technical assistance programs. What steps should be taken in order to assure (a) that on a permanent basis programs of technical assistance will be continuously and searchingly reexamined in the same manner contemplated in the first instance by this study; (b) that the experience hereafter derived by this government and by other governments and organizations extending technical assistance will be systematically collected, analyzed, and disseminated for the purpose of effecting new and further improvements in technical assistance programs; and (c) that the problems of technical assistance will be the subject of the same kind of thorough, organized, and extensive research that has been directed toward the solution of problems in the field of national defense? 45

Private Investment in Underdeveloped Countries 

The activities described in the preceding paragraphs make clear the conviction of the United States that a fundamental contribution can be made to the rate and soundness of the economic growth of underdeveloped countries by the strengthening and expansion of the private sectors of their economies. We are convinced that government measures, essential as they are, cannot substitute for the vitality and initiative of private investment, which provides not only capital, but the technical and managerial skills essential to economic growth. 48

The aggregate of private investment capital potentially available to these countries far exceeds available U.S. Government funds; thus a favorable investment climate in an underdeveloped country serves to broaden significantly the financial base of the mutual security effort. 

As the private sectors of these economies are strengthened, they become better able to service debt obligations and to attract more private capital from domestic, U.S., and non-U.S. sources. 

In many of these countries significant amounts of domestic capital could be invested in productive ways if the incentive existed. An energetic and successful class of local investors would have a decisive effect in attracting foreign capital. And when outside investors enter they bring not only capital but the dynamism, organizational talent, technical skills, and modern business standards which are the stockin-trade of private investment. Such new investment normally includes training for personnel in the host country and can facilitate the introduction of a diversity of small and medium business enterprises, as well as large ones, thereby promoting more satisfactory and balanced growth. This is an area in which government aid programs can assist to an extent, but in which private enterprise must ultimately do the job. 

Obstacles to Private Investment in Less Developed

Countries 

The obstacles to private investment are not the same in kind and degree for all the less developed countries, but they tend to be similar. 

Most of them are at once both causes and effects of underdevelopment. 

They deter equally all potential investors, local as well as foreign. 

POLITICAL UNREST. In many underdeveloped countries an in creasing awareness of living conditions in more prosperous parts of the world has given rise to discontent and impatience, often manifested in hostility toward the existing government, with resultant political unrest. Serious social cleavages and population pressures frequently add to the difficult task of governing and of maintaining civil order. 

• ATTITUDES AND POLICIES WHICH DETER INVESTMENT. It is im portant to remember that the underdeveloped countries do not have our private enterprise legacy and traditions. Many have had little or no experience with a society oriented toward economic development, much less private enterprise as we know it. In consequence an investor looking into possibilities in less developed countries may encounter suspicion stemming from extreme nationalism, fear of monopoly, and lack of understanding of what constitutes a satisfactory investment climate. On the other hand, in some instances, there is a reasonably well-informed understanding of what is required to attract investment. Where attitudes are hostile or uninformed, they 49

are likely to be reflected in the laws and administration of the country; these may be restrictive or hostile and in rare and extreme instances may allow expropriation without adequate provision for compensation. Happily this area of attitudes is one in which progress is being made. 

• UNDERDEVELOPED HUMAN AND PHYSICAL RESOURCES. In many of the less developed countries the potential investor is confronted with serious deficiencies in terms of basic public works; i.e., roads, transportation systems, communications, power facilities, and so forth. 

These are generally taken for granted in the more developed areas and are not normally financed by private capital. An equally important limitation is imposed by unskilled and untrained populations. 

In Africa, for example, the average literacy rate is 10 percent; in South 

Asia it averages about 20 percent. This limitation is obviously particularly true as to managerial and technical skills. 

MONETARY INSTABILITY. Fears that profits realized overseas can not be remitted to the parent company or doubts as to the exchange rates applicable to such remittances have been a major deterrent to investment in certain countries overseas. Monetary instability and its attendant dislocations continue to plague many underdeveloped countries. In an effort to minimize the exchange risk some companies borrow a maximum of local capital. An accompaniment of monetary instability is the flight of local capital, critically needed in the country, to safer investments in developed countries.

COMPETITIVE INVESTMENT POSSIBILITIES AT HOME. Investment possibilities in the United States and in other industrialized countries are plentiful and attractive, and it is axiomatic under our system that private capital will flow in channels where opportunities for security and profit are good.

ADEQUACY OF INFORMATION. Not only does investment require attractive opportunities, but investors must be aware of such opportunities. Potentially rewarding foreign investments may be overlooked by investors due to lack of knowledge or sufficiently complete information about such investments. 

United States Private Investment Abroad

U.S. direct investment abroad, including new investment and reinvested earnings, averaged about $2.4 billion per year over the period 

1955-58. Historically our investment has been heavily concentrated in Europe, Canada, and Latin America. Only around $100 million a year goes to the less developed areas of Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. A significant share of this has been in petroleum, leaving little for other purposes. 50

Conclusion 

The promotion of an adequate level of private investment in underdeveloped countries is a long-term, difficult job requiring the exercise of patience, ingenuity, and persistence. Each country must necessarily play the leading role in removing the obstacles to the development of its own private sector, but the importance of free-world economic growth is such that the United States must help to the greatest extent possible in order to promote mutual security objectives. 

The problem is an urgent one that does not lend itself to leisurely solution; the gap in standards of living between the West and the underdeveloped countries continues to widen despite the progress made in recent years in the underdeveloped countries. 51

Chapter 17 Special Problems IMPACT OF SCIENTIFIC DEVELOPMENTS ON THE MUTUAL SECURITY PROGRAM 

In the course of the last century science and technology have remade the physical existence of the people of the developed countries. 

Scientific developments, modern medicine, newspapers, radio, and nearly universal literacy have made the average man in these countries an entirely different person in outlook and attitude and requirements than his ancestor of 100 years ago. 

Although these changes have been taking place at a constantly accelerating pace in the economically advanced countries, most of the rest of the world has until recent years continued to live much as it had for centuries. Now, underdeveloped countries, largely through the medium of modern communications, have suddenly begun to be aware of the material benefits the economically advanced nations have achieved by utilizing scientific knowledge. With increasing rapidity and insistence they are demanding for themselves the same things that science and technology have made possible in the west. In terms of human skills and financial and industrial development, however, their limited resources are not adequate by themselves to provide these things for their present populations. 

This revolution of rising expectations, coupled with the additional pressure of rapidly expanding populations in these underdeveloped countries, provides a convenient vehicle for any political force which offers promise of meeting their growing demands. 

Without some visible improvement and justified hope for the future, moreover, communism, which holds itself out to be the "easy" solution to their problems, can take advantage of the political immaturity of most of these countries and make increasingly success- 55

ful inroads with disastrous effects on the world position of the 

United States and our allies.

It is therefore of pressing importance to identify some of the more basic difficulties in these underdeveloped countries which can be addressed by the Mutual Security Program (MSP) and to utilize and adopt the most promising means provided by scientific development to meet them forcefully and intelligently by raising standards of living to tolerable levels within the coming decade. 

The problems mentioned here are only a few of the many facing us in MSP which can be helped by science. They are identified separately but are actually all closely related. Further progress in solving the problem of communication of ideas, for example, would simplify the problem of adapting techniques and in every other respect greatly lighten the burden of development for the United States and other sources of external assistance. It would also, of course, ease the problem of transition and development for the underdeveloped countries. 

Adaptation of Techniques and Communication of Ideas 

As a prerequisite to more effective economic development there are at least two important fields where more intensive scientific activity is badly needed. These are (1) adaptation of our present complex methods and machines for use in simpler, less advanced economies and (2) increased knowledge about how ideas and techniques can be transmitted from one culture to other and perhaps radically different cultures.

Many of our highly complex industrial and business techniques are not useful to underdeveloped countries in their present state. 

They must be refashioned and adapted to the needs and available skills of the underdeveloped countries. 

Our factory methods also are often not easily utilized in underdeveloped countries where little capital is available but where labor abounds. Adaptation may be needed here to develop manufacturing techniques using more labor and less capital. 

The techniques of agricultural production and marketing also require conversion to other but often equally complicated situations. 

Much of the work of adapting inventions from the present reservoir of such inventions and techniques can be done by research and development institutions in the underdeveloped countries. 

The diffusion of the necessary knowledge and techniques embodied in the adaptations-and having them adopted with reasonable speed -presents a difficult problem. This is an area where new scientific advances possibly could be most useful and productive. The transmission of ideas from one society to another is difficult under any 56

circumstances but especially so in conditions of poverty and illiteracy, where there are shortages of skilled manpower and capital and where social customs may strongly resist change. 

Although we have moved forward at tremendous speed in the physical sciences, the accumulation of knowledge about human behavior has been much slower. There are gaps in our knowledge, for example, about how acceptance of new ideas grows within a nation or how they are passed from one nation to another. Nor do we know with exactness why some ideas are accepted and others are rejected or what controls the speed (usually very slow) at which an accepted idea is acted on. 

As applied to the food and population problem, described later, advances in the techniques of communicating ideas could be of crucial importance. For example there is little doubt that we now possess the necessary scientific knowledge to cure the problem of food production in the Far East and Latin America, where per capita food production has still failed to regain the prewar level in the 13 productive seasons that have elapsed since the war. Yet how to transmit this information to the people who need it, have them put it into the context of their own cultures, and then act on it effectively is a process we understand imperfectly.

The whole process of communicating ideas is of vital importance to all sectors of MSP, but it is particularly important to the Technical Cooperation Program (TC). A fundamental purpose of the TC 

Program is to convey the techniques and skills of the advanced nations to the less developed nations, and TC must deal regularly with the problem of communication described here. TC has done much pioneer work in this field and the experiences of TC technicians are being drawn on for what they can add to our knowledge of communication of ideas. This problem is one of those with which the study of TC, described in chapter 11, is concerned. 

Diversification of Economies and the Food Problem 

Two further problems, among many faced by MSP which must be solved if standards of living are to be raised to tolerable levels and to which science can contribute, are the diversification of the economies of present "one commodity" countries and the production of food sufficient to meet the demands of increasing populations. 

The underdeveloped countries in many cases have only one major export commodity that they can trade abroad; e.g., coffee, rubber, tea, wool, cotton. Although scientific developments to come will undoubtedly create new and enlarged demand for some raw materials in the future, it is equally likely that the development of synthetic materials will similarly challenge and perhaps eventually displace certain raw 57

materials. This possibility exists for natural rubber, for animal and vegetable fibers, and for certain beverages, natural waxes, and industrial diamonds. Reinforced plastics will compete increasingly with metals, wood products, paper, and ceramics.

Although the population problem is often thought of mainly in terms of the food supply, general economic development involves much more than merely an expansion of food production. We know from our American experience of recent years that even a modest population increase requires-in addition to food-new housing, schools, hospitals, roads, clothing, and services. All of these things are similarly needed in the underdeveloped countries, although not on such an elaborate scale. All of them also require trained human skills and massive amounts of capital, and these requirements are among the most conspicuous needs of underdeveloped countries.

The following conclusions regarding the impact of scientific developments seem warranted: 

1. The “revolution of rising expectations" is rooted in the conviction of the underdeveloped countries that modern science and technology can make life better for them. 

2. The speed at which the underdeveloped countries wish to move forward—and find it politically imperative to do so-calls for not only intelligent and farsighted economic development programs but also the new methods of communicating ideas from one culture to another. 

3. Even with substantial external assistance, the underdeveloped countries find the job of raising living standards a difficult one. THE MUTUAL SECURITY PROGRAM AND THE U.S. BALANCE OF PAYMENTS 

In recent months, the U.S. balance-of-payments position with the rest of the world has been the subject of wide public interest and concern. The Mutual Security Program (MSP) is involved since it spends money in foreign countries. This section examines the role played by the Mutual Security Program in our current balance-ofpayments position.

In 1959 U.S. payments exceeded receipts by a wide margin. The payments deficit as measured by net transfers of gold and liquid dollars to foreign ownership was $3.7 billion as compared to $3.4 billion in 1958. Whereas there has been a net outflow of gold and liquid dollar assets in each year since 1950, with the exception of 1957, these previous yearly payments deficits had beneficial aspects in that they strengthened the reserve position of other countries and facilitated a free and unhampered international exchange of goods and services. The U.S. payments deficits of 1958 and 1959 also had this effect, but the sudden sharp increase in the rate of outflow has given rise to some legitimate concern about the causes and duration of our deficit position.

A wide variety of factors combined to bring about the payments deficits of 1958 and 1959. Among these factors can be included the following: Differences in timing of short-term cyclical fluctuations as between the United States and other industrialized countries. 

Changes in the supply and demand conditions of goods and services figuring importantly in exports and imports (e.g., meat, coal, automobiles, cotton).

Broader changes affecting the general levels of prices and costs in the United States compared with these in other countries (steel, electrical machinery, automobiles, etc.). • Fluctuations in conditions affecting the flow of capital between the United States and other countries. 60

It is difficult to weigh with certainty any of these factors and disentangle them in their complex interdependence. It appears that although at some times during the last 2 years all these elements reinforced each other to bring forth the high rate of U.S. payments deficit, the trend reached its bottom and reversed about the middle of 1959. The balance-of-payments deficit is expected to decline in 

1960, perhaps very substantially, if the present favorable trend in increased exports and leveling off in imports continues. The trend in late 1959 and early 1960 is indicative of a major improvement. 

Many factors entered into the growth in the U.S. balance-of-payments deficit in 1958 and 1959, but the Mutual Security Program played no part in these increases.

In evaluating the role of the Mutual Security Program in the recent balance-of-payments trend it may be useful to compare the trends between 1953 and 1955 and between 1958 and 1959. The selection of fiscal years 1953-55 as a reference period appears justified because these were relatively "normal" years between the Korean conflict and the Suez crisis and preceding the latest cyclical experience in domestic and international trade. It has been said that part of the large balance-of-payments deficit of recent years has resulted from increases in the Government grant and long-term capital outflow accounts. 

These accounts have substantially increased but the increases were due primarily to operations under P.L. 480, consisting of sale of agricultural surplus commodities and increased lending of the Export- Import Bank (Eximbank). Grants and loans under the P.L. 480 programs and Eximbank operations are fully counterbalanced by corresponding exports of U.S. goods. Thus although it is true to state that there has been an increase in the Government grant and long-term capital outflow accounts, balanced by an offsetting increase in U.S. exports, this only describes changes in the various categories composing the balance-of-payments account and has no relation to the underlying causes of the changes in the payments deficit. 

The Mutual Security Program has not caused an increased balanceof-payments deficit, but this does not exclude the possibility of remedial action within the Mutual Security Program which could contribute to a reduction of the balance-of-payments deficit. The executive branch has closely studied the Mutual Security Program and its various elements from the point of view of what changes in policy and operations could be undertaken in the light of existing conditions and in order to further U.S. policy objectives. After carefully reviewing various considerations the executive branch decided to institute a change in the worldwide procurement policy of the Development 

Loan Fund (DLF) but not in the operations of the International

Cooperation Administration (ICA). Policies pertaining to the military assistance programs, where offshore procurement had already been reduced to a minimum, also remain unchanged. The new DLF policy provides that particularly in financing the foreign exchange costs of development projects and programs, DLF will place primary emphasis on the financing of goods and services of U.S. origin. This policy was undertaken in view of the growth in the economic strength 62

of other industrialized countries of the free world and the changes which have taken place in the world balance-of-payments structure. 

There is now a fair presumption that other industrialized countries which export capital goods to the less developed countries are in a financial position to provide long-term loans on reasonable terms to assist such countries in their development programs. Thus in addition to the favorable effect which this new policy is expected to have in time on the U.S. balance of payments, this policy is also intended to induce a greater participation by other developed countries in the common task of assisting less developed countries and areas. This has been a long-standing objective of American policy, and it is believed that this is a particularly propitious time to take a strong initiative in this direction.

The ICA program was also reviewed in the same context. It was decided that the procurement policy of ICA should not be changed at the present time. However it was decided to expedite the current program of transferring assistance for specific development projects whenever possible from ICA to DLF. 

This decision was based on the recognition of the substantial differences between the type of assistance provided by ICA and DLF. 

DLF provides long-term development assistance. DLF loans for the foreign exchange costs of development projects are largely spent on capital equipment which should properly be financed on the basis of long-term loans, which the industrialized countries of the free world are now able to provide. ICA assistance, on the other hand, is primarily designed to provide basic economic strength to countries of importance to the United States. Defense support assists those countries carrying heavy military burdens in the defense of the free world, and special assistance is designed to maintain or promote political and economic stability in other countries. These programs are largely accomplished by the furnishing, on a grant basis, of industrial raw materials and other consumable commodities. 

In view of the grant nature of practically all ICA assistance and the specific U.S. interests advanced by this assistance, the adoption by ICA of a policy similar to the new DLF policy could not be expected to stimulate additional financing by other industrialized countries.

In addition the ICA programs are handled largely through private trade channels and contribute substantially to the development of private enterprise in the newly developing countries. If U.S. procurement were to be required for these consumable items, the recipient governments would have to work out a system of subsidies which would lead to governmental controls inimical to our objective of promoting free private enterprise. 63

Finally a significant amount of ICA offshore procurement takes place in the newly developing countries themselves where it serves the purpose of furthering the development of such countries, thereby lessening their need for assistance from the United States.
