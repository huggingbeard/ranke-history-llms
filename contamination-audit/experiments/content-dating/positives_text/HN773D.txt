INTRODUCT 

This book will give you a general picture of America's armed forces. If you are a man of draft age, keep asking yourself this question as you read it: "Where will I best fit in?" If you are a woman, think seriously about the proposed women's auxiliary corps. It will give you a genuine opportunity to serve.

Modern war is a war of machines and men. Once you are taken into the army, if you set your mind to it, in addition to learning to be a good soldier, you have an opportunity to learn a skilled trade. In fact, if you know that you will be called for service, you might prepare yourself by taking a brief course in machine-shop practice or metalworking. That will put you ahead of the procession when your number comes up. 

The Army, the Navy, the Marine Corps, and the Coast Guard are looking for men. If you have some training you will be all the more welcome. 

The Coast Guard, for example, will start trained cooks, radiomen, and machinists with ratings as second-class petty officers or better.

Whether you are trained or untrained, these branches of the armed services are calling for your help. If you want to learn a trade you can volunteer in the various branches of the Naval Reserve, for specific training. In either case, remember this: It takes training, and plenty of it, to lick the Axis. 

Remember this too: Opportunities in the armed forces mean opportunities for hard work. It's going to take every ounce of muscle, energy, and intelligence you have to turn yourself into the best possible soldier, sailor, or marine that you can be. In warfare second best doesn't do. You have to win or else you lose, and lose disastrously. 

If you take advantage of the opportunities described in these pages, you will have the satisfaction of knowing that you have done your part in licking the Axis. Opportunities for training, promotion, improving yourself, exist on every hand. But to get the best out of these opportunities will require plenty of effort on your part. Remember, whether we win or lose depends upon you. This war will be pretty much what you choose to make of it. 27

MERICA ON THE MARCH 

Far down the valley, across the line of rolling hills, small clouds of dust spurt from a shady country road. Heads bent low over their handlebars, dirt outlining the goggles on their tense faces, the motorcycle scouts speed along. Behind them jolt the fast scout cars. The American army is on the march, and these motorcycle scouts know it's their job to make the first contact with the enemy.

The soldiers cover an entire countryside. Far out on the flanks where even the trusty jeeps and half-tracks can't maneuver, the horse cavalry plunge headfirst through a rugged terrain. And everywhere the scouts go the radio experts of the signal corps, nervously twisting the dials of their equipment, follow. As soon as the enemy is sighted, news of his location will flash back to the commanding officers' posts.

Armored units head the columns as they move relentlessly forward. Fast light tanks and slower medium tanks can smash their way through the ranks of enemy opposition. Mile after mile of tanks speed at forty miles an hour through the dust. Where they know a road that isn't mined, they follow it. Where no road exists, they make one. 

Following the tanks, working in closest collaboration with them, come the-you guessed it our pals the doughboys.

The Queen of Battles? 

Back in the old days, furrow-browed military strategists used to call the infantry the queen of battles. Queen of battles still? Well, maybe, but the old girl has changed a lot from the way she looked when your father dodged across No

Man's Land in a stiff choker collar and wraparound puttees.

Many Modern Doughboys Ride. 

The motorized divisions you've read about in the newspapers are just that. Across the hills come infantry trucks, jeeps, peeps, bantam cars, and all. Some have heavy machine guns pointed toward the sky, ready to repel an enemy dive bomber. Others carry radio equipment that keeps all sections in constant touch with one another. The rifle is no longer the sole infantry weapon. The old-fashioned Springfield is being rapidly re-

placed by the quick-firing Garand. Some of the infantrymen have Browning automatic rifles, others are part of crews that operate heavy or light machine guns.

The tube that catches your eye on one truck is part of a trench mortar. And the slim weapon following some of the infantry trucks is a 37-mm. anti-tank gun.

Quite a collection of weapons for an army unit that used to rely on the rifle, the hand grenade, and the bayonet! 

Factories on the Go

But that isn't all. The modern army is a far cry from that when all seemed bright uniforms, perfect marching step, and blaring bands. It's more like a selfcontained factory. Plunging after the motorized infantry come the trucks of the supply units. The Quartermaster 

Corps have bakeries and cookstoves on wheels that keep up with the line of march, to make sure that no soldier goes unfed. Motorized machine shops race after the other trucks. The cavalry blacksmith-in units that have horse cavalryfollows the troopers in a special vehicle.

Some trucks and trailers even contain shower equipment that can be quickly set up when the column halts for a day near a water supply. Other units can carry the Chemical Warfare Service troops toward the front. Ambulances move down the roads. Ordnance trucks that supply and maintain the arms and ammunition carry the sinews of war right up into the front lines. The Signal Corps, complete with radio, telegraph, and telephone equipment-and pigeons-is there to see that orders and reports are quickly sped to the proper troops. 

Everybody at the Front

In the last war, the infantry bore the brunt of the battle casualties. Not so in this. Everybody runs risks in this man's army. 

The artillery, for example, may move along with the first advancing wave, ready to shell the enemy positions from the closest possible vantage point. Building in the Face of the Enemy. The army engineers are often in the front line of the troops. Is there a river to be crossed, a bridge to be dynamited? 

Call for the engineers! When there's a road to be built, they'll build it. And if the enemy turns up unexpectedly they'll drop their picks and shovels and turn themselves into first-class fighting men. 

Far behind the lines, where the railroads are still untouched, the heavy "railroad guns" raise their earth-shaking muzzles toward the sky. And in the wake of the army as it moves off toward the front come the never-ending supply trucks. It takes transportation to keep this army of ours on the march. 

Soldiers of the Sky

While the army covers the ground in a khaki-colored cloud, an air force is covering the sky. Reconnaissance planes fly ahead to spy out the enemy, to do from the air what cavalry scouting units do on the ground. Spotter planes hover to direct artillery fire. Fighter planes zoom through the sky like hawks that have tasted blood, daring enemy bombers to approach. And high overhead the heavy bombers zoom toward the enemy's lines of communication, his ammunition depots, his ships, his factories, to drop their death-dealing "eggs."

This Means Teamwork 

Does this army of ours look like a team? Well, it is. The old fixed divisions between infantry, artillery, and cavalry have disappeared. Now the soldiers who man the guns that sway along behind the trucks work together with the other arms as a close-knit team. Every man knows what his job is, and every man is prepared to do his job. This army is as different from the blue- and gray-clad hordes that drenched American soil with blood for four long years during the Civil War as a modern textile mill is from a spinning wheel.

The secret of mass production is to split up a job, and give every single man a certain part to play. Then a production line can be organized to produce the greatest possible output, whether of auto-

Great Numbers of Tools. Like a factory, this army is different also because of the vast number of tools each man has to work with. Just as a turret lathe speeds up machine-shop production, so a Garand rifle or a 50-caliber machine gun can easily do more work than an oldfashioned Springfield.

These are two of the distinguishing characteristics of the fighting horde of olive-drab-clad soldiers that form the

U. S. Army. One is teamwork. The other is equipment. 

Unfortunately, our enemies know this secret too. It was superiority in both that made possible the Germans' helterskelter victory dash through the Low

Countries and France. Among many other things, superiority in both will help lead us to victory in this war.

The Navy Knows It Too 

The battle fleet of the United States resembles in more ways than one the army on the march. Just as the army sends out scouts, the navy sends out scouting planes and destroyers to form a protective screen. Submarines lurk beneath the surface to raid behind the enemy battle lines. Light cruisers, like light tanks, move forward, prepared to dash in, attack, and run away. And like the heavy tanks and the artillery, the mighty battleships stand haughtily aside, prepared to step in when the combat grows hot and deal the final devastating blow. 

Here again, in the navy, teamwork counts. Stoker, gun loader, navigator, gun pointer, radioman, all have parts to play. They have practiced them a hundred times. Victory or defeat depends on whether or not those parts are played properly.

Win or Lose? These teams will determine victory or defeat in this war.

There is a place for every able-bodied American on these teams. Only this time it isn't a question of a base-hit or a touchdown. It's the ninth inning, the last two minutes to play. On the outcome doesn't hang a game won or lost, a traditional enemy beaten. If we win, we win the world. If we lose, we lose America. 

Find Your Place! 

If you're of draft age and able-bodied you will find yourself playing on this military team within a very short time.

Before the war broke out Americans held varied opinions about military serv ice. Now there can be only one opinion.

The man who is called to serve, and that includes nearly every able-bodied man between the ages of 18 and 40, can have but one aim. He must fit himself to play the most efficient possible role to help defend America. 

Citizen Army. America depends for its existence upon its citizen army. The soldiers in that army must know two things: what they are fighting against, and how to fight. We have always been a nation that felt it could take care of itself in case trouble started. Pearl Harbor should have finished a lot of our false optimism. Being Americans doesn't automatically make us better able to throw a hand grenade or drive a tank. This war, more than any other, is a job. It is a job that has to be learned from the ground up.

The task won't be pleasant. Some of those grim-faced, iron-hard lads riding in the tanks, sighting down rifles, looking through range finders, won't come back again. But the ones who do will be the ones who have learned their military tasks the best. It's axiomatic that the good soldier lives a lot longer than the poor one. Remember Sergeant York? 

Do Your Best. Whatever you serve in the armed forces-the Army, Navy, Marines, or Coast Guard-it's going to be your job to be the best possible soldier, sailor, or marine that you have it in you to be. If working a little harder will recommend you for promotion, it's up to you to work a little harder. If hard work will lead you to a commission, start perspiring. No American can hope to get by in this war on the basis of what the other fellow does. It's up to each individual, himself, to produce to the best of his ability.

ET'S LOOK AT THE ARMY 

Let's look at the army. 

The army comes first and occupies most space in this book because it uses more manpower than any other of our forces. Nobody knows-except the army heads themselves --just how many men are now wearing the olive-drab khaki uniforms, shooting

Uncle Sam's guns, eating Uncle Sam's food. Certainly it can't be less than two million. Before the year is out, at least three and a half million will be under arms. It took a brave man just a few months ago to speak of an army of five million. Then the ante was hiked to eight and a half million. Now conservative figures talk of ten million under arms.

The army differs from the other services also in that it relies mainly upon draftees for recruits. Both the navy and the marines are made up of volunteers, although the navy may eventually be forced to rely upon conscripts. If you want to become a sailor or a marine, you must go around and apply to your nearest recruiting office. 

Arms and Services-They 

Go Together

The army is split right down the middle into two different groups: the arms, and the services. Roughly, the arms do the fighting, while the services keep the arms supplied so they can do the fighting. In some cases, such as in those of the Corps of Engineers and the Signal Corps, the specifications overlap. "Combat Engineers" means just that; one hand on the rifle, the other on the shovel. That's true of the Signal Corps as well. 

Before you get a wrong idea of the courage of the services, you might put the distinction between the two this way. Both the arms and the services get shot at, bombarded, bombed. The arms, however, shoot back. The arms are primarily concerned with fighting. The services see to it that the arms are in condition to fight.

Tools of the Foot Soldier

When your rifleman steps into battle he carries a Garand rifle in his hand.

More than a hundred rounds of ammunition are strapped in his cartridge belts. At his side swings a bayonet. If he wants hand grenades, he knows where they are available. This is his basic equip. ment.

Even before he went on the rifle range, he spent hours learning how to hold a gun, how to sight it. He spent further hours on the rifle range shooting at targets, meticulously noting his score, trying for medals which would indicate his proficiency as a marksman, sharpshooter, or expert. He learned how to pull the pin out of the hand grenade, and lob it with an easy motion toward the enemy. A nation of baseball players can readily turn to the deadlier business of throwing hand grenades at the enemy.

Arguments rage about the value of the bayonet. Since the Russo-Japanese war the bayonet has steadily declined in military esteem. The twisting trenches of the last war made soldiers turn to pistols, daggers, entrenching tools, in preference to the clumsy bayonet. Nevertheless, the modern doughboy gets thorough training in bayonet fighting.

In addition, this infantryman has learned how to advance through a thick forest or across an open field. In both cases he knows how to expose the smallest portion of his person to the bullets of an enemy. The entrenching tool he carries on his back is quickly available to dig a small trench or "foxhole." Our troops in the Philippines have already assured the phrase, "the foxholes of Bataan," a glorious niche in American history.

The Army Has Prepared Every thing. The motions in a bayonet attack are prescribed. If a soidier is running across an open field toward an enemy outpost and a machine gun starts to sputter, he knows precisely how to fall to the ground. The smart tricks of the 

Indian in the forest have been taught him. These measures come under the head of "Scouting and Patrolling," but the tough American doughboy long ago learned to refer to them as "Snooping and Peeping."

Gone are the parade-ground charges of earlier wars. Each squad advances in short rushes, taking advantage of the terrain as they go. It's the infantryman who meets the enemy hand to hand. The

What's on His Back

Unquestionably, the American doughboy is the best clothed, best fed, best equipped infantryman the world has ever seen. Both his summer and winter uniforms are certainly on a par in quality with civilian clothing. The longer the war continues, the better clothed by comparison the soldier will be, for the army takes the pick of the materials. Up in the frozen North the soldier wears a furlined parka, heavy boots, even a leather mask when the thermometer drops to 60 degrees below zero. Down in 

Panama his uniform is of lightweight cotton; a specially designed tropical helmet protects his head except in actual battle.

The Art and Science of the Pack. 

The soldier's pack seems to weigh a ton the day he returns from his first long hike. 

Actually, it weighs less than fifty pounds. 

It's as carefully designed as a watch. The midnight oil has burned long in the quartermaster's department; tempers have snapped in lengthy arguments over just what should go into that important collection of assorted items. Every possible combination was tested and tried before the present arrangement was fixed on. 

In his haversack the soldier carries his mess kit. The pack itself contains a blanket, half a "pup" tent, poles, tent pins, and toilet articles. Gas mask, entrenching tool, and his reserve ration are further distributed about his person. A small first-aid kit is attached to his belt, along with a canteen. The problem of the pack has always been to get the most into the least. The ideal pack would make the soldier completely self-sustaining in the field. Naturally, such a goal is impossible. Trucks carry an extra day's rations and an ammunition supply into the battle lines for the doughboy's use.

Machine Gunners on the Attack

The basic advantage of all the heavy weapons now used by the infantry is their extreme mobility. In the front ranks of the advancing infantry are the 

30-caliber machine guns and the 60millimeter mortars. The heavier machine guns and mortars are not far behind. 

These weapons are readily demountable, and each part can be carried by one man. 

Play or Burst? Most people think that a machine gunner "plays" his weapon on the advancing enemy much as a suburbanite "plays" a hose upon his lawn. That's not the case. A machine gun can shoot off 500 bullets a minute when it's going full blast. That's a minimum of 31 pounds of ammunition a minute. Not all the ammunition carriers in the great world can keep machine guns going at full blast for any length of time.

The machine gun-also commanded by a corporal-fires only in short bursts. It tries to cover a certain area, and prevent the enemy from taking possession of that area. Often, the smoke and dust of battle being what they are, the machine gun fires at no visible enemy, but only along certain sighting stakes that previously have been placed in position to indicate the line of fire. 

Three different bullets are used in machine guns. There are the ordinary 30or 50-caliber kind used only on troops. 

Then there are the tracer bullets that fill the night with gaudy stripes. These are seldom used in war, for they are too revealing. And armor-piercing bullets can penetrate the armor of light tanks. 

Don't Pat this Baby

The 37-mm. anti-tank gun looks like a toy cannon. Its metal shield barely serves to hide the two men who operate it. In action it works on the flanks of the advancing infantry. On the battlefield it can easily be towed by a jeep. And it can fire thirty shells a minute with devastating results. 

Like everybody and everything else in the army, this 37-mm. cannon has a specific job to do. Against heavy tanks it is almost useless. But against light tanks, armored cars, and so on, it is as nearly perfect as any weapon can be.

The Infantry-Jack of All Trades 

Your infantryman is truly a jack of all trades. He has been taught how to handle all of the weapons discussed, from every position in every crew. True, he eventually specializes. He becomes an ammunition carrier, a rifleman, a loader on a 37-mm. cannon. But enemy bullets and shells aren't selective. When the man who sights a mortar is disabled, another member of the crew has to step into his place, and step quick. If worse comes to worst, the doughboy will manage to operate one of these weapons entirely on his own. It won't be too efficient, but the piece will still be in action. 

In addition, the infantryman learns military history, courtesy, and how to take care of himself and of his weapons. 

He receives lessons in basic first aid. In case he is wounded in the field, he can take the first steps to prevent infection. 

His first-aid kit contains simple bandages, and even sulfanilamide pills to stop dangerous bacteria dead in their tracks. 

His Buddy, the Rifle. The infantryman learns from the start how to keep his rifle in first-class working condition. That rifle, whether it is the 

Garand or the Springfield, is the result of long experiment. It has been tested under the worst possible conditions. If necessary, the soldier can take it apart and put it back together with his eyes shut. Any old soldier will tell you how soon an infantryman becomes fond of his rifle, regards it as a buddy. 

And in spite of motors and mechanization, the infantryman learns to take good care of his feet. They're what keep him going. A doughboy with bad feet is like a blind painter or an armless acrobat. "The Cannoneers Have

The record of the artillery begins at the battle of Crécy, when the first crude cannon hurled rounded stones at the advancing French knights. Napoleon owed much of his success to his cannon. 

It was the superior artillery of the Prussians under Moltke and Bismarck that led to the victories that resulted in the 

German Empire.

Right now, the artillery is in just as much of a state of flux as the infantry is. Formerly, the field artillery dashed into its battle positions lashing its horses. 

The good field artilleryman had to combine all the best qualities of a mathematician, longshoreman, mechanic, veterinary surgeon, cowboy, and stable hand. Now the horse as the main source of motive power has been replaced by motorized

Bigger and Better Guns 

The high-explosive shells fired by the artillery wreak havoc among enemy troops. Fifty years ago it was estimated that the artillery caused about ten per cent of the casualties on the battlefield. Now it causes about seventy per cent. During the 

First World War the artillery played a major role in trench warfare. The guns were located well behind the actual fighting lines. And there were plenty of them, even then.

At the battle of Arras there was one gun for every nine yards of front. 

In the beginning, the artilleryman sighted his gun by merely squinting down its crude barrel toward the enemy lines. 

Then, particularly during the First World

War, it was necessary to devise some means of sighting the gun when the enemy was invisible beyond the crest of a hill. By complicated mathematics sighting instruments were devised to make this possible. Now, with light artillery units right up in the front lines again, new methods of direct firing have been developed for some ten years. 

Deadly Varieties. There are two basic kinds of weapons in the field artillery. Guns fire almost directly at their objectives; their shells curve only slightly in flight. Howitzers throw a shell high in the air so that it lands on the enemy from directly above. A gun, for example, will be most useful in smashing a tank or some other barely visible target. 

A howitzer can drop a shell on a concentration of enemy troops that lies just behind the crest of a hill.

In terms of size there are three different kinds of artillery. The light artillery consists of 75-mm. guns and howitzers. 

These guns are useful mainly against enemy troops themselves or against such targets as machine-gun nests. The medium artillery is the 105-mm. howitzer, the standard weapon, which smashes at troops, trenches, and the foe's guns.

The heavy guns, the ones that shake the earth with the violence of their recoil, go after valuable enemy real estate -heavy fortifications, and bridges. The 

155-mm. gun, for example, weighs fifteen tons and throws a 95-pound shell nearly fifteen miles. 

Organization, the Key to Artillery

The battery is the basic unit of artillery organization, and is roughly comparable to an infantry company. Four batteries plus an ammunition and supply battery make up an artillery battalion. The battalion is now the largest independent artillery unit. This change from the brigade was made because of the increased necessity for making the artillery and infantry work together in teams. Such teams at present consist of an infantry regiment and an artillery battalion, or an infantry company and an artillery battery.

In addition, each artillery battalion has an anti-aircraft and an anti-tank platoon. 

Here the cannoneers manipulate 50-caliber machine guns and 37-mm. and 75mm. anti-tank guns. These weapons are added to make the artillery unit self-protecting in the field.

New Frontiers for the Guns 

War changes faster than a chameleon trying to hide itself on a piece of Scotch plaid. The victorious weapon of yesterday may be responsible for tomorrow's defeat. When generals found that light tanks had their armor perforated like paper by the 37-mm. anti-tank gun, the armor was thickened, and against these heavier tanks the defending forces had to develop the 75-mm. anti-tank gun. 

Tomorrow's anti-tank gun may be even larger, to smash heavier tanks. 

Pictures from the German war front show huge guns mounted on caterpillar treads, their gunners shielded behind sheets of steel, right in the thick of battle.

For able-bodied men under thirty-five, the question is how they can most efficiently serve their country when they are inducted.

And There's a Joke About It.

By the time they get through, the army has a pretty good picture of where you will fit in. A few years ago they used to tell a funny story about the army personnel testing. According to this tale, the new recruit was carefully tested by trained personnel officers. When they were through, they assigned him to the particular arm where he would best fit in. 

Everything science could do was used to classify each man. The new soldiers were loaded on trains and taken to their camps. 

Upon arrival, old-time sergeants would board the train, order the men to fall into line and then count off. The first hundred were arbitrarily assigned to the cavalry, the next to the artillery, a couple of hundred to the infantry, and so on, until all the new recruits were assigned. 

They don't do things like that any more. The bigger the army, the bigger the need for making sure that every man is fitted into the place where he can best serve his country in her hour of need.

Soldiers' Pay Is Up

At this writing, Congress is in the process of raising the pay of the troops. 

Many people have long felt that $21 a month was inadequate pay for the men on whose shoulders rested the burden of defending this nation. All personal profits from the war are an abomination. 

And it seemed to many that the men who made the greatest personal sacrifices were being inadequately rewarded. The new pay rates will make the United States soldier or sailor the highest-paid fighting man in the world except the Australian. 

And there is no reason why this wealthy nation should lag behind Australia. Soldiers' salaries are among the least costly aspects of war. If there ever was a case of the laborer's being worthy of his hire, this is one. 

How the Raises Run. Formerly, the private had to serve four months at $21 a month, then he was boosted to $30.

The navy's assortment of planes is at least as great as the army's. Its aircraft are of four general classifications. There are the ship-based planes, bombers, fighters, scouters, and torpedo carriers which operate off the decks of aircraft carriers.

There are the dive bombers, the "Hell

The navy patrol planes offer splendid opportunities for young men who want to set up light housekeeping. That's literally true. Our Own Skytroops 

Before leaving the air forces, let's discuss a new variety of soldier. New, of course, only in a very special sense. Parachute troops are an American invention. 

The Russians and the Germans borrowed the idea from us. But we almost forgot about parachute troops for a decade while European armies went on developing them. Three years ago the newsreels were full of the floating parachutes of the Russians. The Germans used parachute troops to conquer Belgium, Holland and Crete.

Both the army and the marines have installed special parachute-troop battalions. The increase of these is proceeding so fast that any guess as to the number of parachute troops would be obsolete before it was made. Naturally, only soldiers and marines who are in the pink of physical condition are accepted. And there is a pay inducement of $50 extra every month. Accidents are not so common as you might think, considering the basic hazard of the work. The first 2,500 parachute troops were trained with fewer than five fatal accidents. 

What's Your Weight? If you weigh more than 185 pounds, forget about the parachute troops. That weight is the maximum the average parachute can be expected to carry along with 40-odd pounds of equipment. And 30 is about the top age for enlisted men.

Planes Without Motors 

Gliding is even older than flying. The glider pilot operates a plane with wings, but without a motor, that flies by its ability to take advantage of air currents. 

In Crete, the Germans used both parachute troops and glider-borne troops. The planes that approached the island towed long strings of gliders behind them.

When the planes released the gliders they floated down to a mild crash landing. 

Troops poured out of them and surrounded the surprised defenders before they could take proper counteraction. 

Glider- and plane-borne troops are a sort of cross between parachute troops and regular infantrymen. Their weapons are similar to those used by the parachute troops, and they are trained to live many days on the country. Such troops, slicing in behind enemy lines, can destroy morale and do much material damage. Who Can Join? The navy wants men between the ages of 17 and 31. If you are under 21, you must have a written statement from your parents or guardian consenting to your enlistment. Battlewagons of All Kinds 

There are many different kinds of ship in the navy. The battleships are the mainstay of the fleet. Sixteen-inch guns superimposed upon 35,000 tons of equipment can smash their way into the vitals of anything that floats. Heavy and light cruisers are used to screen the battle line. 

Their guns are smaller, their armor plating thinner, their speed greater than those of the more cumbrous battleships. The aircraft carriers are hornets' nests on the move, ready at a moment's notice to send aloft swarms of spitting fighters, zooming bombers, deadly torpedo planes. Here the complement is different in degree. 

There are more mechanics and fliers, fewer gunners.

The destroyers that buck their way across the seas, escorting merchantmen and sending submarines to their doom with depth charges, offer quite an attraction for many sailors. One former naval officer when asked about his destroyer service replied, "I served eight years aboard destroyers, and for seven and a half of them I was seasick." But the ship's crew is more homogeneous and the action faster than on the heavier ships, which are so valuable that they aren't often exposed to the risks of battle.

There are also the auxiliary ships, the mine layers and sweepers, the tankers, the seaplane tenders, all the slow ships that must follow the fleet and carry its supplies. 

Then come the submarines, the "pig boats." The job is tough but the pay is higher in the submarine service, among the men who wear the dolphin insignia.

Only the fittest sailors are accepted for this dangerous work, and there is always a waiting list. In spite of all precautions, the air sometimes becomes foul when the ship is submerged, and the quarters are always cramped. But though the submarine sailor takes long risks, he's happy knowing that he plays the role of a David. 

One torpedo in the right place, and a mighty Goliath of a battleship may be sunk.

Last but not least are the latest additions to the fleet, the small mosquito boats, which have already won their first laurels in the waters off the Philippines.

The long fins attached to its caterpillar tread function like a paddle wheel in water, like useful lugs on land. The models are armored and carry guns, and are strong enough, if need be, to push over seven-inch trees. 

They Fight on Water, Too 

As "Soldiers of the Sea" the marines are known. That is what they are. They have served aboard the ships of the U. S. 

Navy since the first period of American history, when they clung to the ship's rigging and raked the enemy's decks with rifle fire. This action often meant the difference between defeat and victory. 

Of course, the day of sails and wooden ships has long since passed. But the marines are still aboard Uncle Sam's capital ships, manning the secondary guns and anti-aircraft batteries. A leatherneck also acts as orderly to the ship's commander, and the marines still retain their function as the spearhead in the event of a landing or boarding. 

Opportunities in aviation also are open to the marine. He may become a flying officer by being commissioned in the Marine Corps after training at a naval air station, or he may be picked for flying duty from the ranks of enlisted men. 

Men are given flying training only after several months' duty with the ground crew. They learn the airplane from the ground up. The enlisted flying marine may earn a rating as high as master technical sergeant. 

A "leatherneck" is a master of all trades in our armed forces and, as has been shown, he is equally at home on land, at sea, or in the air.

Defense Battalions 

As a part of the Navy Department, marines have another job to do. That job is to guard the shipyards and stations of the U. S. Navy, throughout the conti nental limits of the United States and at all foreign outposts.

This explains the presence of marines at the many Pacific islands, at Iceland, and at the new stations in the Western 

Hemisphere. The problem of protecting these outposts-outposts which enable the navy to operate effectively far from the shores of the United States-has led to the development of Defense Battalions by the Marine Corps. They are exactly what they are called. They defend all bases against every attack from any enemy. 

Marines are also responsible for the security of navy yards, supply depots, radio stations, training stations, and so on, in this country. The fact that marines are and always have been entrusted with this extremely important work speaks highly for the integrity of the corps and the efficiency of the guards. 

Promotion as Officers 

Enlisted marines may become commissioned officers in the corps. They may rise from the ranks of noncommissioned officers by virtue of meritorious and extraordinary service. These men must show the qualities of leadership that have marked Marine Corps officers since the inception of the corps on November 10, 1775. 

There is another way of winning a commission. A certain number of Annapolis graduates are commissioned as second lieutenants in the Marine Corps.

Model Troops 

In addition to serving as the spearhead of landing parties, units of the marines are often assigned to duty with the regular army in campaigns. That happened in the last war, and has happened in this war. In this war the marines have served courageously with the army on Bataan peninsula in the Philippine Islands, under

One word of caution, however. The marine's training is tough. From the very first, marines are taught to drive their bodies to the uttermost, to be able to put up with anything that comes their way. 

Fighting is their business, and it's risky.

This war is no respecter of sexes. The armies being put into the field are the largest the world has ever seen. Every available man who can shoulder a gun, steer a tank, shove shells into the butt-end of a 105-mm. howitzer, is going to be called to the front. The more men at the front, the more jobs behind it, both in the factories and in the army. When the corporal is taken from his desk at the quartermaster office; when the chief yeoman is sent on board a battleship from his paper-work job in the navy yard, somebody has to be found to take his place.

Right now, it looks like the people who slip into these administrative and desk jobs are going to be women. 

Soldiers in Skirts

Women are already serving in the army. There are nurses, to take but one example. The army wants all the registered nurses it can get. It is willing to make them commissioned officers and pay them substantial salaries. Hostesses are another example. Many recreation halls in army camps have hostesses to help direct the soldiers' social life, to prevent them from becoming lonesome.

This is only the beginning. Plans for women to volunteer for the army and the navy as auxiliary members already have been proposed. Britain's successful example is to be followed. And how did they do it in England?

The Navy, Too

The U. S. Navy is already planning to fill its offices with women yeomen, office workers just as they were in the last war. 

Every branch of the army will soon be staffed with women to handle the paper work. And the women in uniform will really be proving that this war affects. everybody. Not only every warring coun try, but every human being in those countries, will have his or her specific contribution to make to the victory.

Already the House has passed a bill calling for the formation of a women's auxiliary reserve in the navy. Senate approval is expected almost immediately. 

Such a reserve would release thousands of men for active service.

This reserve would be open to any woman over 20. If the navy goes up to a million men—and the future may prove that to be a conservative estimate—about 

150,000 women would be required for the Naval Reserve. More than 1,000 women would be used for decoding work alone. Past experience, on a small scale, has shown that women in such work are fully the equal of men.

Grade for grade, women would receive the same pay as their brother gobs. 

As proposed, the law sets no limitation on the rank they may attain, although high naval officials believe that the rank of lieutenant commander will be as high as they will go.

No Bed of Roses 

This book has avoided telling you how to get the most out of the armed services by giving the least. The army is no bed of roses. This war will not be won until 

Americans venture their very all, their lives themselves, in the great effort to crush the Axis. Production is all to the good, but tanks themselves never kill enemy soldiers, smash enemy positions. 

It takes men who are willing to risk their lives inside the tanks to do that. You can build airplanes until there aren't enough landing fields in the world to accommodate them, but unless you have fliers who are willing to risk their lives inside those planes, they won't win a single battle. 

War is a terrible thing. Nobody denies that. So are surgical operations. Sometimes the body becomes so corrupt with disease that unless the person is willing to risk his life in a serious, painful opera. tion, he will die. So too, the world be. comes so corrupt that armed resistance against an enemy, with full consideration of the risks involved, is the sole possible course of action.

This book has one purpose, and only one. It is to show you where you can best serve. Pay increases, commissions, promotions-all these things are incidental. The main thing is that you mobilize yourself on a basis for total war. If you, in the army, can work up to a corporal's stripes but stay a private; if you have the talent
