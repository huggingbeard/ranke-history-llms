FOREWORD 

This booklet is prepared to meet the special safety hazards that may be imposed on any local community in the present war emergency. All the basal safety teachings needed are amply provided for in THE ROAD TO SAFETY. How ever, the application and extension of these safety principles to war conditions will do much to prepare our boys and girls to meet war emergency situations. It is to be hoped that we shall all be spared the horrors that modern warfare has brought to civilians of other countries. Our children, however, are fully aware of what has happened elsewhere in bombed cities. They shudder in silence now over the horrors. It will be a relief for them to learn that much of this fear is groundless if they follow some simple 

Safety rules; to learn that obedience, orderliness, and the use of common sense go far toward insuring safety even in war.

A New Lesson 

"We shall not have our spelling this morning," said Miss Brown. "I have something to talk over with you. It has to do with the safety of each of us." 

The teacher spoke quietly but she was very earnest. The class sat up straight. 

What had happened? "You "It is about the war," she said. have all seen pictures of cities ruined by bombs dropped from airplanes. You have heard your parents tell of the terrible things happening in other countries. Well, we are in the war now, as you know. The things we have read about may happen here. We must all be ready. "Some of us have brothers in the army. 

They have gone to fight for our country. 

We, too, can all be soldiers. One of the first things we can do is to keep ourselves and our homes safe from harm. 3

"This morning I am asking you boys and girls to join our new army. It will be a big army, made up of millions of girls and boys, men and women. There will be in our ranks firemen, policemen, aviators, sailors, and soldiers. Our army will be called the Defenders of Safety. All who are ready to join this army will rise, face the flag, and salute!"

There was a moment of silence. Then every boy and girl stood at salute, proud to be of help to his country. "Fine! Be seated. We shall be on the march in a few minutes. As Captain of this company I give you these orders. 

Keep cool and obey. "Order 1. When the signal bell rings, fall in as you do for a fire drill. "Order 2. When I say 'Forward! 

March!' proceed quietly and slowly to the inner hall on the second floor. Take your places in rank along the left wall.

"Order 3. Stand quietly while other companies assemble, and await orders. "Any questions? The signal will ring soon." "Captain Brown, do we take our wraps?" asked Mary Little. "No, Private Mary. Do exactly as you do in a fire drill. But march to the hall, not to the outdoors."

Bzz! Ting-a-ling. Bzz! "That's the warning signal! Fall in! 

Forward! March!" 

Tramp, tramp, tramp! Not a whisper! 

Not a jostle! The young soldiers led by

Joe Wilson and Tommy Traddles marched bravely ahead. Captain Brown followed.

Tramp, tramp! The whole school was marching! Into the big empty hall they marched, and the pupils of each room lined up in their proper places. A large poster was on the wall. It read: "Air 

Raid Safety Zone, Rooms 2, 4, 7, 8, 9.” 5

"Children," spoke the Principal. "This is an air-raid drill. You did it perfectly the first time. You are good soldiers. You are good Defenders of Safety. I am proud to be your Colonel. I want you to meet the Fire Chief. He will explain why we are doing this.”

The Fire Chief was a big man and he looked very serious. His brass buttons glittered as he spoke. "Boys and Girls. All over our state this kind of thing is happening today.

We hope we shall never have an air raid, but we must be ready. Take a look at this room. It is part of a strong brick and steel building. There are no windows. 

Solid walls are on all sides of us. When bombs are dropped from planes, most of them explode on hitting the earth.

Few of them hit buildings except in crowded cities. The danger comes from the flying metal from the exploded bomb.

If these pieces of iron hit a window, then the shattered glass is an added danger. 

Keep away from windows in a raid. "We chose this room because it has no windows and has strong walls. It is also above ground so that pieces of rock and earth will not reach us. 

It has many doors. This is the safest place for us to be now. "Outside, one of our fire trucks is ready to take care of any blaze. Your duty is to stay here quietly until your Principal gives you further orders.” 

Then the Police Chief stepped in, and the "Colonel" asked him to speak. "The job of the police," said he, "is to see that everybody obeys orders. This must be done in an air raid. You know what to do when you are in school. Suppose you are coming to school or going home when the alarm sounds. Well, there are air-raid wardens in every neighbor-

hood. They will tell you what to do. So will any policeman. "If nobody is near, get into the best shelter you can find. If no houses are around, get off the street and lie flat, face down, in a lot or field. Stay there till the 'All 

Clear!' signal sounds. You might pick out good shelter spots as you go to and from school. Keep cool! Do not run!

Take care of any little brothers and sisters who are with you. Brave soldiers do not lose their heads. And talk this over with your parents. They can help you."

Bzz! Ting-a-ling. Bzz! "The air-raid drill is over for today," said the Principal. "Be as orderly going back to your rooms as you were coming in. Miss Brown, you are first." "Fall in! Forward! March!"

And tramp, tramp, tramp! Back to their classroom marched the new soldiers, with Joe Wilson and Tommy Traddles

proudly leading the way. Captain Brown brought up the rear. 

This is the way we made up our army to fight for our country. We are happy to be Defenders of Safety. 

A Home Blackout 

"So you had an air-raid drill today," said Father as he laid down his paper. 

Grace shut her speller, and Bob put his science book on the table. It was after dinner. Mother was knitting for the Red 

Cross in front of the open fire. "Do you think such a thing is needed, John? The war seems so far away." "It is good to be ready, Margaret. Many people in the world were not ready once. 

They have paid dearly. We are in the war now, and planes fly a long way. Has our air warden been here yet?"

"Yes," said Mother. "I meant to tell you that Mr. Robbins called this afternoon, took our names, and asked us to get ready for a blackout." "A blackout!" shouted both children. "Yes, he said we may have a drill. And 

John, which is our safest room? I got some blackout shades last week." "You did!" smiled Father. "I see we all think alike about being ready. Have you picked out the room?” "I vote for the basement!" shouted Bob. "It's below ground and safe from pieces of shell and earth.” "I vote for a bedroom," said Grace. "If planes come, I can hide in the bed!" 

Bob laughed. "You can't do that now, Grace. You are a soldier. Dad, we all belong to the Defenders of Safety. It is an army to save life and property." "Why, that's fine! You are a fighting 

Red Cross, aren't you?" said Mother. 10

Father saluted. "And now, soldiers, how about the room? Let's take a look." 

Upstairs, downstairs, and basement!

They marched all over the house, and back to the living room. "Before we vote," said Father, "let me read something from the paper: 

Your safest room in the home may be on the ground floor. It should have two or three doors, and few windows. Food and water should be near 

"I vote for the kitchen!" broke in Bob. "Make it two," added Grace. 

Mother laughed. "You children vote for food! So do I! I have already made the shades for its two windows!" "There!" said Father. "I have no chance to vote. But you are right. We can step from the kitchen into the pantry. 

Then only walls are around us." 11

The doorbell rang. It was Mr. Robbins. "Come in," called Father. "We are ready for you. Margaret, will you bring the shades? Mr. Robbins can look the job over."

The heavy, black shades were put up. 

Bob turned out all the lights but one in the kitchen. Then they all went outside. "Black as a coal!" said the Warden. "Good job! Do you know the blackout signal? Good! Remember, too, to unlock the doors and open the windows. 

Bring your radio to the kitchen, and tune in on a local station. The Army may give us news. But do not use the telephone. Good night!"

The family hurried back to the fire. "Well, children," said Father, "this is a good day's work. We hope there will be no raids. Anyway this home is as ready as it can be made." 12

Caught On The Road 

It was Friday night. Father, Mother,

Bob, and Grace were in the car, going to see a war picture. Bob and Grace hoped it would show a blackout. "Dad, they told us today about raids in vacation time," said Bob. "If we are playing near a home, we go in. All homes are open. If we are in the fields, we scatter, and lie face down." 

Then it came. The siren cried out long and loud and often. "It's the blackout raid signal, Daddy!"

Father slowed to the right-hand curb. "All out! Car lights out!" said he. "And there go the street lights, too." "There's a house!" said Mother. "And there go their lights off. Let's go in. I don't know who lives there." "We shall soon find out," and Father rang the bell and opened the door. 13

"Come this way," said a man's voice. "Here's our safety room, with light inside. And here's my family."

They all began to talk. People like company at such times. "Our lights are all out at home," said 

Mother. "And your shades are like mine." "Your dining room is a good safe place,” spoke Father. "Any news on the radio?" "No. The station gave the blackout signal. Now they are off the air." "Will we be late for the movie?" asked Bob. "No. The picture house is dark, too."

A signal came from the radio. Then a voice: "That's the All Clear signal."

Bob and Grace saw the movie. It showed a city blacked out. The street lights had only slits of light showing. "I'm glad, John, ours was only a drill," said Mother. "There's some good to these drills. We come to know our neighbors." 14

Some Things to Know 

Sand is good to put out fires. Fire

Wardens throw it on fire bombs. In some cities, bags of sand are put on the roofs and inside rooms of big buildings. 

Sand in bags is also good to pile up around buildings. It stops flying pieces of bombs. 

Some cities have many air lookouts. 

Men and women stay there day and night watching for airplanes. They telephone all news to the Army Staff. In this way these cities have time to blackout, and to send up fighter planes.

Many boys and girls now are making airplane models. This is a good way to learn all kinds of planes. Can you tell the kinds of planes apart? 

Other boys and girls study First Aid. 

Can you bandage a hurt finger? 15
