Twenty-four faces of the snub cube correspond to the faces of the gyroid (Being a hemihedral form of the Hexootahedron). 

Sixty faces of the snub dodecahedron correspond to the faces of the gyroid (Being a hemihedral form of the Hexicosahedron). Both of these solids may be made in right hand and left hand models. 

The solids illustrated have edges of equal length, all except No. 11 and No. 16 have regular polygons as faces. No. 11 and No. 16 are the semi-regular solids. No. 1 to No. 5 are the regular Platonic solids. The remaining thirteen are called Archimedean solids. (2-3) (4-5) (11-13) (16-17) are reciprocals, that is to say, when the two solids are properly oriented, every vertex on one solid will correspond in position with the center of a face on the other solid. 

Other combinations of faces may be arranged so that the solid is perfectly symmetrical but all of the faces are not neoessarily regular polygons. A sheet illustrating forty-eight of these regular and irregular faoed polyhedrons is available for ten cents in coin.

Another interesting combination of solids are the penetrating polyhedrons. These consist of two, or more polyhedrons, but one inside of the other, a portion of the faces of the outer polyhedron being cut in such a manner as to allow the corners of the inner polyhedron to project through. When the inner and outer polyhedrons are made of different color card the penetrating polyhedrons are very attractive. A sheet illustrating forty-eight of the penetrating models is available for ten cents. 

A sheet containing illustrations of forty-eight ideal crystal forms of minerals is available for ten cents.

As stated before all of the geometric solids illustrated on page two have edges of equal length, it should therefore be possible for the student to design his own templates for the arrangement of the faces. 

There may be several ways of arrangement of the templates to produce the same results, and the student is at liberty to follow his own ideas. It is not necessary to include all of the faces in one template, in most cases much better results are obtained by making a template with only one half, or even one quarter of the faces and then making the necessary number of forms to suit. This arrangement allows some adjustment in case the template or the forms are a little out of true. 

A few hints are given for laying out some of the templates.

A study of the diagram will show the relation of the cube, tetrahedron and octahedron. The line KM is equal to length of an edge of cube, RK represents diagonal of square face of cube, it also represents length of an edge of tetrahedron. The outline of the cube is represented by the figure RKMS, the tetrahedron by RKP and the octahedron by CLDT. The centers of the three solide coincide and all vertices lie in surface of a sphere of same diameter as circle. SK and PM are drawn through the diagonal corners of the cube, and these diagonal lines also pass through the center of the faces of the tetrahedron and of the octahedron. As the lines CL and RP represent the altitudes of the triangles forming the faces of the octahedron and the tetrahedron respectively, then the octahedral axes will cut these altitudes at one third of their height from their bases. As diagram No. 22 is used for making solids that are modifications of the cube, in the same way diagram No. 24 is used for making irregular faced solids that are modifications of the dodecahedron. 

First draw a circle and inscribe a regular pentagon, draw line AB also center line. Now draw the diagram by making side of square FK equal AB plus CD. Make NL and JQ equal to AB. Make UW and HG equal to CD. Draw the diagonals UG, WH, NQ and LJ. Draw EN, EJ, DL and DQ. 

The figure bounded by ENLDQJ is the outline of an icosahedron (A4). 

The figure bounded by UMHGYW is the outline of a dodecahedron (A5). 

The figure bounded by UNLHGQJW is the outline of a triacontahedron (A16). The diagonals UG and HW pass through the center of the triangular faces of the icosahedron. The diagonals NQ and LJ pass through the center of the pentagonal faces of the dodecahedron. In the diagram JQ and NL represent the length of the edges of the triangular faces of the icosahedron. UW and HG represent the length of the edges of the dodecahedron.

As an aid for making the irregular faced solids, instruction folders are available, diagrams are drawn out similar to Fig. 23 and the method for laying out the shape of the face is given in detail, so that the student by making the diagrams and following the printed instructions may complete the model of the solid. 

On the following page are illustrations of twenty-four solids, the numerals refer to those described in the instruction folders. The first eight illustrations are of solids having faces composed mostly of irregular shaped polygons, but the solids are symmetrical in every way. The next eight illustrations are of solids of the penetrating type. Nos. 41-45-52 and 67 are some of the reciprocal cases referred to on page 11. If coloring is added to the projecting corners, the individual solide will stand out clearly. The last eight illustrations are of models of the crystal forms of minerals; the making of the crystal models is very interesting.
