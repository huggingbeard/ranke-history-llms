UNIVERSITY PAYMENTS IN LIEU OF LOCAL PROPERTY TAXES INTRODUCTION 

The problem of local tax exemption of university property has been a Generally, university property is not troublesome one in many communities. subject to local taxation. As a result, relations between university administrators and officials of communities serving as the site of such institutions have not always been as amicable as might be hoped for. 

But over the past 30 years, a growing number of educational institutions, both public and private, have attempted to offset the loss of tax revenue to local communities from the land which they occupy, by making payments to local governments in lieu of local property taxes, or by making contributions toward the cost of services provided by local government. 

Scope of Survey 

This report is based on information received from the five other state universities located in New England (The University of Connecticut at Storrs; the University of Maine at Orono; the University of New Hampshire at 

Durham; the University of Rhode Island at Kingston and the University of Vermont at Burlington); from two eastern state universities (The Pennsylvania 

State University at University Park and Cornell University at Ithaca, New York); and two midwestern state universities (The University of Illinois at Urbana and the University of Michigan at Ann Arbor). One southern university, which has requested anonymity, is also included in the survey. 

In addition, information from a few private institutions is also included. They are: Harvard University, Radcliffe College and the Massachusetts 

Institute of Technology at Cambridge, Mass. and Princeton University at Princeton, New Jersey.

Types of Payments 

With the exception of Connecticut, which by statute reimburses towns for loss of taxes on land owned by the state and used for the University of Connecticut, all state and private universities which make payments do so in accord with voluntary agreements with the towns or cities concerned. Usually these agreements are on a contractual basis. 

The bases of such payments follow no uniform pattern. Payments in some cases are "in lieu of taxes"; in other cases, to defray total costs of certain facilities jointly used by the university and the community or for projects mutually beneficial to the university and the community. In some cases, no payments are made but there is a cooperative arrangement in force to provide services which would otherwise be duplicated. (For example, residents of Durham, New Hampshire may use the University Library, and the town pays only a nominal cost for this general privilege for its inhabitants). Some universities remain completely aloof from financial problems of their local governments.

POLICIES OF STATE UNIVERSITIES New England 

(a) University of Connecticut at Storrs.

Connecticut law provides for reimbursement of towns for loss of taxes on state-owned property, as follows: "On or before August first, annually, the tax commissioner shall determine, as of October first of the year preceding, the value of all land owned by the state and used ... for the University of Connecticut ... The commissioner shall determine the value of the land so used, classifying each tract, and the state shall pay to the town wherein the same is situated a certain sum as a grant in lieu of taxes thereon, which sum shall be ascertained by multiplying the number of acres of such land, treating each class separately, by the average assessed value per acre of similar land which is taxable in such town, and the result so obtained shall be multiplied by the rate of the tax last laid by the town wherein such land is situated, provided land of the state shall be valued on the basis of acreage ... ... "Valuations so determined for the purpose of reimbursing towns for loss of taxes on property acquired by the state after January 1, 1929 for the University of Connecticut ... shall, in no case, be less than the assessed value of the land with permanent improvements thereon at the time of its acquisition by the state and an average assessed valuation shall be taken for a period of five years prior to the acquisition by the state as a basis for arriving at a fair and just valuation of such lands and improvements." (Section 1030D, 1955 Supp.)

No concrete figures were provided by the University. (b) University of Maine at Orono. From 1947-1957, the University has made funds available to the town of Orono in various amounts averaging $4,000. to $5,000. per year. These funds have been earmarked for mutually beneficial projects, with the majority of the funds being used for fire protection services.

(c) University of New Hampshire at Durham. The General Court of New 

Hampshire has never considered a bill to authorize in lieu payments nor has discussion ever reached the level of legislative debate. 

However, the University cooperates extensively in providing services to Durham, Residents of the town may use the University Library at token cost to the town. The University helps to provide fire and police services; it operates the town water supply plant; it has contributed to the construction of a sewage disposal plant. In short, the approach is to develop a system of mutual benefits to offset tax exemption. (d) University of Rhode Island, Kingston. The University makes no payments to the town. It does maintain its own police force and it has a close working relationship with the local fire district. Fire district headquarters and equipment are located on the university campus and fire district personnel is made up mainly of university employees.

2. There is one fire department, maintained by the city. No fire protection unit is maintained by the University. Three years ago, the University began paying 18% of the city's annual fire department expenses.

The University also pays the established charges for all direct customer services rendered by the city such as water, sewer and the like. From time to time the University has entered into street and sidewalk improvement programs with the city where University property is adjacent to the street improvement. (b) University of Illinois, Urbana, "In lieu payments" are made by the University to the cities of Urbana and Champaign under terms of an agreement between the University and the two cities. No "in lieu payments" are made for student dormitories but payments are made on staff housing. A few years ago, the University began buying houses in the community which it rents to newcomers who usually have a limit of three years occupancy. On this type of property, the University pays an amount equivalent to what the current property tax would bring.

In the case of an apartment house jointly occupied by students and staff, it is necessary to determine the occupancy as between the two types of persons. Any person taking the equivalent of a half-time program of study as a student is classified as a student and no payment is required for the unit which he occupies. Payment is made for those persons who are teaching full time or teaching one-half time or more. 

The University owns several farms and payment is made on those which are income-producing but no payment is made on experimental farms. 

With respect to special assessments, the University always enters into an agreement with the local communities and pays its share of special assessments for streets, sewers and the like.

(c) Cornell University at Ithaca, New York. Cornell is both a private and a public institution but tax exemption or payment policies do not vary between the public and private portions. 

Lands and improvements for non-profit educational purposes are exempt from all taxes in New York State. In 1951, however, special provision was made to permit sewer charges to be separated from general tax levies and imposed upon otherwise exempt institutions as a rental or special charge (Chapter 165). Since 1951, Cornell has paid the City of Ithaca a contract amount for sewage disposal, aggregating a charge of $25,000. for 300 million gallons per year.

No faculty housing is owned by the university but in recent years it has erected apartments for married students. These apartments are exempt from property taxes but the University has adopted a policy of paying to the local school district "an amount in lieu of taxes equal to its cost per child minus state aid per child for each school age child living in such apartments." (d) The Pennsylvania State University at University Park. The University does not pay any property tax and it does not make any payment in lieu thereof.

Although it is not obliged to do so, the University withholds a local wage tax from employees required to pay the tax, which is said to be a substantial assistance to local tax authorities. POLICIES OF PRIVATE UNIVERSITIES In Massachusetts 

The city of Cambridge made a 20-year agreement in 1928 with Harvard 

University, Radcliffe College and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. 

Under the terms of this agreement the educational institutions agreed to stagger over a period of years the number of real properties taken off the city's tax rolls. This agreement was made at a time when homes were being built along the Charles River and University property amounted to a sizeable value. 

The percentage was

The Harvard agreement provided that land and buildings owned by the University on July 1, 1928 which were not withdrawn from taxation, but which could be exempt if used for academic purposes would not be withdrawn at a rate greater than 10% a year of all such land and buildings. determined on the basis of 1928 assessments both for property withdrawn and for the total of the property. Land acquired after 1928 was subject to taxes, to be computed by applying the current tax rate to the assessed value of the land only, at the time of its acquisition. 

The M.I.T. agreement applied specifically to a former hotel converted to a graduate student house. It provided for annual payments on the property in addition to payments called for in its basic agreement. 

In 1948, a second 20-year agreement was made by Harvard, which was separate from new agreements made by Radcliffe and M. I.T. Under the new agreement, Harvard would continue to pay the city in lieu of taxes on property bought by the University after 1948, with payments to be based on the assessed valuation of the land in the year the property was purchased but according to the tax rate current.

Payments are made only on land values and not on dwellings.

Outside Massachusetts

In 1946, Princeton University was making annual payments to the Borough of Princeton in the amount of $35,000 per year. A 1946 breakdown indicates the following sub-totals: Annual donation, $9,000; Board of Health, 1/ $700; Police, $2,185; Incinerator, $1,430; Sewerage system, $21,125. No formal agreement governs the first four items listed. In As far back as 1909 the University made a donation equal to 10% of the total taxes raised by the borough for schools and local government. 

1914 the basis of payment was changed and the University made a gentlemen's agreement to donate a flat sum annually ($9,000 in 1946). 

In 1924 the University donated land to Princeton Borough as a site for an incinerator plant provided that the land would revert to the University when not needed for this purpose. The incinerator was built and by a tri-party agreement (University, Borough and Township) the University paid 25% of the construction cost (not to exceed $4,500) and 30% of the annual operating costs. 

In 1935 the plant was replaced by a new one and a new agreement was made whereby construction, amortization and operating costs were figured according to the percentage of total tonnage delivered to the plant by each of the three parties. A joint committee meets annually to make payment decisions. 

A tri-party agreement also governs the cost of constructing, amortizing, operating and maintaining the sewerage system. Metered water consumption compared to volume of sewage passing through the plant determines the share to be paid by each party.

APPENDIX A SAMPLE OF INDIVIDUAL LETTERS SENT TO THE PRESIDENTS OF UNIVERSITIES January 30, 1958 Dear Dr. 

We have been asked by a member of the General Court to obtain information relative to property tax policies adopted by cities and towns which serve as the site of a state university. 

In this case, a pending bill would authorize a locality to collect property taxes from the faculty buildings owned by the state university. This proposal will fail because an instrumentality of the state cannot be so taxed.

The legislative leaders evidently wish, however, to make a reasonable arrangement whereby a payment would be made by the university in lieu of taxes. It is with respect to that kind of solution that I am asked to uncover information as to similar agreements made by other universities, and particularly the amount of the payments made. 

Would you be kind enough to inform me what your policy is relative to such payments, adding specific dollar data when applicable and available?

When writing me I hope you will include any special authorities or other setup which may remove the ownership and operations of certain properties from the direct control of the university because the problem remains despite such peculiarities of place and time.
