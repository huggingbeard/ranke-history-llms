INTRODUCTION 

The estimates published in this bulletin are complete so far as they are available. For the years 1918 to 1938 fresh market estimates are not strictly comparable with later years. The earlier estimates related largely to production in well recognized commercial areas producing primarily for shipment to distant markets; and, hence did not include some local market production in areas near consuming centers. Furthermore, all estimates relate only to commercial production for sale and therefore all production for home use is excluded. 

The United States Census of Agriculture which is conducted once every 5 years serves the purpose among others, of providing "bench-marks" from which annual crop estimates are made each year by the Statistical Reporting Service. This bulletin contains selected census data by counties for each crop. Although the census totals for many crops are in many cases not in precise agreement with the official estimates because of necessary adjustments, the totals by county provide reliable statistics on the number of farms reporting and acres for harvest. By applying State average yields and value in census years and by using interpolations between census years approximate estimates by counties can be derived in most cases. 

The official series of estimates covering potato production in Pennsylvania are not included in this publication. Historically these estimates have been treated as an individual field crop series. A separate publication issued from this office covering this topic is available free of charge upon request.

This bulletin is likewise available to the general public upon request.

VEGETABLE PRODUCTION IN PENNSYLVANIA 

Total income from the sale of all vegetables by Pennsylvania farmers in 1960 is estimated at 39.8 million dollars or 5 percent of the State total gross income from sale of all agricultural commodities. Although not a major component of the total farm income situation it can be readily seen that vegetables make an important contribution to Pennsylvania agriculture. 

Because vegetable production is particularly suited to the areas of Erie County, Potter, Lackawanna, Centre, Columbia, Northumberland, Luzerne, Schuylkill, Allegheny, Adams, Franklin, York, Berks, Bucks,

Chester, Lancaster, Lebanon and Montgomery, the production of vegetable crops is localized to some extent and their importance is intensified within these areas. 

There are also numerous other local areas not mentioned in the counties above which derive at least part of their income from the sale of vegetables. 

Within the fresh market segment in Pennsylvania, a much larger share of vegetable production than is usual for most other States is sold at the retail level by growers. There are numerous growers who attend their booths at one or more city markets each week. 

In addition, because of their proximity to urban areas. there are a great variety of roadside stands, huckster routes, etc., where growers market their vegetables.

In a typical year recorded rail and truck unloads in the large commercial wholesale markets account for a fairly small percentage of total sales. Average prices received by growers tend to average somewhat higher than usual because of the higher percentage of retail sales.

A thriving vegetable processing industry is also located within the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. There are more than 40 individual vegetable processing plants that each year manufacture a wide variety of canned. and frozen products made from tomatoes, sweet corn, snap beans, lima beans, green peas and other vegetable crops purchased from Pennsylvania growers. The proximity of large metropolitan areas is of benefit again in that good markets are close at hand. 3

PENNSYLVANIA VEGETABLE FARMS ARE DECLINING STEADILY 

Since 1939 and with the exception of the World War II period, the number of Pennsylvania farms producing vegetables for sale as well as the acreage devoted to commercial vegetable production has shown a steadily declining trend.

In 1939 the number of farms producing vegetables for sale in Pennsylvania stood at slightly over 19,000. 

Towards the end of hostilities during World War II 

the number had climbed to nearly 29,000. By 1949 the number was reduced to a peacetime level of nearly 15,000. Ten years later in 1959 the number had declined further to a level of slightly over 8,000 farms. 

During the 20-year period 1939-59 the number of commercial vegetable farms in Pennsylvania declined 57 percent.

The number of acres of commercial vegetables harvested for sale since 1939 followed generally the same trend as the number of farms. Probably the only outstanding difference was the fact that the rate of decline in acres harvested was less than that for the number of farms. During the period 1939-59 the number of acres of vegetables harvested for sale declined 18 percent.

In 1939 each vegetable farm in Pennsylvania produced an average of 4.7 acres for sale. The ratio increased to 5.4 acres per farm in 1944 and has continued to show a steady increase every year. By 1959 each commercial vegetable farm produced an average of 8.9 acres.

ASPARAGUS 

Acreage in Pennsylvania increased from a level of 680 acres in 1918 to a peak of 2,500 acres in 1932. This was followed by a steadily declining trend until 1953. 

From that time until the present the level has been generally stabilized in the 800-900 acre range. 

Average yields per acre since 1918 have not demonstrated any particular trend either up or down. 

Since 1918 average prices received by growers hit a peak in 1920 and a low in 1932. Following this low, prices were on the up trend until 1957 where another peak was reached.

The principal production areas are located in Lancaster, Bucks and Erie counties. With the exception of a small acreage in the Lancaster area which is utilized for processing each year, the remainder of the crop is sold in fresh market channels. Most of it is sold for local consumption. Recorded movement through commercial channels in 1961 accounted for less than 10 percent of the crop in that year.

SNAP BEANS 

Total acreage in Pennsylvania since 1918 increased steadily, reaching a peak in 1944. Following this high, acreage leveled off to some extent. The introduction of the mechanical picker in 1960, however, gives promise of the possibility at least of some increase in future years.

A large part of the Pennsylvania crop is used for processing each year. In 1961 nearly three-fourths of the total was used in this manner. Processors in turn produce both a canned and frozen pack. 

In 1961 snap beans ranked third among all other vegetable crops produced in both acreage harvested and in total value. 

Potter County ranked first in 1959 for the production of snap beans. Following in order were York, Erie and 

Bucks Counties.

All of the crop is normally sold in fresh market channels except for occasional small quantities purchased by processors for freezing.

CABBAGE 

Cabbage acreage followed an increasing trend during the period 1918-1944. Following the peak reached in 1944 the trend has been declining. 

In 1961 cabbage ranked fifth among all vegetable crops in terms of total acres harvested. It ranked fourth among all vegetable crops in total value with receipts to growers amounting to over 1.5 million dollars.

The leading counties in order of total acres harvested in 1959 were Luzerne, Schuylkill, Erie, Indiana and Lackawanna.

CARROTS 

Production of carrots in Pennsylvania followed a rising trend in acreage from 1929 through the World War II period. Since that time output has been declining. 

Of note is the fact that Bucks County produced 55 percent of the total acreage in 1959. Montgomery 

County was second and Lancaster County third. 

A large part of the crop normally moves into processor channels. In 1961 over four-fifths of the crop was used in this manner.

SWEET CORN 

In terms of total acres harvested sweet corn is first among all vegetable crops produced in Pennsylvania. 

In 1961 nearly 27,000 acres were harvested, bringing cash receipts to farmers of nearly 3.5 million dollars. 

During the past 5 years fresh market acreage ranged from 15,000 to 18,000 acres. In 1961 the fresh segment accounted for nearly three-fifths of the total acreage and income amounted to 2.7 million dollars. 

Processing records going back to 1918 show a steadily increasing trend in acres harvested with a peak of nearly 17,000 acres in 1943. Since that time there has been some decline in acreage utilized for this purpose. 

However, higher average yields have helped to maintain income at a generally steady level. 

In 1959 York County was the leading county with 5,061 acres harvested. Bucks County was second, Lancaster was third, Adams fourth and Luzerne County ranked fifth.

GREEN PEAS

878,000 dollars. 

York County in 1959 with 1,020 acres ranked first, Centre was second, Columbia third, Adams fourth and 

Potter fifth.

SPINACH 

Total acreage of spinach ranked sixth among all Pennsylvania vegetables produced in 1961. From a low of 1,350 acres in 1929 acreage climbed steadily, reaching a peak of 7,200 in 1949. Following this the trend has been sharply reversed. In 1961 the figure stood at 

1,850 acres.

In farm receipts spinach ranked sixth among all other vegetable crops produced in the Keystone State. 

Gross income from spinach in 1961 totaled 740,000 dollars.

Published estimates include fresh market and processing components. In recent years most of the crop has been sold in fresh market channels because of higher average prices received by growers. In 1961 fresh market acreage accounted for over 97 percent of the total.

U. S. Census figures for 1959 show that Bucks was the leading county with 1,703 acres. Monroe County was second, Dauphin third, Luzerne fourth, and Montgomery County fifth.

TOMATOES 

Acreage planted to tomatoes in 1961 amounted to 24 percent of the total for all vegetables. They ranked second among all vegetable crops harvested in the Keystone State. In 1921 acreage was at a low of 890 but by 1945 a high of over 37,000 acres was recorded. The trend since this high point has been steadily declining. 

Average yields per acre have been on an uptrend. 

Average processing yields in 1918 stood at 3.5 tons per acre but by 1961 a record of 12.9 tons per acre was set. 

A major part of the increase in average yield per acre has taken place since 1958. 

Income-wise tomatoes rank first among all vegetables in Pennsylvania. Gross receipts to growers in 1961 totaled 6.9 million dollars or 39 percent of the total vegetable income. 

Tomatoes are used for both fresh market and processing. In 1961 processing acreage accounted for nearly four-fifths of the total. Products are catsup, canned tomatoes, juice, tomato paste and others.

Lancaster County in 1959 with 3,265 acres ranked first, Bucks County was second, York third, Adams fourth, Chester fifth, and Luzerne sixth.
