Most of the facts recorded in the following pages can be found in the Good Will Record, but they are scattered through forty-seven volumes, beginning in 1888. It may be said that the facts are not of sufficient importance to be thus assembled. My only reply is that I rather enjoy doing this sort of thing anyway.

"He grew in wisdom and stature and in favor with God and man." In the earliest days of Good Will, and even before the first material foundations had been laid, I used to explain that the undertaking which I was cherishing, talking about and hoping to see materialize was best illustrated by the spirit of the Y. M. C. A. The churches had placed the emphasis upon spiritual things-the development of the right spirit in the heart of men; the schools were emphasizing the intellectual-the development of the mind; the aim in military circles was the physical-the putting of the body in fighting trim; while a "worldly element" gave heed to the requirement of society only-the social side of human nature. But as years passed, the Y. M. C. A. triangle seemed imperfect for the purposes of the Good Will Home Association, and I was convinced that a square rather than a triangle or circle or any other figure best symbolized what was to be called "The Good Will Idea."

Ray W. Tobey had graduated from the Good Will High 

School and was spending four years at Dartmouth College. 

One day he told me of a bit of fence which, from year to year was bequeathed by the Dartmouth seniors to the class which was to immediately follow them on the campus. Mr. Tobey had been impressed by the seriousness with which the college students, class after class, accepted the fence, and in turn bequeathed it; the ceremony was not, apparently, a joke, but had a serious significance. He had wondered if something of a similar nature could be planned or evolved at Good Will. 

Mr. Tobey returned to Dartmouth; but his suggestion lived 11

Fred P. Rawson, a Good Will boy, had graduated from the high school; he had unusual mechanical ability-he loved to do things with tools. In the process of time he was asked to fashion an emblem of wood (Greek, Em blama-meaning "inlaid") which was provided for him from near the Equator; it was to be inlaid with four pieces of wood which were also provided-olive wood from Jerusalem for ancient or Scriptural history; white pine from the battleship "Constitution"-Old Ironsides"-for national history; a bit from a rafter in the first boys' home-Good Will Cottage-at Good

Will-for local history; and a piece of pine, delicately tinted red, from the cradle in which a mother had rocked her children, and beside which, as she rocked them, she offered her prayers for them and sang her lullabies, the most remarkable to me of which was "Hush, my dear, lie still and slumber, Holy angels guard thy bed."

Inlaid on the underside was to be a square plate of silver, on which was engraved a single word in old English characters, which, theoretically, was to be known only to alumni of the Good Will High School and the members of the senior class in each succeeding year.

The way the idea was adopted, the heartiness with which the senior class each year escorted the emblem to the Senior 

Spring, and after the Junior Class Day exercises at Senior

Spring had been carried out, each junior becoming a fullfledged senior by drinking water from the Senior Spring out of the senior cup, the emblem was passed in silence to the newly-made senior class, and accepted by its new guardians in silence, was escorted back to the library buildingall this has become familiar to friends of Good Will. The gist of the entire ceremony is in the fact that by general consent, the four sides represent the four sides of a symmetrical character-the physical, social, intellectual and

REINSOPHY 

13 spiritual or religious. 

The ceremony meant that in its educational and philanthropic scheme, the Good Will Home Association was recognizing the four great interests, giving due prominence to each, without undue emphasis upon any one of them. It was understood, too, that when the newlymade seniors at the Senior Spring accepted the emblem and the cup, symbolizing a well-balanced life and character, it was accepting responsibility for the maintenance and support of these ideals through their next or senior year. 

Each year the ceremony at the Spring was improved, until it became a ritual, always interesting, always impressive. 

In 1931 the Averill High School Building was dedicated. 

A niche in the foyer of the structure had been prepared for the cup and emblem; before the building was open for inspection the emblem was escorted by the entire community in an impressive procession from the Carnegie Library to the niche prepared for it, and it was placed there, behind plate glass; the building was then declared opened, ready for visitors. This was the place for the emblem-the Good Will 

Emblem and the Senior Cup, which had been presented for this special ceremony by Y. M. C. A. boys on the Pacific

The heart of the "Good Will Idea" is embodied in the four-square block of wood, which has frequently been described; the wood, with its inlaid pieces, symbolize the life at Good Will. Anyone can see it by paying a visit to the 

Averill High School Building; what the Ark of the Covenant was to the Ancient Hebrews, what the statue of Pallas was to the ancient Greeks, the "Emblem" is to the Good Will community. But it is not a fetich; there is no superstitution attached to it; it is a symbol-nothing more. But to put all that the emblem symbolizes into one word was an interesting task-problem, if you please; but I hoped some day it could be done.

The word I sought must not be too long; it must not be complicated; but it ought to be of Latin origin. But the

REINSOPHY 

dictionary already has so many words that it suffers from fatty degeneration; it is plethoric beyond comprehension, and growing worse every year. There are some beautiful words in our language-monosyllabic, expressive, dear to heart-give, take, heart, love, earth, plow, home and others -how much they mean to us, through association all the years of our lives!

In the rim of the Good Will Roundel are the six words, Home, Education, Industry, Discipline, Recreation, Religion; 

all save the one beautiful Anglo-Saxon word "Home" are Roman contributions to our language. I had been pondering the matter when, in the night of January 8, not very sleepy, my head on a pillow, I suddenly said in my heart, "Eureka, I have it." The next day the new word was coined and put on paper.

The word is "re-in-so-phy." It is compounded of the first syllable of each of the four words that express the "Good 

Will Idea"-"Religious," "Intellectual," "Social," "Physical" -the four sides of the square. What does "Reinsophy" mean? Well, it means the quartette of interests which must be recognized in the wisest and best work with boys; it means the basis-four-square-of all successful efforts at the development of symmetrical character; it means the key to ultimately successful work with youth. Efforts with the young which cannot be expressed by the word "Reinsophy" are likely to be in some respects a disappointment, bearing undeveloped, half-matured, unsymmetrical fruits. 

How about the word? Is it awkward? Not more so than "philosophy"—"philos," "love," and "sophia”—“wisdom;" not more so than "Geography"-"Geos," "earth," and "Graph," "a treatise or writing;" not more so than telegram, "tele," "swift," and "gram," "a message." 

Personally I like the word; you see its the first, the lastthe only word I ever coined. I receive no prize for it-no prize was offered; it will not appear in any dictionary; it will not become popular; but it is a meaty word-full of significance, and it expresses an old, but a tremendously important truth, namely, the four sides must be recognized or the symmetry of character is lost.

Here follows the address in the Good Will Chapel, Sunday, afternoon, September, 3, 1933.

The year 1933 is not a special year in the calendar; it is neither "Jubilee Year" nor "Centennial Year" of the nation; nor is it a Presidential year-meaning the year in which a Presidential election is to be held; but in it, early in this September, is taking place an event, more important than "Jubilees," "Centennial" or "Sesqui-centennial" celebrations; thousands of students assemble in universities and colleges, millions of youth and children enter upon the work of a school year-a year in which intellects will develop, bodies will grow, ideas of life will be formed; no student, youth or child who will not either move forward and upward, or, refusing to be inspired to make the best of opportunity, fail at the end to make the step upward. I have no anxiety about Commencement Week next June; nor have I any fears about diplomas, intellectual attainment or success. 

My one anxiety is lest some student at Good Will fail to make a successful "get away" in the beginning of the year's course, the end of which is next June, and thus fail to reach the goal of the year.

Fortunately for the students at Good Will, there are ample provisions for all-around development. This is symbolized by the Good Will Emblem, which you all understand is four sided, and each side is equal to any one of the others. It is a reminder of the fact that if a man has a strong body, but has neither developed intellect, social qualities or spiritual life, he is not a good citizen-he may be a mere brute, a menace to society, a burden to himself; if a man be an intellectual giant, but have neither moral principle, physical stamina or spiritual force, his place in this world is of little note, he cannot be of any great use anywhere; if a man have all the social graces but an undeveloped mind, a broken diseased body and a corrupt character, he can be of slight service to God or humanity; if a man be religiously strong, but only religious-thinking only of his own spiritual welfare, incapable of associating with his fellow men, weak in intellect and a wreck bodily, the place he ought, as a real

To meet the requirement of an all around development, a vast amount of money has been spent here at Good Will; the farm, the forests, the area devoted to athletic sports, the swimming hole, provide for the bodily; the homes and the cottage life provide the basis for development of the social side; the school building and equipment, the libraries, the general educational system afford means for intellectual advancement; the recognition of God in the cottage life, the 

Chapel exercises in the day school, the Sunday services, Sunday Schools, Y. M. C. A., Y. W. C. A., all help in the growth of the religious and spiritual side. 

You are not asked to become athletic phenomena; you are not expected to develop into intellectual prodigies; you are not required to shine or grow into Beau Brummell's or social butterflies, lacking the more worthy qualities of manhood or womanhood; you will not, under the system here become religious fanatics, ignoring the inspired injunctions to be strong in body, "not slothful in business, fervent in spirit." 

The man who has recently been proclaimed and acclaimed the greatest American preacher of the twentieth century is quoted as saying recently that the most important person in these days is the average citizen. I have not conferred with him as to his idea of the average citizen; but this is true, that in any community, in the last analysis, you will find that the most important persons are people who have an all around development of powers-physical, social, intellectual, religious. This has not been as noticeable in the last twenty years, but it will be more and more evident in the future; it is more and more apparent every day.

I have been groping around for a word-for one word that will express it all. Often in this pulpit the derivation of words is explainedsome Greek, Latin, Anglo-Saxon-and how they were made. 

These are classic words. In the commercial world words have been compounded in recent years, which somebody found as soon as they were really needed. One big firm offered a generous prize for a name; a young woman won the prize; she took the first syllables of "National Biscuit 

Company," and made "Nabisco," which caught the popular ear; another firm, the New England Confectionary Company, by similar route, arranged the "Necco Sweets"-the first letters and a "Co" for Company. The Standard Oil 

Company, in need of a short word to tell the public much, took first letters of its corporate title, and made a word familiar on every highway in the land-"Socony."

There is, however, a kind of business flippancy in such words that would not serve our purpose. Words of Latin derivation are more complicated, at first sight, but more significant in analysis. Our word "extraordinary"-Latin, "ordinis," order, and "extra" beyond; "extraordinary," "things that are out of the ordinary"-simplicity itself. "Re-in-carnation" is "carnus," "flesh," "in," which means “in;” “re," "again;" "reincarnation" is literally, being in the flesh again"-a second time; and our English dictionaries contain an almost endless array of similar compounds. 

And to those, if you have studied these things-words, words, words-my new word will be simple from the first; it is

RE-IN-SO-PHY 

I say it is simple. If you take the first syllable of each of the four words suggested by the Good Will Emblem—“re,” from "religious," "in" from "intellect," "so" from "social" and "phy" from "physical," it is apparent that a word has

REINSOPHY 

been formed which means an equal recognition of the four greatest interests of life. Hence, to "re-in-so-phize" is to strive to recognize and develop in one's own life the four great qualities that go to make a complete character or personality. A re-in-so-phied man is one in whom the four great interests have had equal recognition and emphasis. 

I am asking you to keep the word in mind, as you enter upon the school year. Re-in-so-phy is the goal. Body is not all; association with others socially and agreeably is not all; religious services, or religious service-attractive as music and other features may be, and helpful as the grasp of spiritual truth may prove-it not all; but to re-in-so-phize is to strive for the ideal, personally, in character, in service. 

We enter upon a year of education-of foundation-laying; this is Primus Sunday-the beginning of a year of re-in-sophy, that is, the all around development of youth.

CHAPTER II THE GOOD WILL CHAPEL 

The most casual observer, as he passes Good Will is informed by a structure of massive granite, dignified in design, centrally located, that he is passing through a community where religion has its due recognition. A high school building near by, costing about the same as the Chapel; an administrative building not far away, of equal or greater cost, library buildings, athletic fields, announce that religion is not the only, or an over-emphasized interest, but it has its place together with the intellectual, the social and the purely physical. 

The Chapel at Good Will was the gift of Miss Frances E. 

Moody, of Bath, Maine. Miss Moody was one of the two sisters who gave the Charles E. Moody School building in memory of their brother, C. E. Moody, who died at the 

Chicago exposition in 1904. At the time it was built, the structure was ample for the needs of the community. But 

Good Will grew. It became necessary to enlarge the Chapel. 

The original plans had been prepared by Mr. W. E. Mansur, architect, Bangor, Maine.

Mr. Mansur was employed to prepare plans for the remodeling; he did it. The plans might not be used for years, so they were bought and paid for at the usual rate for plans without supervision. The wait was not as long as might have been; for after a few years it was decided to build. 

Mr. C. M. Daggett, of Waterville, revised the plans to reduce the cost; the plans were carried out; when the remodeling was completed, there was a threatened delay; the structure was completed; the pipe organ, in memory of Mr. and Mrs.

THE OLDEST ROD 

The Old Stone House in Guilford, Connecticut, now the State Museum, was built in 1639-40. In 1874 it was remodelled and a few timbers taken out. Mr. Walter Hinckley was employed to saw these beams into small pieces for mantel ornaments and watch-charms, the wood being highly valued. This is the largest piece in existence. The oldest in the collection, the wood is as bright and apparently as strong as the day, now nearly three hundred years ago, it was in the form of a beam put into the house, which became the residence of the first minister in the town, and it represents the first prophecy or promise in the Bible: "It shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel."

GRANDFATHER LEE'S SUPPORT 

My only recollection of my grandfather Elon Lee, of

Guilford, Connecticut, is that one day I sat on his knee, in father's house, while he was talking with mother and the tears rolled down his cheeks. I was sorry for him though 

I did not know why he was weeping. I wanted to brush the tears off his wrinkled face; but I did not quite dare do it lest I offend him. My recollection of the scene, and of what was going on in our home is very vivid, though I could not have been more than five years old.

It seems to be customary in these latter days, when writing the words "individualism" and "rugged" to encase them in quotation marks, and we have "Rugged individualism." 

Of course everybody understands why it is done, and, on general principles, it is an appropriate thing to do. "Rugged" is a good word-Anglo-Saxon and expressive; "individualism" is equally expressive and Latin; there is no reason why they should not be happily wedded. We are deeply indebted to "rugged indiviualism" for much that we enjoy and value; and, I opine-opine has been regarded as obsolete for years, but is coming back again-I opine that there are great tasks waiting for rugged individualists to face and master in the future. CHAPTER VII PILGRIMAGE OF SENTIMENT 

I had looked forward to the pilgrimage with pleasure; but at no time was I sure that I would live to make it. Several such pilgrimages had been made; they had come every fifth year on the "fours" and the "nines" of each decade.

The reason for them goes back to 1869; in that year, on the first Sunday in March-March 6-I was received into the membership of the First Congregational Church in Guil ford, Connecticut; it was an event of so much importance to me personally that eventually I began to make these journeys of sentiment and gratitude. Years ago I established a kind of routine on them; when the first bells for church rang at 9:30 A.M., I would go into the beautiful edifice at the north end of Guilford green-it's the "North Church"-sit for a few minutes of meditation in the first cushioned pew from the east door, on the right hand side of the aisle; then rise and proceed over the route which I took on that Sunday in March, 1869, to a place in the central aisle three pews from the front; stand there for a momenta moment of re-consecration-and then return to my starting place, or leave the edifice until the 10:30 A.M. bells rang for the morning service. The last few of these journeys I have made, I have been invited to deliver a sermon at the service, and also to assist at the communion. 

At this sixty-fifth anniversary I was to be again the guest of Mr. and Mrs. Charles D. Hubbard. Mr. Hubbard is a deacon of the church; assistant superintendent of the Sunday School; teacher of a class of youths-mostly high school students.

My plans were all made. I would arrive Saturday afternoon; spend a quiet evening with my host and hostess, retire early—around nine o'clock; make my preliminary trip to the church in the morning; preach the sermon; assist at the communion; and that would complete my activities- 71

REINSOPHY 

that plan when carried out would be my celebration of the "sixty-fifth." And it would be to me, worth the round trip from Good Will to Guilford and return. "Plan your work; then work your plan," is a fine rule to follow, but how about it?

Well, in the first place, Mr. Hubbard sprung a glad surprise on me. He said that some of the boys in his Sunday 

School class would arrive at about eight o'clock that very evening for a pie social in Hobgoblin Hall. Hobgoblin Hall is artist Hubbard's studio, on the third floor of the ancient house in which he lives; Hobgoblin Hall has a skylight, a wood-burning stove, and such things as you would expect to find in an artist's studio-an easel, paintings on the wall; oil sketches on the floor, leaning, edgewise, against the wall. 

And a pie social? All I know about a pie social is what 

I have been told by Mr. Hubbard and his class, and what I saw and tasted and felt that Saturday evening. If you are going to a "Pie Social" you take pie with you-nothing else, and only one pie, standard size, attractive in appearance, and toothsome. Suppose there are ten at the social-that means ten pies; each pie is cut into ten pieces, and each one in attendance has a piece of each pie, and in conclusion, each has had ten pieces of pie-apple, custard, squash, pumpkin, lemon, blueberry, mince and what not-and there is no pie left. I think there were nine of us in Hobgoblin Hall; and of all the parties, socials or occasions that can be imagined, that party was the happiest that could have been arranged for me.

They were social youths; one or two of them already members of the church, and one, a senior in the high school was to be received into the church on the sixtyfifth anniversary of my own reception, and, he was living with his parents in the house in which I was born. And the co-incidence pleased me; touched me; added to the significance of the pilgrimage for me personally. It was a lively period we spent together on that third floor, under the skylight, the stars up in the heavens above us. And long before the time to test the pies arrived I felt as though I had known that group as long as Mr. Hubbard had, or I'll say, all my life.

CHAPTER IX WILLIAM H. HALL 

A letter and a number of clippings from Hartford, Connecticut, newspapers announced to me the death of Mr. 

William H. Hall of West Hartford. A special to the Hart ford Times, March 23, 1934, said: "Longer in active service as an educator for one community than any other man in this country so far as records show, William H. Hall died of the infirmities of old age at his home, No. 1006 Farmington Avenue, at ten o'clock this morning. He had been in failing health since the first of this month. "Sixty-two years continuously connected with the West Hartford schools, the last twelve as superintendent emeritus, Mr. Hall was probably the best known and best loved resident of the town. CHAPTER XI CAUGHT IN THE ACT 

Honesty is an asset; a reputation for honesty is an asset; if a man be strictly honest and have an earned reputation for honesty he is strong, and to be congratulated; he is rich in moral assets. An honest man who has in some way acquired a reputation for dishonesty is unfortunate; a dishonest man who is reputed to be honest is in danger; to be honest at heart, with a reputation for honesty and then to be caught in a dasterdly crime, or to appear to be thus caught-is bad, very bad, to say the least. If I may judge from the number of invitations I receive in a year to dine or sup with friends, I do not believe that anyone who knows me fears for his silverware in case I sit at his table. But it was a strange thing that happened one evening in February, 1932.

I had been invited to take tea with Principal Martin of the 

Good Will High School, and Mrs. Martin, in their cosy apartment in Keyes Cottage. At the table we were engaged in an interesting discussion. A boy in whom we were all interested had been dishonest; he had also been suspected of dishonesty of which I was inclined to think him not guilty. 

I had related one or two incidents to illustrate how easy it is, if a person is once known to be dishonest, to trace all cases of dishonesty to his door. 

I was so interested in the conversation that I hardly knew what I was eating; the host passed me a dish; I hurriedly laid down my knife, beside my plate and very close to the edge of the table, to accept the proffered cake. As I made this quick movement, quick because I had not noticed for an instant that my host was holding the food before me, my coat sleeve hit the handle of the knife. I thought something dropped, but I heard nothing reach the floor, and instantly concluded that I was mistaken. But I leaned over and looked downward; the floor was covered with a very heavy 97

Had I tried to hit a knife lying beside my plate so it would slip off the table into my coat pocket, I would not have succeeded in a thousand trials. It just happened. CHAPTER XII AS I REMEMBER IT Family Correspondence; Love Letters 

While I was in my first pastorate still writing one or two sermons such as they were each week there were two ministers in the same denominations, for whom I had great admiration. They were brothers. One was the pastor of one of the largest churches in New England; the other was a well-known, and very successful evangelist Both were publishing their sermons. In conversation with one of them, I was surprised when he referred to his brother, and said: "We very seldom write to each other and when we do our letters are nothing more than very brief notes." 

This seemed strange to me. It seemed strange because the men were in the same profession; both were ready writers, and I would have guessed that correspondence between them was continuous and voluminous. 

In later years I came to understand it, because, as I look back, correspondence with my own family has been meager indeed; first, as to my brother and sisters and then with my sons and daughters. This was probably due to the fact that there was a complete understanding between us; and if everything is plain to all parties-fully understood, accepted and digested—why write letters?

Good Will was to be a farm-that is basic; there must needs be broad productive acres-soil that under cultivation would produce provender for man and beast, for families, for herds of milch cows, for horses and young stock, but not a farm for farming operations alone.

CHAPTER XVII OUT-DOOR-PHYSICAL 

One of the bronze tablets in Bowdoin Circle at Good Will -the "Tree Tablets"-carries this: "The groves were God's first temples."

It is safe to assert that the woods were man's first dwelling place this statement appears to be supported by both science and revelation. If the woods or groves were a fit temple for God, and a fit dwelling place for man, then it is a pardonable inference that the woods are a wholesome place for the development - all-round development of youth. 

When people began to visit Good Will-in the days when there was one cottage for boys and a vision of a school house to be provided in some way, the visitors would look about, then glance across the field to the Kennebec, and someone would say: "What would you do without the river?"

This question was asked so many times that it might be recorded as a uniform query, and my reply was therefore uniform: "If there were no river or lake here, I would not be here myself, neither would Good Will be."

In other words, it is an old idea of mine cherished now for more than half a century that woods and water are essential in a plan for re-in-so-phy. The public generally agrees that woods and water are very desirable, an essential for health in summer time; but there is more virtue in the woods in one day in winter than five days in the summer. 

If one questions this statement, he should make a visit in the Good Will woods some winter's day, when the older boys are swinging axes, the younger fellows are burning brush, and all are singing or whistling,-muscles are hardening, shoulders are broadening, lungs expanding, appetites growing, and manly qualities developing. One by one the bronze tablets have been provided for the completion of four groups-the tree tablets were all given and unveiled in a surprisingly brief time, the floral group is nearly completed, several contributions have been made in honor of the four footed creatures and a number in honor of our feathered friends. CHAPTER XIX A VISIT FROM SANTA CLAUS 

It was just Christmas time; the Christmas spirit was in the air; and Mother Nature had generously covered field and tree and bush with a wealth of moist snow. Everybody at Good Will and round about, and for that matter, everywhere, had been moving steadily toward the season of good cheer, and merriment. Thanksgiving was past; Good

Will Night, Home Night, Stunt Night had come, been enjoyed to the full and had become history. The music master of the place-we call him "Director of Music," now-adays had put on the long-anticipated concert-a combination of chorus and orchestra-and the effort had proved good, and the showing of the two musical organizations excellent; the Kennebec River had frozen over, and two extra half holidays had already been proclaimed so that all who wished, without regard to age, attainments or "previous condition of servitude," could enjoy the great iceway that stretches from Moosehead Lake to the sea, and is especially pleasing and enjoyable as it passes through the Good Will country. I had even issued invitations for my annual supper for the farmers, gardeners and mechanics and the affair had proved to be pleasing and satisfactory. 

For more than a week, the radio broadcasts had been influenced by the approaching season; metropolitan newspapers were reporting the biggest shopping crowds in a decade; the graded schools were already free from study periods for the usual two weeks "in the holidays," and the high school was to enter upon a corresponding period of intellectual quietude in a few hours; there was nothing to do but just face the ordinary duties of life and get ready for the great day. I was at Ellis Acres alone. The night before a cluster of electric lights had flashed intermittently, just to let the passer know that I was fully aware of the season we were approaching. I was sitting at the table in the living room, the big bay window in front of me, the open fireplace just back of me, where birch and oak had blazed quietly the night before. That morning, over at Good Will, three or four men had engaged in a controversy-humorous, playful, and yet each contestant had pretended to be in dead earnest. 

One asserted that there really was a Santa Claus-he had seen him; one of the other men pretended to share his belief, but the other two were not to be deceived-no, there wasn't any Santa Claus, never had been, never would be; he was just a fraud. I was not present; but the report of the conflict showed that men as old as I, younger than I-yea, men of all ages-were imbued with the Christmas spirit. 

Sitting close in front of the fireplace, but back toward it, I was writing with a pencil in long hand-stuff to be typewritten in the office later in the day. The silence had been unbroken, for two hours at least, aside from the quarterly chimes of the clock on the mantel.

Suddenly, without any warning, there was a rattling behind me; it was in the fireplace, where I had thrown a few discarded and crumpled sheets of writing paper. "Hello," said I, though there was no one to hear it, and then within myself, I reasoned, "I never saw or heard that before-not in this chimney; the wind is rising; it has blown some of the snow resting on the chimney into it; on its way down it has dislodged a handful of soot. Funny! Unusual! Queer!" 

But notwithstanding it was so near Christmas Day and the Christmas spirit was in the air, I did not once think of 

Santa Claus.

Suddenly the silence was broken again as a fresh installment of soot came down chimney and landed on the crumpled papers. It startled me; it would startle anybody to have such a noise close to him, but behind him-being in a big, silent house, all alone. "Of all things!" I exclaimed, mentally, "of all things! 

But that's no crust of snow blown into the chimney by this increasing wind; it's something bigger than that; I haven't seen a starling in two weeks, but it must be a starling is trying to get in here just the way they used to come down the chimney at Applehurst-I know the ways of the starling and how sometimes they prowl around in dark places, and become prisoners and victims of their own uncanny, prying disposition."

This explanation was satisfactory to me. It did not occur to me that anyone might be putting up a trick for my benefit by causing some disturbance in the chimney, and the thought that the real Santa Claus could be hovering around the chimney top at Ellis Acres or annoyed by soot in an effort to make an early visit to my mantel-no stockings hanging there yet-did not once enter my pre-occupied heart. 

I continued to write in the dead silence of a house in which I was the sole occupant. Perhaps five minutes passed; I was so busy with my task that I had no sense of the passage of time, when-crash!! Right behind me, close to me in the silent room, a load of soot landed on the pile of crumpled paper in the fireplace, and with it came Santa Claus. He seemed as startled and as bewildered as I was; but while I quickly recovered, he continued to be ill at ease-perhaps discomfitted that he had got caught at his own game so early in the Christmas season. Without pausing to greet me, he rushed past my chair, under the arch, into the front room and took refuge for a moment in a big, upholstered easy chair, in which I have taken a deal of comfort; but he appeared to be anything but comfortable or at ease. "Glad to see you, old chap," I exclaimed, as soon as I recovered from my surprise. "Glad to see you, Santa Claus:

Santa Claus spoke never a word. He suddenly movedalmost leaped-from the easy chair to the sofa, south side of the room, near the radio. "Don't get nervous, Santa," I said. "Calm yourself; I'm as much surprised as you are, but it's all right-I'm glad you're here." "Say," I continued, as a happy thought occurred to me, "what did you bring me, Santa, hey? What did you bring me? It isn't Christmas yet, old man, but I don't mind; what've you got for me?"

Santa Claus, contrary to all my notions of him, and the generally accepted opinion of his disposition, was sullennot a word did he utter; but he remained motionless at times long enough so I could admire him. To my great satisfaction, he was clad in soft gray from head to foot; there was an absence of the brilliant red which later in the season and nearer the great holiday, he is supposed to wear. I know now that in ordinary, every-day working costume, when he is not on exhibition, Santa Claus appears in gray-a beautiful cool, soft gray.

But shortly my visitor began to behave unseemly; he climbed to the top of a window, using the lace curtain for a ladder-I did not like this; he attempted to leap from one point to another, and in so doing, he hit the corner of a picture frame which contains one of Lund's oil paintingsa marine of which I am very fond-and left the frame, tipping from north to south at an angle of forty-five degrees. 

Reminding me of Jack and the beanstalk, he climbed toward the ceiling, making use of the steam pipe on his journey. "Why do you act so?" I asked him, beginning to lose patience, "Santa, why do you act so? You are just spoiling all my notions of what Santa Claus is like-no one thinks of

Santa as acting the way you do behave yourself."

The men finally moved the book-case. Said one of them, "I can see him," and then he acknowledged that Santa had eluded him again. "I want the door wide open," said I, "for while I've no hard feelings, I want him to go." 

And so, finally, eventually, and at last, Santa Claus-still clad in his gray suit, just as he had come down chimney the day before, and dragging his long bushy tail behind him, passed out of the door and over the veranda, across the yard, near the garage, past the big elm and disappeared around the corner of the barn. "Good-bye, Santa," I said, "good-bye." "That was a gray squirrel, wasn't it?" exclaimed Mr. 

Lawry, as he caught a glimpse of him, "a gray squirrel." "Yes," I replied, "I named him 'Santa Claus' as soon as I saw him because he came down chimney at Christmas time.”

CHAPTER XX THE LAW OF LABOR 

A successful business man, who owed his success to his unwavering faithfulness to principle and conscientious effort, said to me: "When I was young, I had to work; I had to work hard; 

I don't want my son to have to work as I did, and he will not if I can help it."

Another man, equally successful in business and in the development of a symmetrical character and high business reputation, said to me: "I'm planning for my boy; you see, I had to work at his age; it was always a struggle with poverty; my boy shall be spared the hard time I had, and that's one fine thing."

Another man, equal to either of the others in reputation and success, said to me: "I had too hard a time in my early life; if I can help it, and I can, my son shall have an easier time of it."

These men are dead; I lived to stand by the casket of each in turn, and mourn because of my personal loss, and knew that the world was poorer as each passed out; but each of the three lived long enough to mourn over the waywardness and the inefficiency of his own son; and the lives of two of the three were shortened by the grief in their hearts; they were like David of old: "Oh, Absalom, my son, my son."

And this is the strange part of it-neither of these men realized how much the work and discipline of early life had to do with the development of his own character, or with the foundations of his business success. There seems to be a law a law everywhere-the law of labor. 

I have somewhere recorded an incident which often comes to mind. One summer morning a Good Will boy brought me a cocoon; he had found it in the edge of the woods; it inter- 159

ested him more than would an ordinary find of the kind, because the chrysalis had begun to obey the law of its being; it had begun to work. On one side of the silken case, there was a hole nearly half an inch long; through this we could see the bright wings of the creature within and watch the movements-apparently painful movements-of the imprisoned creature in an effort to get out. We watched it for some time, and I explained to the boy what was in progress. 

Then a thought-it semed to me a happy thought-occurred to me, and I said to the boy: "Get me the scissors from the other office." 

Why hadn't I thought of this before? Nature had imposed a certain law on this chrysalis, but it was a hard law-a cruel law-and I could circumvent it in this case. 

It was the law in the chrysalis state that each and every chrysalis must work-labor to develop its powers of flight; in no other way could the wings be strengthened for service. 

I seized the scissors; carefully, and without touching the chrysalis with the steel in any way, I enlarged the opening which the chrysalis, by his own efforts, had already made, and it rolled easily onto the desk. We watched it-this boy and I. The beautifully colored wings were crumpled as they had been in the cramped spaces of the cocoon, but, of course, they would quickly straighten out. But the butterfly had nothing against which to brace; there was no way, now that it could develop its wings and fit itself for flight; there was no substitute for the obstacle of walls of the cocoon. We watched as long as we had time to spare, and visited it at intervals; but no progress was made. Closing time for the office came. "It will be fully developed in the morning," said I, and we left it on the desk and closed the office. The next morning, entering the office, I went straight to the chrysalis. Not a wing, nor a leg, nor an antennae had been straightened; it was as helpless as it had been the moment I had thwarted its law of development; it had been robbed of the privilege of life through individual effort; it was dead.
