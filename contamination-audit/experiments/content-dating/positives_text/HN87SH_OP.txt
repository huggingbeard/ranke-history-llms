mans. The divine-human life of our Lord, extended to us through the Sacrament, makes the 

Church one.

If we may resort to metaphor, Protestantism commonly views the relation of the individual Christian to the Church in a way analogous to a stone lying in a heap of other stones, a merely casual and external relation. Romanism is inclined to view it as similar to the relation of a brick in a brick wall-a more important relation but no less external and lifeless than the former. But Catholicism views it as resembling the relation of a cell or a limb in a living body, to the body as a whole. The stone cast at random into a pile of other stones is not altered in its essence by its relation to them; a brick placed in a wall is not vitally altered or improved by its new position; but the tiniest bit of matter, taken into a living organism, is vitally changed and transformed by its relation to the organism as a whole. Such, according to the historic Catholic doctrine, is the relation of the Christian who has been baptized by the one Spirit into the Body of Christ-his union with that Body is not external and casual, as in Protestantism, nor external and mechanical, as in Romanism, but internal, organic, vital. This view harmonizes perfectly, moreover, with the

findings of modern science. As Professor Whitehead tells us: "An electron within a living body is different from an electron outside it, by reason of the plan of the body. . . . This plan includes the mental state." (Science and the Modern World, p. 116.) The Christian's nature, as a soul cell in the Body of Christ, possessing the mind of Christ, and energized by His Holy Spirit, is radically different from that of a member of a voluntary association or the subject of a despotic monarchy.

This difference runs through the entire sphere of religious life and practice. Take the matter of faith-the Protestant is content with an unlimited private judgment, which feels itself competent to disregard the corporate experience and collective mind of the Church, when it runs counter to his own opinion. The Roman 

Catholic, since the Vatican Council, must accept the individual judgment of the Pope, which is "irreformable of itself, and not from the consent of the Church" (non autem ex ecclesiae consensu), as the infallibility dogma runs. Protestantism and Papalism are heartily agreed in reducing the Church to an ornamental cipher, and in exalting one man's private judgment, whether it is that of Pope Pius the eleventh or of Jack Doe the eleven millionth, above the

corporate mind of the Body of Christ. Similarly, with regard to the relation of authority and freedom, "Romanism exalts authority to the point where it leaves no trace of a Christian man's liberty; Protestantism pushes liberty to the extent of dissolving the unity of the 

Church," writes Khomiakoff, the great Russian

Orthodox theologian, and adds, “Romanism is an unnatural tyranny; Protestantism is an unprincipled revolt." Catholicity adheres to the deliberate decisions of the corporate mind which it has a part in forming through an Ecumenical Council ratified by the whole Church. 

Or take the doctrine of the consecration of the Eucharist. Protestantism in general regards the Sacrament as an incidental symbol, rather than as a vital reality ministering to the organic life of the Christ Body, and hence treats the question of the Priesthood and the Apostolic Succession as non-essential. Romanism, on the other hand, treats the Priesthood as practically a caste, set over against the Church; there is the widest separation between the pew and the altar, where the Mass is mumbled in a dead and unintelligible language, and the priest "conficit 

Corpus Christi," the congregation playing a merely passive part. But according to the truly

Catholic conception, the priesthood is simply an organic differentiation within the Body of Christ—the priest acts always for, with, and in behalf of the organism as a whole, which is in itself a priestly Body. 

The doctrine of the Communion of Saints furnishes another example. Protestantism rudely snaps the organic link which binds the faithful departed to ourselves; it neither prays for them nor asks their prayers. “Out of sight, out of mind," is its working principle. Romanism treats the Church as a three-story house consisting of the ground floor, the Church militant; the attic, the Church triumphant; and the cellar, the Church suffering. The Church on earth is practically a separate body, needing a separate head of its own. Also it makes a sharp and rigid division between those it prays for and those it prays to, between those whom we help and those who help us. True Catholicity prays for and with the whole Church, on earth or departed, and asks the prayers of all. Thus Orthodox Easterns not only ask the prayers of the holy Mother, but also, in their venerable Liturgy, pray for her and for all the saints, as members of the one Body of Christ; and similarly an Orthodox boy will pray for his mother at her grave, then kneel and ask her

prayers. This surely is the true doctrine of the 

Communion of Saints the fellowship of prayer and life of the one Body in the one Spirit.

The Church, then, is the soul of Christianity. 

The Church is the body of Christ. To make these two statements may appear bold to the verge of rashness in these days of church-less Christianity-a natural enough reaction from a narrow sectarianism-but they are no more and no less than the truth of God. To say "I believe in the Holy Catholic Church" is to sum up the entire Christian religion; it is to affirm that article of Christian faith which carries with it all the rest.

Why should the Church be an object of faith? Faith, we are told, has to do with things unseen; faith has to do with things divine; therefore we "believe in" the soul, we "believe in" God; but why should we say we "believe in" the Church? The answer is that while it is easy, fatally easy, to recognize a host of churches these are objects not of faith, but of sight-it is a difficult, a sublime act of faith to believe in one Catholic Church. For the unity of the Church is by no means easy to affirm. We see a multitude of local churches, and we confess one Church; we see a multitude of human

assemblies, and we acknowledge one divine organism.

Of course, if we meant simply that all Christians are in some vague sense one, we should be stating an obvious and not very interesting commonplace. If we meant that all the religious organizations in Christendom-whether they rest ultimately on a human or a divine foundation-constitute one "Church" (for short), our belief would not be particularly important, even if it were true. But if we say "I believe in one 

Catholic Church," and really mean just thatthat amid the Babel of human sects there is one unique Society (comprising one or more communions) which has its origin from above, not from below, the creation of God not of men-not simply a human organization but a divine organism, filled with the very life of Christ, and animated by His Spirit-then we are making an act of faith which is terrific, heroic, overwhelming in its significance. When we say that the Church is nothing less than the life of God embodied and extended to men; that the Church is the larger Christ gathering all creation into union with Himself; it will be clear that we are stating what is either the most audacious falsehood or the most vital and imperative truth which can confront the human

mind. The Church, the divine-human organism, the fellowship of God and humanity, recreated in Christ Jesus and indwelt by the Spirit, is either the most frightful illusion, or the most magnificent Reality, with which we have to deal. And if it is true, it is not a truth which we can meet with impunity; to reject it is to reject 

God; to accept it is to surrender oneself, body and soul, to the One Spirit in the one Body; to give oneself, without stint and without reservation, to the Beloved Community and to its divine Lord, for time and for eternity. It claims not only the assent of the mind, but the devotion of the heart, the allegiance of the will, of the whole personality, all that we have and are and by God's grace shall be.

It is not to be denied that this is an inspiring ideal. For it implies, among other things, that the Church is the great Family of God, the universal human brotherhood which is to draw all men into unity-a unity centering in God, the only possible Center of unity. It is the Society of Christ, who breaks down all partitions between God and man and between man and man, uniting all in the bonds of vital fellowship. 

The unity of the human race has been the dream of mankind down through the ages-a dream which has often become a nightmare,

due to the means taken to realize it. The captains and the kings have deluged the earth with blood in their strivings for world unity, conceived as world empire, and all in vain. Statesmen and diplomats have schemed, plotted, and intrigued in their ambition-sometimes noble, sometimes basely selfish-for world unity or world federation:—again in vain. Philosophers have constructed lofty systems of thought, they have described in burning words the ideal society, yet that ideal has remained with them unrealized and unrealizable. Plato came very close to the heart of the matter, for he recognizedunlike the great statesmen and conquerorsthat the basis of the ideal society must be spiritual. It must be rooted and grounded in the eternal world, in the Idea of the Good and not in material power or force of arms. But Plato's system labored under two disastrous defects. In the first place only the ruling class, the philosophers, could share this communion with the Idea of the Good-others had no direct contact with it. Moreover, his ideal republic is after all only a Greek city-state, perpetually at war with other states and in no sense (even potentially) the Universal Society which is to unite all men. 

But Christ, the Son of God made man, supplies-He alone supplies-the basis of a uni-

versal fellowship, a brotherhood of all mankind. In Him man finds union, not with the abstract Idea of the Good, but with the living God, and only on this foundation can true unity and universal fellowship be built. The brotherhood of man is a chimera apart from the

Fatherhood of God, and Christ is the way to the Father-no one comes to the Father but by 

Him. Spiritual truth must be the very root and matrix of an ideal society, and He is the personal Truth of God made flesh. In Him men may behold the Father's glory, in Him dwells all the fulness of the Godhead bodily, in Him the Spirit abides eternally. As many as receive 

Him, to them He gives the right and the power to become sons of God. He brings into being a new family, born from above-born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God. This family, this ideal society, this universal fellowship is the Church. 

Christ did not merely preach brotherhood in the abstract-He founded a concrete Brotherhood, His Church, which was to transcend all barriers of race, class, sex, etc., and unite all in Him. In Christ Jesus there is neither Jew nor Gentile (the most bitter and deep-rooted of enemies is overcome), male nor female (no inequalities of sex are to exist), bond nor free

(class distinctions fade into nothingness before the all-compelling fact that one belongs to Christ and to His Body, the Church). In Him there is neither countryman nor foreigner, black nor white, capitalist nor proletarian—in Him alone can strifes which threaten the downfall of human culture, if not the extermination of the human race, be done away. His Church does not simply teach and inculcate brotherhood but it is the Brotherhood-into which all men may, and must, be brought. 

But this is not all. In the Church, not only all mankind, but through it all creation, is to be made one. All creation is to find union with 

God in the mystical Body of Christ. This is a great mystery, but none the less a great reality -the reality for which the universe was called into being, the goal toward which it strives and labors. This thought is the great theme of the 

Epistle to the Ephesians. The Son of God, sharing the Father's life through all eternity, brings the divine life down to earth, to creation, that all creation may be caught up through Him into the divine life. The organism in which all this is being accomplished is the Church. Christ, in His own person, unites the human and the divine. But in His Church all creatures are to be increasingly united to God, made sharers in

His life, penetrated by the divine Spirit and unified by the divine love. The Church, says the 

Apostle, is His Body, the fulness of Him that filleth all in all. The Incarnation-the coming of God into manhood-was in one sense an event which happened once for all; in another sense, it is an everlasting process, for Christ wills to incarnate Himself in all men, in all creation, to draw the whole universe into oneness with Himself, with God, and make of it His Body, His Temple, His fulness. 

From all this we see that the Church is much more than a mere organization-it is an organism. Organization it has, but this is not the most significant thing about it. Its organization must always express, and be controlled by, its nature and function-by the divine life of which it is full. Moreover, whether we consider the 

Church as the universal human fellowship, or as the new creation of God, it is obvious that the Church must be one and not many. Only a society founded by God can be all-embracing, and claim the allegiance of all men. And from the nature of things there can be but one such society. If every man may found his own church the Church becomes, instead of a principle of unity, a new source of division. The Church is exclusive, because she is all-inclusive-that is

the paradox. There can be only one Universal 

Society-there can be only one Catholic Church. 

And there can be but one organism created by God, and filled with His Spirit, for the purpose of bringing all creation into vital union with 

Himself. One Christ, one Church. One humanity, one Church. One God, one Church. It cannot be otherwise.

Here however, we meet with objections. Not the old-fashioned Protestant objection, that there is nothing resembling the Catholic Church in the New Testament, but simply an invisible fellowship of believers, a society of the elect, known only to God. Modern scholarship, especially modern critical research, has shattered this hoary myth past all hope of resurrection. 

The Church which we see portrayed in the Book of Acts, or in the letters of St. Paul and 

St. John, has far more resemblance to the Catholic Church of history, Catholicity represented especially by the Episcopal and Orthodox Eastern Churches today, than to modern Protestantism. All the essential elements of Catholicism-the Creed, the Sacraments, the Priesthood, etc. are present at least in seed, and the seed already shows unmistakably what sort of flower and fruit it is going to grow into. Recent Protestant scholars are coming more and

more to recognize that the Church of the New Testament is the great Catholic Church as the world has since known it. We do not, of course, find the man-made perversions of God's ideal for His Church-the autocracy of Papal Rome, or the "prelacy" of established Churches (England, Russia, etc.), or the subservience to the 

State, or to power and wealth, which have all too often concealed the divine Reality from the eyes of men. But we do find the Catholic 

Church of Christ-unmistakably Catholic, unmistakably Christ's-as God meant her to be —as she shall again be, when she has recovered the radiance of her youth. however,

Modern liberal Protestantism, while abandoning the romantic figment of an invisible Church, and recognizing the Catholic Church as plainly present in the writings of the Apostles, seeks to show that at least Christ 

Himself never founded, nor had any idea of founding a Church. In one sense, the objection is true, for Christ did not found the Church, He found it-it was there before He came to earth-founded by God under the Old Covenant. The whole Jewish nation was a Church. 

It was in a unique sense God's people. It had its sacred Scriptures, its revelation given through inspired law-givers and prophets, its

divinely-sanctioned worship, its fundamental faith in God and in His Kingdom and the Messiah to come, its priesthood, its sacrifices, and its rites. What Christ did, therefore, was not to found a Church, but to re-found it, to transform its inner spirit, to re-center it in Himself, to fill it with His life, and bestow upon it His 

Holy Spirit.

The objector, however, is not silenced. He will not hear of an ecclesiastical Christ in any sense. Forms and externals, creed and Church, have nothing to do with the pure spiritual religion of Jesus Christ. Of course the old conception of Jesus of Nazareth as a militant inconoclast, violently assailing all external forms and ordinances, has received its coup de grace from modern scholars, Protestant no less than Catholic. The religion into which our Lord was born, and in which, as Man, He lived and moved and had His being, was institutional and corporate in the highest degree. Its fundamental principles he did not attack, but rather He sought to heighten them and to bring the institutions of religion back to their true spirit-to fill them with new vitality. He came not to destroy, but to fulfil. Even when He denounces the official representatives of religion, He proclaims their divine authority and right to obe-

dience. He constantly appeals to the Law as 

God-given. He takes part in the devotional life and activities of the people, and keeps their feasts, whether prescribed in the Scriptures or not. He recognizes the Temple as sacred, and therefore not to be turned into a house of merchandise.

The very objection that Christ did not found a Church-this, we have seen, is true in a sense -proves the strength of our Lord's Churchmanship. Though He encountered the bitterest opposition from the ecclesiastics of His day, this never led Him to oppose ecclesiasticism as such. Even under the strongest provocation He refused to withdraw His followers from the communion of His people, His Church, which He still recognized as the Church of God. He never sought to establish a rival community, He strove only to win the ancient Church to accept Him, the fulfilment of all for which it stood, the flower of its own religion. As Von 

Hügel has noticed, our Lord's attitude to the Church is that of Savonarola-faithful to the Church even when its faithless ministers outlawed him to death-rather than that of 

Luther and Calvin.

Still the idea lingers in many circles that Christ merely tolerated the externals of re-

ligion as so much background and undermined them, if He did not directly attack them, by His spiritual teaching. There seems to be in the mind of many the fixed idea that forms and institutions are somehow unspiritual, if not antispiritual, or else that some forms and institutions may be necessary, but it is a matter of complete indifference what they shall be. They are no more than the outward dress of religion, which may be discarded or changed without loss. They fail to realize that the Church, in her fundamental nature, is not the dress of Christ, but the Body of Christ. They do not appreciate the fact that Christianity is not simply the religion of the Word, but of the Word made flesh-with all that that implies. 

But a careful study of the contact of Jesus with the institutions of religion and His attitude toward them, will show us that it was not one of unwilling compliance nor indifferent tolerance, any more than of entire acceptance. He seeks to spiritualize all the externals of religion till they shall indeed incarnate the reign of God on earth-into all of them He would breathe the spirit of life, above them all He would write His supreme commandment of love. Our

Lord's religion presupposes the teachings and institutions of the past, of the Old Covenant

-at least in essence-on them He builds for the future-for eternity-only transforming them by the gift of His Spirit. We shall develop this thought later. One must not think that the religion of Christ consists only, or essentially, of its novel and distinctive elements. 

And as we shall see, our Lord provided for

His own band-the little community within the great one, the church within the Church-external institutions of unique inner power and significance. The apostolic ministry, the two greater Sacraments, the confession of faiththe world-wide commission-these cannot be too strongly emphasized if we would seek the true mind of our Lord regarding institutional religion. Those who had the best opportunity of knowing His mind-those who had known Him in the flesh and received the fulness of His Spirit-act in a way that leaves us in no doubt of how they understood Him. The result is the Holy Catholic Church as she has existed ever since. The Apostolic Church and the 

Catholic Church are one Church, not two-the Church of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. 

A more difficult problem is raised by those who claim that the thought of a Church would be wholly alien to our Lord's mind, because He expected the immediate, or almost immediate

end of the world. This is the "Copernican revolution" of modern theology which is supposed at one blow to destroy the whole structure and foundation of the Church, as due to Jesus Christ. He would scarcely have made provision for the apostolic band as a lasting community if the end of all things was immanent. If our 

Lord expected to return very shortly on the clouds of heaven the question of ecclesiastical organization would not present itself at all. It is a problem which must be faced squarely, for the supposition just mentioned seems to strike at the very root of the Church's authority, and indeed of that of Christ Himself. There are to be sure sayings in the gospels attributed to our

Lord which might seem to indicate He expected to return in judgment within a generation. 

There are others which point to a coming in the more remote future. The question is a difficult one, and is closely akin to the problem of the Kingdom of God, the central theme of our Lord's teaching. In one sense, the Kingdom has come, for He, the Messianic King, is in the midst of men, working the mighty works of compassion-healing the sick, raising the dead, preaching the Gospel to the poor, casting out the spirits of darkness, which prove that the Kingdom has come upon men sooner than they

expected-it has surprised them, overtaken them when they least anticipated it. The forces of the Kingdom are already released in the Person of our Lord, and in the little group-the faithful remnant of Israel-who have accepted Him. But these forces are immeasurably increased by a fresh gift of power from on high after the death and glorification of the Messiah -the Spirit is to be poured out upon them by the exalted Lord—the Kingdom of God is to come with power. In one sense, this was a return of Christ-a return with power. Again faithless Jerusalem is to be destroyed, the Old Covenant completely abolished. This too represents a coming of the Son of Man-a return to judgment upon the apostate city. But there lies in the future a great consummation-the 

Messiah is to return to judge all mankind, the 

Christ will come in manifest glory to judge men according to the attitude they have shown to Him in His humiliation and hiddenness and the Kingdom of God in its full beauty and perfection will then be established.

It is small wonder if the disciples should sometimes have misunderstood our Lord and applied sayings of His with reference to one of these events-Pentecost, the judgment on Jerusalem, and the world judgment-to another

of them. To the followers of Christ, with their 

Jewish background and outlook, the coming of the Messiah was not only the climax of history, but also the end of history, or at least the immediate precursor of the end. There could be no real history after that-all else would be anti-climax. This made it extremely difficult for them to make anything out of what Jesus told them about His coming death and resurrection from the dead. It made it difficult for them to receive or understand the hints of our Lord about the coming of the Spirit-His alter ego. 

It made it difficult for them to see that it was not for them—or even for their Master while on earth-to know "the times and the seasons" which the Father had reserved in His own power. Above all, it made it difficult for them to conceive of the "last days"-the Dispensation of the Messiah, following His comingbeing indefinitely prolonged-or of the long interval to elapse between the judgment on Jerusalem, and the final universal judgment, on the background of which it was depicted by their Lord. We must, therefore, admit a confusion of thought on the part of the disciples with regard to the different senses in which Christ should come-a confusion which is reflected in some passages of the Gospels them-

selves. It does not affect the essence of their message a message inspired by God. 

As to our Lord Himself, however, the case stands differently. He plainly declared that He does not know the time of His second coming. "Of that day or hour knoweth no one, neither the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the 

Father." This passage is as certain as any saying of our Lord's-it could not and would not have been invented by the disciples, for it raises difficult theological questions-involving, as it does, a limitation in the (human) knowledge of God's Son. Nor is it sufficient to suppose, as some have done, that our Lord foretells the end of the world within a generation, but says simply that He does not know the exact day and hour. Our Lord denies absolutely any knowledge as to the time-men are always to be expecting it, preparing themselves for itbut the Father alone knows when it will be. 

It must be remembered that Christ as a prophet-the supreme Prophet-was not vitally interested in clock time as such. He was interested in the inner spiritual significance of events in their inner connection with His own. person. He gives, therefore, no exact map of the future-His human mind had no such exact map before it but rather his profound spirit-

ual intuition grasps all the essential events in their inner unity with Himself and with one another. We may admit, too, a prophetic foreshortening of time as our Lord's vision scans the future-His return in the power of the 

Spirit at Pentecost, the destruction of Jerusalem (abolishing the last vestiges of the Old Covenant), His judgment of the entire world at the close of history-these are not isolated events, but part of one mighty process, the process of the manifestation of Christ which He comprehends and portrays as a unity. The disciples, however, with their limited spiritual insight, sometimes blur the picture—their own ingrained expectation of the final end following almost directly upon the revelation of the Messiah could be corrected only by the logic of events. 

Our Saviour, then, denies any knowledge of the Day of Judgment. Though the final judgment is imminent-in every age-that does not mean that it is (necessarily) immediate. On the contrary, much of His language points to a delayed consummation. Time must elapse for the leaven to leaven the whole lump, for the mustard seed to grow into a mighty tree in whose branches all the birds of heaven shall lodge, for the wheat and the tares to grow together till

the harvest. Several of His parables would be meaningless apart from a delayed comingthe virgins, both wise and foolish who sleep; the servants who invest their Lord's money, and accumulate interest, while he journeys to a far country; the wicked steward who abuses his authority because his Master tarrieththese need no comment. In the very discourse in which the words occur-"this generation shall not pass away till all these things be fulfilled," He refers to a series of signs-international wars, great disasters, the rise of false prophets, the preaching of the Gospel throughout the whole world, and its final apostasy on a wide scale-signs which could not reasonably have been expected to be fulfilled for many generations. Therefore "all these things" cannot refer to "that Day" but rather to the beginning of the process-Pentecost, the downfall of Jerusalem, etc., which should at last culminate in the great Judgment. In this way, we can understand why the disciples did not lose their faith, when the return of Christ in glory was indefinitely postponed-they realized that their expectation, their understanding of 

His words, was at fault and not He Himself.

He had given them no clear knowledge of the time of His visible return because He had none

to give. His sayings which implied a long interval before the end unfolded their full significance to their minds.

There was, then, nothing either in our Lord's attitude toward institutions, or his anticipations of the future, which would have prevented His founding, or rather re-founding, the Church. 

The question is not whether He would have done so or could have done so, but simply did 

He? What is the evidence of the Gospels? We have seen that our Lord lived and died in the midst of a highly institutional and corporate religion, which He recognized as coming from God. He Himself was the fulfilment of this religion-not its destroyer. On the other hand, the bulk of the nation-God's people-rejected Him, and the hatred of their leaders was especially marked. What then was our Lord's course? It was to turn from the more extensive to the more intensive method. The teaching of a small group of loyal disciples, and especially the training of the twelve Apostles,' becomes His main work. The faithful remnant of Israel who accept Him is the little flock to which He promises the Kingdom, which in some sense al- 

The attempt to deny that the "Twelve" and the "Apostles" are one group is surely absurd in view of the clear identification of the two by Matthew (10:2), Mark (6:7, 30), and Luke (6:13).

ready is a present and visible reality. The Apostles with Peter at their head are to be the stewards of this household. To them is given the power to bind and to loose. They are to sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israelthat is to say, the new Israel, the Church of God, revived and reconstituted by Christ. If we may, with the more moderate critics, accept the "Rock" passage (Matthew 16:18) Christ promises to them, in the person of their leader, the keys of the kingdom of heaven, the symbol of stewardship. The parable of the vineyard witnesses to the same truth. The Master has sent His servants (the prophets) in vain—they have been spurned, beaten, wounded, killed. He now sends His beloved Son. But they reject and kill Him too. Therefore the Lord's vineyard-His people, His Church—is taken away from the false stewards (the Jewish priests, scribes, etc.) and given to other stewards (the 

Apostles). It is not that the old Church is destroyed, and a new one put in its place, but rather (as St. Paul so clearly perceived) the faithful Jews who accept the Messiah are the Church, the true Israel-at once the remnant of the Church under the Old Covenant, and the nucleus of the Church under the New. If the rest of the Jewish people have not, during

the earthly life of the Saviour, cut themselves off completely as yet from the Church-if they have not entirely ceased to be God's peopleat least it is on the faithful remnant and particularly on the Apostles that the Son of God builds for the future.

The institution of the Sacraments confirms what has been said. Baptism and the Holy

Eucharist were instituted by our Lord-only a quite arbitrary scepticism can deny this—and they are the means by which men enter the life of a visible society and sustain that life in fellowship. Baptism, whatever else it is, is the

Sacrament of admission into a visible institution, and Communion is the bond of a visible community. When we have, therefore, a ministry of Christ's appointment, Sacraments ordained by Him, a world-wide commission to teach the truths He had committed to His ambassadors, the power to remit or retain sins in His name, to bind and to loose, the inauguration of a New Covenant for His people through His outpoured blood, and a sacrificial Feast to be continually pleaded for His memorial-it is clear that we are dealing with a Churchthe Church of God, built anew on the foundation of Christ's Apostles and Prophets, Jesus Himself being the chief Cornerstone. This

Church, and this alone, enjoys His promise to abide with it always, even unto the end of the world-this Church-and it alone-receives the promise and the gift of the Holy Spirit to guide it into all the truth of Jesus, and to indwell it forever more. Therefore, this Church -one, holy, Catholic, and apostolic-moves out into history as a divinely-founded Brotherhood, in which all men are to become brothers in Christ—the all-embracing organism which is together creation in itself, into union with God, that the entire universe may become the Body of Christ, wherein God shall be all in all.

CHAPTER II THE CHURCH IN HISTORY HERE IS ONE BODY and one Spirit,' 

TH 99 cries St. Paul. Christ did not merely refound the Church-He revivified it, transformed it by the gift of His Spirit. Out of an organization He made a living organism, His mystical Body, the new creation, filled with the life of God. Though His visible Presence was to be withdrawn from His disciples, yet He would not leave them orphans, He would come to them in a new way-through the Holy Ghost. We can contemplate the picture with mingled awe and joy. The disciples are gathered together in the upper room. Their 

Master has been vindicated. The same Jesus who was crucified by the hands of wicked men, God has raised up and exalted to be a Prince and a Saviour—a Head and a Life-giver. Jesus has returned to them, no more to suffer, no more to die. He has shown Himself to be alive by many infallible proofs-alive forever more. 

Christ being raised from the dead dieth no

more, death hath no more dominion over Him; for in that He died, He died unto sin once, but in that He liveth, He liveth unto God. Therefore His followers are enabled to reckon themselves as dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto

God through Jesus Christ, their risen and ascended Lord.

All is ready. The Apostles are appointed, the larger body of disciples are met together, the commission has been given by the risen Christ to go into all the world with His saving Gospel, His life-giving Sacraments. Yet they do not go. 

Not a sermon is preached, not a Sacrament is celebrated, not a conversion is attempted. All is ready-except one thing. They have not yet been endued with power from on high. The 

Holy Spirit has not yet been given. 

And then it happens. The Spirit descends.

The Apostles and other disciples are knit into a new union with God and with one anotherthey are made a living fellowship-a Body filled with the life of Christ, animated by His Spirit. The Holy Ghost is not given to individuals as such, but to a fellowship, assembled together "with one accord" in prayer-and it is He who deepens and vitalizes that fellowship, and makes it permanent. The Catholic 

Church is born. The community of Christ be-

comes the Body of Christ, filled with Messianic power and life. All those who accept Christ are added to this Body by Baptism, and they tooin and through this fellowship-receive His Spirit. The Lord adds to His Church daily those who are in the way of salvation, the way of life. There is nothing at all resembling a coalescence of individuals or groups into one Church-but rather the one Church adds individuals and groups to itself. All grows from the one root. Church-less Christians simply do not exist in the New Testament. There is one 

Body and one Spirit-the idea of contrasting and opposing the two occurs to no one. To belong to Christ is to belong to His Body, His Church; to belong to the Church is to belong to its Head, Jesus the Christ. They continue steadfastly in the Apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in the Breaking of the Bread (the

Holy Communion), and in the prayers. One 

Christ; one Church; one Body; one Spirit.

The heads of the community are those who have been ordained and consecrated by the Lord Himself. Theirs is the responsibility for guiding the Church, and building up its life. 

Theirs is the authority and power, though in matters of great moment they consult the brethren. Among the Apostles, too, one is

clearly the leader-St. Peter-their leader, but certainly not their lord. When the Samaritans enter the Church by the gate of Baptism, the 

Apostles send Peter and John to bestow upon them the Holy Spirit by the laying on of hands -Confirmation. We note that it is the Apostles who send Peter, not Peter who sends the Apostles. Anything less papal can scarcely be imagined. The Apostle of authority defers himself to the authority of the Church. On the other hand, St. Paul has very decided ideas, the fruit of divine guidance and inspiration, as to the terms on which Gentile converts were to be admitted. The question was a vital one. Yet he comes up to Jerusalem to consult those who are best qualified to voice the faith and tradition of the Church-the pillar Apostles: Peter, James, and John. The Apostle of freedom diligently seeks and secures through them the 

Church's approbation. Anything less Protestant can scarcely be imagined. 

It is not to be denied that there are texts in the New Testament which, taken by themselves, would be considered somewhat papal in tone; just as there are others which, by themselves, would seem rather Protestant. There are elements in the development of the early Church which would tend to papalism, if not balanced

by other equally clear and vital elements. So, too, there are elements which would make for Protestantism if not balanced and corrected by other trends. But the whole mainstream of development is neither papal nor Protestant—it is Catholic. Take the crucial question as to whether Jewish and Gentile Christians might eat together (perhaps at the common meals connected with the Eucharist). The real issue was whether Jews and Gentiles were really one in Christ-whether membership in Him made one a Christian in the fullest sense of the words, or whether the keeping of the Law was also essential. With that went the question of whether Jews and Gentiles could meet as equals to receive Christ's Body in the Eucharist, or whether the Gentiles should be regarded as an inferior caste. St. Peter had his opinion, St.

Paul had his. But neither did St. Peter-supposed to be the first Pope-impose his view upon the Church, nor St. Paul-supposed to be the Protestant Apostle-insist on holding his in disregard of what the judgment of the Church might be. The mystical Body of Christ decides upon the question which so nearly touches the sacramental Body-the Spirit guided fellowship decides the question as to the terms of fellowship. A Council of the

Church is held. The same Spirit who was first given to the Church in unison speaks through the Church in unison. 

The decision rests not with every individual speaking for himself—the Protestant viewnor with one individual autocratically speaking for all the Roman Catholic view. Neither St. 

Peter, nor St. Paul, nor St. James speaks the decisive word, but the Church Catholic, represented by its Council. The Spirit speaks through the whole Church-not simply through the Apostles, but through "the Apostles and elders, and brethren." The whole Body decides, and its decision is the voice of God. "It seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us"-so runs the decree. The whole transaction eloquently represents the spirit and constitution of Christ's Church, as He willed it. The Council of Jerusalem remains a model for Catholic Christendom of all ages, and provides a conclusive refutation of both Protestant and Romanist aberrations.

Such, then, is the fellowship which Christ brought into being for the salvation of mankind. It is one with the Jewish Church-the ancient people of God-yet how different. The flower grows upon the ancient stem-yet how unpredictably beautiful. In place of a national

organization, we have a world-wide organism, bound together not by an external Law, but by a life-giving Spirit. Yet externals are not despised, for the One Spirit lives and manifests

Himself in the one Body. In this lies supremely the uniqueness of Christ's religion. He-and 

He alone of the founders of religion-brought to life a permanent organism, a permanent union of minds and souls, centering in Himself. 

Christians are not merely followers of Christ, but members of Christ, and of one another. 

Gautama, the Buddha, developed his society of the awakened ones, but the relation between them is purely external-the relation of teacher and pupils. It is his doctrine which unites them, not his life. The same may be said of Zoroaster, of Socrates, of Mohammed, and the other religious geniuses of the race. But Christ is not only the Teacher, He is the Life of Christians. 

What He founded was not a society which studied and propagated His ideas, but an organism which lived by His life. A Body indwelt and guided by His Spirit.

This conception is developed with marvelous power and lucidity by St. Paul but it is not confined to him. It is found no less in the teaching of St. John about the one Vine with the many branches, and in St. Peter's figure of the spirit-

ual temple built of living stones on the one foundation, Christ. The relation between Christ and Christians is organic-they are soul cells in His Body, they are vitalized by His Spirit. 

Christ is the Way to the Father, but no one can find that way and travel it except by the gift of the Holy Spirit. And that Spirit is found in the one Body, the one Temple, the one 

Vine: the Catholic Church. 

This Church, then, is the organism of the living Christ—it is a unique fact in the history of religion-it is the Spirit-possessing and 

Spirit-possessed community of God. And the same Spirit who indwells it guides its teachings and sustains its life. But its life and its teaching must both be sought in fellowship. This fellowship of truth and life—the possession of the Body as a whole-is in direct contrast to Protestantism, with its false spirit of independence, of individual self-sufficiency, its atomic conception of the Christian life. It is also sharply different from Romanism, which substitutes for the life and truth found in fellowship -the common possession of the organism as a whole the autocratic government, the oracular pronouncements, of a human head, as its principle of unity. As we have seen, neither of these two erroneous conceptions appears in the

New Testament. There is one Body-not a multitude of separate individuals. There is one 

Spirit-not an autocratic, external authority. 

The truth is found in fellowship-in the voice of the Church as a whole-not in the isolated opinions of self-sufficient individuals, nor in the arbitrary judgment of one individual, however high his station, imposing his judgments upon all. The many live the one life-they experience and bear witness to the one truth in lovein their unity and fellowship, and in this alone is the clear manifestation of the Spirit of Christ.

So it was for centuries. The Pentecostal Spirit lives and teaches through the Pentecostal community, not through isolated individuals, however clever, or however lofty their official position. A great heresy arises and threatens to engulf the Christian world. What is to serve the Church? Not the brilliancy of human intellect, for many of the most brilliant are carried headlong by the torrent. Not the authority of official position, for many of the Church's official teachers, including a Pope, apostatize. But the Holy Spirit does not desert the Church.

The fulness of apostolic truth and grace is found within her.  is still inspired by His Spirit, and His tongues, the

Pentecostal tongues of fire, are flashing forth upon chosen heads at the moment of crisis. A simple deacon arises. He voices the Church's truth, as he has received it. Through him the 

Spirit speaks, while many more clever, many more exalted, are dumb. The Council of the 

Church assembles. Her bishops, the successors of those Apostles who met at her first council, deliberate. The same Spirit who presided at 

Jerusalem presides at Nicæa. The faith of 

Christendom is saved-she confesses and worships her divine Lord, and not a creature, however exalted.

The battle is not over, however. Many worldly Christians, many-in fact the majority -of the Bishops, betray the faith. But this deacon-now a Bishop-rallies the faithful few. The Spirit preserves a remnant. The heretical creeds put forth by timid courtierbishops do not win the approval of the Church -amid travail and persecution the faithful minority persevere in the Catholic faith, until, by God's grace, the whole community is once more won to it. When the dust of battle has disappeared, the heresies and schisms have vanished into oblivion-the one Body and the one Faith remain, the gift and pledge of the one 

Spirit. Athanasius has prevailed against the

world. Christ has preserved His Church. God's truth has survived and conquered. Out of Zion hath God shined forth in perfect beauty.

Through the ages following, the story is often repeated. Christians fail, Bishops fail, Popes and Patriarchs fail, Councils fail-but the Church as a whole does not fail. The Spirit within preserves her. The Councils which tamper with her divine truth fail to win the assent of the Body as a whole, the erring Popes and Bishops are cast out of the ranks of the faithful, or brought to a better mind. Numbers mean little. The Council of Nicæa brought together only three hundred or so Orthodox bishops; that of Atiminum assembled six hundred heretics. But "there is no restraint to the Lord to save, by many or by few." When the Church does speak, Christ speaks through her-she is still, as at Pentecost, "the Church of the living 

God, the pillar and ground of the truth." Ages pass; kingdoms, empires, civilizations rise and fall; but she remains, and her truth remainsunshaken and unshakable.

But a sad day was to dawn. As in the history of the Jewish Church, the ancient people of God, men were not content to have God for their king, but demanded a visible, human monarch, so it was now. The rising peoples of the

West were not satisfied with the voice of the Holy Spirit speaking through the Church as a whole; they must have a centralized oracle of faith, the Papacy. They could not rest content with Christ, the invisible Head of the Church, they must have a visible monarch, the Bishop of Rome. "Nay, but we will have a king to reign over us." So it was in the West-the East remained loyal to the divine constitution of the Church. The West, arrived at intellectual maturity-self-confident, self-assertive, self-sufficient-felt itself competent to improve both the 

Creed and the Order of Christ's Church. An additional clause is inserted into the Creed, without the consent of the community as a whole. This, too, at a time when the East was submitting the decrees of the seventh great Council for the approval of the West. The East acted in a manner both lawful and brotherlythe West behaved in a lawless and loveless. fashion. The unlawful addition-which, regardless of its truth or falsehood, had no place in the Creed of the Church Universal-was first resisted by the Popes, then timidly accepted, and at last boldly championed and propagated. Together with this went ever increasing lust of power, ever more arrogant claims to lord it over the entire Christian world.

The Eastern half of the Church, true to the guidance of the Holy Spirit within her, resisted these claims. Admitting but One Head of the 

Church, she could not see a fallible mortal intrude himself into the place of the Son of God. 

Believing in a Spirit-guided Body, she could not conceive of one province or see of Christendom possessing a monopoly of divine inspiration. The breach was inevitable-Catholic 

Christendom was divided. The Great Church was rent in two. The East, recognizing the inspiration of the Spirit in the whole Body, stood over against the West, limiting that inspiration more and more to one man. The breach remains unhealed to this day. 

Did the West cease to be part of Christ's 

Catholic Church? Did it add one more to the array of heresies and schisms, cut off from the true Vine, amputated from the true Body, cast out of the living Temple? It would be easy to answer yes-certainly the greater part of the blame for the schism falls upon it—and certainly the East remained true to the ancient constitution of the Church, and to the divine Spirit to whom that constitution was due. Yet the nations of the Catholic West-unlike the schisms before them-retained the entire Catholic Faith (though with a dubious addition),

they retained the ancient hierarchy, deriving its orders from the Apostles, they retained the life-giving Sacraments, and the inspired Scriptures-all that they had possessed before, all that Catholics had ever had to make them Catholic. For the Papacy itself little or no excuse can be found; for the Churches in communion with it, which scarcely recognized what was taking place, every excuse can be found. 

On their part, there was no deliberate breach of communion-they were in communion with Rome, they continued in communion with her, unaware for the most part of the momentous consequences involved in her quarrel with the unchanging East, unconscious of the tremendous issues at stake. Had they wilfully and formally excommunicated the East, had they deliberately abandoned any essential part of the Church's heritage, then we should have to admit that they had rebelled against the living Spirit, that they had been cut off from the living fellowship, and were no longer parts of the Catholic Church of Jesus. But this did not happen-God overruled the breach, we may mercifully judge; the Body was not hacked into two, though it was grievously wounded; no limbs were amputated, though many were frightfully mutilated. East and West still formed, in the

eyes of God-in the eyes, too, of many of God's children-one Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church.

Yet the West had absorbed the poison and it was bound to do its work in the system. 

Whatever may be said for the dependent Churches, the action of the Papacy in regard to the East was an act of moral fratricide. And this moral fratricide culminated in the physical fratricide of the Fourth Crusade, when the

Pope's spiritual children turned aside from their goal and, with every species of wanton cruelty, sacked Constantinople, massacred their brother

Christians, fired churches, convents, and monasteries, outraged women consecrated to God, violated sanctuaries, spilled the Precious Blood of Christ on the altars, and trampled His sacred Body under foot. The Papacy, to be sure, had not intended this outrage, but it did not shrink from enjoying the fruits of this procedure. Nor has it ceased in the succeeding centuries from its determination to "rule or ruin" the Churches of the East-it has preferred to see them enslaved to the Turk, rather than to have them flourish independently. It has unscrupulously plotted the betrayal of those who refused to betray the ancient Order of the Church. It has labored assiduously for the de-

struction of those who would not consent to the destruction of their liberty in Christ, who would not substitute the working of an external authority for the Voice of the living Spirit, guiding the Beloved Community as a whole, and inspiring it from within.

In the West the poison continued to work. 

An alien principle had been admitted into the Church, which from being a Spirit-guided organism was more and more altered into a mancentered organization. An external authority was substituted for the inner workings of the 

Spirit of life. At first the authority was the hierarchy as a whole, though the Papal power increasingly overshadowed that of the Bishops. -and its sovereignty, once admitted, grew more and more absolute. The Christian was no longer a member of a living organism, but the subject of a semi-spiritual monarchy. 

The "Church" increasingly came to mean the hierarchy, with the Pope at its head. The 

Church was now something external to the Christian, not something of which he was a free and living part. There was a divorce between the Body and its members, or rather between the institution and its subjects. Accepting the Church as something external to himself, the Western Christian submitted to it. The

day was to come when he would no longer submit.

At last the storm broke. The Papacy had rebelled against the ancient order of Christ's Church, and substituted the mind of one man for the mind of the Body as a whole. It had replaced the voice of the Spirit indwelling the organism by the voice of a human autocrat presiding over an organization. It had sown the wind, and now it was to reap the whirlwind. 

Half of Europe rose in blind fury against the hoary despotism which ground down men's souls in the name of Christ, wrecking good, as well as evil, in its mad revolt. If one man's mind might be set against that of the Church, so might every man's. If one man's judgment could be exalted above the collective judgment of the Church, every man's judgment might be proclaimed independent of it. Protestantism is the legitimate child of Romanism. The Pope is indeed the first Protestant. Luther and Calvin are the true successors of Hildebrand and Innocent III. Individual autocracy inevitably gener ated individual anarchy.

Not that individualism and anarchy were intended by the leaders of the Protestant revolt.

They indeed set up a new authority in faith, the Bible. But it was a Bible divorced from the

Spirit-guided Church, which had given the Bible to the world, guarded, interpreted, and propagated it through the ages. One external authority—a chair-was replaced by another -a Book. Quite as much as Romanism, Protestantism depended on an external authoritythe internal authority of the Spirit dwelling in the unity and wholeness of the Church and expressing His voice in its collective teaching, was a conception quite alien to it. Moreover, the authority in question proved to be valueless, when separated from the Church. For what did the Bible mean? Who could say? Luther had his opinion, Calvin had his, Zwingli had his, Knox and countless others had theirs. Schisms and “isms" multiplied. "Wherever you find a 

Macleod," said a Scot proudly, "you find a 

Presbyterian church." "Yes, and where you find two Macleods, you find two Presbyterian churches," replied his friend. Such has been the general course of Protestantism. Moreover, the 

Protestants cut the last links which bound

Western Christendom to the ancient Churchnot only did they abandon more and more of the ancient faith, but the ancient sacramental system was overthrown, several of the Sacraments being abandoned and the rest mutilated;

the Priesthood was lost, Church-life and Church-consciousness were stricken with a deadly wound. The hundreds of sects in modern Protestantism, the loss of all authority in faith and morals, the confusion, the weakness, the fratricidal strife, the disappearance of vital elements of Christianity, the whittling down of the Christian religion into a naturalistic philosophy or an ethical code-such have been the logical results of the Reformation principles. 

Yes, and the logical result of Romanist principles as well. Just as in ancient Israel, the attempt to set up a visible kingship instead of the reign of God resulted not in unity but in division, so it was now. Just as in the inspired legend of Babel, the endeavor to establish a mere external unity led to a complete and lasting confusion, so again history repeated itself. The 

Papacy, far from being the center of unity of the Christian world, has been historically the fruitful parent of discord and schism. The attempt to build on a human foundation has been a miserable failure. "Other foundation can no man lay but Christ Jesus," says St. Paul. He alone is the cornerstone, the sure basis, of 

God's Church. Roman Catholicism and Prot estantism alike bear witness to the failure to

build on any other-Church history, especially from the Reformation onward, is eloquent testimony to that effect.

The Spirit-guided man has been substituted for the Spirit-guided organism in both Romanism and Protestantism. The first replaced the living, organic fellowship of the Church by an external, monarchical, governmental unity; the second, rebelling against this man-made, tyrannical unity, failed to replace it by the true, divine unity of Orthodoxy. An unnatural despotism was succeeded by an unprincipled revolt. 

Yet Catholic Orthodoxy was not dead in the West-it was not dead, but sleeping. The conception of the Episcopal Catholicism of early ages, as opposed to the Papal Catholicism of the Middle Ages, still survived deep in the bosom of the Church. The conception of a mighty organization had not quite destroyed that of a Christ-filled organism. The corruption of the Papacy, the spectacle of the three rival Popes, denouncing and excommunicating one another in the name of Christ, had served to revive it. And in one part of the West, at least, the lesson was not lost. The Church in 

England, heeding the voice of the Holy Spirit, broke with the autocratic power of the Papacy,

purged herself of medieval accretions in doctrine and practice, but refused to throw herself into the arms of the continental Protestantism. 

She declined equally the absolutism of Rome and the anarchy of Protestantism. Her Refor mation was just that—a reformation, not a revolution; a renewal, not a reconstructionthe purging of the old Church, not the creation of a new one. And the basis on which she reformed herself was the ancient, undivided Church-that is to say, the whole Church as opposed to its divided branches, or to the sects outside it. In the agreement of East and West, in the consentient witness of the divine fellowship, she saw the guidance of the Spirit of God, rather than in the pronouncements of an ecclesiastical autocrat, or the vagaries of masterful personalities. The Orthodoxy which had slumbered deep in her heart was awakened, revived, stirred to new life by the summons of the Holy Spirit. Again and again it seemed as if she must succumb to the assaults of Romanism and Protestantism from without or to the weakness, error, and sinfulness of her own children within.

Again and again foes and friends alike began to toll her funeral knell, but again and again she proved that the power of the risen Christ

lives on in the Beloved Fellowship, that the

Holy Church shares her Master's power of 

Resurrection from the grave.

The history of the Anglican communion after the Reformation is not by any means the history of a self-evident, ever victorious Catholicism. On the contrary, the Church, finding herself between the upper and the nether millstones of Romanism and Protestantism, had to fight continually for her very life, and her Catholicity frequently seemed most unstable and precarious. While the original purpose of her Reformation was a non-papal Catholicism, there soon arose a party, dominated by the influence of the foreign Reformers, who wished to protestantize the Church and her doctrine. 

It would be idle to pretend that this party was not influential, at times predominant, in the Church, but the Catholic element, and above all the Providence of God, preserved her Catholicity from shipwreck. The faith of the Church remained Catholic, though expressed at times in ambiguous formulae of a somewhat Protestant flavor, and the standard of appeal throughout remained Holy Scripture as interpreted by the "Catholic Fathers and ancient Bishops" (Canons of 1571, etc.), and by the General

Councils of the undivided Church. It is to this

standard that every dubious or ambiguous statement in the Prayer Book and the "Thirty-nine Articles" has to be brought; it is this appeal to the undivided Church, that is to say, the whole Church, which determines the nature of the Anglican Church and the meaning of her formularies.

The Anglican Church has been called "a 

Catholic Church with a Protestant people."

The description is suggestive, though not entirely accurate. Even when Protestant views with regard to the meaning and importance of the Priesthood, the Sacraments, etc., were most common, the Church preserved the Catholic Priesthood and Sacraments, along with the Scriptures, the Creeds, and the other essential elements of the Catholic religion. Even though a Protestant interpretation might be popularly given to the teaching of parts of the Bible and of the Prayer Book, yet the Church's appeal to the Fathers and the undivided Church supplies the key to their true meaning. The Church never denied any part of the Catholic faith, though many of her children did; Bishops and priests might err from the Church's religion, and "teach in her name what was not her doctrine, "but that doctrine remained Catholic and true. And each period of Protestant ascendancy

has been followed by a new and greater Catholic revival; each period of deadness and stagnation by a greater and more glorious burst of life; each seeming crucifixion by a new and more wondrous resurrection, for the power of the risen Christ is hers.

Especially is this the case with the Oxford Movement, the Catholic Revival. This movement, beginning with Keble's sermon on National Apostasy in 1833, has in the past century reawakened in the minds and hearts of

Churchmen everywhere the meaning of the "Holy Catholic Church," and of the Sacraments. It has aroused a new Christ-centered zeal and devotion; a fresh spirit of sacrificial love has raised up a multitude of heroic missionaries, religious, confessors, and saints, both men and women. The dry bones of conventional religion have revived as the Spirit of the living 

God breathed into them once more the breath of life. In spite of fierce opposition within the 

Church as well as without it, in spite of misunderstanding, bitterness, and persecution, the movement has gone forward, and is still advancing, bringing home to Churchmen truths long forgotten or neglected—the truth of the

Church as a divine organism, the Sacraments as the means of grace, the Real Presence of Jesus

Christ, priest and victim, in the Eucharist, the Eucharistic Sacrifice, Confession as the means of securing assured pardon and renewal, the Priesthood as an organ of divine institution and power, the Communion of Saints, with the blessed certainty of the efficacy of our prayers for the departed and of their prayers for usall these truths, never denied by the Church, but long neglected, have once more received the emphasis that is their due, alike in faith and in practice. In England and in America-wherever the English tongue is spoken-the Church has arisen from the dead and given witness to the Pentecostal power within her. And this has been due chiefly, under God, to the Catholic Revival, absorbing in itself all that was good and true in Evangelicalism and Liberalism. But the greatest triumphs, we are sure, lie in the future. And so, while contending earnestly for the "faith once for all delivered unto the saints" as the inspired writer bids us, we seek none the less earnestly to give heed to the other divine message which summons us to "hear what the 

Spirit is saying unto the Churches." The same

Spirit who brought the Church into being as Christ's Body, who has directed and animated her through the ages, is still with her, guiding her into all the truth, purifying and perfecting

her from the blemishes of earth, that He may present her unto her Lord as "a glorious

Church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing."

God, in His infinite compassion and lovingkindness, wills not to remain an unknown God, nor to be known only through the partial and fragmentary intimations of nature, but to be known as He is in Himself, as He has revealed 

Himself in our Saviour Christ. He has not said to the sons of man, "Seek ye Me in vain,' rather He "who commanded the light to shine out of darkness hath shined in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of 

God in the face of Jesus Christ." 99 How is the knowledge of divine truth to become ours? How are we to find the faith, the light which is to guide our souls to life eternal? 

Surely, as the first Apostles and disciples found

it, not in the first instance through the written Word, not even through the spoken Word alone, but through the gift of the Holy Spirit in the Church. The Pentecostal Faith is to be found in the Pentecostal Fellowship, in the Body of Christ, in which alone is the fulness of His Spirit. Hence the title of this chapter-for "the faith is the faith of a Fellowship, before it is the faith of the individual Christian". and only in the Body of Christ can the truth of 

Christ be known with assurance.

Revealed truth is not to be found by an isolated individual with a book in his hands, nor does it consist in the pronouncements of an external authority, whether that authority be the

Pope, or a council of Bishops, or a group of scholars, or even the "assured results" of critical investigation. The truth of Christ is revealed not by flesh and blood, but by His Father which is in heaven, and it is the possession of the Spirit-guided organism, the Holy Catholic Church, which guards it, or rather experiences and lives it, from generation to generation. The Church knows Christ with an interior knowledge, due to the indwelling of His Holy Spirit, who guides her into all the truth and bears the ark of faith safe and invulnerable

over the seas of error. Only in vital union with the Church, in living unity with all her members, can the individual find the assurance of truth, which the Son of God came to bring him, and which He sealed with His own Blood.

The position which I am advancing presupposes that God has given us a definite, unique, and final revelation of Himself in Christ, and that that revelation is to continue accessible to men to the end of time. It presupposes a Spirit who preserves it and an organism which enshrines it. It presupposes that the Church is not a debating society, not an anarchist club, not a research society for the discovery of truth, but a truth-possessing, truth-teaching Body, the Body of Christ, possessing the mind of Christ and His Spirit.

It may serve to clarify the point of view taken here if we contrast it with other approaches prevalent today, not with a view to controversy, but to positive exposition. First with that of Protestantism. Khomiakoff, the great Russian Orthodox theologian, has summed up the modern Protestant conception of the Church as "a society of good men, differing in all their opinions, but earnestly seeking for the Truth, with the total certainty that

it has not yet been found, and with no hope at all of ever finding it." This position, which every day draws closer to absolute scepticism, is a natural recoil from the Reformation assumption that every individual, with the Bible before him, is able to discover the truth for himself, independently of the Church which gave him the Bible. It rests on a false individualism, which treats each Christian as a selfsufficient unit, and ignores his social and organic relation to the Body of Christ. It fails to realize that the Christian life is lived in an organism, and that Christian truth is to be known with assurance only in that organism. 

The Roman Catholic approach is seemingly quite the opposite of this. Christ has withdrawn from the world, to be sure, but has left a visible representative, His Vicar, who knows the truth infallibly and under certain conditions utters it infallibly for the benefit of the general Church.

The Protestant and Roman conceptions, superficially opposed, are in reality at one in their basic position: the disregard of the organic nature of the Church, and the substitution of one man's judgment-whether of the Pope or the higher critic, or the average Christian-for the corporate mind of the Church. 

Protestantism and Romanism are twins. The 

Protestant exalts his private judgment, or the private judgment of Luther, Knox, Fosdick, or the latest critic, above the teaching and experience of the Body of Christ. The Roman bows to the ex cathedra decrees of the Bishop of Rome, which are "irreformable of themselves, and not from the consent of the Church." According to the truly Catholic conception, the individual is neither shut up within the narrow confines of his own mind and personality nor enslaved to the mind and personality of another he loses himself, or rather finds himself, in the larger life of the organism, the Body of Christ, which has the mind of Christ. 

Protestantism and Romanism are agreed, moreover, in making the truth something exterior to the body of Christians, something to be approached from without. Both have only an indirect and mediate, not a direct and immediate, union with the truth as it is in Jesus. 

Catholicity, with its organic outlook, its strong conviction that the whole truth, the whole Christ, can be grasped only by the whole Church, knows Christ from within, as indwelling the Church which is His Body, in the might and power of His Holy Spirit. She has no need to seek the truth, the Christ, apart from her own organism, nor will she admit any such hard and fast distinctions between a teaching Church and a learning Church as would make of the

hierarchy a caste instead of an organic differentiation within the Body of Christ. 

Our Lord could scarcely have said, nor could anyone plausibly have attributed to Him the saying: "It is expedient for you that I go away," if He intended to leave only a human. representative, a phantom of Himself, an external teacher of the truth. Better that we have the Truth Incarnate than His delegate. It would not have been expedient for us that Christ go away if we were to have contact with Him only through heaps of written pages, whether of the Bible or of the critics. Better that we have the living accents of the Son of God than their distant echo. It was expedient for us that He go away if the truth no longer remains simply an outward fact (as it was even during the earthly life of our Saviour) but an inward experience and presence, the presence of the Holy Spirit, the true Vicar of Christ, animating the whole Body in all its members, and guiding it into all the truth. The Church's faith rests on no external oracle-whether an infallible man or an infallible Book-but on the living Christ, and on the living Spirit, leading her into a vital assimilation, an interior perception, of the truth of God. She knows of no formal, juridical, external "authority"-for even Christ Himself

is not that but a stream of life, a Spirit-guided organic experience of Christ the Way, the 

Truth, and the Life within her.

The Protestant and Roman theories have a further point of resemblance, in that, since they both rest only on one man's private judgment (whether of the individual Christian or of a single Christian bishop) they offer no divine assurance of truth. The truth which they do hold, even when it approaches most nearly to Catholic Orthodoxy, is in reality only a matter of opinion (the opinion of the Bishop of Rome or of the individual concerned) and has not that invincible solidity of faith which rests upon the consentient witness of the Church. Moreover, there is no certainty as to what their creed will be tomorrow. Has the Church an infallible organ of expression? That is, has it any agency, such as a Pope or a Council of Bishops, which of itself has binding authority and infallibility in matters of faith? To this our answer must be an unqualified No, as leading theologians both among ourselves and among the Eastern Orthodox recognize clearly today. Nothing less than the total organism, the whole Body of Christ, is immune from error. Patriarchs have erred, Popes have erred, the majority of Bishops, in council and out of it, have erred, only the whole Church as a Body has never erred. Thus in the Arian conflict in the fourth century, when the Pope and the majority of Bishops denied the faith, that faith was saved by the steadfast laity, led by a handful of the clergy. Hence the sharp Roman division of the Body of Christ into a teaching Church and a learning Church-a division which some Anglicans and Orthodox have uncritically admitted-cannot be for a moment maintained. The real distinction is between a teaching Spirit and a learning Body, or between "a teaching Church and a learning world." The Eastern Patriarchs in their Response (1848) to the Encyclical of Pius IX, set forth this truth clearly. "The real guardian of the faith," they say, "is the body of the Church,

that is to say, the people itself." Wholeness (in other words, Catholicity) of faith depends upon the Church as a whole, not simply upon the hierarchy, much less any one member of it. 

The Eastern Patriarchs reject the rigid division of the Church into Ecclesia docens and Ecclesia discens and recognize that mutual love, and unity with the whole Body of Christ, is the only security of Orthodox belief. Even an Ecumenical Council derives its significance from the fact that it has been accepted by the Church as a whole, clergy and laity alike, and thus recognized as expressing truly the latent consciousness of the Body. A recent Anglican writer has given lucid expression to this view: "The General (here-ecumenical) Councils are held in honor, not because they were infallible, and could not possibly have erred, but because the whole inspired sense of Christendom was convinced that in these particular cases their members did, in fact, give a true interpretation of the Gospel of Christ." It would be a mistake to conceive of Anglo- Catholicism as a timid via media between Protestantism and Romanism (it is not between them, but above them), or on the other hand as "Popery without the Pope." The Papacy has so colored the whole range of Roman faith and practice that while the Churches in communion with Rome are essentially Catholic, their Catholicism is overlaid with imperialism, feudalism, and Italianism. The organic conception of 

Catholicity has fallen into the background. To take just one illustration: the doctrine of the 

Communion of Saints, and the blessed interchange of prayer between them and ourselves, as members of the one Body of Christ, has been corrupted by such conceptions as that of dulia (servitude) and hyper-dulia (extra-servitude), ideas quite appropriate to the feudal system, but scarcely adequate to describe the organic relation which exists between fellow servants of

We repudiate not only the Papacy, but all the accretions that the Papal system has brought along with it. For example, we, in com-

mon with the Eastern Orthodox, reject wholeheartedly the "Romish doctrine of Purgatory," of the Treasury of Merits, of Salvation as earned by external works, and of Indulgences, with the thousands of plenaries and tens of thousands of quarantines, and all the rest of the mad mathematics of a system which tends to import into the sanctuary of Christ the mechanism and the atmosphere of the stock exchange, and to substitute the wage-slavery of hirelings for the glorious liberty of the sons of God.

The Church, by virtue of her vital union with her glorified Head, and the sure guidance of the living Spirit within her, is incapable of error in faith. No doctrine which has once been set forth as an essential article of faith has ever been discarded, nor could it be. [Despite loud assertions to the contrary, materialistic ideas of heaven, of the Resurrection Body, of the Ascension to the "right hand" of the Father, and the "three-story universe," have never been dogmas of the Catholic faith. So, too, the immediate return of Christ to judge the world was a general expectation of the early Christians, including St. Paul; it was not, like the 

Resurrection and Eucharistic dogmas, one of the things which they had "received from the

To do so would mean that God's Revelation had to all intents and purposes disappeared from the earth, that the assurance of truth had been lost forever, that the Spirit of truth had deserted her. All development of doctrine, all "re-interpretation," all "re-statement," must be understood from this point of view.

Modernism, for example, ignores this principle, and so, while claiming to "re-state" or "translate" the historic faith into modern terms, it really substitutes something radically different, something which is not Christianity at all. We are reminded of the scene in A Midsummer Night's Dream, where Bottom, after his metamorphosis, appears with an ass's head, whereupon his friend exclaims: "Bless thee, Bottom, bless thee, thou art translated!" So, after viewing the work of the Modernist wise men and magicians, we rub our eyes with amazement and cry out: "Christianity, thou art translated!"

Let us re-state the Catholic faith in modern terms, by all means; but let us make certain that it is the faith, and not a man-made substitute. 

The faith, to be sure, is not a static but a dynamic thing; it develops, in the sense that not only individuals, but the whole Church, sees continually a new wealth of meaning, value, and application in it, but certainly not in the sense that dogmas change their fundamental meaning. 

The dogmas of the Nicene Creed, of the seven Ecumenical Councils, and those voiced by the consent of "the Catholic Fathers and ancient

Bishops," to whom our Church appealed at the Reformation, as true interpreters of Holy Scripture-in short, all doctrines held as essential by the deliberate judgment of East and West in common, are the utterance of the Church herself, and hence of the Spirit who indwells her. 10 Thus the doctrines of the Real Presence, of the Eucharistic Sacrifice, of apostolic succession, of the sacramental (i.e., grace-conveying) character of ordination are not open questions, optional opinions, "non-essentials," which Anglicans are (morally) free to accept or reject. 

They come to us on the authority of the undivided Church (that is to say, the whole

Certain doctrines, to be sure, come to be seen in a different context in one age from that of another—thus we today view the mode of the

Resurrection of the Body, or the mode of the inspiration of the Scriptures in a different way from that of our forefathers. What of it? The 

Church has never defined the mode, she has simply asserted the fact—and she continues to do so. To make this change of context an excuse for making a change of content in dogmasfor example, for denying or explaining away the Virgin Birth—is absurd, and opens the door to Rationalism of the wildest and most irrational sort, or worse still to a vague sentimentalism which believes only what is "felt" to be true. The Church's faith-which is not simply a matter of past decisions, of "authority," but a living tradition and experience-forms an organic whole. To deny one part is to undermine all; it is to set oneself up as a teacher and critic of the Spirit-guided Church, instead of a son and disciple; it is to lose that certitude of faith which our Lord promised to those who are His. It reduces the Church to a debating society, and substitutes the guess-work of man for sure revelation of God. We cannot have it both ways either the Holy Spirit has guided the Body of Christ in the past into assured truth, preserving her from corporate error, in which case we can depend upon His sure guidance in the present and future; or He has not

so guided her, nor is there any evidence that He will do so, in which case we may well relapse into utter scepticism. Faith disappears, and only a multitude of warring opinions remain. 

If it be asked why there is so much dissention and disagreement among Anglicans, the answer is that the dissention is due, not to the following of this rule of faith, but to its violation. Wherever it prevails, as it does among the 

Orthodox Easterns, both unity and freedom are found in a harmonious synthesis. This will be the case among ourselves when the Catholic Movement has won its way, as it will, to universal acceptance.

The Catholic conception of the faith, which I have endeavored to outline, combines the search for truth and the possession of truth in an organic unity, in marked contrast with rival theories. The latter appertains to the Church as a whole, the former to her individual members. The search for truth is the right and duty of every Orthodox Christian, who is no less free in the employment of his intelligence and all his native energies (assisted by grace) than the most extreme Protestant. In fact he is more free, for he has access to data (supplied by Catholic tradition) and to whole ranges of religious experience (to a unity of truth and

life) from which the Protestant is necessarily excluded. The possession of truth belongs to the whole Church, the total organism, where it can never fail. There it is not tied up with any inanimate thing-any fetish-such as a Book, the sense of which, and therefore the soul of which, is lost, apart from the Church's life, (of which it is a precipitate), or a Chair, a privileged See, a particular organ of the hierarchy, exalted above the Church (to which it owes its organic functions). Both types of Fundamentalism, Roman and Protestant, are utterly alien from the mind of the Church and represent a reversion to Judaism, the religion of a Book and a Chair. Nor does her faith shift like Modernism, with every wind of radical criticism, for it is wholly based upon, and centered in, Jesus 

Christ, the same yesterday, today, and for ever.

Rather the truth abides in her as a living experience, allied with the mighty upthrust of the dynamic, creative forces of the indwelling Spirit. Catholic Orthodoxy alone unites in perfect harmony the utmost freedom in the (individual) search for truth, and the utmost security in the (corporate) possession of it. Hence she awaits with eager confidence the coming of her Judge and Saviour, the vision of the kingdom of God, and the life everlasting.

This is the faith of the Body of Christ, to which we were committed at our Baptism and to which every member solemnly pledges himself anew each time he comes to offer the High

Sacrifice and to receive the Bread of Life. This is the truth which we Anglo-Catholics, or Western Orthodox (to use a supplementary title which we might well adopt), hold as from God in common with our brothers of the Eastern Orthodox Church, without addition or subtraction. It is not something which we are free to keep for ourselves, or fritter away-it is a trust with which we are entrusted from above not for our own sakes alone but for that of all 

God's children. To us, though utterly unworthy,

To sum up very briefly: "Ye shall know the truth," says Romanism, "or rather ye shall submit your minds and consciences to a man who knows the truth, and the truth shall make you slaves." "Ye shall be free," says Protestantism, "free to be ever learning, and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth." "Ye shall know the truth," says Catholicity, "and the truth shall make you free. If the Son shall make you free, then are ye free indeed!"

Such is the constant faith of the Church, such her unshakable witness. And that faith and that witness, though a stumbling block to Judaizing Fundamentalists, and to Hellenizing Modernists foolishness, remains to them that believe (however fundamental their approach, how-

ever modern their outlook), the revelation of the power of God and the wisdom of God, made manifest to men through Jesus Christ our Lord.

CHAPTER IV THE WORSHIP OF THE CHURCH 

JORSHIP is not only the most divine thing in the world, it is also the most human. The man who does not worship is only half a man.

The worship of the Church revolves around the central article of the Christian faith-or rather, it is the living expression of that faith -the unity of the divine and the human, of Creator and creation, in the God-man, Jesus Christ. In union with the living Christ, true 

God and true man, as living members of His mystical Body, we are ourselves united through Him with the eternal Father, by the action of the life-giving Spirit. This is the meaning of every Sacrament of the Church, of every rite and every act of worship. The offering up of our human wills and lives to God through Him whose will and life are alone perfectly acceptable, is met by the gift of God's life to us, in the Church which is Christ's Body, the fulness of Him that filleth all in all.

The Church, then, is a worshipping fellowship, a worshipping Body. Therefore, the 

Church's worship is corporate and organic. In the prayer of the Church, the prayer of each of her children and of all of them together is heard her Prayer Book is truly a "Book of

Common Prayer." The Catholic Christian is not left alone in his individual isolation, nor is he dependent upon the individual activity of some one else, who will undertake his cause before God. He is not shut up in his own narrow individuality, condemned either to engage in his own private devotions while the great Act is celebrated in an unknown tongue, or to play a merely passive part and pronounce his Amen to whatever the minister, according to his individual taste and temperament, may see fit to utter.

The fellowship which is developed in worship is the deepest of all fellowships, for it is rooted and grounded in the eternal God Himself. This is particularly the case with Catholic worship, since, according to the Church's conception, the sacraments, especially the Blessed

Sacrament of the altar, do not merely express a human fellowship, but create or deepen a divine fellowship. Thus "we the many are one 

Body, because we all partake of the one Bread."

Only in the unity of the Church, only in the fellowship of the worshipping Body, is the God whose own very nature is fellowship-the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit-truly and rightly apprehended. And only in union with the Triune God, manifested and approached in His Son Jesus Christ, can a true basis be laid for universal and enduring human fellowship. 

For this fellowship springs from the nature of the eternal Reality, and the more one enters into it, the more does one partake himself of reality, in union with his fellow worshippers. 

Here he apprehends himself, not as a cog in a social and economic machine, but as an end in himself in organic union with other ends-inthemselves. He apprehends himself most vividly as a spiritual personality, when he recognizes himself as one of a family of spiritual personalities. He gives concrete expression to that recognition in the worshipping fellowship which is not the product of men's efforts and aspirations, however lofty, but of God's giftwe should say, of God's self-giving. The

Church which is of God, not of men, which is orientated not toward time but toward eternity, makes possible a social experience which far transcends that of any human association. The worshipper at-oned with God in Christ, is at

the same time at-oned, in His Body Mystical, with the worshippers of all ages, Apostles, martyrs, prophets, virgins, confessors, and all saints the living, the departed, yes and with those yet unborn.

In this fellowship, whose center is God Himself, the true nature of personality becomes evident. It is not the self-sufficient, self-complete, atomic sort of thing which the pure individualist conceives it to be. It finds both its source and its perfection in an organism. On the other hand, the personality which is discovered or created in worship attains an inner life, a depth, an immeasurable worth, of which the ultrasocialist can form no conception. There is the paradox; individualism is not the friend but the foe of individuality. Man, in abasing and annihilating himself before God, realizes and completes himself in Him. In losing himself in the organism he finds himself, his true life, unutterably enriched and perfected. All the false barriers of class and caste fade away in the light of this supreme experience; the things which raise one man above another are seen to be nothing in the sight of the transcendent 

God. The loftiness of man is humbled, and the haughtiness of man is bowed down; and the 

Lord of hosts alone is exalted. Yet with this.

equality, indeed as its necessary condition, there is a hierarchy of function. This the individual gladly accepts, and therein finds his true place and meaning, his divinely-intended and all glorious destiny. There are many members in the one Body; yet each has his own distinctive office. He may easily be dispensed with by the world; but to God he is irreplaceable in his post and station. Confessing his betrayal of these, he passes through penitence to peace, and finds himself a member-a living, unique, indispensable member-of a Body in which God's will, His love, His very life, is incarnated. A new individuality, a new self-consciousness, grows up within him, the more he shares the organic consciousness of that worshipping fellowship which has the mind of Christ. All things are his! Having nothing, he possesses all things; for to him to live is Christ.

The Churchman's ordinary posture in worship is kneeling. He worships with his whole personality, body and soul together. This is psychologically as well as religiously sound and valuable. To sit through most of the service, as is the custom with some religious bodies, or to stand with one's arms crossed or one's hands in his pockets, seems to the instructed Catholic unspeakably slovenly and irreverent. We do

not behave thus in the presence of a great human potentate-how much less in the presence of the King of kings. The Church aims to develop in her children a keen sense of the mysterious, the "numinous" in religion; she would have them appear with awe and reverence and self-abasement before the aweful majesty of the All-Holy God. No easy familiarity, no light carelessness of manner, befits those who would approach the High and lofty One that inhabiteth eternity, whose Name is holy. Especially is this the case when we draw nigh to partake of the sacred Body and the precious Blood of our Saviour. The spirit of the Church is truly expressed in one of her best-loved Communion hymns, taken from one of the ancient liturgies: (Hymnal. No. 339)

The Church at prayer re-lives and reproduces in each of her faithful children the experience of prophets and saints in their apprehension of the supreme God, "the utterly

Abraham cried out: "I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, who am but dust and ashes." Job confessed: "I have heard of Thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye seeth Thee; wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." Jacob exclaimed: "How dreadful is this place, this is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven!" Moses was commanded: "Take thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou treadest is holy ground." Saul at Damascus gate fell blinded though regenerate before the Lord of Glory. The seer of Patmos on beholding the Son of Man fell at His feet as one dead. So the Church would have us enter the

God is not the good-natured, invertebrate creation of the modern sentimentalist's imagination. 

Our God is a consuming fire! And so the 

Church reëchoes, now in hushed, awe-struck whisper, now in the shout of triumphant ecstasy, the song of the seraphim: "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of hosts. Heaven and earth are full of Thy glory. Glory be to Thee, O 

Lord most high."

Yet the Church as a worshipping fellowship conceives of God not only as utterly transcendent, utterly other, but also as most intimately present in her midst. Her God is not only a God afar off, but is also a God nigh at hand. “Out beyond the shining of the furthest star," yet living, energizing, and pulsating in the hearts of His children. As His majesty is, so is His mercy. The very sense of our own nothingness, our own unworthiness, our own alienation from God, makes possible, in and through Christ's mystical Body, a reconciliation, a nearness, a union, which without it would be unthinkable. 

Therefore the accents of penitence and contrition in the Church's prayers are mingled with those of joy and exultation. Beginning with the vision of ourselves as God sees us, in all the naked loathsomeness and foulness of our sin and ingratitude, our backslidings and treacher-

ies, we go on to the vision of ourselves as God sees us in Christ, as living members of His Body, and therefore as potential saints, "other 

Christs." From the "grievous remembrance" and "intolerable burden" of his sins, the worshipper goes on to the peace which passeth all understanding, from his loneliness, his estrangement from God and men, he advances to a fellowship with the Father and with His Son 

Jesus Christ, effected by the Holy Spirit, and to a true communion with all God's saints, with the whole Body of Christ. The old sin-stained, isolated, and self-centered humanity gives way increasingly to the sanctified, God-empowered, Christ-centered, Spirit-filled humanity, the

Church in her togetherness. Already there is a partial realization of the goal of creation -"to gather together in one all things in Christ, both which are in heaven, and which are on earth❞—the breaking down of all partitions between man and man, and between God and man, the return of all creation to God, and a union of the Creator and His creatures in which God shall be all in all.

All the mystery, all the beauty, all the power, and all the sublimity of the Catholic religion are concentrated in the Holy Eucharist. One cannot be indifferent to the Mass-he loves it,

or he hates it; and according as he does one or the other, he will live differently and die differently. There the supernatural, awe-compelling, mysterious character of Christianity strikes us with full force, and it is this which makes the Liturgy either the foundation on which we build our spiritual life, or the rock of stumbling and stone of offense on which we bruise ourselves. Fr. Figgis has truly remarked: "Taking the religious sense as a given fact, are we right in supposing that a religion without mystery would satisfy its needs? Are not those very mysteries, which are most repugnant to the rationalist, the very elements which make the faith so great? I take one instance the cardinal one-the Eucharist. Believe in it or not you cannot deny that no other rite has gathered round it such tenderness of devotion or stimulated so deeply man's sense of God's nearness and love; nor will it be disputed that here is mystery enshrined in the actual and the concrete-not far off, but in the daily life. For this reason the Eucharist is inevitably the first object of dislike to the rationalistic temper, and offends men who will accept other and less immediate mysteries. Yet Europe, since Wyclif, affords ample evidence that where this mystery is ignored or denied, religious life-except for

spurts of individual piety and mysticism-becomes chill and commonplace, and in time the other supernatural aspects are also seen to vanish, as in Germany and Geneva" (The Gos pel and Human Needs, J. N. Figgis, C.R.). 

Yet it is needful to emphasize, what has been said before, that the strength of the Church's worship, the energy of the Church's life, is derived precisely from the fact that they are not built on the needs or instincts of human nature, though thoroughly consonant with them, but on objective reality-they are not man-centered but God-centered, or shall we say God-and-man centered?

The Catholic Christian conceives of worship in terms not of getting but of giving. He comes to Church not to hear a sermon, nor to get inspiration or help, but to offer God the one acceptable sacrifice-a broken, and a contrite heart. But since our contrition is always so poor and imperfect, and the offering of ourselves so marred and stained with sin, we can offer ourselves to God only in union with His sole-begotten Son. We can be acceptable to God only "in the Beloved." Therefore the Christian

Sacrifice is necessarily nothing less than Christ 

Himself, Christ crucified, Christ pleading His death and extending His life in our midst,

Christ both Priest and Victim, offering Himself and His mystical Body in union with Him to the Father through the eternal Spirit.

The idea of coming to Church in order to "enjoy the service"-in other words, to be entertained-or to accumulate a sum of merits, indulgences, etc., seems to the true Catholic something foreign to the Christian spirit. The former smacks of the theatre, the latter of the banking house. Both seem to lack the organic conception of the one Body in union with the one Head, offering itself, or rather being offered, to God, by Him and in Him. Both appear to leave man in his individual weakness and isolation, set over against God, making terms with God, or satisfying some need or instinct of his own nature rather than yielding himself up to His Maker in a mighty movement of self-forgetfulness, which is at the same time one of supreme self-fulfilment.

In Catholic worship, the altar, not the pulpit, is the central fact. The Godward aspect of religion thus receives greater emphasis than the manward. There is far less danger of the priest at the altar coming between the worshipper's soul and God, than of the preacher in the pulpit doing so. The personality of the man in the pulpit is of tremendous importance; the personal-

ity of the man at the altar is of no importance at all. The very vestments which proclaim the office and function of sacrifice conceal the individuality of the priest, and prevent its intruding into the foreground of consciousness. The word of man, whether it be that of the Protestant preacher or of the Roman Catholic "director of conscience," assumes undue prominence in other systems; in Catholicity it is the living 

Word of God, the Word made flesh and sacramentally dwelling among us, who focuses all our attention. Unlike the other two systems, Catholicity permits of no human mediation, strictly so-called, between the individual and God-none but that of the "one Mediator between God and man, the man, Christ Jesus." 

The priest viewed from one angle is merely the organ of the Body (which through common prayer in a common tongue is able to associate itself with him). From another, he is simply "dramatis persona" through whom God acts in the great drama of redemption, which is at once symbolized and made available to us in the celebration of the Divine Liturgy. The 

Church produces countless holy souls; she is indeed the Mother of saints; yet the experience of "holiness" is primarily generated in the divine Mysteries themselves; it is not dependent

upon the "holiness" of individual ministers. 

The Christian is sanctified, "polarized," by the Holy Church; not the Church by individual 

Christians. Thus again the Liturgy conserves and exemplifies the truth that salvation is God's act, not man's; man's part is simply to lay hold on the great salvation offered him, and make it his own. It is not so much that he finds God as that he is found of Him; he seeks only to apprehend that for which he is apprehended in 

Christ Jesus. And the liturgical, sacramental experience is, by God's ordinance, the great experience of "holiness," of God. Through it, the whole personality, the whole life of the worshipper, is to be "polarized," consecrated, offered to God and transformed by Him. And this experience is given directly by the all holy God; it does not require the mediation of "holy," human personalities to impart it.

It is easy to cast charges of magic and materialism at the Church's worship, but such charges rest upon a fundamental misunderstanding. The revolt of Protestantism against liturgical and sacramental worship is one which it is easy for us to understand, when we consider the abuses which had been rampant for some time. As Fr. Carson, a distinguished Roman priest and scholar, and a genuine lover of

But to account for a reaction, even to sympathize with it, is not the same thing as to justify it. The abuse of a thing is no argument against its right use. Everything connected with religion has its dangers; but abolition is not the remedy. Protestantism itself, after seeking the experience of the "holy," the numinous, in revivalism and evangelism, on the one hand, and in the inner light, on the other, has become distressingly aware of the dangers that beset both paths. The one may easily barbarize religion; the other evaporate it into nothingness. Both are liable, far more than liturgical and sacramental worship, to issue in a religion divorced from practical everyday life; one which leaves the outer world to the mercy of the mere materialist and secularist, while one looks within only for peace, and not also for power.

Let us consider briefly the historic background of the Eucharist: The Jews, like all other races of men, drew near to the most High 

God through sacrifices. Sheep, goats, and other animals were offered up. Of course these sacrifices could not really take away sins, they could only symbolize the Sacrifice to come which would do so; and as the prophets pointed out, they were of no use at all unless the worshipper offered himself along with them. True sacrifice always involves self-sacrifice. "The sacrifice of 

God is a troubled spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, Thou wilt not despise."

But here was the difficulty. The sacrifice of animals was no worthy oblation-"it is impossible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sin." On the other hand, the offering of ourselves to God is unworthy of Him, because we are so soiled and defiled with sinour contrition is so imperfect, our motives (even at their best) so mixed, our lives so largely self-centered instead of God-centered.

How, then, shall I approach the eternal God? "He is the Holy of Holies, and I the scum of sinners"-how then can I be accepted by Him?

The Church and the Bible give us the answer. 

Man can be accepted by God only "in the Beloved," only so far as he is united to God's dear Son. "No one cometh unto the Father, but by Me," says our Lord. On the Cross our 

Lord, by His obedience unto death, by His infinite love for God and men, made a sacrifice which outweighs, in the sight of His Father, all the disobedience, all the lovelessness of mankind. For He who hung there is God the Son. 

We should not think of the Atonement as a commercial transaction, as if it were the offering of so much blood for so much sin. Such crude, barbarous conceptions, unworthy of God, have sometimes been taught in the past, so that intelligent men have turned from the whole idea of sacrifice in disgust. As St. Bernard reminds us, it was not Christ's death that was pleasing to the Father, but His will, in voluntarily laying down His life on our behalf. "Non mors placuit Patri, sed voluntas sponte morientis." It was not so much His death, as His obedience unto death; not so much the shedding of His blood, as the infinite love which the blood-shedding expressed, which achieved the

salvation of the world. Now men can turn to 

God in trust and confidence. The Cross is not the revelation of God's wrath, but of His love, giving His only-begotten Son, on our behalf.

But to enter into the spirit of that sacrifice we must sincerely and wholeheartedly offer ourselves to God, not simply at the altar but in our daily life as well. The altar must become the center, the focal point, of our entire life. At home, in our place of business, in our recreation, at work or at play, the spirit of sacrificial love must predominate. We must consign to the scrapheap where it belongs the current cant and nonsense about self-expression, living one's own life, being one's self, and all the rest of that trite foolishness. Self-denial is the only road to true self-fulfilment; self-sacrifice is the only way to true selfrealization. The separation of Scripture from tradition, the destruction or weakening of the organic tie which binds each to the other and both to the Mystical Body of the Lord, results in a mis-

understanding of the one which is retained. 

Neither tradition nor Scripture can stand alone. 

For "Scripture is written tradition; tradition is unwritten Scripture." Each becomes dead without the other. Both Protestantism and Romanism have greatly impoverished their religious and spiritual life by this unnatural separationthe former attempting to live by documents from which the living Spirit who speaks and interprets through the Church's tradition has been abstracted; the latter content to drink from a stagnant pool instead of from the fountain-head of the Word of God. Scripture and tradition are two forms, two manifestations of the same truth, and each is to be apprehended aright only in connection with the other, in the living and organic unity of the Church.

This point of view has been expounded with striking force and clarity by an honored Bishop of the American Church: "The revelation of God to the Apostles was not limited to the ancient books, but was fulfilled in His gift to their living selves. And the revelation to the Church was not limited to what its founders recorded, but was to be fulfilled in the living body of the Church, as it should be led by the Spirit. The Word of God

was in human flesh, and the oracles of God are in the Church-in the spirits of men united in Christ under the tuition of the Spirit. HERE is no part of the Catholic Religion more liable to be misunderstood, and as a consequence rejected and condemned, than the sacramental system. How can a few drops of water poured upon a person, an unconscious child perhaps, create an inward change in his nature and powerfully affect his ultimate destiny? How can bread and wine convey to us the grace of God and the personality of Christ, so that through them we may find vital contact with the divine? Why all this emphasis on the outward, the material side of things? 

Is not the whole process a mechanizing, a materializing of religion, and thus a degradation of the highest thing in life? Is it not a survival of primitive magical conceptions?

Before we answer these questions, we must examine the basic principle underlying the sacraments, and see how it is related to life as a whole. That principle is simply this: the entire universe is, at least potentially, no

A little consideration will convince us that the sacramental method, (the imparting of the inward, through the outward-the spiritual through the material) is no eccentric peculiarity of the Catholic religion, but a universal law of human life. All life, as we know it in our experience, is a union of matter and spirit; nor does the purely spiritual exist within the human sphere. Man himself is a sacrament, a union of matter and spirit; and his duty consists not in getting rid of his body or of his animal nature, but in spiritualizing them, making them the ready instruments of his soul, of his personality.

His self-expression utilizes his bodily organism at every turn. He cannot communicate with a friend, he cannot even think a thought, without it. He thinks through a material brain; he communicates his thoughts through the movement of material lips, the stirring up of material vibrations in the air, through the receptive action of material ears and of another material brain and nervous system like his own.

Or some mighty genius takes a material pen, and imposes on a material paper thousands of strange material emblems, and men's spirits are fired by these outward media, and a revolution in human thought and life is effected. The clasp of a friend's hand, the pressure of a mother's kiss, what more material than the bare external phenomenon; what more spiritual than the inner reality? Yet the two are intimately conjoined; and of their union new vital experiences are born. 

Furthermore, not only does truth leap from spirit to spirit by means of material channels, but life itself is communicated in the same way. 

Life is never seen; it is known and communicated only through its outward instrumentalities. The phenomenon of human birthwhere a physical act calls into being not only another physical organism, but an immortal soul-weaves together the material and the spiritual in a deeply mysterious, sacramental union as truly as any sacrament of the Church. 

Never, perhaps, does the effect seem so incommensurable with the cause, as in this process.

From this arises the extreme importance which the Church attaches to the Sacrament of Penance, as it is called in the West, of Repentance as it is known in the Orthodox East, This sacrament is the medicine of the soul, or rather it is the direct and gracious activity of the Good Samaritan, pouring oil and wine into the wounds of the stricken wayfarer. Man cannot raise himself from death, from the state of spiritual ruin to which mortal sin has brought him; only He who called forth Lazarus from the tomb can do that. He alone can effect the pardon of the guilty; He alone can give life to those who are dead in trespasses and sin. He who regenerated the soul and made it a partaker of the divine nature, a sharer in the divine life, can restore it to that life, after the suicide of sin. His mercy endureth for ever, and there is no limit to the power of grace. Since the Sacraments are no mere symbols, but the heart's blood of the Church, the very channel of her life, the conditions of their valid celebration and administration become of supreme importance. It would be the height of presumption for a man to presume to celebrate the Sacraments—to say "I baptize thee," "I absolve thee," "this is My Body"—who had not received a divine commission to do this. This divine commission was given by Christ to His Apostles, and later by the Apostles to those whom they ordained to succeed them in the ministry.

Not only the commission but the power, the grace-gift (charisma), necessary for the valid celebration of the Sacraments was bestowed. 

To some, the Apostles imparted their full apostolic power, including the power to ordain, to others, for instance the deacons, they imparted only part of this power, not including the power to ordain. It matters not what the chief ministers have been called at any time— 

Apostles, Eminent Men, Apostolic Men,

Presbyter Bishops, Bishops, or what not-the fact stands out that there have always been

men in the Church possessing the fullness of the apostolic power and office, and that they, and they alone, might pass on this office to others.

The threefold ministry-Bishops, Priests, and Deacons-took time to develop, largely in response to local needs, but the main principle was there from the first. No one might execute any function which he had not been ordained to execute, no one might ordain who had not been ordained to ordain. This cuts at the root of the Protestant conception of the ministry -Presbyterian as well as Congregational. It is made by God, not by men-like the Church herself. The ministerial, sacramental powers flow down from above-beginning with Christ's Apostles-not up from below. The less is beyond all contradiction blessed by the greater, not vice versa. And with every function, every office, in the Church, divine power was given to perform its duties and to live worthily in it. The Bishops, the successors of the Apostles, receive this power in full, others in accordance with the work to which Christ has called them.

The statement is made repeatedly that the Anglican Church, while maintaining the practice of episcopal ordination, and the threefold ministry of bishops, priests, and deacons, is not committed to any theory The fact that a first or second century "presbyter" may have ordained does not give the slightest countenance to a sixteenth or twentieth century presbyter doing so. The former received this power at ordination; he was ordained to ordain; the latter was not. It is the office and function, not the title, that matters.

about this fact, nor to any interpretation of its significance. This statement, itself ambiguous, is taken to mean that our Church does not hold the doctrine of Apostolic Succession. This, if true, would be startling. 

It would mean that the Anglican Church had abandoned a doctrine to which the Catholic Church as a whole is committed; that she had ceased to be Catholic; that all hope of reunion with the Roman and Old 

Catholic Churches or with the ancient Orthodox Church of the East must be discarded; that no Catholic could conscientiously remain in her. Some would welcome this conclusion; most Anglicans would not.

In the supposed interests of reunion, some ardent but impatient spirits have indeed urged that the fact of episcopacy should be sufficient, and not any interpretation of it as if fact and interpretation could be separated in so vital a matter. But one cannot have bishops in vacuo, nor can they ordain in vacuo; one must have some theory as to what a bishop is, and what ordination is, and what happens as a result of the bishop's ordination. We moderns are all too inclined to overlook the obvious fact that a thing is what it is because of its whole setting, its context, and cannot otherwise be understood. We are, as Chesterton somewhere remarks, content to walk in upon the last scene of the last act and find someone killing or kissing someone, and not care in the least who is killing or kissing whom, and why. But the context, and therefore the interpretation, is supremely important. Episcopal ordination, like marriage, is degraded to something dangerously near a farce, unless one knows what he is about and why. No sane person would say that it did not matter what theory one held about marriage-whether

it was an indissoluble sacrament, or a civil contract dissoluble for weighty reasons, or an agreement to try the experiment of living with a person of the opposite sex until one tires of the other. The interpretation of marriage enters vitally into the fact; they cannot for a moment be separated. The same is true of episcopal ordination, of the three orders of the ministry, of the whole conception of the priesthood and of the Church. Our bishops at Lambeth (1930) apparently recognized this truth. "When we say that we must insist on the historic episcopate, but not upon any theory or interpretation of it, we are not to be understood as insisting on the office apart from the functions. What we uphold is the episcopate, maintained in successive generations by continuity of succession and consecration, as it has been throughout the history of the Church, and discharging those functions which from the earliest times it has discharged" (Lambeth Conference, 1930, pp. 115, 116; S.P.C.K.). This, if it means anything, means that a bishop is a high priest of the Church, a successor of Christ's Apostles not only by an unbroken series of holders of sees, but by an unbroken chain of consecrations, transmitting the plenitude of apostolic powers, including the power to consecrate and ordain.

It is indisputable that the Anglican Church at the Reformation kept the offices and titles of the three grades of the historic hierarchy, bishops, priests, and deacons. But it is claimed by Roman controversialists who dispute our orders and our Catholicity, and by their ultra-Protestant allies in the Church, that these offices were retained in name only, that the titles are titles and nothing more. There was no intention of

continuing an order of priests, with the power to offer sacrifice, absolve, etc., and an order of bishops, who in addition to all other priestly powers could ordain. The title "priest," as Hooker says, is no more to be taken literally than the title "senator" (old man).

The answer to this is twofold. The great majority of bishops in 1550, when the Ordinal was put forth, were definitely Catholic, and as definitely opposed to the views of Cranmer and the Protestant party. Only six out of the twenty-four bishops sympathized with the Reformed views. Fourteen of them continued as bishops in Mary's reign, under the restored papacy. (Three others were deprived, not because of heresy, but because of their marriage.) Several of these bishops subsequently suffered deprivation and imprisonment by opposing the (utterly illegal) destruction of the altars, which they rightly regarded as a blow at the doctrine of sacrifice and priesthood. There can be no doubt, therefore, as to the Catholic intention of the compilers of the Ordinal.

But the intention of the Church as a whole is no less clear. The Preface to the Ordinal speaks in no uncertain language. After declaring that "from the Apostles' time there have been these Orders of Ministers in Christ's Church-Bishops, Priests, and Deacons," the intention of the Ordinal is stated: "to the intent that these Orders may be continued," etc. These orders, and no others, are to be continued; no new ones are being introduced. We note that it is the orders that are to be continued, not simply the titles or the salaries. For those who speak and understand English, no further proof should be necessary. It is difficult to see how the intention to continue the historic episcopate and priest-

hood, in the known meaning of the terms, could be more clearly expressed. Further, the words "for the 

Office and Work of a Bishop in the Church of God," "for the Office and Work of a Priest in the Church of 

God," were specifically added to the "form" in 1662, to emphasize the difference between the two orders, in the face of Presbyterian denials. Now the chief, if not the only essential, difference between them is that a bishop can ordain, while no other order of the ministry can do so. The doctrine (as well as the fact) of Apostolic Succession was thus clearly implied. And even 

Hooker, whom ultra-Protestants are so fond of quoting (in certain connections), admits that "whereas presbyters, by such power as they have received for administration of the Sacraments, are able only to beget children unto God; bishops, having power to ordain, do by virtue thereof create fathers to the people of God" (Eccl. Polity, VII. 6. 3). This is almost an exact quotation from the fourth century father, Epiphanius, and voices faithfully the teaching of the ancient Church. Nor can Lambeth be quoted as seeking to reverse the historic Anglican position on this doctrine. 

Some of its language was ambiguous; some of its propositions will have to be modified before they can be acceptable to Catholics generally, but in the main it held fast to the teaching as well as the practice of our Church. It gave a limited and qualified approval to trying the South India experiment-outside the Anglican Church. Our episcopate agreed unanimously that 

Holy Orders is a Mysterion (Sacrament), in the sense in which the Orthodox Eastern employ the term, that ordination is not simply the appointment to an office

but the imparting of a spiritual gift or charisma, that we had always preserved the unbroken Apostolic Succession, and considered it a link with the Apostles. On all these points, as well as on the doctrine of the Eucharistic Sacrifice and the Real Presence, the bishops bore witness to the fact that the Anglican Church stands for Catholic truth, and not for ultra-Protestant denials of it.

Thus we see that the statement that the Anglican Church insists merely upon the fact of episcopal ordination, but not on any theory about this fact, is at best a dangerous half truth. We are not indeed required to hold any detailed theory about the steps by which the episcopate emerged from the apostolate. But the doctrine of Apostolic Succession, i.e., the principle that none might validly ordain to the priesthood of the Church except an Apostle or one who had received by ordination from the Apostles the plenitude of Apostolic power (including the power to ordain), and that only those ordained by them can celebrate a valid Eucharist, absolve, etc., is certainly the doctrine of the

But what happens when a Protestant minister has become a convert to the Church, and wishes to minister among us? 

First, he is ordained deacon; then the bishop, some months later, says over him the solemn words: "Receive the Holy Ghost for the Office and Work of a Priest in the Church of God NOW committed unto thee by the imposition of our hands. Whose sins thou dost forgive, they are forgiven," etc. The convert in question becomes now, for the first time (by virtue of

the imposition of the bishop's hands), a priest of the Church of God, and receives thereby at the same time the grace of the Holy Spirit for that office. This ordination is unconditional and certainly implies a very definite theory as to what a bishop is, and as to what the priesthood is, and as to how it is, and how it is not, acquired. The Apostolic Succession is treated as being not only of the bene esse but of the esse of the official ministry, and hence of the Catholic Church of Christ. 

The Church's "Office of Institution" bears witness to her faith in unmistakable terms: "O holy Jesus, who hast purchased to thyself an universal Church, and hast promised to be with the Ministers of Apostolic

He prays not "My Father," but "Our Father."

True Christians "do not forsake the assembling of themselves together, as the custom of some is"-they never forget that it is as members of a mighty brotherhood, a universal Church, that they have access to God. And they are mindful of their vital relation, not only to their fellow-Christians on earth, but to the "spirits in prison" who await the vision of God, and to the "spirits of just men made perfect" who have attained that vision, who behold the King in 

His beauty, yet cease not to pray for their brethren here on earth. In all our life of work and worship, we are surrounded by a "cloud of witnesses" (to God) and their presence serves to renew our confidence and energy in running the race that is set before us.

The doctrine of the Communion of Saints is a necessary corollary to the nature of the Church as the Body of Christ. "Truly our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son 

Jesus Christ." Moreover this divine fellowship, if not destroyed by grievous sin, makes possible and inevitably involves a universal all-embracing human fellowship: "If we walk in the light, as He is in the light, we have fellowship one with another, and the blood of Jesus His Son cleanseth us from all sin." Only when man is in harmony with God can he be in harmony with himself and with his fellow men, and conversely no one can come to God alone; he can come only as one of the fellowship redeemed by the blood of the eternal Son, and bound together by the life-giving Spirit.

The Church, then, is a community of prayer, faith, hope, and love. The organic union of her members carries with it the fact that everything that one does or suffers or enjoys affects for good or bad all the rest. "If one member suffer, all the members suffer with it; and if one member be honored all the others rejoice with it." If any part of the human body fails to perform its functions, the vitality of the whole body is lowered, while the healthful activity of each contributes to the healthful activity of the

whole organism. It is the same with the Church. 

No sin, even in thought, is so private that it does not injure the fellowship, and lower the sinner's contribution to its welfare; no virtue, be it only an aspiration toward the good, is so hidden that it does not tend to the well-being of the whole. We are all bound together in the bundle of life-in this case, not merely human life but the spiritual, superhuman life of the family of Christ.

Therefore the spiritual gifts which each Christian possesses, bestowed by the Holy Spirit, are in no sense his own private possession. Every faculty is given him for the service of "the Beloved Community," and only there can it attain its full fruition. The spirit of mutual service, the sense of mutual responsibility, must characterize all true organs of the Body, whether they have to do with the external administration or the internal life of the Church. The very differences of endowment, associated with differences of function, are meant to enrich the Society as a whole. The gift of new life in Christ, the gift of the Holy Spirit, the sacramental self-giving of God, are not meant to iron out all individual differences into a dull level of uniform monotony, but to vivify and consecrate them all to the service of the

one Lord and the one Body. Individuality is not destroyed but heightened and enriched by its social and organic relations in the Church. 

Whatever our vocation and function, whether it be that of prophecy, ministry, teaching, exhorting, or ruling-callings which have to do largely with the external aspects of the Church's life- -or the more personal and hidden activities of generosity, mercy, and the universal duty of love, we act in any case as organs of the Body of Christ, for the service of all, and in living unity with the other members. The Body needs them all. Just as the life of our physical bodies would be maimed or crippled by the loss or injury of eyes, ears, feet, or other organs, and no part can dispense with all the other parts, so the Church needs both her external organs of administration, her Apostles and their successors in the sacred priesthood, serving the "statics" of her structure, and the internal organs of holiness, the saints, the Christ-like soul cells of the organism, serving the inward "dynamics" of her life. Some are stronger and more glorious, others are feebler and less honorable; all are necessary. Those who have been faithful to her ideals on earth will share her triumph in heaven, and enter into the Kingdom prepared for them from the foundation of the world, that Kingdom which is nothing else than the Church perfected, transfigured, and glorified, the masterpiece and goal of the divine Craftsman's activity. Man's salvation is first and always the result of the gracious work of God, yet it is always a social work, begun, continued, and ended in the fellowship of the Church. 

The Church Expectant and the Church Triumphant should not be rigidly separated in our thought, yet it is convenient to make this distinction, in common with Western Catholics generally, though we may not press it. First, as to the Church Expectant. Many Christians, perhaps the great majority, leave this world at peace with God, yet far removed from that perfection of purity and love which alone can

We cannot think of them as condemned to hell, the sphere of eternal darkness and exile from God, yet equally we cannot imagine them as received at once into heaven. To the sinner, even though he be penitent, hell itself would be more tolerable than the blazing light of the great King. His holiness is so perfect that "His angels He chargeth with folly and the heavens are not clean in his sight." Into the sphere of His unveiled manifestation, nothing spotted or unclean may enter.

If we were to stop at this consideration, we should be plunged into despair. The soul's direction is, normally at least, fixed at death—either one has his face turned away from God, or toward Him-but in the latter case there still remains a long journey before the goal is reached. The end is in sight, it is no longer doubtful, but much remains to be done before it is achieved. We cannot think of death as working a magical change in a person, transforming him from an ordinary sinner into a perfected saint. That is

not God's method. There is still needed a continued growth in His love and service, the perfecting in man of the image of Christ, the purification from the least stain or taint or affinity with sin. This realization of the vital part played by the Blessed Virgin in the world's salvation, and the resulting devotion to her, is no late addition to the faith, but goes back to the most primitive times. God preparing through long centuries a people among whom His Son could be born and manifested, so we necessarily think of Him as preparing the Mother who should give birth to Him, in His human nature. We think of her as the flower of humanity, the pinnacle of creation, stretching forth her arms to the Eternal and Divine, wholly yearning for Him, wholly surrendered to Him, and thus capable of receiving and possessing Him by whom she was already perfectly possessed. Humanity, even at its height, cannot raise itself to the higher life of God, nor usher in His Kingdom; yet upon its moral preparation, its spiritual receptivity, the great Event is conditioned. And Mary makes the right decision, "and the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld 

His glory, the glory as of the only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth." But the

Communion of Saints always implies reciprocal action, and we have not grasped its full meaning unless we enter into the deep mystical truth, emphasized by some Eastern writers, that at the moment of the Annunciation the Blessed Virgin was assisted to make her act of complete self-surrender by the prayers of all those who since the world began, both before and since that crisis, have prayed: "Thy will be done.'

The oneness of all things in God is wonderfully exemplified in the unity of all saints and the Queen of saints at the threshold of the world's salvation. "9 The Church, then, while glorifying God in all His saints, glorifies Him especially in the highest of all His creatures, the Mother of

Jesus. In Mary she sees the prototype of her own life, and of her own relation to God. In the 

Body of Christ, function follows vocation, and we rightly infer that the function of the Mother of God is as unique as her vocation. The saints are no idle spectators of the spiritual combat on earth, but, by virtue of their prayers, active participants in it. And shall not she, through whom we received the Author of our salvation, powerfully assist us to attain it? "Blessed is she that believed, for there shall be a fulfilment of those things that were told her by the

Lord" "-shall not her faith, which made the

Incarnation possible, still energize in prevailing prayer to distribute its blessings far and wide? 

As she magnifies the Lord, and rejoices in God her Saviour, can we suppose that He no longer looks upon the lowliness of His handmaiden, or that the intercessions of her whom His angel declared to be "filled with grace," and "blessed among women," ascend in vain? Does He not still do great things to her and through her?

If every prayer in harmony with the will of God, and in the name of His Son is granted according to the faith of him who offers it, shall not she who is preeminent in obedient faith-the mother whose whole life was a continual "whatsoever He saith unto you do it," -shall not she be heard when she unites her spiritual prayer-energies, her singular understanding of the will of God, to our own feeble petitions and aspirations? On the Cross her divine Son entrusted her to us, and us to her, in the words of agonized love, "Woman behold thy Son," "behold thy Mother"—and shall not that tie, crimsoned with the Blood of Him who hung there, remain for all time the brightest, the most sacred, link in the Communion of 

Saints? Can we ever forget her, His Mother

and ours? Can she ever forget us, His brethren and her children?

Never can we separate Mary from her divine Son. All that she is she owes to Himher life, her virtues, her salvation itself-and in honoring her we honor Him, and never for a moment do we forget the infinite gulf between the Creator and even the highest of His creatures. The very fact that we ask her prayers is the surest proof of her creaturehood. We can scarcely conceive of asking God to pray for us. She has nothing of her own; her highest glory is not that she bore the Incarnate but that she heard the word of God and kept it. The fear some good Christians have that, in venerating her, they may detract from the supreme position of Him whom she brought forth, is abundantly answered by the fact that those who venerate and invoke her without exception confess Christ as their God and Saviour, while the religious circles which most emphatically deny her honor are, as a rule, the very ones which are increasingly worm-eaten with every species of rationalism and Unitarianism. A true Mariology, in fact, is a great safeguard of the Incarnation. Accordingly Catholics refuse and will always refuse to put apart those whom God has joined together in the deepest and highest

intimacy known to man, Jesus and Mary, inseparable at the manger, inseparable at the 

Cross, inseparable for ever in heaven!

The tender words of one of the most conservative of seventeenth century Anglican bishops, Bishop Hall, must surely strike a responsive chord in all Christian hearts: "But how gladly do we second the angel in his praise of her, which was more ours than his! How justly do we bless her whom the angel pronounced blessed. How worthily is she honored of men, whom the angel proclaimed beloved of God! O blessed Mary, he cannot bless thee, he cannot honor thee too much that deifies thee not! That which the angel said of thee thou hast prophesied of thyself: we believe the angel and thee. 'All generations shall call thee blessed,' by the fruit of whose womb all generations are blessed."

CHAPTER IX THE REUNION OF THE CHURCH HE RENEWED EMPHASIS on the 

TH Holy Catholic Church has led, in the Anglican communion as elsewhere, to a deepened sense of shame and grief at the divisions of Christendom. The American Episcopal 

Church has been a leader in the cause of Christian reunion-witness the inception of the 

World Conference on Faith and Order, and other movements looking toward unity-but the unity must be real and not superficial. 

Churchmen, and not least, enthusiastic Anglo- 

Catholics, hasten to recognize all who belong to Christ as brethren, but this is a very different thing from recognizing all denominations as equally "Churches," equally parts of the one Catholic Church of Christ. The Catholic cannot recognize the right of any human being to secede from Christ's Church and found a new one of his own; he cannot recognize a man-made sect as being a part of, or on a level with, the divinely founded Church of Jesus. Nor can he

recognize a ministry as possessing Christ's authority and promise which has its origin in the will of man rather than in the appointment of Almighty God. The divine commission is essential to a valid ministry. God sent Christ, Christ sent the Apostles, the Apostles sent other bishops, and so on down to the present day. A ministry founded by some man or woman cannot claim this divine commission, and there must remain the gravest uncertainty as to its sacramental ministrations. Whatever blessings

God may bestow upon those who, in all good faith, make use of such ministrations—and we do not deny the existence of such blessings in great degree-the loyal Churchman cannot himself make use of them, nor can they have any place in the reunited Church of the future, which must be based upon the divine will, not on human self-will; on God's appointments and not man's.

It is quite beside the point to insist, as some good Protestants do, on the "spiritual equality of all Christians before the Lord," as a reason for admitting as valid all the ministries and sacraments in Christendom, and hence demanding intercommunion among them all. Equality does not mean identity. The humblest Christian may be the spiritual equal-indeed he may, in

God's eyes, be the spiritual superior-of any priest or bishop in the Church; but that does not give him the right or the power to celebrate the Sacraments, and to exercise in the name of Christ and the Church an authority which he has not received. The Church does not, for example, permit her own people, not even all her own clergy, to celebrate the Holy Eucharist, or to bless or absolve-deacons may do none of these things-so why should she recognize the right of ministers of other bodies to do this? 

Why is it narrow-minded for her to refuse them the right and power which she refuses to her own children? Why should it be narrow for her to deny to them something they vigorously deny for themselves—namely that they are priests, in any other sense or degree than that in which laymen are priests? Even laymen may exercise the "ministry of the Word." 

It might be answered that the narrowness lies in refusing to recognize as ministers those who are recognized by the groups of Christians to which they belong. But that is not narrowness -it is simply a difference of belief as to what is the source of the ministry-Christ and the Apostles, or the various congregations of Christians. And the Church, taught by her divine Lord and those whom He has sent,

answers that authority and power are from above, not from below. "As My Father sent

Me, even so I (not the general congregation of disciples) send you." "The less is beyond all controversy blessed by the greater." There is no narrowness in this God-given principle, any more than there is in the insistence that a valid ministry must be one recognized as such by the whole Church-and the threefold ministry of bishops, priests, and deacons has alone secured such recognition-and not one deriving its authority from a small part, separating itself from the whole Body to which it once belonged. The divine origin of the Church, and her organic structure alike, demand the historic Priesthood and not a man-made ministry. The Church would stultify herself, and deny her fundamental principles, if she were to admit the two as equal. She cannot possibly admit a sect or ministry originating in the will of man to be the equivalent of the Church and the Priesthood originating in the will of God.

An equally fundamental difficulty is the growing lack of any definite and certain belief, either doctrinal or moral, among large numbers of present-day Protestants. When our Lord is regarded more and more simply as a good man

-in other words, the Incarnation, the foundation doctrine of the Christian religion is denied; when the Christian conception of Matrimony as a lifelong, sacramental union-the foundation of a Christian society-is increasingly diluted or discarded in favor of neo-pagan outlooks; when more and more tenets of the Christian faith and Christian ethic are treated as open questions, one cannot look to the future of Protestantism with much hope. Modern

Protestantism, indeed, appears to be sinking in increasing measure into a mere system of ethical culture or a bureau of social service, and even where this is not the case, its whole conception of the Church and of the process of salvation (life-bringing) is so different from the Catholic view that the attempt to harmonize the two seems hopeless. With the positive teaching of Protestantism the Church has little quarrel-in fact, practically all its positive elements are found in Catholicism-but with the denials and negations of Protestantism, many of which are of its very essence, the Church can make no terms. If truly Christian reunion is to be attained, moreover, denominationalism must go and go forever-the conception of "Churches" must yield to that of the one Church.

But is not the conception of one Churchand one alone-narrow and bigoted? On the contrary, as Bishop Gore so clearly perceived, "it is schism which is narrow, not Catholicity." 

Schism with a separate Church for each nation, temperament, and class of men; with one

Church for the poor and another for the rich, one for the whites and another for the blacks, one for the educated and another for the uneducated-this is narrow indeed, but the conception of one world-wide Church, sent with authority to all mankind and seeking to embrace and unite the whole creation within her organism-this is at once the broadest and highest and deepest conception possible to man. The 

Church is exclusive because she is all-inclusive. 

In the nature of things there can be but one universal Church. There can be, too, but one organism created by God, having Christ for its head, and the Holy Spirit for its life, its soul.

The claims of the Church can, of course, be presented in a narrow and bigoted manner, but that is another question. This is no fault of the faith, but of those who hold it in an unlovely and unloving way. "Ideals are dangerous visitants to narrow minds," but it is the minds that are narrow, and not the ideals. The true

Catholic will realize that it is due to no merit of his own that divine truth has been given him, but only to the infinite mercy and graciousness of God. It is given to him, moreover, that it may be given through him-given to others who, perhaps, are far worthier of it than he -others who will show forth its fruits in their lives far better than he. Therefore his sure confidence in the Church's faith will be mingled with humility, with a keen sense of his own unworthiness, and with the dread knowledge that the fuller measure of truth and grace which he possesses imposes upon him greater responsibilities, and, if he is recreant, will lead to more (not less) severe judgment. "What do ye more than others?" this question of his 

Master will at once humble and warn and inspire him.

But what about the salvation of those outside the Church? Are they to be considered as hopelessly lost? Certainly not, if by the Church we refer to the visible organism of the Church on earth. Yet there is a real truth in the saying "outside the Church there is no salvation."

For Church-life is salvation; the Church is the organism in which divine life is extended to man, or, to put it the other way round, by which men are incorporated into the life of God.

And in this our salvation lies. Nevertheless, the immeasurable wealth and vitality of the Church is not exhausted by her visible manifestation -important as this is. There are some who, in the language of Western theologians, belong to the soul of the Church, though not to its body-just as there are others, unhappily, who belong to the body of the Church, but not to its soul (grievous sin has cut them off). Or to express it in the language of some Eastern Orthodox thinkers, there are some who are united to the Church by hidden links of faith and love, which will be manifested in the next world, though not in this. However, it is God's will that all should belong to the Church visibly as well as invisibly-for the grace of living unity and fellowship is meant to be clearly manifested before the whole world, and those who deliberately, wilfully reject His call to enter the visible Church cut themselves off from grace and salvation. And even the others -who, in all good faith, are yet separate from the communion of the Church-will be saved in her at the last. Men are to be saved, whether in this world or the next, not as isolated individuals, but as a family, a fellowship, an organism. And the Catholic Church is that organism-no other Church or sect can plaus-

ibly make that claim. She alone was established by God for the salvation, the eternal life, of the human race, and in her alone will creation attain its divinely intended goal.

The condemnations which the Church necessarily pronounces on separation of every kind are aimed not at individuals, but at organizations. Moreover, they are aimed, not at other 

Christian bodies as such, but only so far as these bodies represent deliberate rejection of Catholic truth, deliberate rebellion against Catholic unity. But in all such bodies there is not only a movement away from the Church, but a movement toward it as well. In comparison with the fulness of grace and truth found in the Church Catholic, all other 

Churches are false, imperfect, unchristian. But there are no bounds to the grace of Christthe Spirit breatheth where He willeth-and by 

God's mercy, each association of those who follow and worship Jesus has carried with it precious treasures from the Father's house -treasures which will yet draw them back to their true home, if only they use them faithfully and wisely. All religions, even the non- 

Christian, have produced saints-though the

Catholic Church has produced more than all the other religions of mankind put together.

God's grace, therefore, has failed no man, no group of men. Christ is the true Light that lighteneth every man that cometh into the world. Rays of His light penetrate into the darkest corners, into the thickest fogs of sin and error-the Light shineth in the darkness and the darkness overcometh it not. And those rays will lead men at last, either in this world or the next, out of the half-mists of imperfect belief into the Light of Light—into the Church where the everlasting Light shines with undimmed radiance-the Church which is the fulness of Him that filleth all in all.

The situation with regard to the Roman Catholic Church is not much more favorable to unity in the near future than as regards Protestantism. So long as Rome tends to conceive of the Church as an autocratic organization, finding its center of unity in a human head rather than as a living organism, unified by a common life-the divine-human life of Christ sacramentally extended to true believers in Him -there seems little prospect of a visible reunion of Christendom. No scheme of Christian reunion can be satisfactory with the Roman communion left out; equally no such reunion is possible "till Rome be other than she is." Rome indeed contemplates not unity, but submission.

The Malines Conference, under the presidency of the saintly Cardinal Mercier, gave for a time a ray of light to those who hoped for a reunion in which other Churches would be "unis, non absorbés," but the Pope's bull "Mortalium

Animos" has since put an end to all such hopes. 

The submission which Rome requires would be equivalent to spiritual suicide on the part of the Anglican and Orthodox Churches; they recognize One alone as Head of the Church, and to admit the modern dogmas of Rome would strike them as treason to the Catholic faith. Sorrowfully, they are compelled to look elsewhere for unity, since the Roman terms are impossible. Moreover, such unity as Rome might offer is scarcely satisfactory; thus those 

Easterns (Uniates) who have submitted to the 

Papacy, though retaining their own rites and usages (liturgy in the vernacular, a married 

Priesthood, etc.), are not allowed to make their Communion at Roman altars of the Latin rite, nor is the ordinary Roman permitted to communicate at Uniate altars. A unity which disappears at the focal point of the Christian religion the Holy Eucharist is scarcely worthy of the name. It is a striking instance of the futility of a union that finds its basis in a human individual rather than in a divine-

human life. "Because the Bread is one," says

St. Paul, "we the many are one Body, for we all partake of the one Bread." We prefer the ancient apostolic bond of unity to the modern Roman device; and we prefer the kind of unity which results from the former. So far as Rome is Catholic, we are in agreement with her: where she ceases to be Catholic and becomes merely particularistic and sectarian, we are obliged to part company with her. In so doing, we are scarcely parting company with Christ. 

With regard to the Orthodox Easterns and the Old Catholics, the situation is very different. 

The Old Catholics-those Roman Catholics who, in 1870, refused to admit the new dogma of Papal Infallibility, and who therefore found themselves cut off from communion with the Roman see-and Anglicans, have long enjoyed friendly and brotherly intercourse, and the past year has witnessed the union of the two Churches. Intercommunion is now a fact, and a matter for much rejoicing.

The relations between the Anglican and Orthodox Eastern Churches likewise provide ground for joy and encouragement. The Great

Church of Constantinople, under the presidency of the Ecumenical Patriarch, in 1922 recognized the validity of Anglican Orders and

this example has been followed by most of the other Patriarchs and Churches which together make up the Orthodox communion (with the exception of the Russian Church, which at present is unable to speak for itself or take part in any inter-Orthodox Synod). There is little doubt that Anglican Orders will soon be universally recognized by the Easterns-hence, on the score of the ministry, there will be no barrier to reunion.

As regards the faith, also, the agreement is striking. Both stand for a non-Roman, non- 

Papal Catholicism. There are, of course, different modes of expression used by the two, which sometimes give rise to misunderstanding, and the ambiguity of some Anglican formularies-together with the Protestant flavor of certain phrases in them-has in the past occasioned some difficulty. But the Lambeth Conference in 1930-the gathering of Anglican bishops from all over the world-accepted unanimously a statement on the doctrines in question (especially the Real Presence, the Eucharistic Sacrifice, the Apostolic Succession, and the Priesthood), which was satisfactory to the Eastern Orthodox representatives there present, headed by His Holiness, the Patriarch of Alexandria. Subsequent discussions have

made real progress toward complete agreement, not by the path of compromise or ambiguity, but by the clear statement of the Catholic faith, as held by both Churches, with the willingness to allow a large variety of expression, where the whole Church has given no explicit definition. But even deeper than the unity of minds-important as that is-is the unity of heart and spirit which has been manifested. On the basis therefore of Catholic truth and Catholic love-and the one cannot long endure without the other-it is probable that reunion between the two Churches is near. And the reunion of all Christendom will, we may reasonably hope, follow not long after. God speed the blessed day!

The summons to unity is the summons of 

Christ Himself. In His great High-Priestly prayer, on the eve of betrayal, He besought the Father for the unity of those who accepted 

Him, not only for the disciples He Himself had made, but also for Christians of all ages. "Neither pray I for thee alone, but for them also which shall believe on Me through their word, that they all may be one as We are One."

The prayer for unity goes up' day by day from the altars of Catholic Christendom. It is not the prayer of isolated individuals, but of the

whole Church, of Christ Himself, the divine Victim. That prayer cannot fail. The One 

Spirit prays in and for the one Body, with groanings that cannot be uttered, for the unity of Christ's flock, and His intercession must prevail. God wills it, Christ died for it, the 

Holy Spirit will effect it.

sponse to the creative activity of God, has manifested itself in various stages called kingdoms. Thus there are the mineral or inorganic kingdom; the vegetable, the animal, and the human kingdoms. With the various kingdoms we associate diverse levels of consciousness. The 

Hindu saying: "God is in the stone, God breathes in the flower, God dreams in the animal, God awakens in man" is misleading if taken literally, but if we substitute the term "Divine Wisdom" it expresses a great truth.

Not only are there disparate realms of existence, but also disparate types of consciousness corresponding to them. With the inorganic kingdom we can associate no consciousness of its own; it has being, but not life-power of thought. The stone exists-that is all we can say of it. The vegetable kingdom, however, 1 ¹ As this phrase is understood by modern Russian Orthodox theologians, "Sancta Sophia.”

possesses life but not consciousness, unless in a very rudimentary form. Yet the plant not only is, but lives-the difference between a living flower and a dead one is evident, if not as striking, as the difference between a living and a dead animal. The animal kingdom affords clear evidence not only of life but of consciousness, varying from "blind" instinct to the comparatively high degree of intelligence manifested by trained dogs and horses. Then there is the human kingdom, which exhibits existence, life, consciousness, and in addition, rationality and selfawareness. In man, the universe becomes selfconscious, and also becomes dimly aware of its own goal and purpose-God. 

Is there a higher kingdom beyond these?

Christianity asserts, and experience proves, that there is the kingdom of God, or of the 

God-man, Jesus Christ. This is preeminently the kingdom of life, to which all other kingdoms look forward, and in which they find their end and consummation. God-consciousness is the distinguishing mark of this kingdom-"this is life eternal that they know Thee, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom Thou hast sent," exclaims our Lord. This God-consciousness is made possible by God's definite Revelation of Himself; it distinguishes His chosen

, people from all others; it shines forth uniquely in His Son, and is communicated by Him to those who are so united to Him as to share His life and possess His mind, in the organic fellowship of His Church. This knowledge is so perfect that it alone deserves the name: "No one knoweth the Son save the Father, neither knoweth anyone the Father save the Son, and he to whom the Son willeth to reveal Him." 

Each of the higher kingdoms rests upon that next below it, and presupposes all the others. 

Thus, the plant requires the inorganic world as the source of its own nourishment and maintenance-it is able to take inorganic substances and convert them into chemical substances, roughly called organic. Animals cannot do this, but live upon vegetables or upon other animals who in turn subsist upon vegetables; man lives upon the animals and upon the other realms below him; and out of man is fashioned the Kingdom of God or of the God-man. And not only is this the case, but each realm historically emerged from the realm next below it. Life did not and could not emerge until the inorganic world had reached a certain state of development; and from this inorganic world it evolved. 

Similarly, the animal kingdom evolved through a process of differentiation from the organic

kingdom when the latter had reached a certain development-man from the animal kingdom at the climax of its evolution, and finally "when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth His Son, made of a woman"; and the 

Virgin-born Christ, the sole-begotten of the Father, who is at the same time the "first-born of many brethren," ushered in a new kingdom, a new race of those who, though taken from mankind, "were born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of 

God." Man and rationality could not have emerged directly from the amoeba nor could the kingdom of God and God-consciousness have been built directly upon the basis of ape man or even of savages. The whole world process aims at the manifestation of God, and this manifestation is necessarily progressive, each stage of it requiring all the others, and growing out of the next lower realm.

The fact that each realm is evolved from that immediately below it and is conditioned by it, does not mean, however, that the lower in any sense produces or creates the higher. The dance of the electrons could never, without the entrance of a new and higher factor, have produced life; blind life could never have created consciousness; instinctive strivings cannot ac-

count for or give rise to rational self-consciousness; nor could self-conscious humanity generate the Kingdom of God. Each stage of the creative process recapitulates all that have gone before it but exhibits in addition a new, a higher, principle of being. Organization prepares for life; it is indispensable to life; yet it is not the source of life. Creation does not, at any level, pull itself up by its own bootstraps. 

Evolution presupposes involution-the invasion of new forces and powers from above, raising up and transforming the old, tending to a new and more perfect manifestation of God. The old supplies the environment, the material basis of the new; but the content of the new is given by God; it makes its appearance on the scene when the stage is set for it. 

The lower realms of existence are so far from being the cause of the higher, that the members of one grade can only pass to a higher through the downward movement of forces from above. Inorganic substances can pass to the organic sphere only through a plant absorbing and assimilating them. Plant life can rise to sentient being only by being taken into an animal organism, which in turn cannot pass to the human level except through a similar process; and at the highest level of all, humanity can

attain the Kingdom of heaven, the sons of men can become sons of God, only through a sacramental birth from above, through the downreaching of the re-creative and transforming powers of the incarnate Son and the indwelling Spirit. The other stages-being, life, consciousness, rationality—are all necessary for the highest, the manifestation of the sons of God, which would be unthinkable apart from any of them, yet this manifestation is the crowning work of the divine activity, not the pinnacle of human or natural achievement. It represents fundamentally not the upthrust of the forces of creation, but the loving condescension and downreaching of the transcendent power of God. 

The Kingdom comes from God, it is given by God, given indeed to those who are prepared for it, as the result of ages of natural and spiritual development and response, but given to those for whom it has been prepared by the Father.

A further point to notice is that the elements of the lower kingdoms are not destroyed or superseded but fulfilled and perfected on the higher level. The chemical elements of the inorganic world do not disappear when taken into a living organism-on the contrary they are composed of such substances, which, however,

on the higher level are no longer mere substances. The laws of the lower realm, moreover, still operate in their own sphere. Similarly, man does not, by virtue of his humanity, cease to be animal, but only to be merely animal. The laws of the animal kingdom still hold for the animal part of him, but in subordination to the higher laws of his total being. Even at the highest level, divine humanity in the Kingdom of God, this principle applies-the Kingdom is made up of men who are no longer merely men, and in it the laws of all the lower realms, inorganic, organic, animal, and human, are still in force, all in subjection to the supreme laws of the Kingdom. The material universe has been slowly molded through long ages of evolution, not to be discarded at the appearance of the spiritual, but to be perfected as its servant and its shrine. The incarnational and sacramental principle is the preeminent example of the law that the lower is not superseded by the higher, but recapitulated in it. As the inorganic realm was meant to minister to lifenot to be abolished by it—as life was meant to minister to consciousness, and consciousness to rationality, so rationality is meant to minister to spirituality in the Kingdom of God. In the 

God-man in whom the Kingdom already exists,

the material is united to the spiritual, the human to the divine, all the levels of created life to the Creator of all, and in every case the lower is not annihilated but perfected and fulfilled when united and subjected to the higher. 

In the organism of the God-man, the Church, the organ which is to universalize His life and bring all into His Kingdom, this sacramental principle is the continual law of her being, and thus we may say that the world was made for the Church (which will ultimately become the 

Kingdom), and finds therein its own perfection and self-realization.

The conception of various realms of being culminating in the Kingdom of God, and in the Church which is its organic embodiment, enables us to perceive the true relation of organization and life. Protestants tend to disparage the importance of the former, and especially of any particular form of organization. Many in fact delight to say that, whereas Catholicism in its various forms exalts organization, Protestantism exalts life. They are likely to add that life will produce some sort of organization -the particular form is of little moment-but that it is not dependent upon it. However, science and history are against them. The human organism is potentially contained in

a tiny bit of living protoplasm. But this potentiality could only be made actual through a mighty biological process extending through ages and ages, involving ever more complex and highly-integrated forms of organization, which in turn make possible higher types of life. Organization always precedes life and prepares for it. It also serves to enshrine and protect it; to express and reproduce it. Organization at every step is the basis of life, not life of organization. Nor is the particular type of organization unimportant. No one will seriously argue that the form of a sponge, a mollusk, or a cuttle-fish, would serve as well for the embodiment of the human spirit as the actual human body. 

The cosmic process bears unmistakable evidence to the contrary. Not only some organization, but a particular form of organization is required, and the higher the level of life the higher form of organization necessary. And as with the material so with the spiritual sphere. 

A horde of ape men or even of savages would hardly make a suitable shrine or basis for the 

Kingdom of God. God selected for Himself a people, constituted it on the basis of the highly elaborate law, and prophets built it up through long centuries, reconstituted it in the Person of His Son and of the Apostles whom He had

chosen, and then-and not till then-poured into the mold of that organization the living Spirit who was to abide with it for ever, bringing it to perfection, and transforming it at last into the Kingdom of God. Just as ages of divinely-guided development culminated at last. in a particular physical organism which alone could adequately enshrine the spirit of man, so ages of divinely guided development culminated at last in a particular social organism, the Catholic Church, which alone could adequately enshrine the Kingdom of God. And neither process is yet complete, both are still in the making; and the perfecting of each requires the divinely-evolved organism as an essential condition. The development is always organic, always true to type; organization is increasingly perfected, and always along its own lines.

The Church is thus the crown and goal of the whole creative process. This being the case, the solution of the old question, "did the Incarnation take place simply to remedy the fall of man, or was it unconditionally willed by God from all eternity so that it would have occurred even if man had not sinned?" seems self-evident. It is extremely difficult to suppose that the greatest honor which could come to our race, the entrance into manhood of the Son of God, and the elevation of the sons of men to participation in the divine nature could be merely the by-product of human sin. The union of Creator and creature in the Incarnation and the Church was the eternal purpose of God. 

This is, of course, no more than a speculation, and yet it is a speculation which we can scarcely escape making, once we attempt, as thinking beings, to set the Incarnation into its place in relation to the whole of life, or better perhaps, to see the whole of life in relation to the Incarnation (which is the only way in which we can see it whole).

But if human sin made no alteration in God's plan to unite man-and in him all creation-

to Himself, the same cannot be said as to the method of realizing that plan. The primordial catastrophe by which men, preferring his own selfish will to the holy will of God, lost that union with God in which he had been created (i.e., when he ceased to be an ape man, and became truly man, a rational and spiritual being, with a conscience and free will), could not be ignored by God, or passed over lightly as a thing of no importance. Man, being estranged from God by his own fault, must by his own efforts (prompted and assisted, of course, by divine grace) win his way back to God. Any other method would have been magical and mechanical, incompatible with the true nature of either God or man. For, as the ancient writer of the Epistle to Diognetus recognized: "Compulsion is not an attribute of God." Power, indeed, is one of His attributes; "God spake once, and twice I have also heard the same that power belongeth unto God." The divine assurance holds good: "The Lord will give strength unto His people" to seek and find Him, and trample down all obstacles to fellowship with 

Him, but only when they shall freely seek that strength, realizing their own impotence without it. Moreover, the power of God which energizes for the redemption of men (the new

and higher creation of men) is the power of self-sacrificing love, and it is this power especially which man must appropriate and assimilate by grace, if he is to be capable of salvation and of forwarding God's plan for the world. 

Hence the shadow of Calvary lies across all the pages of the Bible, from the first sin of man and the promise of the coming Deliverer, to the day when three crosses crowned a hill-top, and on the central one hung the Son of God. It is a cross which all must share who are in the true life of spiritual succession which converges in Christ, and radiates out from Him. Man must seek God, as God must seek him, amid the conditions and circumstances which man's sinful will has created. It has always been necessary in a fallen world to enter into the Kingdom of God through great tribulation, and even the preparation for that kingdom involves sacrificial suffering at every step. The Divine Wisdom (the eternal Archetype of the Church), whose delight it is to be with the sons of men, never ceases to proclaim in their ears, "Come, eat of the bread and drink of the cup which I have mingled," yet oftentimes the bread which she has prepared seems to be ashes, the cup plenteousness of tears to drink. Yet the great fact remains that there is a way back to God, the

Church is yet a real possibility, and suffering is a small price to pay for its attainment. 

All creation shares this suffering. For all creation shared in the tragedy of man's fall from God. In him the Godward movement of creation ceased, just when it should have come to its consummation; therefore the whole creation must continue to "groan and travail in pain together until now." Yet there is a gleam of light in the darkness of this world's night. 

There remains the God-given hope that "creation itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the sons of God," and so "the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation, the unveiling, of the sons of God," in whom it is to attain, through toil and sorrow, its own self-fulfilment. So the Church of the 

Old Covenant, in slavery and tutelage, must approach God through lowly and often crude sacrifices, which could not take away sin, but could only express its hope and longing for deliverance. So, too, the loyal servants of God must unite their own sufferings with the sacrificial gifts. Abraham, the friend of God, the father of the faithful, must go forth as an exile, not knowing whither he goes; he must be prepared to sacrifice what is dearest to him, his

own son; he and his spiritual children in God's 

Church must confess that they are strangers and pilgrims upon earth, enduring a loneliness and an estrangement from creation and man, if they are to escape the loneliness and estrangement from God which is the wage of sin. There is the loneliness of Christ, or the loneliness of Judas-we cannot escape both, we must choose. 

But the loneliness of Christ, and of His servants on earth before and after him, tends to the reconciliation and restitution of the very world and race from which they are estranged -the complacence of the worldling continues and confirms its isolation. So the forerunners of Christ in the Jewish Church-yes, and His heralds among the Gentiles-must be signed with the sign of the coming Cross. The new creation, the Church, must be born amid the birth pangs of the old. Socrates, in the pagan world, must drink his cup of hemlock; while the saints of God among the chosen people have trial of cruel mockings and scourgings, yea, moreover, of bonds and imprisonment. They are slain, they are sawn asunder, are tried, are slain with the sword, they wander about in sheepskins and goatskins, they are destitute, afflicted, and tormented by a world which is not worthy of them. Her vocation, like theirs, is a vocation of suffering, the sorrowful mysteries follow close upon the joyful, the sword pierces her own soul also. In the obedience of the handmaid of the Lord amid all her tribulation, the disobedience of the first woman is cancelled out so far as human effort could cancel it; in the new Eve, filled with grace and yielding herself wholly to its influence, the ascent of creation toward' God, which is the condition of the descent of God to creation, reaches its climax, and so "the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, and we beheld

the Father, full of grace and truth.” In her, humanity at the pinnacle of creation, stretches forth its arms to God; through her, God, entering once for all into the life of humanity, brings to birth in His own person the new creation. As Eve, according to the inspired myth of 

Genesis, was taken out of the side of Adam, so the Church, on Calvary, is taken out of the wounded side of the new Adam, and amid the flow of water and blood the seer beholds the two great Sacraments of the Church, which should bring the life of the Crucified to all who receive Him. The process is complete, "It is finished"; humanity—and in it all creationwhich fell from God by disobedience, has been restored to God by the obedience even unto death of the God-man. The sacrifice is consummated-and in it all the prayers and cries of suffering creation are heard and answered. The sacrifice is accepted by God-the Resurrection and the Ascension are the proof of this-and is crowned by the outpouring of God's Spirit.

Having been reconciled to God by the death of His Son, much more, being reconciled, we are saved by His life, which evermore flows to us in the Church, His Body and His fulness-His new creation and His Bride.

Yet the Cross abides, as with the Head so with the members. It is the life principle of the new organism. Pleading the Sacrifice of the 

Cross, perpetuated in the Eucharist, she offers herself with that Sacrifice. The Church must be bearing about in her Body continually the death of the Lord Jesus, that the life of Jesus may be manifested within her in the Mystical Body of Christ, and the gradual gathering in of all humanity, all creation, into this organism. Even the natural and secular progress of men, outside the Church as well as in it, is to minister to this end. The command to subdue all things to himself must be set in the context of the purpose of the God-man to subdue all things to Himself. The entire world, inorganic and organic-earth, sea, and sky-ultimately (after the Resurrection) the entire universe, is to become the body of man, the physical medium of his spiritual self-expression, in which he is everywhere present, everywhere working and energizing in it, penetrating it all with the power and intelligence God has given him. But if the world is becoming man's body, it is in order that it may, through him, become the Body of Christ, the medium of self-expression of the God-man, in whom alone creation is com-

plete. The revealing of man is subsidiary to the revealing of the God-man; the incorporation of all things into the body of man, their subjection to his spirit, is subsidiary to their incorporation into the Body of Christ, and their subjection to His Spirit, since He is the "heir of all things." 

However, in this whole process there is, necessarily, in a sinful world, the tension between sacred and secular, natural and spiritual, finite and infinite, so not only does the whole creation still groan and travail awaiting the complete manifestation of the sons of God, but "even we ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the 

Spirit, groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, the redemption, of our body." The present Church, therefore, must needs be a Cross-bearing, as well as a Spirit-bearing Body; "and Zion in her anguish with Babylon must cope"; she must not only contend with a world which assumes an independence of God, if not an active hostility to Him, but must also wrestle with spiritual wickedness in high places, with the unseen but fearfully real powers of darkness which work to thwart or pervert her victory. On earth she still shares the humiliation of the Suffering Servant; the triumph of her

And what about the meantime? Is human history or the history of the Church a blank, a colorless interim? Many of the early Christians thought so, in their expectation of the immediate end of the world. They did not understand that the sudden coming of Christ was not equivalent to an immediate coming, or that the imminent return to judgment was to be measured not by clock-time but by the prophetic foreshortening, or rather transcendence of time, which sees things as God sees them sub specie aeternitatis.

The inspired writer of the "second Epistle of 

Peter" had to correct their understanding. They were right in their recognition that the return of Christ to judgment and the consummation of all things was near- -as God counts nearand that we should live as befits those who recognize this truth, and its overwhelming importance. They were wrong in supposing that the interim was of little concern. The long-suffering love of God has permitted an interval—long, as the world counts it, short, as God and His

prophets count it, though it should be a billion years-in which man may progressively appropriate and assimilate as widely as possible the great salvation offered him. It is the privilege and vocation of the new race, the sons of God, the holy Church, to coöperate in achieving the great divine purpose of putting all things under the feet of man, that is to say, not of the mangod in his illusory independence, but of the God-man, not of a self-centered human divinity but of a Christ-centered divine humanity.

Every advance of man, individually or collectively, has therefore an important relation to the Body of Christ, whether this relationship be conscious or not. But whether the "advance" is a true movement to the final goal, the triumph of Christ and the incorporation of all things into His mystical Body, depends largely upon the spiritual attitude and activity of those who take part in it. The bringing of the whole world together by means of the airplane, radio, television, etc., may work for the unification of the world family in the Church, or it may inflict the gravest injury on the human race, and constitute a tremendous set-back to the divine plan, if the unity is merely superficial and material, while spiritual disruption still reigns, if the inward division between God and man and

between man and man is left unhealed. Science operates with blind indifference to blot out disease or to blot out the human race. Industrial, social, economic, and mechanical progress may mean either the "humanification of creation, or the dehumanization and mechanization of man. 

The dominant political and economic systems often tend powerfully to such dehumanization and mechanization. At the present time, materialistic capitalism reduces mankind to an aggregation of atoms, while materialistic socialism works for a mechanical amalgam of these atoms in a superficial unity. Both ignore the fundamental requirement for true personality and true unity alike, the spiritual-organic unity which culminates in the Body of Christ. Only the true unification and deification of humanity in this organism can assure the humanification of creation, or even the continued (and perfected) humanity of man himself, at a time when industry, economics, politics, sociology, philosophy, psychology, literature, art, and nearly everything else are so frequently tending to reduce him to a beast or a machine. In such an age the Church and her faithful members are called upon to endure a continual crucifixion, whereby alone the conditions necessary for the glorious manifestation of Christ and of the

sons of God may be prepared. It is not the rôle of the inventor, the captain of industry, or the philosopher-important as these are—which will be decisive; but rather that of the saint who, like his Master, learns obedience by the things he suffers, and like the Apostle fills up what is wanting in the afflictions of Christ for

His Body's sake, which is the Church. 

Yet the Church's conflict with the world, her crucifixion by the world, where she is true to herself, are not of known choosing. Her quarrel is not with the world as God's creation, for the life of which He gave His only-begotten Son, but with the world as it attempts to be a self-contained, self-sufficient entity apart from God, apart from its true center, its true being. 

All life, all truth, all being, and all values are affirmed by the Church-she can affirm and assimilate everything except sin and error. For she is divine "truth and love as an organism."

She is the true home of all the deepest and highest culture of mankind, and indeed apart from the organic unity with God which is possible in her, man more and more ceases to be inwardly creative, ceases indeed to be man. The Renascence of the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries revealed the creative and cultural resources of man united to God, and nurtured for ages in

the organism of the God-man; the machine age is revealing the exhaustion of his creative powers, his spiritual and cultural poverty, the drying up of the inner springs of thought and life, which attend his separation from God and the divine organism. Civilization is as wretched a substitute for culture as invention is for creation.

The Catholicity of the Church includes, then, the affirmation of all truth and all life, the capacity for assimilating them all, and subsuming them into her own organism in preparation for the great consummation. In this respect Catholic Christianity differs in toto from the rival systems of belief which compete with it for the conquest of the world. Materialism, of course, gives, and can give no rational account of the world, either as regards its origin or its goal. 

It is not an explanation of the universe, but the negation of an explanation—and of human reason as well. For to suppose that something arises from nothing, or that the greater-lifemind, reason, spirit-arises from the lessblind atoms-is a manifest absurdity, and an insult to man's intelligence. Today the existence of the higher-the intellectual and spiritualor to attempt to explain it in terms of the lower, is equally contrary to sound reason. It has well

been said that "a philosophy which treats pork chops and indigestion as real, and the deepest and highest experiences of the human soul as unreal, is a philosophy which is fit only for swine." The ascent of being and life from the lowest levels to the highest is not explainedit can only be explained away-by materialism. 

The same is to be said of the mind, including the materialist's mind, which recognizes the problem and attempts to grapple with it. Is man, who thinks that he thinks, no more than "a digestive tube with a hole at both ends"? ("Mann ist was er isst".) From the whirl of the electrons to the whirl of the planets, from the vibrations of the tiniest ray of light to the pulsations of the most illuminated sage or saintis all senseless, meaningless, inane? The organic structure of all life and all being, the presence and existence of everything in a system of relations (the evident mark of an objective mind and spirit), means nothing to the mechanist.

The religions of India, especially Buddhism, sought indeed some goal for life, but it was a purely negative one. All matter, all individual or personal existence, is illusory-therefore renounce it. This rebellion against materialism, this spiritual awakening and self-consciousness, was a great advance upon all that had gone before, but lacked a positive ideal. It leaves the material world and human society alike unredeemed, and awakens the human soul only to plunge it into eternal oblivion. And even the

Bhakti religion, so noble in many respects, and with many points of contact with Christianity, can find no adequate place for this world as the sphere of the realization of God's purpose and the coming of His kingdom. The world process is left quite as empty and meaningless as in the other Hindu religions. The Orthodox Christian conception of the Divine Wisdom, Sancta Sophia, the principle of unity in plurality, rooted in the Being of God the Blessed Trinity and embodied in increasing degree in creation till it culminates in the Body of Christ, is far removed from the religions of India where

plurality is simply illusion, where "the many" is no more than a bad and inexplicable nightmare of the One, where life is "a dream without a dreamer," where the phenomenal world and personality alike have no real existence, and hence no goal, except annihilation. Such a conception seems to underlie even the beautiful words of Tagore: "It is philosophical wisdom, having its universal radiation in the popular mind in India, that inspires our prayer, our daily spiritual practices. It has its constant urging for us to go beyond the world of appearances, in which facts as facts are alien to us, like the mere sounds of foreign music; it speaks to us of an emancipation in the inner truth of all things, where the endless Many reveal the One." To the Orthodox Christian, who grasps the significance of the Incarnation, the Resurrection, the Sacraments, and the Church, this world, the creation of God, and the raw material of the new and perfect creation, can never be a world of mere appearances, for the supreme Reality shines through it, and not simply above it; facts as facts are not foreign to us, for apart from the personal Word of God was

Greek thought, especially in its Platonic and 

Stoic forms, represents a great advance upon 

Buddhist and Hindu belief. For Greek philosophy does not stop short with the illusoriness of merely material existence, but goes on to seek a positive goal, which it finds in the realm of Ideas (Platonism), or in the Logos, the Universal Reason (Stoicism). It not only denies the false end of material satisfaction, but sets

up a true and positive end, participation in the ideal. But this participation is possible only through the intellect, through pure thought; truth does not and cannot dwell on earth; ideal truth and ideal being can be dimly reflected by material existence, it cannot be embodied in it. 

The world which the senses present to us is at best "a reflection of the Idea" as Platonism holds, or "the seed of Reason" as Stoicism maintains. There is left a hopeless antagonism, an irreconcilable dualism, between the ideal world (in which man can participate only through his intelligible nature) and the world of our environment. Pure idealism can only condemn the world which surrounds us; no more than Buddhism can it penetrate and redeem it. 

Everything has two aspects, a higher and a lower, but the two are never brought together; the lower is dismissed as false and illusory, a counterfeit and caricature of reality, the material world is left not only unsaved but unsavable (and inexplicable). 

Buddhism condemned what was false and renounced it, Platonism affirmed what was real and sought it (but only through intellectual participation); it remained for Christianity to bring the real and positive (which is also the ideal) down to earth and embody it. The whole

evolutionary process, out of which man's mind. (including the idealist's mind) arises, by which it is conditioned, and to which it is organic, is neither a bad dream, as with Buddhism, nor a false and hateful entity opposed to reality, as with Platonism-it is an ascent toward God, of which God is both the Author and the Finisher, and which culminates in the Creator sharing the life of His creation, that creation might share and embody the life of God. The universal connectedness of all things-matter and spirit, the actual and the ideal, the individual and society-is explained by the universal goal of all things: God. The higher is built upon the lower that in it the lowest may find union with the Highest. At each stage the higher involves and includes the lower-raising it to its own level-nothing is lost; and at the highest stage of all, the Incarnation, human nature is united to the divine in Christ, without impairing either, and through the downward movement of God to creation, the upward movement of creation toward God is crowned and completed. But for this purpose, the individual incarnation of God in Jesus Christ demands as a consequence and a correlative the social incarnation in the Body of Christ the Church. The Incarnation as an act has a final-

ity, a once-for-allness about it; the Incarnation as a process must be continuous and universal, till the whole universe becomes the Body of 

Christ, the Living Temple of God. 

The conflict of the Church and the world, the warfare of Christ and Antichrist, symbolically depicted in the Apocalypse, can only delay, it cannot frustrate, the final consummation. The forces of sin and death, already vanquished in the Person of Christ, are to be similarly overcome by the Holy Church, which shares the risen life of her Lord, and hence is invincible against the gates of death. The victory is won -it only remains for the Church to reap and universalize the fruits of victory. Already we are reconciled to God by the death of His Son; already we are in process of being saved by 

His life. Christ "by His death has overcome death, and by His rising again has opened to us the gates of life everlasting." The Tabernacle of God is with men, and they do dwell with Him; they are His people, and He is their
