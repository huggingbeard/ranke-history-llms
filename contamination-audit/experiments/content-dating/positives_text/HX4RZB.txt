CAMBRIDGE, MASS., U.S. A.

THIS brief unpretentious sketch of Lucius W. Nieman is a newspaperman's story of a newspaperman. It is the record of a talk to the Nieman Fellows at Harvard (April 7, 1941) by Harry J. Grant, long-time associate of Mr. Nieman, and his successor in publishing the Nie man paper, The Milwaukee Journal. 

It is published by the Nieman Foundation to preserve an intimate record by a contemporary of the journalist whose fortune was left by his widow, Agnes Wahl Nieman, to Harvard College to "promote and elevate the standards of journalism in the United States and educate persons deemed specially qualified for journalism." 

In furtherance of the bequest, the Nieman Fellowships were established at Harvard in 1938, to provide working newspapermen the opportunity to increase their competence in the practice of journalism by a year of study at the University in fields of their own choice. The first season's announcement brought 309 applications from newspapermen all over America. Nine Nieman Fellows were appointed the first year, about a dozen each succeeding year. Those selected by the Nieman Committee on the basis of the annual applications come to Harvard on leave of absence from their papers for a college year. 

Their stipends, from the Nieman Foundation, approximate their newspaper salaries during the period of their residence at Harvard.

ÚCIUS WILLIAM NIEMAN abhorred pretense and avoided "guff" — his term for the more mod- 

ern "bunk." There was no land of make-believe in his world. He called for a straightforward, factual story and his story should be written in such terms. 

It is difficult to interpret the workings of another man's mind and to analyze his motives clearly. Sixty years of 

Mr. Nieman's life- thirty-five of them with The Journal had passed when I first met him. He impressed me by his straight thinking and his genius in digging out facts.

He possessed great charm and the hardships which had come his way helped to give force to his character and effectiveness to his work. Early in life he decided to be a newspaperman and for more than sixty years he followed his idea to the end of a most successful career. "Lute" Nieman, as he was intimately called, was born in Bear Creek, Sauk County, Wisconsin, December 13, 1857. He died in Milwaukee on October 1, 1935. Child of pioneer parents in pioneer country, his methods through life remained those of the pioneer. Away from beaten paths, he went alone, frequently lonely but always bravely, true to his principles, with an instinct of the hazard in all new movements and a caution that demanded factual assurance. His father, Conrad Nieman, was the child of early farmers in this undeveloped country, as was his mother, Sara Elizabeth Delamater. Lute, and his elder sister Violette, were the only children. 

Grandfather Delamater was of French pioneer stock and his wife, Susan Cuppernall, had come with her family from Oneida County, New York. When Lute was two years old his father died and his mother took the two (5)

small children to live with her parents near Mukwonago.

After a few years the mother died and Lute was cared for by Grandmother Delamater, who accepted the responsibility of his early training. He received the usual gradeschool education of the period and discussed the news of the day with the family and the schoolteacher who boarded with them.

The influence of this small group was indelibly marked on Lute's future life and it was in this village of one thousand persons, twenty-five miles southwest of Milwaukee, that he first came in touch with events of the outside world. He was fascinated by them and formed his boyhood resolution to be a newspaperman. Life in such a place could be expressed in very simple terms and its values were evident in the struggle for existence and by the social life of the period. There was no labor-saving farm machinery; the muscle of man and beast got the work done. Power resources remained untouched and wood-burning stoves and oil lamps sufficed in the total lack of modern household devices.

Some early history may help us better to understand Lute's background and later activities. In 1673 Father 

Marquette and Joliet record a trip along the shore of Lake Michigan from the Illinois country to Green Bay, and five years later Father Zenobe Membre came with a party that included Sieur LaSalle, Father Louis Hennepin and Gabriel de la Bourde. He recorded the Misquoites and Outagamies "who dwell at about 40° north on the banks of the river called Mellioke which empties into Lake Dauphin (Michigan) very near their village." 

Less than one hundred years before Lute Nieman's birth Lieutenant James Gorrell, British Commander at

Green Bay, records on June 5, 1762, a party of one hun- (6)

dred warriors "Taways" (Ottowas) who came to trade from "Milwacky." At this time, Charles Langlade, first settler of Wisconsin, arrived in full British regimentals and by means of an aged Indian ceremonial dogfeast induced the Indians to help fight the colonists. 

Samuel Starrow visited the place in 1817, forty years before Mr. Nieman's birth, and referred to a Pottowatomie settlement on the right bank of the "Millewakie" river. The Menomonees left their name to the west branch of the river, and these two tribes gave title to the federal government in the treaties of 1831-33. "Milwaukee," according to K. A. Linderfelt, a former public librarian, is a Pottowatomie term meaning "gathering place by the river," the proper form being "monah-wauk-sepe." 

Solomon Juneau and his family were the only white residents in Milwaukee between the years 1820 and 1833. 

Trails reached northward through unbroken forest to 

Ft. Winnebago and Green Bay and southward to Ft.

Dearborn (Chicago). A few log houses were built at random and there were only three or four wagons in the entire district. A trip from Detroit required three weeks. 

Eastern travelers journed by rail to Albany, New York, and thence to Chicago by stage, with some lucky ones traveling by boat. From Chicago to Milwaukee one walked or rode one's own horse.

On January 31, 1846, the City of Milwaukee was incorporated with 9,500 residents, and Solomon Juneau was elected its first mayor. The first newspapers - little more than periodic handbills - were The Advertiser, started in 1836 by Byron Kilbourn, and The Sentinel, established the following year by Solomon Juneau.

The state legislature licensed the Milwaukee and (7)

Waukesha Railway Company in 1847 and two years later $100,000 was subscribed and the company organized, with Byron Kilbourn as president. Payment for work on the roadbed was made by orders drawn on merchants and farmers who had signed subscriptions. The struggling community of Milwaukee bonded itself for nearly a quarter million dollars to aid the railroad. The first trains to Waukesha, sixteen miles distant, commenced running regularly in February 1859. Wisconsin had been admitted to the union as a state in 1848 and by 1850 Milwaukee had an estimated population of 20,000. 

This was the scene when Lute Nieman was born in 1857 in the tiny settlement of Bear Creek. In response to his constant pleas to become an editor, Grandmother Delamater called upon the friendly assistance of the former schoolteacher, Theron Haight, then editor of The Waukesha Freeman. At the age of twelve, Lute became a printers' devil on that weekly newspaper. He ran errands, folded papers and willingly did whatever he was told to do, finally learning to set and distribute type. He boarded at the home of Mr. Haight and his active world was that of the country newspaper. He had never been away from home and on week-ends he visited Grandmother Delamater at Mukwonago. At times of bitter homesickness he received encouragement and inspiration from her.

Two years later, in 1871, he went to work in the composing room of The Milwaukee Sentinel. He enjoyed his work and took pride in becoming an expert. The president of The Sentinel Company advised him to get into the "writing end" of the business. With this in mind, Mr. Nieman spent the following winter at Carroll College in Waukesha, residing with his Grandmother Delamater (8)

who then lived there. Here he first became an "editor" when he served as Waukesha correspondent for The Sentinel. Later he returned to Milwaukee to become a reporter for that paper and act as their correspondent in Madison, the state capital, during the year 1875. He was managing editor of The Sentinel from 1876 to 1880 and his grasp of the important events of the day and the true values of the news made him, at twenty-one years of age, the successful executive of the then largest newspaper in Wisconsin.

He had become a "newspaperman" and did his best to collect, edit and print the news fully and truthfully. 

There were no side issues, no secret concessions or compromise of any sort, and this was the cornerstone of his success, because newspapers at that time were largely political organs or toys in the hands of wealthy men with outside interests to which their newspapers were subservient.

It is not difficult to understand Mr. Nieman's desire to become the owner of his own newspaper. In casting about for such an opportunity, he went to St. Paul as managing editor of the St. Paul Dispatch, then owned by 

Governor Marshall of Minnesota. Here he acquired a third interest with the offer of majority stock if he could restore the property to leadership and profit. He accomplished the task within a year but relinquished his interest because he was homesick for Milwaukee.

About this time James C. Scripps of Detroit discussed backing Mr. Nieman in the establishment of a new Milwaukee paper, though there were already several. The 

Sentinel had absorbed the Republican and News, and shared leadership with the Evening Wisconsin. The Chronicle had just been sold; the Freie Presse, the Herold and the Seebote (9)

were German dailies. Mr. Scripps lost interest when a new newspaper, The Daily Journal, was started as a campaign sheet by P. D. Deuster, a candidate for congress.

In those days it was common practice for newspapers to be started as "organs" to serve varying political interests. Mr. Nieman saw his opportunity in this event and on December 11, 1882, twenty-two days after The Daily Journal had been started, he purchased a half interest and later a majority interest. The boy who wanted to be a "newspaperman" was now, at twenty-five, the controlling owner and executive head of his own newspaper. 

The beginning was small, indeed, - a single sheet, both sides printed on a flat-bed press, folded to make four pages of five columns each, the columns being lengthened whenever the news volume required it. Seldom was there even a wood-cut illustration in the early issues. The circulation price was two cents; its competitors sold for five cents. This differential was to play an important part in the paper's future growth. The presses were leased from the Seebote, as was the limited space for composing room, at 96 Mason Street. The paper's entire business was handled from one desk with one hundred square feet of office space. The chief responsibility fell to Mr. Nieman. 

He was publisher, editor, and circulation manager; he served as typesetter, a tedious job, when the extra load was too great for the small force. 

For half a century The Journal was guided by Mr. Nieman, a record seldom equalled in American journalism. 

Yet it was never a one-man paper in the sense of being influenced by petty personal whims. It has never been a party controlled paper. It has valued its freedom above everything else. It has never absorbed any other paper nor been in any way affiliated with any outside interests. (10)

It has followed Mr. Nieman's code to make a newspaper without fear, and without bias in its news columns. Its editorial opinions have been soundly based on the facts within the news. It has believed in the ability of the public to judge it correctly.

Though resources were small, the first Journals revealed a completeness and care in editing and preparation that commanded attention. Spurred on by necessity, at times desperately in need of funds, accepting hardship as part of growth and development - so did Lucius 

William Nieman come by a better understanding of, and confidence in, himself which was not easily shaken. Hard, intelligent work and the good qualities of his character either created what was needed or left him empty-handed. 

His deep-rooted convictions and well-founded principles kept him from being cynical and ruthless. There was no self-pity or bitterness only the job to be done - printing the news and keeping faith with the readers, who were soon to show their appreciation and confidence in the new Journal.

On February 5, 1883, a corporation was formed with three stockholders. The capital stock was $15,000, but only $9,000 of that was subscribed. Mr. Nieman and 

Mr. Michael Kraus held nearly all the stock. In June 

1890 it was increased to $20,000 and Mr. Kraus retired from the company; and Mr. Lloyd Tilghman Boyd became its business manager, in which capacity he served for a quarter of a century.

Growth was gradual, the circulation being 1000 copies in 1882; 14,000 in 1892; 29,000 in 1902; with an increase to 71,000 in 1912.

In 1884 The Journal acquired the United Press (not to be confused with the present United Press) leased wire (11)

-- service and it was not until 1894 that a service — the forerunner of the Associated Press became available. 

In newspapers of those days telegraph news was usually given importance far beyond its worth because of its cost and extra wire tolls.

Mr. Nieman edited such news carefully and stopped the practice of padding ordinary stories. His objective was to get and print the news as it occurred, rather than as he might have wanted it. He acquired a capacity for facts. A wholesome respect for these facts helped form his editorial opinions and gave him courage as an editor to meet issues in a way that made his decision seem simple and inevitable, and in no way courageous. He was conducting a newspaper and its duty was to print the news; the news was a presentation of available facts, and that was all there was to it. The reading public soon learned to discriminate between articles designed to influence opinion and the honestly edited articles printed in The Journal. 

Under Mr. Nieman's guidance, The Journal stood squarely on the side of the people instead of any class in all questions involving political rights, economic welfare and social justice. This was done as a matter of principle and also in the firm belief that it would safeguard our institutions to make government truly serve the public welfare.

In this phase of its career The Journal was a crusading newspaper - not from design, but because Mr. Nieman held fast to his idea of freedom and accuracy in printing the news. Some two months after Mr. Nieman bought 

The Journal, Milwaukee's leading hotel, the Newhall

House, burned to the ground, causing the death of more than seventy guests. It proved to be a firetrap, though (12)

advertised as fireproof. Efforts were made to hush the scandal that ensued, but The Journal rewarded its readers' confidence by printing the story in full. 

Mr. Nieman believed that the city's educational program should be free from legislative influence and restrictions, as he had always fought to keep the courts free from political control. For this reason he opposed the 

Bennett law which made the teaching of foreign languages illegal in the grade and parochial schools of the city. It had been the practice of Wisconsin state treasurers to credit to their own account the interest on state bank balances, and from 1893 to 1900 The Journal conducted a campaign to force them to comply with the law and refund such moneys. More than half a million dollars was restored to the state treasury by this work. 

When in 1888 The Journal supported Cleveland for president, its financial life was threatened because of moneys which it owed to the backers of another candidate. Again in 1896, when bolting William Jennings 

Bryan on the silver issue, it lost temporarily one-third of its circulation. One thing that was never lost was Mr. 

Nieman's courage to follow his own convictions. He was always broadly independent in his political views and did not hesitate to defend them.

The Journal led the successful fight to eliminate national party labels from municipal election ballots and to elect city officers by a majority vote. While Mr. Nieman was never convinced that such new political measures as primary elections, the initiative, the referendum, and the recall would be of permanent value and usefulness, he repeatedly declared that the misdeeds of politicians and abuse of the people's confidence by public officials justified the popular demand for these changes in our system (13)

of government. For this reason he advocated that such legislation be enacted in good faith.

The Journal took a strong position in favor of such democratic measures as the election of United States senators, and even a president, by direct popular vote. It fought long and strenuously for municipal home-rule the right of cities to formulate their own local policies and to manage their own affairs. 

On the Fiftieth Anniversary of The Journal, Mr. Nie man said:

There was no partisanship in the editorial direction of The Journal. It set out to print the news; its policies were based on sound economic principles regardless of political, financial, or other considerations.

For example, when the Republican party went on raising tariffs to make high wages, as it said, and to make a market for the farmer, as it said, The Journal fought it, knowing this was humbug and distrusting the purpose of men who preached such things. That was one thing that was unsound.

When in 1896 the Democrats set up the idea that they could put into fifty or sixty cents worth of silver the value of a dollar in gold, The Journal fought that, knowing it was unsound and destructive. In national politics it has come about that The 

Journal oftener supported Democrats than Republicans because the Republican party was founded on belief in the power of laws and has oftener proposed laws as the sovereign means of making and unmaking economic and social conditions.

The Journal has grown up with the people of Milwaukee; it has lived close to them. It has never cared about classes, but about people; children's interests have been close to its heart and baseball on the sandlots has meant more to it than the professional leagues.

One of its most earnest policies has been to get all of the information it could about matters of importance to the public, giving them all sides of a question. It has never denied any (14)

man who had a case the opportunity to state it. In its news stories it has kept up unceasingly the effort not to do injustice to any one or bring into its stories what might hurt people innocent of all offense. This we do not think of as something particularly virtuous, but simply as trying to be square; a policy which makes a newspaper trusted and wins it a place in the life of its community. 

So spoke Mr. Nieman, clearly and to the point, as usual devoid of all pretense, always seeking the facts as a basis for printing the news. Many, many times he would ask: "Whom does he represent? Is there a call for this or that? Is the thing real or artificial? What results can be expected, given this or that turn of events?" His was a direct and homely approach, showing a sympathetic interest in all classes of people. 

In 1892 The Journal for the first time became owner of its mechanical plant in the Montgomery Building on East Michigan Street. In 1907 it built a modern newspaper plant near the corner of Fourth and Wisconsin Avenue, which was expected to take care of its future. 

In little more than fifteen years it was found necessary to build its present efficient plant at Fourth and State streets, which contributes greatly to the paper's present success. The circulation price of The Journal had been reduced to one cent at the time of the Spanish-American War in 1898 and it was at this low price that the Sunday edition was launched in 1911 when competitive Sunday papers sold for five cents.

The Pulitzer Prize was awarded The Journal for the 

"most disinterested and meritorious service rendered by any American newspaper during the year" (1919). This reward came as a result of Mr. Nieman's keen understanding of issues at a time when various racial groups (15)

endeavored to use this country in behalf of their own homelands. To dig out the material being used as propaganda by foreign governments it was necessary to translate nearly five million words. The Journal carried on this work so that the facts might be kept straight. 

Mr. Nieman stated that he did not consider The Journal's activity as anything particularly courageous beyond trying to print the news fairly as an American newspaper. 

He recalled that The Journal was not a one-man paper; that it had been printed at that time for nearly thirty-six years in its community and had been held close in the confidence of its readers. He called it "a great responsibility, but a greater opportunity . . . the opportunity to speak up for the birthright of Americans, the dearest thing that anyone of us can possess as the voice of a community and a commonwealth our duty was plain . . . no thought of courage came to us . . . we had but the alternative of sinking into contempt.' • • 

Mr. Nieman, the "newspaperman," had the usual outside interests and hobbies of other men of his time, and it was his understanding of such things that helped him make an even more interesting paper for his readers. He was an enthusiastic horseman and bicyclist in his early days. He owned one of the first automobiles in the community; he enjoyed baseball, a drink and game of poker.

Later he became a golf and bridge devotee. He traveled widely in the United States but spent little time abroad. 

On November 29, 1900, he married Agnes Elizabeth

Gunther Wahl, a leader in Milwaukee's social and intellectual life. She was the daughter of the late Christian 

Wahl, one of Milwaukee's most public spirited citizens who, as a result of his efforts to serve the public person- (16)

ally as well as publicly, became known as "the father of 

Milwaukee's public park system.”.

Mrs. Nieman was Lute's best friend and almost constant companion, as well as a devoted wife. Their many mutual interests made for a genuine and happy household. She was to live less than six months after he passed away and was to create a fund in his memory for the Nieman Foundation at Harvard University to "promote and elevate the standards of journalism in the United States and educate persons deemed especially qualified for journalism." 

Mr. and Mrs. Nieman left no direct descendants, though Mr. Nieman is survived by a niece, Miss Faye 

McBeath. Mrs. Nieman's only nephew, Cyril Gordon 

Weld, died January 13, 1936.

The growth of The Journal under Mr. Nieman's guidance during half a century from a four-page handbill to one of the leading metropolitan dailies in the United States is bound up with the development of Milwaukee and Wisconsin. As an institution The Journal's interests progress with the economic and social life of the community which it serves. 

The career of Lucius William Nieman deserves a tribute from a free press and from a public that seeks unbiased news. His own descriptive item in Who's Who in 

America consisted of two words: Newspaper man.
