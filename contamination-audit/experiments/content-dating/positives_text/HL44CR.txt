EXERCISE OF JUDICIAL FUNCTIONS BY LAY JUDGES 

The Judicial Council recommends (a) that all judicial functions be transferred as quickly as possible from courts having lay judges to courts having law-trained judges; (b) that all judicial functions of the County Court of Lake County be transferred immediately to the Circuit Court of Lake County; and (c) that jurisdiction of adoption proceedings be immediately transferred from county courts to circuit courts in all counties where jurisdiction in adoption proceedings still remains in the county court.

The above recommendations are predicated on the report printed on the following pages. 

The report was prepared by a study committee of the Council and approved by the Council's Executive Committee with the suggestion that specific suggestions of ways and means of effectuating transfer be reserved for further study.

It was adopted with amendments (b and c above), by the full Council on June 22, 1956. -4-

COMMITTEE ON EXERCISE OF JUDICIAL FUNCTIONS BY LAY JUDGES 

Your subcommittee heretofore appointed to consider the two questions; (1) Should judicial functions be exercised by lay judges, and (2) Should judicial functions, now exercised by County 

Judges, be transferred to other courts presided over by law-trained judges. 

Herewith reports as follows:

We have submitted the above questions to the Bar Associations of all judicial districts in the state, to Circuit Judges in all judicial districts and to all County Judges. While a few responses were not received, the answers in general have indicated that the members of the bar and the Circuit Judges have almost unanimously agreed that in principle the exercise of judicial functions by lay judges is improper. 

The same opinion has been expressed by a very large majority of County Judges although their opinions in some details have differed. A few of them think that they are better qualified to handle juvenile cases than a busy Circuit Judge would be, they being better acquainted, so they think, with the people involved and having the time to give these matters more attention than a Circuit Judge would have, As to the propriety of transfer of judicial functions, now exercised by County Judges, to courts presided over by law-trained judges, the responses from Bar Associations and Circuit Judges, as well as those from almost all of the County Judges, indicate that such transfer should be made wherever possible, considering availability of judicial man-power. In all instances where such transfer has been heretofore made to Circuit Courts or District Courts such action has been almost unanimously approved. Practically, the only opposition to such transfer arises in areas where population in a given county is so small that a District Court is not considered feasible and the visits of a Circuit Judge are too infrequent to give these matters adequate attention, The analysis made by your committee of responses to inquiries, as above stated, appears to indicate that such transfer of judicial functions from County Courts should be made at this time to Circuit Courts in all counties which constitute an entire judicial district. These would be Lake County, where the change is requested by County Judge, Circuit Judge and the bar, and Baker County where the change is opposed by all of them. The result in both of these counties would be that the Circuit Judge would not be available as much of the time for work outside his district. This committee does not consider this a valid argument against the change. Those counties asking for a District Court, should be granted one, if at all possible, and judicial functions transferred to it, and perhaps with all Justice of the Peace Courts abolished. Copy of portion of Judge Fortis letter 

First, I shall comment on the question of jurisdiction in juvenile cases. By agreement with the other Judges here for the past 10 months I have heard virtually all of these cases, averaging at least 300 per year. My experience has impressed me greatly with the grave importance of the work and its serious, long-range implications. I am reminded of the opinion of Judge Skipworth following the transfer of jurisdiction to him at a time when he was the only judge in this county. When jurisdiction was first transferred to him he was not happy about it. However, about two years later in discussing the problem with me, he told me that he had come to feel that it was of grave importance to the proper handling and disposition of these matters that they be heard in the circuit court. Two factors contribute importantly to this. One is the prestige which attaches to Circuit Court work and its resulting ability to command greater community and parental responsibility. 

Certainly the resources of the community will respond far better to the efforts of a Circuit Court Judge than can ever be anticipated when these matters are presided over either by a county judge or a district judge. In the second place, the Circuit Court sits as a court of record, which, in my observation, has important significance in this field. 

In view of the decision of the Supreme Court in Re Estate of Myers, 197 Or. 520 and particularly at pages 525 and 526, in which the Court establishes the rule that an adoption decree is subject to collateral attack at any time and may be set aside for virtually any technical deviation from the statutory requirements, it appears to me to be important that adoption proceedings be reviewed by a Circuit Judge. 

The number of adoption cases in which procedural requirements have not been complied with that have been observed in the Circuit Court of this county since the transfer of jurisdiction raises serious question as to the validity of a large number of adoption decrees heretofore entered. It is to be remembered that the 

Court when handling adoptions does not sit as a court of general jurisdiction although it does in probate. The legal consequences which flow from any error in adoption proceedings therefore are so drastic that scrutiny by a Circuit Judge seems to me to be highly desirable and should be the goal in all counties of the state. Furthermore, the time element is not of great significance, and adoption decrees can be examined by the judge at his leisure without impairment of the rights of the persons concerned. It is therefore my recommendation that all adoption proceedings be transferred to the Circuit Courts throughout the state. 

It seems to me that probate jursidiction is primarily something that must be determined by what is practical in the different counties. Even the County Court sits as a court of general jurisdiction in probate and its orders cannot therefore be collaterally attacked after jurisdiction has attached. In addition as a practical matter, probate proceedings affecting real property are subjected generally to the independent scrutiny of the title companies which affords a valuable protection to the orderliness thereof. 

By way of summary therefore I recommend: 1. 

In all counties which constitute an entire district, the transfer of jurisdiction in all judicial matters should be forthwith transferred to the Circuit Court. -7-

SUPREME COURT BACKLOG 

The Judicial Council recommends that two more Justices be added to the Supreme Court to help cut down the steadily increasing backlog of appeals in that court.

The above recommendation is predicated on the oral report of a study committee composed of Associate Justice William C. Perry, Chairman,

Associate Justice Earl C. Latourette, Circuit Judge James W. Crawford, Circuit Juage Frank B. Reid and Circuit Judge Paul R. Harris. 

The oral report, presented at the June 22, 1956, meeting of the Council, was made after a thorough study of all facets of the problem, and consisted of two major propositions. 

The first, to add two more justices was unanimously approved, while the second, to authorize paid, nonvoting commissioners, was almost unanimously disapproved.

The Council further requested the Oregon State Bar to assist with and sponsor legislation providing for two additional Supreme Court Justices. -13-

To a limited extent the bibliography reviewed consisted of various books, journals and reports of bar associations supplemented by personal contacts as above stated. 

OREGON AS COMPARED TO OTHER STATES 

It should be carried in mind that the caliber of Oregon citizens is high and that what might be most desirable in other areas does not hold true for Oregon. 

Numerous suggestions made in the bibliography reviewed are now in vogue in Oregon at least in framework.

PRACTICAL ASPECT 

What might be termed a platitude for a desired end result is not difficult of statement but to develop and carry the same out in practice presents the problem. 

In metropolitan areas various well-meaning and good-intentioned groups hold to different ideologies. Would these different groups agree that a jury commissioner or a commission of three or any other member could and would select jurors representative of a cross-section of the community that would be satisfactory to all factions concerned? If selectivity is attempted would various groups demand proportional or percentage representation according to occupations or on the population basis of every precinct? On the other hand would the Chambers of Commerce, labor unions, parent teachers' associations and the like want a representative percentage of their group selected? Sometimes the theory that the least governed are the best governed is the better course. We are not opposed to improvement or progress but do urge caution and mature reflection.

SELECTION OF JURORS IS NOT A JUDICIAL FUNCTION 

The effect of the holding State ex rel V. Holman, 68 Ore. 546, 549 is that the selection of a jury list is legislative and not judicial. 

Some judges feel that the court should have a voice in the selection of the master panel; that the County Court where it does select names for jury duty is not interested and does not make any effort to provide for the most competent" to serve. 

The majority hold to the view that this function brings into play the separate branches of government and that the obligation rests with the legislative rather than the judicial branch. Further, that selection independent of the judiciary forestalls criticism especially in the smaller communities, and safeguards against possible abuse.

The opinion has been expressed that judges are not in the best position to make jury selections

SELECTIVITY DISCRETION VERS US CHANCE 

It may be stated roughly that two methods for the preparation of the master jury list are employed in Oregon. The method employed in the Fourth Judicial District (Multnomah County) is entirely by chance selection. In all other Districts there is a certain amount of selectivity and discretion employed. 

Opinion of Attorney General 1928–1930, page 549 states that the Jury Commissioner and Clerk are to work jointly and must use discretion. 

In these so-called "other Districts" than the Fourth, the "master jury lists" are drawn and selected from the list of either or both the registered voters or taxpayers of the district. The selections for these "master jury lists under ORS 10.110 are accomplished in the following ways which reflect however, a general pattern, to-wit; (1) By full County Court; (2) By County Judge and County Clerk; (3) By full County Court with Sheriff "Sitting in and in some instances the Circuit Judge being. present from time to time during drawing. 

Those who are known to lack competency or the requisite qualifications of ORS 10.030 or exempt under ORS 10.040 are generally eliminated from call. Likewise the drawing of husband and wife or known relatives is avoided.

ORS 10.130 provides that the names of "those persons only must be selected who are known or believed to be possessed of the qualifications prescribed in ORS 10.030 and not entitled to exemption as provided in ORS 10.040. Again, how is this to be determined?

In at least one instance the Circuit Judge of his District suggests the number of jurors to be drawn for the master list. 

In Counties having County Courts the jury list shall contain at least 500 names if that number be on the assessment roll or registration books and not more than 2500. 

Where activities of the County Court are transferred to the Circuit Court the list shall contain at least 1500 names and not more than 5000. (ORS 10.140) 

The number of jurors generally drawn run from 350 to 500 depending on the size of the district.

MISCELLANEOUS SUGGESTIONS 

It has been intimated that at times jurors are excused from service other than by the Court. Under ORS 10.040 and ORS 10,050 we conclude that only the Court should excuse a juror. If there be any doubt legislation should eliminate it. 

The Statute does not state who should be in control of jurors after they report for service at the various terms. In some circumstances well-intentioned courtesies have been extended other than by the Court to the jury panel by the way of explanations, advice and the like. It is suggested that if need be legislation be enacted that in the discretion (not mandatory) of the Court, jurors drawn for any term shall be subject to the full and complete orders of the Court. 

An opinion has been received that by a larger master jury list a better crosssection of jurors is obtained; also that the size of the master jury list should be fixed by the Court rather than by statute. This for legislative consideration, if advisable, -22-

CONCLUSION 

As stated in the beginning your committee has misgivings as to this report. Its length and lack of any definite recommendation is self-apparent. Many references to Code Sections that have an indirect bearing have not been attempted. However, the problem of securing a desired result cannot be accomplished by merely passing ill-advised legislation which is not the product of studied reflection and feasible of practical application. We feel guarded action is required and that we should proceed with caution and only after we are sure of our ground in actual operation. 

The general consensus outside of the Fourth Judicial District, is that satisfactory jurors are selected and this is true of the Fourth considering what can be accomplished by any other method that would be satisfactory to all concerned. 

It is thought that in the Fourth District, the Jury Commissioner could and should make a closer check for physical infirmities, at least such as hearing, illness and the like.

WESTERN JUDICIAL COUNCILS 

Although there are probably thirty states having Judicial Councils or Judicial Conferences, there is no effective body in existence for an exchange of ideas and experience.

Each Council works more or less independently and in the dark as to the projects of other Councils. 

The December 10, 1955, meeting of the Oregon Council, therefore, passed the following resolution: 

Whereas the Judicial Council of Oregon is a new organization, and can benefit from the experience of other agencies in the field of judicial administration, and;

Whereas, several other states in the far west have similar official judicial administration agencies, with much experience of value to Oregon, Be It Therefore Resolved;

That the Executive Secretary is hereby instructed to explore the possibilities of establishing an organization of State Judicial Councils, Judicial Conferences and Administrative offices within the area of the 9th U.S. Judicial Circuit, for the purpose of exchanging experience, ideas and information with such other agencies." 

As of this date, much information has been exchanged with the Washington Judicial Council and a similar plan is being worked out between Oregon and California. -27-
