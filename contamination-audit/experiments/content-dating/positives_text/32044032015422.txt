THE ELECTORAL COLLEGE THE ELECTION OF PRESIDENT AND VICE PRESIDENT OF THE UNITED STATES 

Although the Constitution of the United States and the twelfth amendment are explicit in directing how a President and Vice President shall be chosen, the term "electoral college" seems to have been only partly understood by the average citizen of this country as neither the Constitution, nor any of its amendments refers to an electoral college. Consequently it will be of some interest to learn just how and when the term came into general use. 

To explain the origin and development of the electoral college it is necessary to look back into the beginnings of this Government, to the days before we were guided by a Constitution. As is familiar to all who have studied our history as a nation, the Continental Congress was gathered together more or less speedily, and with very few models upon which to pattern its procedure and authority. The Delegates from each of the Thirteen Colonies, eager to free themselves from the oppressive laws and practices of the mother country, assembled in Philadelphia in 1774, with only the slenderest shred of a program. 

When assembled the Delegates naturally selected a presiding officer from among their number. In giving this chairman a title, they evidently desired to show their independence in ideas, and, instead of calling him Mr. Speaker, by which title the heads of colonial assemblies, as well as the leader of the British House of Commons were known, they called the presiding officer of the Congress "President." Up to that time this title seems to have been used only by the heads of certain scientific bodies.

Until the adoption of the Constitution of the United States in 1787, all executive business of the Government was conducted by committees of the Continental Congress, and the President of Congress was not an executive officer. When, in 1776, the Commonwealth of 

Pennsylvania adopted its first constitution, that document provided that full executive power should be vested in a president and council; and this action probably afterward had some influence on the convention which framed the Constitution of the United States, for it divided the Government into three branches the executive, the legislative, and the judicial, and the executive officer was to be a 

President.

This valuation of their worth seems to be indicated by the fact that although finally adopted by the Congress in 1777, it was not until 1781 that the last State, Maryland, ratified the document. The desperate condition of the country caused a general feeling that relief in the shape of amended Articles of Confederation or in another plan, was immediately necessary to put the country under a basically sound government.

For instance, Roger Sherman, of Connecticut is reported to have observed that he thought the people should have as little to do directly with the Government as possible. To illustrate the significance of thus ignoring the citizens in an election for President, it is enough to remind one that George Washington was elected to the office of Chief Magistrate of the Nation, by 69 votes the total number cast by the electors. At that time, three

Therefore, of an estimated population of 4,000,000 people, a President was chosen by 69 voters, who had not been selected by the people, but appointed by State legislatures, save in the instances of 

Maryland and Virginia.

We get a glimpse of what the Delegates to the Constitutional Convention had in mind from one of Alexander Hamilton's papers in the Federalist. Referring to the plan for electing a President, Hamilton wrote:

The process of election affords a moral certainty, that the office of President will never fall to the lot of any man who is not in an eminent degree endowed with the requisite qualifications. Talents for low intrigue and the little arts of popularity, may alone suffice to elevate a man to the first honors in a single State; but it will require other talents, and a different kind of merit to establish him in the esteem and confidence of the whole Union, or of so considerable a portion of it as would be necessary to make him a successful candidate for the distinguished office of President of the United States. It will not be too strong to say, that there will be a constant probability of seeing the station filled by characters preeminent for ability and virtue."

FIRST ELECTORAL COLLEGES 

After a sufficient number of States had ratified the Constitution, the Congress, before dissolving under the Confederation, passed a law directing that elections for Presidential electors be held on the first Wednesday in January 1789, and that the electors should assemble in their respective States on the first Wednesday in February and proceed to vote for a President and Vice President.14 Little difficulty was encountered in concluding the selection of electors, before nightfall of January 7, in nearly all of the ratifying States, except New York, where the voting was done by the legislature, which was divided on the question of Federal power. The Assembly of New 

York was strongly anti-Federal, and the Senate was just as strongly Federal, although at the time these were not organized parties, but rather associations of persons having similar ideas on the subject. 

As the two Houses could not agree, the legislature adjourned without selecting Presidential electors and, therefore, New York was unable to vote in the Federal election.

There was some discussion in the New Hampshire Legislature, where the Senate claimed the right to negative the choice of the Assembly. Shortly before midnight on January 7, the lower house yielded and chose electors, every one of whom had Federalist leanings. 

In Maryland the people voted for electors, and two tickets were in the field. By means of speeches and electioneering, the voters were told that Washington was their candidate; and the Federalist ticket won. In Virginia, too, the people had the opportunity of selecting their electors, and in Massachusetts still another plan was tried. 

There, two electors were to be selected at large, and eight by the legislature. In the remaining States electors were chosen by the legislatures, and even these were not all conducted on the same pattern; for in some States there was a joint ballot, and in others a concurrent vote of both branches named the electors.15

After the States had selected their electors they did not meet for almost a month to name the President and Vice President. This meeting was held on February 4, 1789, which was the "first Wednesday in February next," as provided in the act of September 13, 1788,16 regulating the time of selection of electors and their assembly to make their selections. But still the country remained in the dark respecting the choice for the two highest offices in the Government, because at that time the new Congress was in process of being chosen and, therefore, would not count the electoral vote until March 4, as provided by law. It is interesting to note the curious ways congressional elections were conducted in some States. In New Jersey the polls were open for 3 weeks, because the laws were silent on the subject of naming the time when the polls should be closed. In Connecticut the situation only differed in kind for it was the custom there to hold two elections. Although the electors in the States which had ratified the Constitution had been chosen in January, and the Congress was elected by the people in February, it was not until April 6, that the first session of the first Congress actually began to take up legislative business, and, as everything was new there was a mountain of business awaiting dispatch. At the date set for convening of the Congress, March 4, only a handful of Senators and Representatives presented themselves in Federal Hall, in New York City, to take their places. The lack of a quorum continued for a month, a few additional Representatives and Senators arriving every few days, but not enough to form a quorum. On April 6, Richard Henry Lee, of Virginia, arrived, and when he took his seat in the Senate, it was announced by the clerk that a quorum was present. 

The first business to be transacted by the Senate was the reading and filing of the credentials of the members present, whereupon that body proceeded by ballot to the choice of a President of the Senate, for the sole purpose of opening and counting the votes for the President and Vice President of the United States. The Vice President, when elected, would then become presiding officer of the Senate. 

The choice for the temporary President fell upon John Langdon, of New Hampshire, of which State he had once been president. Langdon also had been one of the delegates from his State to the Con stitutional Convention.18 

The first business transacted with the temporary President in the chair was the ordering of Oliver Ellsworth, Senator from Connecticut, to notify the House of Representatives that a quorum of the Senate was present; that a President was chosen for the sole purpose of opening the certificates and counting the votes of the electors of the several States for the choice of the President and Vice President of the United States; and that the Senate was ready to proceed in the Senate Chamber, in the presence of the House, to discharge that duty; and that the Senate had appointed one of its Members to sit at the Clerk's table and make a list of the votes as they shall be declared; submitting it to the wisdom of the House to appoint one or more of its Members for the like purpose.

The Speaker and the Members of the House of Representatives attended in the Senate Chamber, and the President elected for the purpose of counting the votes declared that the Senate and House of Representatives had met and that he in their presence had opened and counted the votes of the electors for President and Vice President of the United States.19

As had been expected, every one of the 69 electoral votes had been cast for George Washington, of Virginia, for President. Only 10

States were represented, since the New York Legislature had not agreed on the mode of choosing electors and North Carolina and Rhode Island had not vet ratified the Constitution. John Adams received but 34 votes, which, while enough to elect him Vice President, was the result of a maneuver by the anti-Federalists to elect George Clinton, of New York, to the second place. By the time the next Presidential election came, in 1792, there was a great deal of canvassing, for two political parties were in the process of being formed. A desperate effort was made to elect George Clinton of New York, Vice President, when it was recognized that the more popular candidate for the Vice Presidency, Thomas Jefferson, was constitutionally ineligible because George Washington, the undisputed choice for President, came from Virginia and another Virginian was not eligible for the second place. However, ignoring this situation, the State of Kentucky gave its four votes for Vice President to Jefferson, as well as casting its four votes for Washington for President. 

John Adams, however, received 77 votes, the highest for the office of Vice President; while Washington's election was again unanimous, he receiving the entire 132 electoral votes.

Toward the close of Washington's first administration, on the eve of the second Presidential election, Congress made some revisions to the law of Presidential succession, and also in the election formalities. 

On March 1, 1792, the President approved an act passed by Congress covering those subjects which needed clarification. From the beginning it had been felt that there were serious weaknesses in the provisions of the Federal law on that subject. Congress did not regard as a matter of importance the method of choosing Presidential electors and seemed equally satisfied whether the States chose them by popular vote or had them selected and appointed by the legislatures. It was considered wise to state definitely the time when electors should be chosen and in the new law it was required that all electors should be appointed within 34 days preceding the first Wednesday in December 1792, on which day they were to meet in their respective State capitals and vote. The voting was to be done by ballot. Each elector was to write on a slip of paper the names of two men, but should make ro mark or sign to indicate which should be President and which Vice President.

The law provided that after the ballot was taken three certificates of the number of votes cast, and for whom cast, should be prepared, one of these to be sent by special messenger to the President of the 

Senate before the first Wednesday in January next ensuing; a second be sent to the same officer by post, and a third, for safekeeping must be given to the judge of the district in which the electors met. Should there be no President of the Senate, the certificates must be sent to the Secretary of State. The day named for counting the electoral vote in Congress was the second Wednesday in February. In the event the President and Vice President should both die, resign, or be removed, the Speaker of the House should act as President till a new election took place. It will be shown that this law did not meet the design intended, especially in the Presidential election of 1800, when

both Jefferson and Burr tied in the electoral count, and the House of Representatives had to elect the President. This situation brought forth the twelfth amendment to the Constitution in 1803, which directed the electors to state definitely which of the two names they submitted was their choice for President. 

In 1796, the electoral colleges, although they had not yet received that designation, either by law or custom, distributed their votes. over a wide field of candidates-13 being named. John Adams who had the largest number of votes, 71, and consequently was elected President, was only chosen by the small margin of 3 votes, his nearest competitor, Jefferson, received 68 votes, which elected him Vice President.20

In 1796, for the first time, there appeared to be a popular and even exciting interest in the Presidential election. In a number of States the electors were now being chosen by district voting, and citizens thus had an opportunity to express their choice. In some States, however, the legislatures still selected the Presidential electors. 

This was true in Connecticut and New York, and partly the case in Massachusetts where no popular choice had occurred. The trend was largely Federalist. In Pennsylvania the Federalists succeeded in passing legislation which required Presidential electors to be chosen on a general ticket, instead of by districts, which resulted unexpectedly in the victory of the Jefferson ticket, the Republican-Democrat Party prevailing in that State. At this election, for the first time, party tickets appeared in the field-Federalist and Republican-Democraticand may be regarded as the debut of the American party system in national politics.

ELECTORAL COLLEGE CHANGES AFTER 1800 

In the Presidential election of 1800, the two political parties were found to be organized and ready for the exciting struggle which followed. Both the Federalists and the Democratic-Republicans (the 

Democratic Party of that day), entered the race determined to be victorious. In nearly all of the States the legislatures resolved to choose the Presidential electors themselves, instead of entrusting this procedure to popular vote. The parties distrusted the uncertainty of a choice by either a general or a district ticket. It is claimed the 

Federalists were father to this plan. However, the Democratic- 

Republicans followed their example only after a resolution to submit an amendment to the Constitution to establish a strict system of election by districts throughout the Union,21 had failed of passage in the Congress.

While the proposed resolution for constitutional amendment had failed in Congress after the closely contested election for President in 1800,22 it became more clearly necessary to simplify the method for electing the President and Vice President. In this election Thomas

Article II of the Constitution of the United States clearly required amending to prevent the recurrence of the confusion which had accompanied the election of Jefferson. In 1803, a new constitutional amendment was proposed in Congress which, popularly speaking, was a substitute for paragraph 3 of the first section of article II of the Constitution. Briefly this directed that the electors-shall name in their ballots the persons voted for as President, and, in distinct ballots, the person voted for Vice President, and they shall make distinct lists of all persons voted for as President, and of all persons voted for as Vice President * * * and if no person have such majority, then from the persons having the highest numbers not exceeding three on the list of those voted for as President, the House of Representatives shall choose immediately, by ballot, the President. 

This became the twelfth amendment to the Constitution when it was declared to have been ratified by the legislatures of three-fourths of the States, in a proclamation of the Secretary of State dated September 25, 1804. The first Presidential election held under this amendment was that of 1804, when there were two candidates for President, and two for the office of Vice President. Jefferson received 

162 of the total 176 votes cast for President; and George Clinton, of

New York, received a like number of the electoral votes for Vice 

President. C. C. Pinckney, received 14 votes for President, and 

Rufus King, of New York, received 14 votes for Vice President.

While it is rather difficult to assign a definite year when the term electoral colleges, as the meetings of the Presidential electors are called, came into popular use, we do know that the name was more widely used after the Presidential election of 1820 when James Monroe, of Virginia, began his second term as President. On February 14, 1820, when the date arrived for the opening of the certificates and the counting of the votes from the various State electoral colleges by the President of the Senate, considerable excitement arose over the return made from Missouri, which actually was not proclaimed a State of the Union until August of that year. In the debates on the floor of the House relating to the returns from Missouri, Representative Randolph several times referred to "the electoral college" or "colleges." Congress was still debating the Missouri Compromise by which that Territory was to be admitted to the Union. For the ceremony of counting the electoral votes, the Senate marched over to the House side of the Capitol and the President of the Upper House opened the certificates, and announced the results as each State's vote was called, a proceeding that lasted until late in the afternoon. 

When the Missouri certificates were handed to the tellers, Representative Livermore, of New Hampshire, objected to the vote being counted since Missouri was not yet State of the Union. This assertion brought Representatives Randolph and Floyd, of Virginia, to their feet instantly. Floyd declared hotly that Missouri was not a State,

and consequently not entitled to vote and promptly introduced a resolution to that effect. Anticipating an angry discussion the Members of the Senate left the House for their own Chamber. However, Clay had the resolution laid on the table, the Senators were recalled and the count proceeded. Floyd and Randolph interrupted the proceedings at times by their comments until they were directed by the chairman to take their seats.23

It is evident that some quiet work had meanwhile been done behind the scenes, for the President of the Senate announced the vote both. ways, by including Missouri and by leaving that State out of the count. As its electoral votes were only three in number and the election was not close, the result was not affected. James Monroe had a majority of the total vote. The President of the Senate announced that Monroe had 231 votes, out of 235, including Missouri's three votes and consequently he was declared to have been elected President. 

This disturbance over the electoral vote attracted a great deal of public attention, and for the first time the term "electoral college” came into general use.

What probably was the first dissertation on the plan of Presidential electoral colleges was issued in a pamphlet, signed "Senex", published in August 1823, in New York; in this interesting essay will be found one of the earliest references in print to the meetings of the electors. as electoral colleges. The title of the essay is: "On the mode of constituting Presidential electors addressed to the people of New York.” 25 "Senex" begins by listing the methods in use by the various States: (1) By vote of the legislature, (2) by general ticket, (3) by districts. 

The first mode, he continuedwas adopted by the State of New York. It is not congenial with the spirit of our political institutions, because it is not calculated to secure the choice which shall express the public will. Thus, the President is the choice of the legislature and not of those whom the legislature represent; the great end of representative government to execute popular opinion is abandoned, and the will of a class of public servants is substituted in its stead; the electoral colleges become mere registers of the decisions of the legislature, and the opinions of the people are superseded by the feelings of interests of a few individuals. This was certainly not the intention of our Constitution. 

Taking up the second mode, the author says:

The second mode of choosing Presidential electors which is obtained in practice of Government is by a general ticket; and in favor of this mode there is this recommendation—that it is executed directly by the people at large in their primary assemblies.

To form a ticket it has been customary for a certain number of persons selected from the mass of the people, so as to constitute a fair representation of the different counties to convene and appoint to be placed on the ticket a number of individuals equal to the number of electors required by the Constitution. The ticket thus formed is presented to the people for ratification, and the persons who compose it are voted for collectively throughout the State. By this arrangement every voter in the State has a voice, not only in the constitution of the elector, who, in the case of voting by districts, would be the representative of the other several districts. There is certainly no mode of election which gives the people so much power in the choice of the electors, and as the voice of the majority of voters would be fairly obtained, it cannot be objected to as an unjust or ineffectual expression of the public will.

The idea of designating the meeting of an organized body, assembled for a specific purpose, as a college was not entirely unknown in this country as for example, the College of Physicians and Surgeons. 

This name describing an association of persons performing a specific function, or having a common purpose, was used in the early days of Rome. The College of Cardinals was known as such since the middle of the twelfth century. Long before that time, in the year 251, to be precise, it is related by an early church historian, that "Pope St.

While the Constitution provided the method of electing a President and Vice President, by means of electors from the States, it did not outline the means of nominating by which the names of representative citizens could be presented to the electors as candidates for office. 28 

In the Presidential election of 1800, the political parties nominated candidates by congressional caucus.29 As early as 1813, the Tammany Society of New York suggested the nominating convention. 

However, this system did not develop nationally until 1832. The idea received some encouragement from the Democratic-Republican 

State convention in Harrisburg, Pa., on March 4, 1824, when Andrew

Jackson was first nominated for President by this procedure. The 

Presidential election of 1824, while not the first to nominate by convention, may be said to have broadly established this system, as the successor to the legislative caucus which was narrowly restricted and dominated by party leaders. Other States were at once inspired to follow the same method in 1824, and by the time the electors were to meet, there were four candidates for President and six candidates for 

Vice President.

In the 1824 election Andrew Jackson received 99 votes out of a total of 261 cast. John Quincy Adams received 84 votes; Henry Clay got 

37 votes, and W. H. Crawford, of Georgia, 41. Consequently the election was thrown into the House of Representatives and John Quincy Adams was elected President, receiving the vote of 13 States to 7 for Andrew Jackson.

The election of 1824 was the beginning of real, popular elections in the United States; for, although Jackson was defeated due to splitting of the electoral vote, he received a popular plurality of 100,000 votes. 

As a result of public reaction against that election, 4 years later he was elected President by even a larger plurality. 

By 1832 there were three political parties in the United States--the National Republican, the Democratic, and the Anti-Masonic, and all of them held their nominating conventions in Baltimore that year. 

Since that time national nominating conventions have continued to be the regular mode of presenting to the voters, the names of candidates for the offices of President and Vice President, and the congressional caucus as a nominating institution was superseded.

THE ELECTORAL COLLEGE SINCE 1876 

The Presidential election of 1876 had repercussions that continued to be felt for some years and finally led to amendments to the laws governing the Presidential electors. Such anamosities resulted from that election that although it was settled by a congressional electoral commission, disputed provisions in the laws were not at once clarified and political feelings continued to run high. 

In that election the dispute at first involved the returns from Louisiana, Florida, North Carolina, and Oregon but was finally confined to Florida and Louisiana. The irregularities were principally those of double and multiple returns from nearly all of the States. involved. Some return boards had thrown out votes in certain

The political parties interpreted the language of the constitutional provision for the counting of the electoral votes to mean that the President of the Senate had power to count or refuse to count such votes. It was found that the deadlock could be broken only by the appointment of an electoral commission to investigate and render a decision on the matter. In response to the report of a joint committee of Congress, the Electoral Commission bill passed both Houses by large majorities. The Commission appointed consisted of five Senators, five Representatives, and four Justices of the United States Supreme Court, the latter to select a fifth justice. Before this last selection was made the Commission consisted of seven Democrats and seven Republicans, but the fifth justice selected proved to be a Republican. The Commission made its decisions along party lines, declaring in favor of the Republican nominee. Rutherford B. Hayes thus received 185 electoral votes to the 184 votes for Samuel J. Tilden, the Democratic candidate.30

The bill which created the Commission in effect reversed the provisions of the Twenty-second Joint Rule, which had been in operation since 1865. With respect to single returns, that rule suffered no returns to be rejected save by the concurrent action of the two Houses. 

At this time joint rules were operative only when renewed at the beginning of each Congress. The Senate on January 20, 1876, had agreed to renew all joint rules with the exception of the rule in question. There was considerable discussion of the failure of the Senate to renew this joint rule and much dissatisfaction was evinced by the House in the session immediately following the election of 1876. 

Resolutions were introduced, and reported, both in the Senate and House, to determine the privileges, powers, and duties of the House of Representatives in counting the electoral vote; but no further action was taken thereon, as apparently it was felt that under the Electoral Commission bill, then pending, adequate means would be provided to satisfactorily determine the electoral count and protect the rights of both Houses in that regard. At the time it was recognized that the root of the difficulty was to be found in the loose laws of some of the States, which gave judicial powers to partisan returning boards; and the absence of laws to forestall the presentation of rival certificates. In the Presidential election of 1884 returns were in from nearly every State in the Union indicating that Grover Cleveland, the Democratic nominee, had won. 

However, the returns from New York remained in doubt. There was feverish anxiety in the country as the vote in that State was known to be close and while there were suggestions that the election 30 Andrews, History of the Last Quarter Century in the United States, vol. I, 218 et seq.

might be thrown into the House, as had been the election of 1876, some of the more confident leaders among the Democrats recalled that the Electoral Commission had made it one of its rules "not to go behind the returns.' ""

Consequently, as New York had adopted that rule, fears subsided and although Cleveland carried his home State by a mere thousand plurality, it was felt that there was no cause for an investigation into the New York returns.

The memory of the deadlock in 1876 and the uncertainty of a few days in 1884, finally led Congress in 1886 and 1887 to act in an effort to simplify and clarify the rules governing the Presidential elections and electors. The act of January 19, 1886,31 was mainly concerned with the Presidential succession outlining how that office should descend in the event of a vacancy in it or in the line of succession. 

This act provided that all persons in the line of succession must be constitutionally eligible for the office. 

The act of February 3, 1887,32 provided that— the electors of each State shall meet and give their votes on the second Monday in January next following their appointment, at such place in each State as the legislature of such State shall direct. 

This act was designed to cover contingencies of rival electoral colleges, of which there had been a glimpse in the contest of 1876. Under this act each State is conceded to have the right of determining all controversies regarding the appointment of its own Presidential electors, and in case of a contest, Congress is to accept the State's decision as conclusive and cannot reject any vote, duly certified, unless both Houses concurrently decide that the vote has been irregularly given In the event that more than one return is received from a State only those votes are to be counted which the State itself has endorsed as regular. If, however, the State has been unable to settle the question owing to its having two or more rival sets of authorities or from any other cause, then the two Houses of Congress are to decide the dispute. 

On March 2, 1932, an amendment to the Constitution was proposed to the legislatures of the several States in a joint resolution of Congress which had been adopted by the House on March 1 and by the Senate on March 2. This amendment was proclaimed in effect on February 

6, 1933, by the Secretary of State, 39 of the States having ratified it, becoming the twentieth amendment to the Constitution. 

The twentieth amendment, section 1, directed thatthe terms of the President and Vice President shall end at noon on the 20th day of January, and the terms of Senators and Representatives at noon on the 3d day of January, of the years in which such terms would have ended and if this article had not been ratified.

As the amendment specified that it "shall take effect on the 15th day of October following the ratification of this article," Franklin D. 

Roosevelt who was elected President in November 1932, before the amendment was proclaimed, was inaugurated President on March 4, the last of our Chief Executives to enter upon office on that date.

ELECTORS MEET AS A NATIONAL BODY 

The Presidential electors met as a national body for the first time in history in 1933, when Franklin D. Roosevelt was inaugurated 

President, and John Nance Garner, Vice President. Michael Francis

Doyle, of Philadelphia, who was to serve as the first chairman and later as first permanent president of the electoral college, had presented to several of Mr. Roosevelt's advisers in 1932 a suggestion that the Presidential electors should meet and organize as a body at the forthcoming inauguration. Mr. Roosevelt later approved this suggestion. Accordingly, the Democratic electors chosen by the States. carried by Roosevelt and Garner received a call for a meeting of the electors to be held in Washington on March 4, 1933, for the purpose of organizing as a national body and participating in the inauguration on that date.

On March 3, 1933, the evening prior to the inauguration, the electors gathered at a dinner at the Hotel Willard in Washington. The members of the new Cabinet, which had been announced on that date, attended as well as Members of the Senate and House of Representatives and leading Democrats who were in Washington for the inauguration. At the first meeting the precedent was established for the calling of the roll of the 48 States and announcing the electoral vote of those States by the electors. At the conclusion of the roll call the total number of votes was announced, and the candidate receiving the majority of electoral votes was declared to be President of the United States. The same procedure was followed with respect to the office of Vice President. This count was conducted by officials appointed by the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House of Representatives, the final announcement being made by Col. Edwin A.

Halsey, Secretary of the Senate. This followed the form of the official ceremony of the election conducted by the joint meeting of the Senate and House of Representatives. 

On the following morning the electors met in the office of the Vice President, at the Capitol, and after electing the first chairman of the body, they approved a program which was intended to be a precedent for future inaugurations. The plan provided for the attendance and participation of electors at the inaugural ceremonies at the Capitol, where they were to be seated with other prominent participants in a specially assigned section. Arrangements were made for the electors to lead the civic section of the inaugural parade from the Capitol to the White House for review by the President and Vice President. Immediately after the parade the President and 

Vice President would hold their first reception at the White House, and the electors would be honored for the first time as a national body. 

The program thus arranged was carried out in detail and subsequently a committee of electors was chosen to continue plans for the next inauguration.

At the inauguration beginning the second term of President Roosevelt and Vice President Garner on January 20, 1937, the electors' program followed that of 1933. 

In 1940 a departure from former rules was taken when the electors' committee decided to invite all electors, chosen by their representative States, to participate in the 1941 inauguration, irrespective of

party affiliation. In the 1940 election Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected for a third term, a choice unique in American political history. 

At the meeting of the electors held on January 20, 1941, Michael Francis Doyle was elected the first permanent president of the electoral college and authorized to appoint the necessary committee of electors to participate in future inaugural ceremonies.

In the Presidential election of 1944, in the midst of the Second 

World War, Franklin D. Roosevelt was reelected President for a fourth term in that office. Harry S. Truman, of Missouri, was elected Vice President. The electors met in Washington on January 

20, 1945, the inaugural day fixed by law. All arrangements for the inauguration had been curtailed because of the war situation, and the electors' program accordingly was reduced. However, the Presidential electors' dinner was held at the Mayflower Hotel on January 19, 1945, attended by nearly 400 electors, including 17 Republican electors. Mrs. Roosevelt attended, as well as Henry A. Wallace, Vice

President of the United States, Harry S. Truman, the newly elected 

Vice President, and many members of the Cabinet, of the United States Senate, and Members of the House of Representatives. It was further honored by the presence of Justices of the Supreme Court, and chiefs of the armed services, including General Marshall, General Vandegrift, and General Arnold, and also the chiefs of the 

Navy, Admiral Leahy, Admiral King, and Admiral Waesche. In all, more than 1,500 distinguished Americans attended the fourth electors' dinner. The dinner was preceded by a procession of military and Navy heroes of the war, including all service branches, carrying the flags of the 48 States. The roll call of the States was conducted by the representatives of the Senate and House of Representatives, and the customary announcement was made of the election of the 

President and Vice President.

On January 20, 1945, the date of the inauguration, the electors attended a reception in their honor at the White House where they were received by President and Mrs. Roosevelt, and Vice President and Mrs. Truman. At the conclusion of the reception an official 

Presidential flag was presented to Mr. Roosevelt on behalf of the electors as a symbol of his reelection to the office of President. 

The electors at a later meeting named a committee to make necessary preliminary arrangements for the inauguration in 1949 in order that the participation of the electoral college in the ceremony of inauguration might continue to symbolize the will of the national electorate in its choice of national officers.
