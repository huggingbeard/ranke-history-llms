I. PURPOSE OF THIS HANDBOOK AN INFORMAL METHOD 

There is an advantage in teaching composition without a textbook in the hands of the children: the teacher can discuss a composition informally with the class in such a way that the children think the ideas are their own and not imposed upon them by the teacher. The teacher starts a conversation on a subject she wishes to use for a composition, and by drawing the children into the discussion and inducing them to state their views, she sets up a situation in which the children assign themselves compositions. To them the project is not an assignment, but something they have decided to do on their own initiative.

Although the children seem to be carrying on the activity themselves, the real motivating and guiding force is the teacher. 

In the following pages are helps for the teacher in this rather difficult but fascinating work. First there is an outline for each semester in the seventh and eighth grades, with illustrative compositions actually written by children in these grades. Then there is a section on methods of conducting the composition period, basic principles of speaking and writing for children, pitfalls to be avoided, and suggestions for securing good results. 

HOW TO USE THE COURSE 

Using the Situation of the Moment. The ideal way to teach composition probably would be to use the situation of the moment to take for the subject matter of each week's exercise a recent happening. However, this practice can be followed only part of the time. In the first place, some weeks are very routine affairs, and only a genius could find good composition material in them. In the second place, in schools where children progress from one teacher to another there is bound to be considerable repetition and confusion if each teacher is doing just what she pleases without reference to others in the building. 

Therefore it is best to have an outline or course of study that 1

provides a progressive plan, with certain general assignments for each week. Such a plan is set forth in the following pages. 

A teacher using this course should feel free to shift assignments within a semester to take advantage of any events that occur. She should bear in mind that it is usually better to use a timely situation for subject matter rather than to use the subjects given with the assignment. Those given here are merely suggestive to use if nothing better comes to light during the discussion. 

Apportionment of Time. One composition each week carefully done is better than several poorly done. A good apportionment of time for a course in English is to use two fifths for composition, two fifths for grammar and punctuation drill, and one fifth for spelling and vocabulary drill. This does not mean, necessarily, that two days of each week will be devoted to composition, two to grammar, and one to spelling and vocabulary. Various schools have different ways of distributing the time during the week, but these proportions for the semester have been found to give good results. Composing periods should be separate from skills periods, though the children should be required to use skills they have acquired when these are needed in a composition.

The Illustrative Compositions. Frequently when teachers want to illustrate a point during the assignment period, they use models from standard literature, from Dickens or Robert 

Louis Stevenson or some other recognized author. Except occasionally, these are hardly suitable for seventh- and eighthgrade children, who, if they are not completely bewildered by the mature thought and phraseology of these selections, try to graft the adult standards on to their own youthful impressions and observations, and as a result turn out a poor imitation of the literary master. Children should be themselves. They should write on their own kind of subjects and in their own manner.

For this reason a teacher should save compositions written by children to use as illustrations with later classes. However, it takes time to collect a body of such material, and a few children's compositions have been included in this course of study to help a teacher who has not yet saved any of her own.

II. A  IN ORAL AND WRITTEN COMPOSITION SEVENTH GRADE, FIRST SEMESTER 

Aims. The child should learn to compose a simple paragraph and to express it in clear, complete sentences devoid of gross grammatical errors. The paragraph he learns to compose should consist of from four to eight sentences about one incident or one small topic, and he should know that frequently the first sentence gives the subject of the paragraph. He should know how to write a friendly letter and an informal note of invitation or thanks.

In giving an oral composition, the child should stand in a straight but easy position before the class, with head up, eyes upon his audience, and hands quiet enough so that their activity, if any, does not distract attention from what he is saying. Nowadays most children come up from the elementary grades with such good habits of posture and speech before a group of their classmates that it is the seventh-grade teacher's duty merely to foster these and to prevent the growth of self-consciousness. A special drive should be made against the and-a habit. This coördinates with the drive against run-on sentences in written composition. An attempt should be made to eliminate the double subject John he. Enunciation must be distinct. The chief aim, however, is to achieve fluency and ease of expression before an audience.

In written composition the child should understand how a paragraph must look and should employ separate paragraphs for the words of different speakers. He should be commended for the use of a new and vivid word, but one must not emphasize vocabulary too much, for the chief aim in written composition is the same as that for oral composition and if one is to achieve it one must not pay too much attention to other matters. The special drive in form is against the run-on sentence; coördinated with this are Exercises 1 to 14 and 34 to 37 in Junior

A NARROW ESCAPE

Bob and I were caught in the rain at Island Park. We stood under the tree by the bridge. When the rain stopped for a few minutes we ran to the pavilion. There were other boys there and we watched the rain. Suddenly there was a flash and a big noise. After a few seconds we smelt something burn't. When the rain stopped we ran over to the place where the noise had been. To our surprise we found the tree we had stood under had been struck by lightning. 

CHEWING GUM 

On the boat on which I came from Europe were many Americans. 

One thing that puzzled me about them was that they were always moving their mouths. At last my curiosity made me ask an American lady who could speak my language what this meant. She smiled and replied, "Oh we are only chewing gum. People can't enter the United 

States without chewing gum, you know."

When I heard this I bought a half a dozen packages of gum. Now never in my life had I chewed gum. I put five pieces in my mouth and began chewing them at the same time. As I was trying to stick another piece in my mother came in. She told me to throw the gum away. Here the hardest job for me began because I wouldn't throw the gum away and it was hard for me to explain because my mouth was full. At last I had to obey. I then told my mother why I was chewing gum, but she only laughed. I was greatly worried because

A CLIMB IN THE WATER TOWER 

One day as my cousin and I were playing ball in the water tower yard we noticed that the lock was off the door. We went and told some other boys about it. First week: An oral descriptive paragraph 

A little practice in giving a description helps to train the child's powers of observation. A description should make the audience see and feel emotionally the thing described. It must be short and must be concentrated upon the salient features of the subject. The details chosen should bring out one aspect of the subject in such a way that a unified and clear impression will be the result. The child should stick to one point of view. 

He should weed out scattering and irrelevant additions to an already finished picture; they only confuse and spoil it. 

Explain that the description must achieve its effect through the use of vivid and specific words. General and inapt expressions are wasteful and blur the effect. The word used should give the exact meaning wanted. There is rarely a place in any sentence for which two words are equally good. Adjectives should be used sparingly; most children pile on too many adjectives because they associate adjectives with description. 

A lasting impression can be made by showing how the subject affects those who come in contact with it. It is not always enough to tell what an object looks like. The enduring image stays in our minds because the author has shown the emotional effect of the subject upon persons who saw it. The deserted house seems lonelier because the author tells how it lowered his spirits and made him gloomy. A beautiful woman appears lovelier if we hear how she bewitched everyone who knew her.

This is a subtlety skillfully used by the great masters of fiction, but children can understand it. Examples of it on a simple scale may be found in "My First School Teacher" and "My

One should choose a time for calling that is likely to be convenient for the person he wants to speak to; he should not call at mealtime or late at night. One should make sure of the number he wants, in order to avoid delay, but chiefly to prevent inconvenience for someone who would be obliged to answer the telephone for a wrong number. He must speak distinctly.

The caller should be the one to make all advances. Now and

then we answer the telephone to be asked abruptly, "Who is this?" This is the height of rudeness on the part of the person calling. He is putting someone to the trouble of answering the telephone and therefore should initiate the conversation by telling who he is and asking by name for the person he wishes to talk to. If his call is worth making at all he should not be ashamed to give his name at once.

However, considerable delay can be avoided if the person who answers the telephone says at once, "Smiths' residence."

This practice is followed by business houses. When a certain woman answers the telephone in her home, she always says, "Mrs. Sorenson speaking," and all who know her bless her for it. She is a gentlewoman who thinks of what will be convenient for the other person.

Warn the children that no one should ever be the bore who calls up a friend and says, "Well, you can't guess who this is," and then goes on to make the person he has called guess his name. In such a case it would seem that the person called has a right to answer, "No, I'm afraid I can't," and hang up the receiver.

Of course no one should talk on one call more than a few minutes. Others may wish to use the line and he should think of their rights. Even if he thinks he has a right to talk as long as he pleases because he himself has a private line, he must remember that probably the person to whom he is talking does not have one.

Second week: Definitions 

This work in defining is put here to give practice in clear and exact thinking. It is an attempt to show children how inaccurate their usual way of defining is "Treason is when you do something against the government," "A ballot is when you vote" and to give them a correct method. 

Begin by explaining that almost everything belongs in a class or family of things similar to itself. Give several examples: a table is a piece of furniture; a policeman is an officer; a coat is a garment; a club is an association; a spade is a tool; a voyage is a journey; honesty is a quality. Then ask each child to write on a slip of paper the class to which each of the following articles belongs: (1) a glove; (2) a piano; (3) a geranium or some familiar plant, preferably one that is in the classroom at the time; (4) a bottle; (5) a robin. It is hoped that the children

will give the answers like this: (1) covering for the hand; (2) musical instrument; (3) a plant; (4) a container or vessel; (5) a bird.

Then say, "Well, is this a good definition, ‘A table is a piece of furniture'? Does that tell what a table is? From that definition would you know a table if you saw one?"

Probably someone will say, "No, there are lots of pieces of furniture that aren't tables chairs and desks and bookcases and others." "Oh," you say, "then perhaps our definition isn't finished. It isn't enough in defining an object merely to put it in its family or class." Then go on to show how the object, after it has been placed in its class, must also be distinguished from others in that class. Use as an analogy a family — the Wilson family, for example. The name Wilson sets apart this group from other families the Smiths, the Browns, the Johnsons - but in the Wilson group there are individuals; the Wilsons are not all alike; each has his distinguishing characteristics and his own first name. You may say, "The family name of table is furniture, but its private name that sets it off from other furniture is table. Now you must add something to your definition of a table that will help us to know a table from other furniture." Here write on the board, "A table is a piece of furniture that ." and leave the space after that blank. Let the children try to finish the sentence by finding a brief description that will fit a table. They will undoubtedly need help. You will have to lead them a little in formulating something like this: "A table is a piece of furniture that has a flat top supported by one or more legs." Then take one of the other words, like policeman or coat. 

Put the first part of the definition, that placing it in a class, on the blackboard, and let the children try to complete the sentence. Do this with as many words as you have time for.

HOW TO RAISE GUINEA PIGS 

The first thing to do is to get a large box that has room enough for twelve guinea pigs. Take off all the boards on one end so that a screen can be put on. Rats will kill the guinea pigs if there is a hole in the box through which the rats can reach them. Next put about three inches of straw or hay into the cage. Clean the cage at least once a week. 

Feed the guinea pigs oatmeal and water three times a day, at morning, noon, and night. You should also feed them carrots, grass, cabbage, and beet tops. Put the oatmeal and water in separate pans because if you don't the guinea pigs will put their feet first into the water and then into the oatmeal and this will make the oatmeal no good.

In the summer time take the cage of guinea pigs outside or get a screen large enough to make a circle into which you can put them so that they can eat grass.

How To Row A BOAT 

Would you like to know how to row a boat? I will tell you how.

You will have to row with one oar first, so it is advisable to bring a companion and an expert rower along with you. Put your oar in the oarlock and you are ready to start. You will find that by pushing the handle of the oar down you will raise the outer end of the oar. 

After you have raised this end of the oar, put your arms at full length before you and bend forward slightly. Next dip the oar into the water just a few inches below the surface. Then pull the oar through the water till you are sitting erect with your hands near your chest. 

Do this over and over again and you will find that the boat will move through the water. Of course the person who has the oar on the other side does just what you are doing. You should not hurry, but work steadily and evenly or you will just do a lot of splashing and the boat will not move.

There are several different fashions of rowing, but this is the easiest for the beginner.

Third week: A paragraph of argument stating politely the writer's reasons for upholding a statement It is not intended that any training in argument shall be given before this paragraph is written. The plan is to let the children find out through writing this paragraph what argument is. 

Each child must choose a subject on which he has a real opinion. 

Then he must present reasons, preferably no less than three, which uphold his contention. Explain that he will not be able to persuade anyone to his way of thinking unless he has good reasons and expresses them in clear sentences.

Not all paragraphs require topic sentences, but a paragraph of argument does. It should begin with a frank statement of the writer's belief, such as "I believe that there should be another playground on the south side of town." Following this should come the reasons, each stated in a separate sentence. 

A concluding sentence is almost necessary.

WHY A PEDESTRIAN SHOULD WALK ON THE LEFT SIDE OF THE ROAD 

A pedestrian should always walk on the left side of the road in the country because it is much safer. Vehicles all travel on the right side of the road. If you walk on the right side you may not hear or see a vehicle coming from behind and be run over. When you walk on the left side you can see a car coming in front of you and get out of its way. When you are walking on the left-hand side and see a car coming you should step to the left and off the concreate. Don't step to the right-hand side because you will get in the way of automobiles approaching from behind and will be hit. When you come to a crossing first look left then right, and be very careful to watch the vehicles turning into the road you are on.

Each child may give an individual talk, or two or three children may give a talk in relays. Third week: A written dialogue between two persons

It should be the aim in these dialogues to secure natural, vivid conversation.

One difficulty of the children in writing dialogues or little plays is that they do not realize that on the stage time cannot simply pass; something must be happening while it passes. 

Sometimes in a dialogue consisting of one or two pages a child will mean to cover two hours. He must be warned that two pages of written dialogue represent only five or six minutes in time.

Another difficulty is that in a dialogue which is to be given in a classroom or on a stage the scene must remain stationary. 

Children have a tendency to start a dialogue in one location and then, with no change of scene indicated, have their characters wind up the conversation in an entirely different place. 

Even in a movie scenario, which uses more rapid shifts of scene than a stage play, such changes would be indicated as separate scenes.

These dialogues are to be written with possible presentation in mind, and the teacher should warn the children of these two pitfalls. Tell them that each must think of his dialogue and action as taking place on a stage before his eyes, and he must not put anything in that he could not hear and see in that circumscribed place and time.

OUR ISLAND 

Out in the river there is an island where one boy lost a dollar. We looked for it but couldn't find it that day. The next day when we went out there again to look for it we started something which came to be an adventure.

One boy found a saw and a hammer and some nails and we decided to make a raft so that we could cross over to the island and look for the dollar. We sawed down trees and before long we had a good raft big enough to hold four boys. We poled ourselves out to the island. 

Then we knew that since we had the raft we could go out to the island whenever we wanted to. 

That day we decided to name this island "Treasure Island." Each boy had to make a promise that he would not tell anybody of our island. It had a big tree in the center, and around the east side the water was shallow. On the west side was a cliff in which there was a big cave where we dryed our clothes. The island had a wide stream of water flowing around it so that people couldn't get there unless they had a boat or a raft.

On the east side we hid a treasure which contained a knife, saw, hammer, ring, bell, mouth organ, shoe horn, shoe hook, some beeds, shells, and nails. One morning when we came there we found that the treasure had been flooded by the river. We waited until the river lowered. Then we dug the treasure up and put it in the cave. I think it is there yet. I can't go out there any more because one boy drowned there. My father won't let me go to the island and the fathers of the other boys won't let them go either. Many years from now someone may find our treasure and wonder who put it there. 

MY FIRST RECITATION 

I'll never forget my first real recitation in public. It was at a May 

Day festival and I was to recite a poem called "Apple Blossoms." I

can't remember the poem, but I know at the time I thought it very silly, a mere jumble of words that rambled on and on saying the same thing over and over again. Miss King, my teacher, had appointed 

Nelse Soderland to sit behind the piano with a copy of the poem to prompt me if I forgot any of it.

The big evening arrived at last. I was dressed all in white and was supposed to carry some apple blossoms, but as it was too early in the season for them I carried cherry blossoms instead. I was fifth on the program. I heard my name called and walked on the stage. 

When I got there and saw all the people I could not remember the first line. I glanced at Nelse and he began to read, loudly and distinctly so that everyone could hear. Some boys in the front row began to giggle and the Adults smiled.

Nelse having read the first line I was able to go on quite well until I came to the last verse. I absolutely could not remember it. I stopped and glanced appealingly toward the alcove behind the piano and horrors of horrors, Nelse was not there. I did not know what to do but I knew I must think fast so shaking from head to foot I began to recite the first verse over again. I got through it and walked off the stage to my seat where I sat down as low as possible so that no one might see me. I was congratulated on my splendid recitation by several people and was told that no one who had not read the poem before noticed my error.

HOW TO CLEAN SOILED CLOTHES 

Dusty or muddy garments should be shaken out of doors and then brushed with a clothes brush. It is better to let splashes of mud dry on the material and then remove them with a hard brush. Be sure to brush with the nap.

For removing grease spots one should apply a few drops of gasoline. 

When the spot is dried it may be removed by moistening it with warm water and soap. Benzine is also a good grease remover. Another method is to place a piece of blotting paper or porous brown paper over the spot and press with a hot iron moving the paper every now and then until the grease is absorbed.

Black cloth is refreshened by sponging with cold tea or ammonia diluted with water. Hang the cloth in the air to dry. 

To remove dirt marks rub them with a rag dipped in gasoline, but this must not be used near a light.

In removing ink spots rub with milk or salt. If the spot is old, the best method is to soap it and lay a little salts of lemon or salts of sorrel on it. The salts should not be rubbed in or a hole may be made in the material.

HOW TO BUILD A FIRE IN THE OPEN 

Fire is one of man's most useful servants, but it is also a hard master. 

Millions of dollars a year are lost because some people are too careless to put a fire out or too ignorant to build a fire correctly. 

The most essential thing about building a fire in the open is the location. The best location is a large flat rock about waist high, for in such a place it is impossible for the fire to do any damage. However, if such a rock cannot be found, clear a spot about two yards in diameter. 

Be sure that the spot is clear of dry grass, dead leaves, and twigs.

The materials needed are a knife or hatchet, matches or other equipment with which to light the fire, and some dry wood. 

First take a small piece of dry wood and with your knife make some little chips. These are to start the fire, as they burn easily. Then with the hatchet make some larger pieces to put on later. Now pile the small chips so that they form a pyramid. Leave a small space on the windward side of the pile. Next apply the match to the chips in the space left on the windward side. When the fire has a good start on the chips, put some larger pieces of wood on. In a little while you will have a good fire. 

It is important that you put the fire out. If you do not, you may start a forest fire. To put out the fire pour water on it until the last spark is gone, or cover it with dirt. Serve your country by saving its forests. Save your forests by building fires correctly. 

HOW CHICKENS ARE HATCHED 

To watch the hatching of young chickens is very interesting. The hen must sit about three weeks before the chickens hatch. She very seldom leaves the nest and would begin to cluck if you came near without her mistress or master, for she is anxious about the safety of her young.

The first sign of hatching is a crack on the egg. That shows that the little chicken inside wants to see what the world is like. It takes nearly a week before he is ready to come out. He pecks around the egg and the heavy outer shell falls off and at last just the thin white shell is all that remains where he has pecked. Often when the chicken gets this far he can go no farther and will die unless you help him. 

That is, you must take the top part of the egg half way off so that he can come out. When a chicken is newly hatched his feathers are damp and his legs are too weak to hold his body. It is best to take him from the nest and put him in a box which has something warm in it, such as an old sweater with a hot water bottle hidden somewhere in the folds. At the end of three or four hours his feathers are dry and his legs stronger.

Second week: A written news item 

A news item is quite different from a story. In a story the climax comes at the end; in a news story it comes at the beginning and the details follow, the most important first and the lesser at the end. In the first paragraph, preferably in the first sentence, the writer should tell the who, when, where, and, briefly, the why of his story. The essential quality of all news-writing is accuracy. We read newspapers to be informed, and no newspaper can continue in existence unless the public has confidence in the truth of what it contains. A good reporter must get the facts and be able to state them with precision. Obviously he is more valuable to his paper if he has the ability to write in an interesting, original way, but he must not develop an unusual individual style at the expense of accuracy and clearness. III.  IN COMPOSITION ESSENTIAL PRINCIPLES 

The first requisite of a good composition is that it be interesting. It should make a worth-while contribution to the group for which it was composed, which is generally the other members of the class.

Choice of Subject. Choosing the right subject does more than anything else to produce interesting composition. The child must write or speak on something in which he himself is interested; he must have a subject with which he feels "at home." Such a subject is concrete, is suited to the child's age and mental grasp, and is based upon or is related to his own personal experience. Composition should be drawn from the personal experiences of the children because (1) children have much information from this source, (2) compositions on personal subjects are usually natural and full of life, and (3) the compositions will be varied, for every individual has had a different experience. If the composition is a story, the child can tell an incident that happened to him or in which he had some part. 

If it is an explanation, he can tell how to make something that he himself knows how to make, or explain a process that he has observed and understands.

Some children may think they have had no experiences worth telling. The teacher can encourage them by relating an experience of her own, or something she has heard, similar to the material for the composition she wants. Another way is to let the brighter and livelier children give their compositions first. These will remind the more backward children of experiences they can tell.

Adapting the Theme Subject to Individual Differences. 

The teacher should weave the discussion around a general subject that each child can narrow to fit his individual experience. 

The principle is this: The teacher gives a subject broad enough to allow for individual differences but still following one line 94

For example, a teacher may wish to assign a little story on "Something I Did That Was Hard but Worth While." She may begin by saying rather casually, "Have you ever in your life done anything that seemed difficult while you were doing it but which gave you great satisfaction when you had it finished?" 

Or she may begin by saying, "Once I was told I must . . ." and go on to tell an incident of this sort that actually happened to her. Or, "The other day I heard of a boy. . .' . ." and tell a story that she has heard. Then she may say, "Do you suppose that people in everyday life—not great writers and painters and inventors, for we know this happens to them - frequently work hard at something and are very happy when it turns out well?"

The children may comment on this, or, if the teacher is lucky, they may begin at once without further inducement to mention instances of this kind that have happened to them. If they don't follow the latter course immediately, the teacher may go on, "Have you ever had anything like this happen to you?”

He can hardly write a good composition on "Trees," but he could probably write a creditable paragraph on "The Apple Tree in Our Yard." He should be taught to focus his attention on one phase of his subject and to leave out

all irrelevant material. This quality is not the same as that of brevity, which will be discussed later. This has to do purely with choice of subject.

I had fished all day and had had only a few unimportant nibbles.

Spontaneity. As has been said, the first ingredient in the composing situation is a subject that stimulates the child's powers of observation and imagination and touches his life intimately. The second is an atmosphere in which he feels easy "at home." The vigor of the children's response is usually in direct ratio to the ingenuity and sympathy of the teacher in this respect. The individual teacher must be alert to influences that will produce the desired atmosphere. She herself must be a real person, alive, interested, and unforced in her manner. A ready smile and a sense of humor will help greatly.

Too much stress laid on form will make children so conscious of every word they are saying that compositon will be stilted. 

Correct-usage drills should not be given during the assignment period. They should be held at some other time, and done so well that children will use the correct form naturally in composition. Then if a child slips and makes a mistake with some form he has been taught, his attention should be called to it when criticisms are given; if the composition is oral he may be asked to say the expression correctly.

There is danger in emphasizing vocabulary too much. The

Working on vocabulary as a part of a composition is likely to take the life out of the composition. Of course, if a child uses an apt expression, the teacher should call attention to it when the compositions are read aloud - that is, after they have been composed and show how it has given strength and color to his conception.

Brevity. Nearly 90 per cent of the compositions written by seventh- and eighth-grade children should be of paragraph length. If we can help children to acquire excellence in the small units, the longer units will not be difficult later, but even as adults we seldom write long papers or make long talks. Few children have anything worth saying that will take more than one or two paragraphs.

Moreover, when children write more than a paragraph or two, errors are likely to creep in profusely. It is interesting to note that in a composition of several pages, or of five or six paragraphs, a child will be able to keep the first page practically free from mistakes, but as he reaches the end of the second page errors in spelling, punctuation, and sentence structure begin to multiply. Children of this age do not have the capacity for sustained effort in composition. What is the value of three or four pages full of rambling thoughts and mistakes in mechanics?

Certainly time has been ill spent. Why not have children compose small, excellent pieces instead of long, faulty ones?

First, have children choose a small subject by concentrating upon one incident or a single phase of an incident. Then teach them to weed out repetition and wordiness. Children can be shown how they defeat their object by sprawling mentally and 'verbally. They can learn to be neat in expression and to make structure clear and compact.

Firmness concerning Requirements. Though the method of making the assignment seems so casual, it is not an invitation to loaf. For each assignment every child in the class should be required to compose something, doing the best work of which he is capable. If someone hangs back after he has been given suggestions and other help, the teacher should be firm with him. 

There is no excuse for not doing any composition suggested in this handbook. If some child is not prepared, he may be required to come to the teacher's room before the next session of the class and prepare something there. Only rarely should one be obliged to resort to this measure. Ordinarily if the methods described here are used, the children are ready and eager. 

CLASSROOM PROCEDURE IN ORAL COMPOSITION 

The English classroom is a laboratory. In it the children should experiment, criticize themselves and others, and revise.

PROCEDURE IN ORAL COMPOSITION 

101 If the situation is right they should experience the joy of creating. How can the laboratory method be used to advantage in oral composition?

Objectives for Oral Composition. The teacher must keep her objectives in mind. Oral composition is not the ordinary giveand-take of discussion. It is a composition, planned and expressed with care, but not memorized. Oral composition offers opportunity for developing fluency and poise. It is well adapted to illustrating excellences and defects as a preliminary to written composition. It brings out the grosser everyday faults of speech that need to be weeded out first. Although oral English fails to show up some bad habits of thought and expression, it is a commoner medium of expression than writing, and because of its very universality deserves a great deal of attention. 

Time of Assignment. A method of making the assignment has already been sketched in the discussion of subjects. Sometimes the assignment can be made at the beginning of a class period and the resulting compositions presented immediately, but for a subject that requires the gathering of information, such as reports, explanations, and travel talks, the assignment should be made at least the day before. Indeed, with all kinds of composition better results are likely to be obtained if the assignment is made the day before. If a composition is assigned more than two days in advance, the children lose interest before it can be given. Their enthusiasm has been raised to a high pitch during the assignment, but if too much time elapses before they can put their plans into effect, their zeal dies down and you may find that on the day the compositions are to be given the children are unprepared.

A Program of Essentials. For the first oral composition of the semester, work out through discussion a basic program of essentials. Write its main points briefly on the blackboard. 

This program can be reviewed for later oral talks, and when a special objective is needed for a composition, it can be added to the basic program. The oral program should be founded upon the following principles: 

1. Was this composition interesting as to subject, details, and manner of telling? 

2. Did it stick to the point?

3. Did the details lead up to a climax, or at least to a clear conclusion in narration? Did the details fuse into a rounded whole in description and explanation? Did it give one effect? 

4. Did it plunge immediately into the subject, or could some introductory matter have been left out? 

5. Did the ideas follow each other in natural order? 

6. Were the sentences clear? Or were they careless and full of slipshod expressions and repetition? Was the English correct? 

7. Did the speaker stand erect, look at his audience, and speak distinctly and naturally?

Explain why erect posture and a direct glance at the audience help an oral talk. A slouching attitude and downcast eyes are usually the accompaniments of indistinct and stumbling speech and rambling, tiresome subject matter. A child who stands straight and looks confidently at his audience is likely not only to hold their attention by his mere presence, but also to give an interesting, clear, and well-connected paragraph. 

Prevision. After the assignment the next step is the prevision of ideas, or planning. If the assignment is made the day before the composition is to be given, much of this prevision will be done by the children outside of class. Each one should turn his subject over in his mind and search for better details than he had available at his first inspiration. In the classroom, just before the compositions are given, about five minutes should be allowed for final prevision. During this time the children just sit and think-first, of what they want to tell; second, in what order and how they want to tell it. Then the teacher calls for volunteers or names someone to begin. It is usually best to have the most original and capable students give their compositions first. They are the pace-setters; they inspire the slower ones.

Criticism by the Class. After the speaker has finished, he should remain standing in his place before the class while his classmates who have anything to say about his talk raise their hands or rise. He should call on them in turn to state their criticisms. He may admit that their comments are just, or if he considers the criticism unjust, he can defend himself briefly. Most of the criticisms should be based on the fundamental qualities that have been decided upon previously. 

Difficulties with Oral Composition. One fault teachers find with oral composition is that at first the compositions are rambling and discouragingly full of grammatical errors. Here is a good reason for spending time and effort upon oral work. 

Another drawback, some say, is that with classes as large as we have in most schools nowadays probably three days are required if every child in the class is to give his composition, and surely everyone should be given this opportunity. This is not a great difficulty. If the assignment period has been well spent there will be a variety of subjects and therefore the compositions will not become monotonous. Experience has shown that children want to be original; they will hate to duplicate each other's subject matter. If the time allotments in the course of study are followed, it will be possible to give three days to an oral composition.

After an assignment for a written composition has been made, suggest that each child sit quietly and plan in his mind the general organization and beginning sentence of his composition. 

As the assignment discussion has gone on, the minds of some children may have leaped ahead so that they are ready to write at once, but on the whole better results are obtained if children can be induced to refrain from writing until they have deliberately made a mental prevision.

The First Transcription. After several minutes spent in getting his subject matter well in hand, the child should take paper and pencil and write the first draft of his story. This

transcription should be rapid in order to catch the ideas and the spirit of the thing while they are fresh in his mind. 

Perhaps the first day's work ends here. Frequently it is a good plan to reach this point by the end of the class period, so that the composition can "cool" until the next day. However, if there is still some time the children can be told to revise their work. Each child reads his story to see where it can be improved. He crosses out superfluous sentences or parts of sentences, cuts down or cuts out entirely his introduction, takes care that the progression of events is natural and clear, rearranges sentences or word order, finds a more suitable or a more vivid word. This done, he goes over his story once more to proofread it for errors in the mechanics - spelling, punctuation, and so forth. If the end of the class period has been reached with the completion of the first transcription, the revision will be left to the next day.

Some Points about Revision. It is remarkable how faults in a composition stand out if twenty-four hours intervene between the first draft and the final revision. The second day the child can look at his work with detachment; he is no longer flushed with the heat of composing. The best means of finding faults in a composition is to read it aloud. The second day's work should begin with the reading of as many stories as time will permit five or six at least, and a few more if possible. After a child has read, members of the class and the teacher can suggest ways of improving his story. Of course the children who have an opportunity to read their stories aloud are more fortunate than the others, but those who merely listen can benefit from the criticisms given to the few, as usually faults common in that type of composition are brought out. The privilege of reading aloud should be passed around during the semester so that everyone has his turn at receiving help. 

Final Copy. After a final revision the children are ready to copy their stories in ink. The pencil copy will probably contain many corrections, with words crossed out and others written above, phrases and whole sentences transposed. Show the children that revision is worth while, that in every activity in life a man tries to appear at his best, even though he may spend hours in private working to achieve a good effect before his public appearance. Now and then hold up as a good example

105 the pencil copy of someone who has made many revisions. It is possible to find in books the facsimile of a manuscript by Robert Louis Stevenson or some other artist. A facsimile of a page by Rudyard Kipling can be found in The Americanization of Edward Bok. Stress the fact that clearness and accuracy pay. Excellent ideas make no impression unless they are effectively expressed.

After the copy in ink has been made, urge a final proofreading for possible oversights, especially in punctuation.

Correction by the Class. In all criticism and correction the principal thing is to stimulate self-criticism. The teacher should comment always as a friendly guide, not as one looking for trouble. Compare work done in the current composition with good work the child has done earlier. Praise him for improvement, or if he hasn't come up to his previous standard of excellence, show him why.

Perhaps once or twice in a semester, not oftener in seventhand eighth-grade classes, the children should exchange compositions and mark each other's work under the teacher's direction. 

An exercise of this sort helps the child to develop his own critical faculty and brings home to him what the teacher looks for when she corrects his compositions at other times. Each student should first read the composition he has and score it for thought, or subject matter. He will look for interest, a feeling of life or reality, and general effectiveness. Then he will read for errors in sentence structure, spelling, capitalization, and punctuation, indicating these as the teacher has previously directed, and will give a score on them.

However, such an exercise is not easy to handle. A teacher seldom has great success the first time she tries correction by the class, but she should learn by her mistakes. It is best not to try it until you have skimmed through that set of compositions yourself to see whether you think they are adapted to this treatment. See that good students are given the themes of the poorer students, and let weak students mark the themes of their more capable classmates. Give clear directions beforehand and then insist that each child take his time and use the best judgment he can muster without bombarding you with questions; put him on his mettle to do his own job well. Allow the children to refer to dictionaries and other sources of informa-

tion. You may feel that the results of this exercise are not perfect, but infrequently held it is valuable. 

The Teacher's Marking. All other compositions should be read and marked by the teacher as soon after they are handed in as she can do it, at least before the class writes another one. 

A child should have his mistakes pointed out to him specifically before he has a chance to make them again.

There is no royal road to composition marking. It is something an English teacher has to do and there is no way of avoiding it, although the short compositions called for in this course of study will not prove a great burden. However, the work can be made pleasanter than it often is. First, don't start to read compositions in the attitude that it is drudgery. 

Believe that you are going to find something interesting or original or amusing in that pile of papers. Second, you will have saved yourself time and trouble in marking compositions if you have pointed out the worst pitfalls to the children when you gave the assignment, though of course all these cannot be anticipated. Third, don't mark every mistake on the paper of a child who has made many. That is tiresome for you and puts you in a disagreeable frame of mind, and it is very discouraging to the child. If a pupil has made many mistakes, mark only the most glaring. That is all the correction he can absorb at one time. Fourth, do not mark compositions too long at one time. If you do so, you cannot react as freshly as you should.

Make your criticisms as definite and personal as possible. 

A sentence or two of approbation or helpful comment written at the end means more than an A with no specific criticism. 

Such a sentence lets a child know why he has received an A or a D and thus opens a road to his future improvement. 

It would be interesting sometime to try for a whole semester the experiment of giving no standings at all on compositions, simply writing individual sentence comments on them. Of course for the sake of report cards it would be necessary to have some record of the child's work. This could be secured by keeping account of the number of compositions he has written and his improvement, and estimating his standing in proportion to his degree of improvement.

Which of these ratings shall be the deciding factor in promoting the child to a higher grade? Here is a child who writes original and delightful composition full of misspelled words and other errors. Shall we promote him? Surely we should encourage his talent. A sensible statement concerning this problem was formulated by a committee of English teachers of the Wisconsin State Teachers Association: 

Averaging such a double grade is not advisable; form, the common decencies of speech and writing, is, in no sense, to be considered as equal in value to content and organization. Society demands certain

66 decencies in language as it does in manners. An individual is not rated by his manners, but he is penalized for lack of them. Fundamental language forms have a similar negative value; the individual who says

He did the work" calls no attention to himself, but the person who says "He done the work" is instantly branded as illiterate. 

Because these few fundamental forms of spelling, of punctuation, and of grammar and usage are so vital to the present and future success of the pupil, it may be said that a pupil's final grade in a composition course probably cannot rise above the form level he finally achieves.¹

This is the principle, but what is one to do specifically with the rare child who seems hopeless in regard to mechanics but who has excellent ideas? First, make him aware of his problem and show him that there is no help for him unless he will study how to help himself. Insist that at least he carry a pocket dictionary. Second, during one semester make a drive to teach him a few fundamental things. Concentrate on some ordinarily easy, common words that he habitually misspells. Try to teach him a few basic sentence and punctuation rules, even though the rest of the class is learning some that are more advanced. Then if he improves in the things you have tried to have him master, if he masters a few things, promote him. 

The Child's Responsibility. After a class has been taught any skill every child should be held strictly responsible for using it whenever he needs it in his work. For example, after a class using Junior Drill Pad II, has studied appositives (Exercises 17-18) every child is responsible for punctuating correctly any appositive he uses, not only in that semester but also in following semesters. An instructor who teaches only one grade should be familiar with the entire course of study, so that she will know what to require from the children.

How can the child receive the benefit of correcting his mistakes and still use a minimum of time and effort? The corrections for the first composition will go on the back of the title page, the corrections for the second composition on the back of the first composition, and so on. Do not have the children write the whole sentence in which an error occurs unless the entire sentence needs recasting. For a run-on sentence have children write the word that comes before the needed period, put in the period, and write the first word of the following sentence with a capital letter. This does not take long and the corrections are easy for the teacher to check, since they are short and each correction is opposite the corresponding error. As the teacher walks around or as the children bring their books to her desk, she can approve each child's work.

TEACHING LETTER-WRITING 

There are no other rules for letter-writing so important as the rule: Let common sense be your guide. All of us, even apparently irresponsible children, want to write letters that are in the most up-to-date form. Details of the fashion change, but always as a basic practice well-bred people write their letters in a natural and unostentatious way. They know it is silly to assume in a letter a manner that they would not use in conversation with the person they are addressing. This is fundamental; but we must give our untrained children more specific help than this. Many of them have picked up crude or old-fashioned ideas about letter-writing before they come to us. It should not be necessary to say that such expressions as "Well, I guess I'll close now" and "I am writing you a few lines" are superfluous and inelegant, but still some children do have to be warned against using them. 

Friendly Letters. Friendly letters should be informal and interesting, as much as possible like conversation. They should reflect the personality of the writer. This will not be difficult if the writer just puts himself into the frame of mind of wanting

Should a friendly letter bear the writer's address? Tell the children, "Put yourself in the place of the person who receives the letter, and think what will be most convenient for him. 

If you are writing to a relative or friend with whom you correspond frequently, probably he doesn't need it, though even a close friend may lose an address. If you are writing to a friend with whom you don't correspond very frequently, it would be thoughtful to put your address on the letter. In any case, it doesn't hurt to include it, and it may be convenient for the person who receives your letter." The address on a friendly letter may be placed either at the beginning or at the end at the right-hand side of the paper above the salutation if at the beginning, and at the left-hand side below the signature if at the end of the letter.

Informal Notes. It seems a waste of time to teach the writing of formal notes of invitation and acceptance to children in junior high school, as practically no child ever has occasion to use these forms. Informal notes should be taught. 

Suppose the assignment is a note of invitation. Again you say to the children, "Put yourself in the recipient's place and ask yourself what he would like to know. The basic rule is to make your prospective guest comfortable; tell him whatever will help him to have a good time while he is with you. He will want to know just when he is to arrive, and in almost every case how long he is to stay; being definite about these matters saves embarrassment for him and for you. If you invite him to a party, let him know what kind of occasion it will be. If you invite him to be your house guest, tell him something of the activities that will be going on, so that he will know what clothes to bring. If information about trains or roads would be helpful, be sure to put this in. If he is to come by train, assure him that he will be met. Every note of invitation has its individual requirements, but the rule for all is that you must tell your guest everything that will add to his comfort and convenience." It seems officious and rather a cause for offense to say "Please answer." As a matter of course the recipient of an invitation answers promptly. 

A note of thanks for a gift or a good time should show the writer's appreciation in a specific way. One needs only to tell

the truth about his feeling when he received the gift, how much he has used it or will use it, how attractive it is. Above all, such notes should sound spontaneous. 

A note of invitation should bear the writer's address, but the address is not necessary on a note of thanks. It is customary now on informal notes to place the address and date below and at the left of the signature.

Business Letters. The business letters children have to write most frequently are orders and letters of inquiry and complaint. Boys may write orders for stamps, air rifles, books, magazines. They may write complaints about nonreceipt of an article that has been ordered or lack of delivery of a magazine. Few children have occasion to apply for a position in writing; training for this in junior high school should be almost exclusively oral.

The fundamental requirement of a business letter is that it be clear. Impress the fact that most business houses receive hundreds, even thousands, of letters daily, and if a business letter is to fulfill its purpose it must be so written that its meaning will be instantly apparent. 66 The supervisor of correspondence of a prominent mail order house, when asked what was the most important consideration in writing business letters, said without hesitation, Clearness is the most important. We constantly have trouble from language that is awkward and obscure, from the omission of necessary statements, and in general, from writers forgetting that their letters are being read by people at a distance who are not mind-readers. . . .' Put yourself in the other man's place. Visualize the conditions under which he is reading your letter. . . . Lack of clearness not only makes delays; it makes misunderstandings and bad tempers. It creates irritation and confusion and destroys confidence.” ¹ 

Courtesy in every letter is essential, first because it adds to the comfort and happiness of life in general, but also, if for no other reason, because it pays. No one need ever be afraid that he will lose dignity by deferring to someone else or by putting himself to some trouble for another even when he isn't required to do so. Tennyson says, "The greater man, the greater courtesy."

Rules Applicable to All Letters. Every letter of any sort should bear the date of writing. 

Margins should be uniform. 

One should always use matched paper and envelopes. White paper is decidedly the best, but very pale tints of gray, tan, and blue are permissible, although even these are out of place for business letters. Gilt ornamentation is considered bad form. 

Try to have children write letters on real letter paper or at least on unlined paper. If one lets children bring letter paper from home some of it is likely to be of an undesirable sort. Many

teachers are able to make arrangements for furnishing inexpensive, tasteful letter paper to their classes for one letterwriting exercise in a semester. If a teacher furnishes this for some members of a class she should do so for all, or the feelings of some children will be hurt.

Abbreviations are not good form. The post office wants 

State names written in full. The post-office manual says: 

Write plainly the name of the person addressed, street and number, number of rural route, or post-office box, and the name of the post office and State in full. When the name of the State is abbreviated, frequently Va. and Pa., Md. and Ind., Colo. and Calif., Miss. and 

Minn., and others are confused and mail missent, as in many instances post-office names are repeated in many different states. 

Then in heavy black type appears the following: "Do not abbreviate or use lead pencil."

Neither is it good to abbreviate the names of the months in headings, or street and avenue. Business firms no longer abbreviate except in rare instances where the address would be awkwardly long. It is permissible to abbreviate Incorporated, and 

Corporation if writing the word in full would make the line out of proportion. Abbreviations look hurried, as if the letter were considered unimportant and had been dashed off in any old way. 

No one wants his letter to give that impression; there is a subtle compliment to the recipient if the effect of care in the writing is given. There is even less excuse for abbreviations in friendly letters than there is in business letters. In fact, abbreviations, except Mr. and Mrs., are so seldom used nowadays that it seems foolish to expend effort on teaching them. The time could much better be spent in teaching the spelling of the complete words.

One should not use st, d, nd, rd, or th after the number of the day. The date should be written in full. It is wrong to write 

2/13/32; the correct form is February 13, 1932. Street numbers of less than one hundred are to be spelled out in words; street numbers of more than one hundred are to be written in figures. 

We use figures for any numbers that require more than two words. In friendly letters sums of money that can be expressed in two words are so written; if more than two words are needed one should use figures. In business letters every sum of money

115 is written neatly in figures, using a dollar sign and the decimal point, because the amount can be quickly and easily read. A sum of money should not be written in figures and then repeated in words inclosed in a parenthesis. One should say "I inclose" instead of "Please find inclosed."

Right. Mr. Curtis gave me twenty-five new stamps for my collection. 

Right. We have 150 fluffy yellow chicks running about the yard. 

Right. I inclose a money order for $2.50.

Right (in a friendly letter). Father has increased my allowance to fifty cents. 

Bad. Please find inclosed a money order for $2.50 (two dollars and fifty cents). 

It is not good form to say "Thanking you in advance" or "Thank you" in a letter that makes a request. That takes too much for granted, as if the person to whom one is writing will certainly do as he is asked. It makes the request take on somewhat the nature of a demand, and the writer doesn't want it to do that. It is also bad form to lead up to the close with a participial phrase, such as "Hoping you are well." That is not only inelegant but also, as it is usually handled, bad grammar; such a phrase does not make a sentence.

The composition handbooks still call for commas after the salutation and the complimentary close of friendly letters and after the close of business letters. Like the vermiform appendix, these commas are still with us even though they serve no useful purpose. A comma indicates a minor separation, a slight pause, but in each of these situations the separation is easily apparent without a comma. The comma here has no value comparable, for instance, to its use with parenthetical expressions. Some business houses are now omitting it after the close. 

The children at least seem determined to leave it out, and in this case reason is on their side. 

The envelope should always bear the writer's address. A return address insures that the letter either reaches the person to whom it is addressed or is returned to the sender. 

One should never send a letter without having read it through carefully, not only to see whether his words express the meaning he wants to convey, but also to see whether he has allowed any errors to creep in that will give the effect of carelessness or

haste. One should use underscorings and postscripts sparingly, if at all. They give the effect of an untidy mind and of ignorance of more effective means of expression. One should never send a letter with a blot on it.

Helpful Books on Letter Writing. The best book on this subject is When You Write a Letter by Thomas Arkle Clark. 

Although written for adults, it is easy to read and not at all beyond the comprehension of children in junior high school. 

Another very good book, especially for informal notes, is Etiquette, Jr., to which we have already referred several times. 

It is too bad that there are not more collections of letters written by children, or at least interesting to children. A Book of Letters for Young People is a compilation by Center and Saul of letters written by famous persons, such as Mark Twain, Robert Louis Stevenson, and Lewis Carroll, to youthful correspondents. Theodore Roosevelt's Letters to His Children contains examples of delightful letters.

TEACHING CONVERSATION 

Much has been written lately about the decline of conversation, which once held an honored place in social life and was of fine quality. Several surveys have recently placed conversation first and second among uses of English in practical affairs. 

In his books on curriculum-making and courses of study in seven of the leading cities of the country, Mr. Bobbitt gives conversation an important place in the curriculum of the junior high school.

The emphasis is placed on secondary schools, for the claims of conversation have been recognized to a much greater extent by the

Indeed the kindergarten and the primary grades lay so excellent a foundation in conversation that it is surprising that the aim of literary excellence should ever have been able so completely to displace it. 66 Conversation is at its best and most representative when it is like a game of battledore and shuttlecock, where each player may strike in at his will, but the shuttlecock must never be allowed to fall to the ground." [A simile clearer to boys and girls today would be "like a game of volley ball or tennis."]

The similarity of the subject of conversation to a ball tossed back and forth between players who alternately become catcher and pitcher is readily seen by boys and girls. The practice too of sending a ball back into the game at a new angle bears comparison with the obligation of the listener to add his thought and feeling to the remark of a previous speaker before sending it back into the game. 

Junior high school pupils can be led to phrase for themselves such basic principles of conversation as "Talk about what is of interest to others, returning the ball as often as you can."

The element of artificiality in a conversation which is being consciously tested can be reduced to a minimum by the selection of subjects in which boys and girls are genuinely interested.. 

If our present conception of the school as an organization whose purpose is to train pupils to do better the desirable things which they will do anyway has any claim to acceptance, the activity of the pupil must be to as large an extent as possible social. The major use of English in the average social situation is conversation. 

In a conversation exercise there should be as much freedom as there would be in a similar situation in a cultivated, wellordered home. The give-and-take should be spontaneous and natural, but everyone should be polite and not interrupt. The subjects discussed should not be merely the weather and commonplace personalities.

In introducing a conversation lesson the teacher should tell the class something about the art of conversation and what an important accomplishment it can be. No doubt this idea. will be surprising to the children. Then she can ask each child to jot down on a slip of paper two or three subjects, perhaps only one, that he thinks he could talk about rather well. 

Collect these and run through them rapidly, telling the class what they are. Then say that for the rest of the period (or the next day preferably) the class will pretend it is a group of people

sitting in the living-room of a home and will engage in conversation.

The teacher will have to be the leader to start the ball moving, and if it seems about to go out of play she must give it an impetus to send it forth again. She must be alert and tactful. 

The children should sit in their seats and talk without asking permission, without raising their hands or standing. A child may speak whenever he wishes to contribute something, provided he doesn't interrupt another speaker. It sounds difficult, but can be carried off successfully. 

The children are likely to respond to the novelty and informality of a conversation exercise, and usually the bell for the end of the period rings too soon. Of course the teacher must be a person who is really interested in what is going on in the world, and she should be as natural and informal as she would be with her own friends.

There are several pitfalls to be guarded against. Explain that much use of slang becomes very tiresome, and that a person who can express himself clearly and neatly and interestingly has a high social value. One must also show beforehand that a bore who monopolizes the conversation and talks down everyone else is rude, and makes himself unpopular. 

Explain that well-bred persons can differ in their views politely and without ill will. If the conversation takes a controversial turn and appears likely to become heated, the teacher must be adept in turning it in another direction; sometimes one is fortunate in having a child in the class who can do this. 

This is not an easy exercise, but it is a fascinating one. It would be more difficult were it not for the eagerness of almost all young people to learn anything that will be a social asset to them.

TEACHING ARGUMENT 

The oral talk or written paragraph in which a child gives "My Reasons for Liking Tom Sawyer" is a simple argument.

The next step in difficulty is a symposium. 

The Symposium. The basic principle of a symposium is that a controversial matter is under discussion and any member of the class may stand and voice his opinion on it provided that: (1) he does so in a polite and orderly way, (2) he has an attempt at good reasoning behind what he says, (3) he sticks to the subject, and (4) he expresses himself in the clearest and most effective English at his command. A symposium is more informal than a debate, and since it allows more people to take part, is well adapted to large classes. It is flexible, and as a teacher experiments with it she may find more possibilities for it. 

A regular debate subject is chosen and tested according to 

Requirements for the Proposition on page 122. Two or three leaders are appointed a day or two before the symposium is to be held, so that they may have time to secure some ideas and arguments. A chairman is appointed. The teacher becomes inconspicuous. The chairman takes his seat near the front of

the room, states the proposition or resolution, and calls upon one after another of the leaders to state his views and start the argument. After the leaders have spoken any member of the class who has an argument or an idea to offer may rise or raise his hand, and after the chairman has called upon him he may state his views. If the subject is a good one there should be a lively give-and-take.

The symposium need not be closed with a decision. The question may be left open, or a vote may be taken as to whether there are more good arguments for the affirmative or for the negative. The class may decide that there is truth on both sides and that the affirmative statement must be modified by the negative. There is no formal judging. The teacher should help in summarizing what has been said. It should be impressed upon the pupils that one who has good reasons and expresses them effectively will be able to persuade others to his way of thinking, but the principal object of a symposium is not to develop speakers or to allow a few members of the class to "shine." It is to induce the habit of thought upon important questions and to show the difference between sound and false lines of reasoning.

One difficulty with a symposium is that it does not provide for equal participation by all members of the class. The voluble talkers tend to hold the floor most of the time, and the quiet children sit back and look on. Some expedient must be resorted to in order to draw out these silent ones. However, sometimes under the excitement of a stirring discussion children who are ordinarily timid or reticent will be moved to say more than they have ever said in class before. Perhaps the subject will just happen to touch upon their experiences or convictions. 

The Debate. By holding debates one can be sure that every child has an opportunity to talk. The debate is held between two opposing sides of two or three members each. As with the symposium, the speakers should be appointed two or three days before the debate is to be held in order that they may have time to gather arguments. Some discussion of the qualities needed for good argument should be carried on at the time the topic is chosen. The chief requirement is that an argument must be supported by reasons which are in turn supported by evidence or proof.

121 When the debate is held a chairman, a timekeeper, and three judges should be appointed from the class. The chairman should state the proposition and then call upon the speakers in the following order: first speaker on the affirmative, George Anderson; first speaker on the negative, Alice Williams; second speaker on the affirmative, Joe Widener; second speaker on the negative, Henry Petrach. After every speaker has had his turn there may be a brief intermission, two or three minutes in length, while the members of each side confer concerning the rebuttal. Rebuttal means to thrust again, and in debate it means counter arguments that aim to refute or disprove arguments that the other side has given. No new arguments may be introduced in rebuttal; rebuttal is, in a sense, an answer. 

When the chairman calls for the rebuttal one member on the negative side gives all the rebuttal for his side. Then one member on the affirmative does the same for his side. affirmative begins and ends a debate. The After this the judges leave the room and form their decision. 

While they are out it is interesting to use the time in a general discussion of what the speakers said and how they said it. Some of the audience may have arguments on the subject that they would like to state. They may rise and state their opinions as the chairman calls upon them. When the judges return, the chairman asks for their decision, which is given by the one they have chosen for this duty. Judges should base their decision on whether the members of a side (1) adhered to the subject, (2) had good proof and examples, (3) refuted their opponents' arguments, (4) kept their tempers, (5) showed no discourtesy, and (6) used good clear English. When the judge states the decision, he should explain on what it is based. 

RULES FOR ANYONE WHO IS CARRYING ON AN ARGUMENT 

1. Stick to the proposition. 

2. Study the proposition and think through your arguments so thoroughly beforehand that you will know just what you are going to say. Thus you will have the advantage of feeling sure of yourself. Preparation is more than half the battle. Do not read your talk; written speeches almost always lack interest and force. Neither should you memorize your talk, for if you should happen to forget some of it you will find yourself more

completely lost than if you were speaking extemporaneously. 

Just have the points you want to make and the proof for them very thoroughly in mind, and then talk naturally. It is a good idea to have an outline or a few memoranda jotted down on a card; you can glance at the card and keep yourself on the track. 

3. Be confident. If you don't believe in yourself, you can't make anyone else believe in what you say. 

4. Keep cool. If you can't control your feelings, your audience will lose sympathy with you and close their minds to what you have to say. Moreover, if you become excited you will forget your arguments. 

5. Don't give up easily. If you keep on going, you always have a chance of winning. 

6. Use foresight. Anticipate what your opponents are going to say and prepare some arguments to refute theirs. 

7. Study how to express your ideas in clear, forcible English.

Think out one or two strong, telling sentences. Many a good argument has fallen on deaf ears because the speaker did not say it so that it could be readily understood. 

8. Play fair. Good sportsmanship is as important in argument as in a football game. Discourtesy counts against you.

REQUIREMENTS FOR THE PROPOSITION 

1. The subject should be one on which a reasonable amount of proof can be secured, with a fair distribution on both sides. 

2. The subject should not be one on which one's opinion is a matter of faith, such as a religious question. 

3. The proposition should not be too broad. A statement that is too sweeping cannot be proved. 

4. The proposition should have one central idea clearly expressed, so that there will be no danger of running down byways into what are really other propositions. 

5. The proposition should not contain any ambiguous words. 

6. The proposition should be so expressed that the affirmative is given the burden of proof. That is, the affirmative must make the attack, advocate something new or overthrow something old. The negative corresponds to the defendant in a suit at law.
