Discussion of Dr. Joseph Beck's paper* on "HOBBIES" HARRIS P. MOSHER, M.D. 

Mr. Chairman, Members of the Society and Guests: 

I have bulldozed the chairman into allowing me to break two fundamental rules of debate. I shall not discuss Dr. 

Beck's paper directly because I did not know his particular hobby, but shall read an effusion of my own on hobbies in general, and I shall run a little over time. 

I am tempted to call the subject of hobbies intriguing, but the word is overworked and smells a bit of toilet powder and perfume. That is, it is a little too feminine. Any activity, in order to deserve the title of a hobby, should be more than a summer flirtation with some subject. Most hobbies arise from the urge to collect. There is a fine but distinct line between a hobby and a business. When this line is often overstepped, the bloom of the hobby disappears. 

Professor James once said that the hardest thing one has to do in life is to let the other fellow play as he likes. No hobby, therefore, however bizarre it may seem to you or to me, should be sneered at. A hobby is a thing to turn to when you or your family turn off the radio, if you or they ever do. A hobby is an escape from the daily routine. 

*Read at the Eastern Section of the American Laryngological, Rhinological and Otological Society, Newark, New Jersey, January 3, 1936.

Often it is the development of an unused talent. The cynical say it can be a cause of divorce. I say that it more often prevents divorce. Now and then a hobby becomes a commercial asset. For instance, Wherry could make a living collecting and selling stamps. In fact, in the early days I think that he did this. I have saved myself quite a bit of money making my own teaching charts and illustrations for medical papers. I sold an etching once for three dollars.

My price has gone up since. This is the only money I ever made by art. A hobby gives something to talk about except the weather. If I find that my partner at dinner has a hobby 

I cease to worry about the conversation. A question now and then keeps things going. To put it crudely, I can listen and eat. 

WOOD CARVING By Dr. Mosher. 

One can hardly speak of hobbies, especially his own, without being autobiographical or reminiscing. Up to now I hope I have pretty well resisted these two temptations which come with gray hairs. I was not sure of Beck's [2]

hobby until today. My guess was that it was either raising pigs or photography.

I had a fling at photography in the early days. It is still a side line with me. I got in on movies when they started and have practiced them with temporary lapses of enthusiasm to the present day. Last summer I was busy with the simplified colored movie. I have movies of the trips of the 

American Board in all parts of the country. They are now a bit melancholy because the dead walk in them. This, however makes them very precious.

Photography is a very practical hobby for a medical man. 

For instance, the men who work with me take their own microphotographs. With these they make their own books on the histology and pathology of mucous membrane and of new growths. Some make their own lantern slides. They illustrate their research papers with their own photographs. 

Most of them branch off into general photography, and I encourage this. Historic Boston and Marblehead are photographed all over again. When anyone gets an especially good film he enlarges it in the laboratory dark room. These figure in our Christmas art show. This is going on now. 

Beck of New Rochelle is a shark at photography, including color plates. Proetz also is almost a professional. He is so good at it, looks so good, is so much of an actor and playwright that I have long wondered why Hollywood did not get him. Dr. Ridpath, too, is almost a professional in photography and surpasses many professionals in his artistic taste. Carmody is a movie fan.

Mr. Larsen, the sculptor who lives across the street from me at Marblehead, has a flair for designing model boats.

Collecting books, especially old medical books, is probably the most common hobby among doctors. In this connection the names of Osler and Cushing at once come to mind. One of the men on our staff said apropos of hobbies that any physician worthy of his calling collects medical books of one kind or another. I wish that I could believe that he is more than slightly right in this belief. 

In Boston, when the word gets round that a medical book of historical value is on the market there is a scramble among doctors for it and the price goes up. One Boston neurologist collects books on witches. This seems rather natural. Dr. Leary, a distinguished Boston pathologistthe cholesterol man-the man who has stopped some of us from eating eggs for breakfast, collects medical prints, especially caricatures. One of our staff who married a wife from the Egyptian Department of the Metropolitan Art 

Museum, naturally became an Egyptologist, and his hobby is 

Egyptian medicine. 

One Boston physician, Dr. Barnes, binds books most. creditably. He makes it a stunt to work with as few tools as possible and most of them are homemade. His favorite tool is the handle of a tooth brush which he has tortured into an appropriate shape. 

Speaking of book binding, a day or two ago Holzer, the old German book binder, came to the laboratory to get a [ 5 ]

set of reprints which I had just received from Negus of London. Usually he sends his errand boy so that I had not talked with him personally for years. I asked him how things were going-the stock question these Rooseveltian days, and added it was fine to see that some of the old craftsmen were left. His face beamed and he said in answer to my question, "Thank you, I am still getting by. Things, however, are different from what they were when you first began to send me books. Cheap people now buy cheap books. Only worth-while people have books bound." shall scrape up another order for him as soon as possible. Quite a few of the Boston physicians make a hobby of medical history. Here again Cushing was a leader and a shining light. Dr. Storey, the elder, collected medical medals. His collection is one of the best, if not the best in the country. His son, also a physician, collects navy medals. 

Dr. Goldstein, not of Boston, of course, but of St. Louis, collects old medical books, especially those bearing on our specialty. During prosperous times he collected paintings and etchings. I imagine that there is a lull in this now. 

Dr. Clarence Blake, who was professor of otology at the 

Harvard Medical School in my student days, and who worked with Bell in the experimental stages of the telephone, had for his hobby music, and playing the piano. He advised all of us to acquire this accomplishment if possible in order to increase the dexterity of our fingers. I have done enough wood carving to recommend it as a splendid training for acquiring greater dexterity in the use of the chisel and gouge. Dr. Hubbard of Toledo, when forced by illness to give up practice, turned to wood carving and, as you know, has become very expert at it.

We have another member of this society who is a shining light in his hobby. I refer to high powered Fenton. He is [6]

an accomplished botanist. To him the soil everywhere the globe around is "The Good Earth." He can talk on equal terms with a professor of botany or the layman who likes and grows flowers and he can give pointers to many nurserymen. In seed time he always has in his pocket an envelop or two filled with seeds. He has pilfered seeds from gardens in all parts of the world.

Next to collecting books and photography as hobbies, doctors are given to amateur art. For some eight years there has been a flourishing physicians' art society in New 

York. For four years a physicians' etching club has been in existence, also in New York. It is named for Haden, the famous English surgeon and etcher. In the exhibitions of the New York physicians' art society it is striking how [7]

well many of the members do in sculpture. Boston also has its physicians' art club. I happen to belong to the three societies just mentioned as well as the Marblehead Art Association, of which I have had the expensive honor, for years now, of being first vice-president.

Speaking of art, Dr. Jackson, of course, has long been. prominent in the use of his artistic ability. As you all know, he has applied it must successfully and splendidly in his medical work.

Many physicians of my acquaintance have as a hobby a work shop. They regularly visit the department stores for new tools which soon wear out or refuse to work. One whose forebears were carpenters and house builders has inherited a magnificent equipment. He has almost a museum collection of old wooden planes. His greatest joy is to use Sundays and holidays repairing old furniture and playing handy man about the house. Another physician friend reproduces antique furniture. It is good enough to sell as such. I do not know whether he does this or not. However, he happens to live in a small New England city whose chief industry is the manufacture of antique colonial furniture for the tourist trade.

In closing, I should like to say a word or two more about my own hobby—namely, amateur art. Cutting wood or linoleum blocks for making prints has become an institution among our house officers. Somehow the fable started that no one would be promoted unless he cut a Christmas card in linoleum or wood. Six or seven years ago I taught a sympathetic and artistically inclined house officer to cut linoleum blocks. Everybody is doing it now, including the technicians. This year the cutting of the blocks in many instances is of professional excellence. Naturally, most [8]

of the designs are copies. I have taught at least three house officers the rudiments of etching and have at times been accused of running an art school.

Students take to the making of casts as children do to mud pies. There is something fundamental and elemental in working with clay, wax and plaster. A Belgian, quite a few years ago, who came to me on money left over from the 

Hoover relief fund, when asked what impressed him most in my graduate course replied, "Mud, plaster, and glue."

This art hobby of mine has led to what I consider my best contribution to teaching, namely, drawing from specimens by the aid of the tracing box so that each student makes his own atlas, to use a rather large word, of the anatomy of the nose and throat, and the making of casts of specimens and dissections. Each student takes away with him some seventy drawings and fifty or sixty casts. The casts are really a library of surgical anatomy. As far as I know, I was the first to teach anatomy this way. This technique has been in full flower now for many years.
