PACIFIC COAST THE MARITIME STRIKE OF 1934 

Some two years ago there was a maritime strike of the longshore and seafaring unions on this coast. The strike commenced on May 9, 1934. On 

June 24, 1934, after the strike had been in progress for some time, the President of the United States appointed the National Longshoremen's 

Board under the provisions of the National Labor Disputes Act. 

At the urgent request of the National Longshoremen's Board, the various Waterfront Employers Associations on the Pacific Coast entered into an agreement with the Board on July 11, 1934, to submit all issues involved in the longshore strike to arbitration. 

That agreement of the Waterfront Employers Association to arbitrate did not include the issues involved in the strike of the seafaring unions which had been ineffective; but Pacific Coast steamship companies later agreed, at the request of the National Longshoremen's Board, to collectively bargain with representatives of their various seafaring employees, to be selected in elections conducted by the Board and to submit to arbitration any controversies not settled by negotiation. 

All of the seafaring employees and longshoremen returned to work on July 31, 1934, after the unions had by referendum agreed to submit all issues in controversy to arbitration and had agreed to abide by the decision of the arbitrators.

A set of four awards was rendered on November 6, 1934. Varying rates were established for the four coast ports, all less than the rates established in the longshore award. Soon after the awards were rendered, the employers voluntarily increased these wages from the rates specified in the award to the 95c per hour straight time and $1.40 per hour overtime provided in the longshore award. 

Later on agreements were also made with the International Longshoremen's Association governing wages, hours and working conditions for monthly clerks in all ports, and supplementary agreements were made providing hiring halls and satisfactory working conditions for daily clerks. 

With the exception of a single agreement affecting clerks at San Pedro, all awards and agreements expired on September 30th of this year. 

SETTLEMENT OF DISPUTES INVOLVING SEAFARING EMPLOYEES 

The National Longshoremen's Board then proceeded to conduct elections to determine representatives of the seafaring employees of Pacific Coast steamship companies for the purpose of collective bargaining. 5

The Shipowners Association of the Pacific Coast, comprising virtually all coastwise steamship companies in the steam schooner trade, recognized the International Seamen's Union as the representative of their unlicensed employees at sea before the ballots were counted. 

The representatives of the Shipowners Association of the Pacific Coast entered into negotiations with the International Seamen's Union for agreements establishing wages, hours and working conditions. The negotiations, however, being fruitful only in part, an arbitration agreement was executed on January 4, 1935.

In that agreement the International Seamen's Union, acting on behalf of the unlicensed seafaring employees, and the Shipowners Association of the Pacific Coast agreed that all disputes would be submitted to arbitration before H. W. Hutton, S. D. Freeman and Albert A. Rosenshine, as arbitrators, and all parties agreed to be bound by their decision. 

Negotiations continued after the arbitration agreement was signed, and approximately 50% of the union proposals were settled by negotiation before the commencement of the arbitration proceedings. 

The board rendered its award on February 2, 1935, settling all remaining controversies between the operators of steam schooners and the International Seamen's Union. 

That award contained the following provision: 

1. A provision already incorporated in the arbitration agreement that there should be no strikes or stoppages of work, and that all grievances and disputes should be submitted to the Labor Relations Board; 

2. Provisions regulating the manner of hiring and directing that no seaman should be required as the condition of employment to register with any private hiring hall or similar institution; 

3. A provision that hiring should be either from the office of the union or from the dock, and prohibiting the use of any rotation or tag system of employment.

Shortly thereafter negotiations between the owners of deep sea ships and the International Seamen's Union culminated in an arbitration agreement dated February 25, 1935.

By that agreement the shipowners extended to the International Seamen's Union preference of employment upon the condition, among other things, that employers should be free to employ men in certain classifications, with peculiar qualifications, without reference to union or non-union affiliation.

An award was rendered by the arbitrators named in this agreement on 

April 10, 1935. That award provided, among other things, the following: "No seafaring employee shall be required as a condition of employment to register with any private hiring hall or any similar institution. Employment shall be at the docks or at the union offices at the option of the employers. The employers shall have entire freedom of selection from those eligible. No system shall be established that directly or indirectly will interfere in any manner with the employers' option to hire from the dock or from the union, itself, or the employers' right to entire freedom of selection from those eligible. No system shall be established that directly or indirectly will compel any employee to register with any private hall or similar institution, or that will interfere with his right to choice of employment." 

There was a specific agreement by the union in the arbitration agreement and a provision in the award that during the life of the award there would be no strikes, lockouts, or stoppages of work, the unions agreeing to cooperate with the steamship companies in the maintenance of adequate service, and to that end not to directly nor indirectly, through its members, discriminate against the steamship companies by refusing to sail under licensed officers, including chief stewards, irrespective of their affiliation or non-affiliation with any particular organization or association. 

NEGOTIATIONS AND ARBITRATION RELATING TO LICENSED OFFICERS 

After the settlement of disputes between the Longshoremen's Union and the International Seamen's Union, on the one hand, and the Employers on the other hand, there remained the Unions representing the licensed

personnel of American ships on the Pacific Coast. Deck officers were represented by the National Organization of Masters, Mates and Pilots, and engineers by the Marine Engineers' Beneficial Association in negotiations with the steam schooner owners (The Shipowners Association of the Pacific Coast), which executed an agreement with the Marine Engineers' Beneficial Association on March 14, 1935, and an agreement with the Masters, Mates and Pilots on March 22, 1935.

Elections to determine the choice of licensed employees of representatives for collective bargaining with steamship companies in offshore trades having been completed by the National Longshoremen's Board, that Board designated the National Organization of Masters, Mates and Pilots and the Marine Engineers' Beneficial Association as the representatives for collective bargaining of the licensed deck and engine room officers of all Pacific Coast companies, except the American-Hawaiian Steamship Company and 

Swayne & Hoyt, Ltd.

The companies who were required by the National Longshoremen's Board to recognize these unions of licensed officers conducted negotiations with the unions, executing an agreement with the National Organization of Masters, Mates and Pilots on May 1, 1935, and with the Marine Engineers' Beneficial Association on April 22, 1935.

The agreement with the Marine Engineers' Beneficial Association provided for arbitration of unsettled disputes, and a Board selected by the parties rendered its award on August 23, 1935, only a little over a year ago. 

In both the agreement with the representative of their licensed officers and in the award for engineers, it was provided that employers should be free to select licensed officers without reference to union affiliation. 

After the completion of those negotiations agreements were signed on May 7, 1935, with the American Radio Telegraphists Association, representing the radio operators on various off-shore ships. In that agreement preference of employment was given to the American Radio Telegraphists' Association, but with the specific understanding expressed in writing that the companies shall be free to select their radio officers from within those eligible.

Each of the agreements and awards, except the awards for longshoremen and clerks, contained the specific provision that there should be no strike, lockout or stoppage of work during the life of the agreement and award, but that work should at all times continue, and that any and all grievances or disputes should be submitted to labor relations committees or labor relations boards to be determined, and that, in the event agreement was not reached, all such disputes should be determined by arbitration. 

The foregoing is the background of agreements and awards, an understanding of which is necessary in following the history of Pacific Coast shipping during the past two years.

STRIKES AND STOPPAGES OF WORK BY LONGSHOREMEN 

Even while the International Longshoremen's Board was conducting its arbitration of longshore issues, from July 31, 1934, the day the longshoremen returned to work, to October 12, 1934, when the award was handed down, there were repeated strikes and stoppages of work along all of the waterfronts of the Pacific Coast. The first occurred on August 1st, the day after the longshoremen returned to work. Twenty-nine such strikes and stoppages were actually recorded and presented to the National Longshoremen's Board in that period. During that time official representations on this subject were made on the part of the steamship companies and the Waterfront Employers Association to the National Longshoremen's Board; official transcripts were taken of the occurrences; repeated requests were made by the National Longshoremen's Board that the Longshoremen's Union discontinue stoppages of work. One of the members of the Board, the Assistant 

Secretary of Labor, joined with the others in giving assurance to the employers that when the award was handed down the full influence of the Government would be exerted to procure compliance with its terms. The Waterfront Employers Association of the Pacific Coast were induced to continue with the Arbitration Proceedings. However, when the award was handed down, far from diminishing, the strikes and stoppages of work increased in frequency and in intensity.

At a very early date in the history of these controversies the question

arose whether the longshoremen had the right to discontinue work for the purpose of compelling employers of longshoremen to make their practices in the handling of cargo conform to the wishes of the Union. That question was submitted to the Hon. M. C. Sloss, as arbitrator for San Francisco, immediately after his appointment in November of 1934. On January 4, 1935, Judge Sloss rendered his first decision, known as the Cocoanut Decision, in which he pointed out that the employer under the award had no right to establish practices inimical to the health or safety of his employees, "but", he said, "the question whether any particular method is so inimical is not to be finally decided by the employers or the employees. Any such question is to be disposed of through the orderly process of consideration by the Labor Relations Committee, and, in the event such Committee fails to agree, by the decision of the arbitrator. In the meanwhile, as already indicated, the work should, in my opinion, go on under the methods directed by the employer". He held in that particular case that the employer's practice was not inimical to the health or safety of the employee, and that the number of loads of cocoanut then being handled was proper. 

The important point in that award of Judge Sloss was the decision, which he repeated thereafter time and time again, that under the arbitration award of the National Longshoremen's Board longshoremen are required to continue at work in the manner directed by the employer until any complaint they may have regarding such manner of doing work is adjudicated by the Labor Relations Committee or, if necessary, is arbitrated. Nevertheless, since October 12, 1934, the date of the longshore award, there have been some 561 recorded stoppages of work and strikes up and down the Pacific Coast by longshoremen alone. The purposes of the strikes and stoppages of work were diverse. Some 

173 of them were for the purpose of compelling members of Waterfront Employers Associations to employ union men in other occupations. 

As already stated, in the elections held by the National Longshoremen's 10

Board for clerks and checkers, the clerks and checkers of many companies did not designate the International Longshoremen's Association as their representative. Commencing, however, on October 12, 1934, literally dozens of strikes were instituted up and down this coast by longshoremen to compel employers to employ only union clerks and checkers. As a result today, it is safe to say, that every clerk and checker on the Pacific Coast is a union man, not because of his desire, but as a result of the strike method by which his membership in the I. L. A. has been accomplished through · force. 

There have been a great number of sympathetic strikes, or, as they are sometimes called, "hot cargo" strikes. The purpose of these strikes is to compel other employers who have no connection with the Waterfront Employers Association to employ only union men in their plants. 

Perhaps the most striking example of the type of strike to which I refer is the so-called Vancouver "hot cargo" strike. There was a dispute in the summer of last year between longshoremen in British Columbia and the Shipping Federation, an association of employers of longshoremen in British Columbia. The striking longshoremen were not affiliated with the 

International Longshoremen's Association in any manner; nevertheless, it espoused the cause of the striking Canadian longshoremen. All cargo transported on Pacific Coast American ships between Canadian and Pacific Coast ports was declared "hot" by the International Longshoremen's Association, and at least one ship was tied up at the docks in San Francisco for over two months, unable to load or discharge cargo because it had "hot" Vancouver cargo in its holds. No American ship was permitted to load or discharge cargo emanating from or destined to British Columbian ports on the Pacific Coast. It necessarily followed, of course, that only the foreign lines could handle that cargo. The International Longshoremen's Association, with the cooperation of the International Seamen's Union, has deprived American ships of all that traffic, because American employers declined to insist on the employment in Vancouver of members of the International Longshoremen's Association, contrary to the practices of that port. 

Many strikes and stoppages of work pertained to the control of working conditions and were carried on for the purpose of obtaining concessions denied to longshoremen by the Longshoremen's Board. We have already 11

mentioned their effort to reduce the size of slingloads and the decision of Judge Sloss on that subject. That decision was rendered in January of 1935; nevertheless, strikes in this very port, as well as elsewhere up and down the Coast, for the purpose of accomplishing the same purpose, have also increased in frequency and in intensity, until there is now of record approximately 90 such strikes and stoppages of work up and down this Coast; in every such case the union has refused to submit the question to the Labor Relations Committee or to the Arbitrator as required by the agreement and award.

In addition to that, there have been various other purposes and reasons back of the strikes and stoppages of work. In some cases the employer has been compelled to increase the size of his gangs beyond his needs. In some cases the purpose has been to compel the employer to pay rates of compensation in excess of those provided for in the award. In some cases it was to prevent the application of the six-hour day as provided by the award. In a large number of cases it has been to prevent the establishment and conduct of the hiring hall in the manner directed in the award, and to prevent application of dispatching rules laid down by the Joint Labor Relations Committees.

There have been strikes for various other purposes. There has been, for example, the strike against the steamer "Yale", when the sailing was delayed for one hour because it carried a football team from the University of California. On another occasion, when the German cruiser "Karlsruhe❞ visited this port there was a cessation of work for one hour on all ships in this port. On every July 5th there is a complete cessation of work up and down the whole Pacific Coast because of a holiday declared by the unions for the purpose of commemorating a day of violence and of bloodshed. 

That has been the history of the strikes and the stoppages of work carried out by the International Longshoremen's Association. They continued to the very day when work ceased on the San Francisco waterfront, despite repeated declarations by the Hon. M. C. Sloss and other arbitrators that such action is illegal.

On March 19, 1935, the Hon. M. C. Sloss, speaking for the whole 

Pacific Coast, the Arbitrator appointed by the Secretary of Labor, said: 12

"I reaffirm the principle of the ruling of the Local Award, dated

January 4, 1935, i.e., that Sections 9 and 10 of the award of the National Longshoremen's Board, providing a method of adjusting or adjudicating controversies, necessarily contemplates that, while this process of adjustment or adjudication is going on, the work of loading or unloading ships is to continue uninterruptedly, and that any attempt to enforce the contentions of either side by stoppage of work is contrary to the award."

Not only Hon. M. C. Sloss, as Coastwise and Local Arbitrator, but other Arbitrators on the Pacific Coast have reaffirmed these principles. On 

March 11, 1935, Gordon S. Watkins, Arbitrator for the Port of Los Angeles, handed down a decision wherein he said: "Collective stoppages of work by members of the International Longshoremen's Union is a direct violation of Section 6 of the Award, which specifically provides that 'All longshoremen shall be dispatched without favoritism or discrimination, regardless of union or non-union membership.' 

Again, he said: "Collective stoppages of work by union men, whether officially sanctioned or not, are essentially a denial of the right of non-union employees to work, as well as of the right of the employer to hire competent, eligible employees without reference to union or non-union membership." 

Finally, on September 27, 1935, in an award entitled "The Hot Cargo 

Award," the Hon. M. C. Sloss, again speaking for the whole Pacific Coast, reiterated the decisions already made, but further held that it was illegal for unions to refuse to handle cargo in the loading or discharging of ships because it fell within the classification of hot cargo. After referring to the question, Is there a right under the award to refuse to work solely because hot cargo is to be handled? Judge Sloss said this: "This is the fundamental question above referred to. In my judgment it must be and it is answered in the negative. A refusal to handle hot cargo (if, as is assumed for the moment, there is no other ground for refusal) is an effort to exert the pressure of obstructing the loading and unloading of vessels by the employing stevedores and shipowners, for the purpose of assisting other crafts or unions in obtaining what 13

they seek from other employers. This purpose is akin to that sought by a sympathetic strike. Whether or not a sympathetic strike is lawful, in the absence of a contract inconsistent with it, is not the question. 

Here the parties have made such a contract. Under the award of the 

National Longshoremen's Board, that award constitutes a series of agreements between the I. L. A. and the various associations of employers who were parties to the award. The award thus established an agreement specifying the terms and conditions on which longshore work was to be done in Pacific Coast ports, and was and is binding upon both parties. So long as that agreement is in force, neither party has a right to violate it for reasons which are foreign to the relations of the parties, and to the conditions upon which they agreed, but are directed to some external conflict between other parties." 

In stating what has occurred up and down the Pacific Coast, we need not rely upon the testimony of witnesses, because we have the judicial decisions of the arbitrators appointed by the Secretary of Labor. We have repeated decisions up and down the Coast in which the arbitrators have specifically held in so many words that the International Longshoremen's Association has violated the provisions of the agreement and award. We have the decision on November 12, 1935 by Harry Hazel, Arbitrator for the port of Seattle, in which he specifically found: "The refusal of the I. L. A. to handle the flour cargo of the Fisher 

Flouring Mills Company at commercial docks is not in accord with the award of date October 12, 1934, but is a violation of the terms thereof; that such violations should immediately cease, and that said cargo should be handled at commercial docks in conformity with the award aforesaid as and when tendered by the employers.'

STRIKES AND STOPPAGES OF WORK BY SEAMEN 

The experience with strikes and stoppages of work by the International Longshoremen's Association and its members was duplicated with the International Seamen's Union and its members on American ships. Strikes by unlicensed seamen on American Pacific Coast ships commenced also the day after the return to work, and owners were harrassed thereby up to the very date when the awards were rendered.

The shipowners hoped that when the awards were given stability would 14

be restored and peace prevail. Not less than 250 separate strikes or stoppages of work occurred after the award of April 10, 1935, and American ships were tied up for periods of from a few hours to several months as a result. The purposes and natures of these strikes correspond substantially with those ashore.

Many strikes were for the purpose of driving old employees from American ships, although under the agreements and award employers were guaranteed the right to retain in their employ all bona fide seamen who had served continuously since prior to May 9, 1934. As a result, there are very few of the so-called local employees remaining on Pacific Coast American ships. In many cases these employees were removed by physical force and violence.

Pacific Coast American ships, particularly those engaged in the intercoastal trade, have been compelled to obtain either new crews or replacements from time to time on the Atlantic Coast. In such cases, upon the return of the vessel to this coast seamen so shipped, although members of the International Seamen's Union, have been forcibly removed or been compelled to leave by strikes on this coast.

Atlantic Coast ships with union crews shipped under agreements with the International Seamen's Union have been treated in a similar manner, and union crews have been forcibly removed or ships delayed by strikes and picket lines of the west coast locals of the International Seamen's Union. 

A notable case of this nature was that of the "Santa Rosa", which arrived in this port on April 15th of this year with an Atlantic Coast union crew and was picketed by west coast union men to compel the Atlantic Coast union men to leave the ship. At that time all shipping was at a standstill in the port of San Francisco for nearly a week because the employers refused to accede to the demands of the union. 

The west coast locals of the International Seamen's Union also participated in the Vancouver hot cargo strike. Members of the American International Seamen's Union refused to ship or serve on any American vessel transporting cargo to or from British Columbia, demanding that all American ships loading or discharging in British Columbia must employ only 15

members of the International Longshoremen's Association, although that union had no relations with any of the employers in British Columbia. Because the American shipowners declined to so agree, ships were unable to move for as much as three months, and in many cases were left stranded in the port of Vancouver, unable to obtain American crews to bring American ships to American ports.

Many strikes and stoppages of work have occurred aboard ship for the purpose of obtaining concessions not granted by the award. 

Strikes and stoppages of work have been carried out to prevent employers from hiring on the docks, as the agreement and award provided; to prevent shipowners from selecting seamen from the union membership that the employers deemed capable and fit for the purposes for which they were desired; and to prevent employers on this coast from selecting seamen from American unions to sail American ships; and to compel employers to accept only those union members designated by the union under a prohibited system of rotation or designated by the whim of union officials. 

The most notable case of this nature was the "President Hoover", which was prevented from sailing as scheduled with some 400 passengers and United States mail on board, for at least six days because the company had dismissed an able seaman who was unsatisfactory, even though the same seaman was offered employment by other owners on other ships. The union insisted that the "Hoover" would not sail until they had accepted for employment the man who had been discharged as unsatisfactory and was, in the master's opinion, unfit to perform his duties. 

So much for the repeated violations of the provisions in every award with reference to strikes and to stoppages of work. In that connection we wish to say that we have no complaint to make with reference to activities of that nature by the Unions of licensed personnel except in that they have cooperated with members of other Unions, through the Maritime Federation, to carry out this job action policy. 

THE LONGSHORE HIRING HALLS 

Under the longshoremen's award it was provided that hiring halls should be maintained and operated jointly by the International Longshoremen's Association and the various employers' associations. That award 16

provided that the personnel in each hall should be selected by the Labor Relations Committee for the port, except the dispatcher, who should be selected by the International Longshoremen's Association. The Labor Relations Committee was charged with the duty of maintaining a list of extra men and of casuals and providing for the addition of new men to the industry. It was given the power and the duty to determine the organization of gangs. Subject to the limitation of hours contained in the award, the employers were guaranteed the right to have dispatched to them, when available, the gangs in their opinion best qualified to do their work; otherwise, and subject to those provisions, the earnings of longshoremen were to be equalized.

Under the award the Labor Relations Committee for each port was vested with the power and the duty to generally manage the hall; to appoint the personnel connected with the hall, except the dispatcher; to regulate the addition of new men to the industry; and to establish rules regarding the rotation of gangs and extra men. The sole right of the International 

Longshoremen's Association, as such, was to select and nominate the dispatcher.

The right was guaranteed to the employer to have dispatched to him, when available, the gangs in his opinion best qualified to do his work; he was also given the right to select his men from those eligible under policies jointly determined. Thus, the award recognized the fundamental right of the employer to select the men to perform their work, and the right of nonunion longshoremen to be dispatched without favoritism. 

Shortly after the award was made, and acting through the dispatcher who was selected and nominated by the International Longshoremen's Union, that union seized exclusive control of the Longshoremen's hiring halls up and down the coast. There is not now and never has been any controversy since the award was rendered as to the use of the hiring halls, or the desirability of employing men through the hiring halls, and the employers have no desire to abolish the hiring halls. All that is desired is that the hiring halls shall, in truth and in fact, be jointly maintained and operated. It is only desired that the employers shall be permitted to par- 17

ticipate in the control of the hall and, within the policies jointly determined, select men qualified to perform their work. 

In all ports of the Northwest the joint Labor Relations Committees have been entirely prevented from establishing the rules governing the hall, or the dispatching rules. These rules have been laid down by the dispatcher acting individually, under the direction of the union. 

With few exceptions, not one of the hundreds of non-union longshoremen, who were entitled to employment under the award, have been able to obtain employment through those halls, notwithstanding the clear provision of the award that there should be no discrimination in that regard. The organization of gangs, their rotation, their dissolution, has been entirely controlled by the dispatcher, dictated by the whim of officers of the Longshoremen's Union. The joint Labor Relations Committee has no actual power over them, whatever.

The award guaranteed the employer the right to prefer gangs—the gangs in his opinion best qualified to do his work; in other words, the award preserved the practice that had existed in the past, of the establishment and maintenance of preferred gangs assigned to the work to which they were peculiarly fitted. In every port except San Francisco all preferred gangs have been abolished. In San Francisco they have been seriously reduced in number by union action through its dispatcher and the efficiency of longshore work has been seriously impaired. 

Through the control exercised by the dispatcher, the union has assumed control of the admission of casual men to work. In the port of San Francisco, alone, some 5000 men have been given employment at the whim of union officials, acting through the dispatcher, without the consent of the Labor Relations Committee or of any employer. The employment of those 

5000 men dictated by the union varied in duration from a few hours to approximately fourteen months.

Thus, the award provisions for operation of the longshore hiring halls, and the rights of employers thereunder, have been entirely defeated, although the employers have always contributed one-half of the expense of their maintenance. 18

THE HIRING HALLS FOR SEAMEN 

The award for seamen, of April 10, 1935, provided specifically that the employer should have the right to hire, at his option, on the dock or at the union hiring hall. It also prohibited the union from establishing any system which would interfere with the full freedom of the employer in selecting his employees from among those eligible. 

At the very commencement of relations the hiring from the dock was abolished. Hiring from the dock has never been a right enjoyed by the employer since the seamen returned to work. 

That is relatively unimportant compared with the practices established by the unions in the halls themselves. Each of the three unlicensed unions, that is, the Marine Firemen's Union, the Cooks and Stewards' Union, and the Sailors' Union have established in each of the principal ports on the Pacific Coast, a separate individual hiring hall. No man is permitted to be hired on the docks. Every seaman must be hired through the hall only. A rotation system, expressly prohibited in the awards, has been established in every hall which is under the complete and exclusive control of the unions. 

These unions can deny employment to anyone and by exercise of these powers have deprived employers of all freedom whatsoever in the selection of their employees. Upon the employer is imposed, as a matter of law, the duty to properly man, equip and supply his vessel, as well as the duty to send his vessel to sea in a safe and seaworthy condition; yet, the employer at the present time cannot select men that he deems competent and able to fulfill their duties. Not only is it impossible under the system as it exists today for the employer to obtain any sailors, firemen, or members of the steward's department that he may choose; he must accept only the men chosen by the union because no other person is permitted by the union to accept the position.

As a result of the establishment and maintenance of this hiring hall system, and the exclusive use of it for the benefit of the union, the employers have been deprived of all right of selection of employees; seamen qualified to fill positions and desirous of obtaining them have been unable to secure employment; employers have been compelled to accept seamen 19

who were wholly unqualified to fill their respective positions; and employers have been prevented from discharging seamen who have been found to be incompetent-indeed, who have been found to be dishonest. 

THE SIX HOUR DAY 

Under the National Longshoremen's award it was provided that the first six hours of work between 8 in the morning and 5 in the afternoon should constitute straight time; in the hearings before that Board it was proclaimed to the Board by the union that it had no desire to obtain this provision for the purpose of increasing earnings, and that its sole desire was the altruistic one of increasing employment; it then assured the Board that new shifts would be provided by the union so that eight hours work would be performed at straight time wages as before, and that the work would be distributed among those who needed it. The six hour day has never functioned in that manner. With the exception of a short period of a month or more there has been no time in the course of the relations between the Waterfront Employers Association and the International Longshoremen's Association when new shifts at 3 o'clock could be obtained. By the control of the hiring hall and job action, the employer has been compelled to retain the gangs until 5 o'clock, paying them overtime, and being denied the right granted by the National Longshoremen's Board and promised by the Union that shifts would be changed at 3 P. M. when desired. 

The whole steam schooner fleet of some seventy ships was tied up for three months because, in violation of the seamen's award which had denied the seamen a six hour day and established an eight hour day, the Maritime Unions went on strike against the steam schooner owners up and down the Pacific Coast. That strike lasted for some 55 days, during which time virtually no vessel in the coastwide trade carried cargo on this coast. In addition, there have been sporadic cases of strikes on off-shore ships to obtain the six hour day at sea instead of the eight hour day provided by the awards.

The last of these occurred only a few days ago. 

DELIBERATE LOAFING ON THE JOB 

Another representation vigorously made to the National Longshoremen's Board of 1934 by the Longshore Union was that the West Coast longshoreman was the most efficient longshoreman in the world. Exhibits 20

were submitted to the Board to show that they handled more cargo per hour than did the longshoremen on the Atlantic or on the Gulf, or elsewhere. 

It is reasonable to assume that the increase in wages, which certainly was not otherwise justified, was in fact based upon that plea. Ever since the return to work on July 31, 1934, the Union has been busy to correct this supposed state of efficiency. Upon their return to work there was installed the so-called gang and dock stewards' system on the docks of the Pacific Coast, by which authority to direct work upon the docks passed from the hands of the foreman into the hands of dock and gang stewards. The dock and gang stewards are appointed by the Union. They have an organization of their own. They meet regularly; they adopt rules; they establish the manner in which, and the speed at which, work is to be performed on the waterfronts of the Pacific Coast. Their announced public purpose is to obtain a constantly lessened efficiency. The reduction in sling loads by job action was but one step in that direction. This program has been officially recognized and advocated in the publications of the International Longshoremen's Association itself. It has also been publicly acknowledged in correspondence. 

As a result, today the efficiency of longshore labor has been scandalously affected. The decrease in efficiency varies with different companies, depending to some extent in what degree of disfavor the company may be with the Union officials or with the dock and gang stewards' organization at the particular time.

The Commission is, of course, entirely familiar with the statutes governing work at sea, particularly in the deck and engine room departments, and with the requirements of the three-watch system. The Commission is entirely aware of the fact that under present conditions the employer must divide his watches equally. Although the eight hour day was confirmed in the award, today virtually no work whatever can be secured from the sailors or the firemen between the hours of 5 o'clock in the afternoon and 8 o'clock in the morning, except their watch duties. During these hours the crews will not perform their regular routine duties necessary for the maintenance and upkeep of the ship. 21

INSUBORDINATION AND REFUSAL TO WORK AS ORDERED 

On American ships and American docks there generally prevails a condition of insubordination and refusal to work as ordered. 

There are innumerable instances where longshoremen have deliberately refused to obey orders; of smoking on the docks in deliberate violation of public authorities and public statutes. During the last two years the pilferage of cargo has grown enormously. 

At sea when vessels are remote from the protection of law enforcement officers the condition is more serious. During the last two years deliberate disobedience by seamen of the lawful orders of their Masters, and the deliberate refusal to perform their duties on American ships has been common. In some instances these activities of the seamen have been mutinous in character. Crews have refused to perform lifeboat drills and refused to furnish steam for ship's work in foreign ports, and many of the crews have undertaken to determine for themselves how the ships shall be managed and the duties of the crew performed. The desertion of American ships in foreign ports by their crews has been common. The Commission will be furnished with voluminous data of such innumerable occurrences at sea.

The need of correction of such conduct is mentioned in a decision of 

Arbitrator Harry Hazel, of Seattle, on November 12, 1935, in which he said: "It seems quite clear to me also that because of the number of instances in which employees refused to do the work as directed by the employer, that such action must have been known to and acquiesced in by the International Longshoremen's Association as such.. 

If it be a fact that members of the International Longshoremen's Association well knowing that a definite remedy is provided in the award for the adjustment of grievances, nevertheless wilfully violated the award, some sort of disciplinary measures should be taken against them by the International Longshoremen's Association.”

Again, in connection with a controversy in Port Townsend on August 

25, 1936, Mr. Hazel said: 22

"Because of the foregoing, I make the following Decision and Award. "That, by its refusal to load the Steamship CHARLES L.

WHEELER and the Steamship PETER HELMS on July 30, 1936, and the Steamship F. J. LUCKENBACH on July 31, 1936, the Interna tional Longshoremen's Association violated the terms of Section 11-B of the National Longshoremen's Board award of October 12, 1934, in that the International Longshoremen's Association refused to perform the work as ordered by the employer." 

THE MARITIME FEDERATION 

In April, 1935, a Maritime Federation was formed. This is a federation of labor unions. So far as its component parts are concerned, it suffices to say that all of the seafaring unions and the port unions are members. 

The Maritime Federation recognized the value of the job action policy and endorsed it by a formal resolution, known as "Job Action Resolution." 

By this means the Federation adopted as one of its purposes the use of strikes and stoppages of work for the purpose of violating awards and agreements and obtaining desired concessions. In that resolution, concurred in by all of the unions, members of the Federation, a detailed procedure for the instigation and conduct of job action is provided to the end that it will be effective and yet so controlled as to prevent any general strike. 

That resolution, announced and publicized in the official organ of the 

Maritime Federation, is as follows: "WHEREAS, we believe and have demonstrated on numerous occasions that job action rightly used, with proper control, has been the means of gaining many concessions for the Maritime workers on the Pacific Coast; and "WHEREAS, inasmuch job action is and should be action taken when any group of Maritime workers desire to gain a concession without openly resorting to a strike; and "WHEREAS, in order to eliminate confusion and insure coordination of efforts in the best interests of all Maritime groups concerned, it is apparent that an organized method of procedure for job action be laid down by this convention, therefore, be it "RESOLVED, that the term 'job action' shall mean only action 23

taken by any Maritime group in attempting to gain from their employers some concessions not specifically provided for in their respective agreements or awards, and shall also mean action taken to enforce the award or agreement to the best interests of the Maritime groups concerned, or to prevent employers from violating agreements or awards, and be it further "RESOLVED, that job action should be confined to a job such as a ship, dock, shop or warehouse, unless otherwise agreed by the Maritime groups affected, and any Maritime group affected or liable to be affected should be notified and the issue in question be placed before them, and be it further "RESOLVED, that a committee of all Maritime groups affected on the job be formed on the job to consolidate action and prevent misunderstandings; such committee's authority not to exceed the constitution of the Maritime Federation of the Pacific Coast, and be it further "RESOLVED that when job action reaches a point, in the opinion of the majority of the Maritime groups affected by having their members pulled off the job, and that to go further may jeopardize the Maritime Federation as a whole, the matter shall be referred where and when possible to the District Council for further action or adjustment."

Thus, the Maritime Federation officially endorsed the policy of violating agreements and awards. This policy has also been endorsed in public statements of the leaders of the maritime unions. 

THE AGREEMENT OF APRIL 21st 

On April 15, 1936, the Steamship "Santa Rosa" having arrived in the port of San Francisco with an Atlantic Coast crew aboard, the Maritime

Federation, which includes in its membership the Sailors' Union of the Pacific and the International Longshoremen's Association, established a picket line around the ship to compel the ship to dismiss its Atlantic Coast crew. The Sailors' Union of the Pacific and International Longshoremen's 

Association furnished the picket line in part. This resulted in a stoppage of work on that ship, the longshoremen refusing to work it from the dock, and thereupon the Waterfront Employers Association of San Francisco gave formal notice to the International Longshoremen's Association of dis- 24

continuance of relations with its San Francisco local. A complete tie-up of the port of San Francisco immediately followed. 

On April 21st the Honorable M. C. Sloss, the Coastwise Arbitrator, called a meeting of all parties concerned before the press. He then presented to the various parties a letter relating to compliance with agreements and awards in the future. In that connection he said: "The employers were at fault in failing, until after a considerable delay, to observe the award made with reference to retroactive pay. 

On the other hand, the International Longshoremen's Association, and its members acting collectively, have consistently and repeatedly disregarded the awards of the arbitrator on two important questions: "One. The ruling that the Union, or its members acting collectively, have not the right to refuse to do work, or to stop work, because of any dispute regarding conditions, but that it is their duty to continue to do the work, as directed, pending the settlement of the controversy in the orderly method provided by the award of the National Longshoremen's Board, through the Labor Relations Committee or through arbitration. This presents the issue of 'job action', a procedure which is inconsistent with the carrying out of the award. "Two. The refusal to handle 'hot cargo', cargo affected by some controversy involving labor disputes outside the scope of the award of the National Longshoremen's Board. The rulings of the arbitrator on this question have not been accepted, and the Union and its members have maintained their position that they will not handle such cargo so long as it remains under the ban imposed by other labor organizations." 

At the request of Judge Sloss on that date an agreement was signed by all the parties in which they undertook and agreed to comply with all agreements and awards of the arbitrator in the future. That agreement was executed by representatives of the local International Longshoremen's Association at San Francisco and by its District Officers. Nevertheless, in San 

Francisco alone, since that time we have had not less than twenty-one recorded cases of job action involving either hot cargo or strikes in violation of the decisions of Judge Sloss. Indeed, at the very next hearing before 

Judge Sloss it was discovered that the International Longshoremen's Association was then refusing to load cargo on an Italian ship for transporta- 25

tion to Italy. Judge Sloss was forced at that time to adjourn the meeting for an hour to give the Union an opportunity to restore their men to the job and continue work, on failure of which Judge Sloss stated that he would resign as arbitrator. 

Mr. Tull, arbitrator for Portland, having found that the International 

Longshoremen's Association had repeatedly violated the award and agreement, the Portland Local of the International Longshoremen's Association, at the insistence of the Arbitrator, adopted an official resolution agreeing that any grievance resulting from the manner in which work was ordered should be submitted to the Labor Relations Committee and condemning so-called quickie strikes and other similar action taken by individual members of the International Longshoremen's Association where hot cargo is involved, in violation of the terms and conditions of the award. Pursuant to that resolution the union advised the Arbitrator of its willingness to comply with his decisions in the future, but in that port we find not less than twentyone strikes in violation of the award after these formal assurances to the contrary.

EFFORTS OF EMPLOYERS TO REMEDY THE SITUATION 

The steamship companies and other employers have resorted to every device at their command in an effort to correct the situation. They have followed the procedure prescribed by the award by resorting to the Labor Relations Board, and, where necessary, to arbitration. Decisions both of the 

Committees and the Arbitrators were disregarded and defied by the International Longshoremen's Association and the Maritime Unions. 

In October, 1935, the employers at San Francisco appeared before

Judge Sloss seeking the establishment of adequate mutual penalties to be imposed on any party who might violate the award or any decision of the Arbitrators. The International Longshoremen refused to agree thereto and declined to submit the issue to arbitration, thus causing the whole process of arbitration under the award to break down.

The employers attempted to refer disputes with seafaring unions to the Labor Relations Board. Its decisions were repeatedly ignored by the 26

unions and its members. The employers sought to have a permanent arbitrator appointed in the hope that the situation might be improved, but the unions declined to agree and so the means provided in the awards for seafaring employees to settle disputes became useless. 

The companies appealed to the Federal Government, particularly to the Department of Labor, but were advised that the Government was powerless to act.

Finally, the time approached for the termination of the various agreements on September 30th, this year. Up to that time no means had been found to obtain compliance with agreements and awards. The employers believed that the American Merchant Marine could not survive and adequate transportation service by water could not be provided on this coast, unless, by some means, all concerned were assured that violations of awards and agreements would not continue as they had for the past two years. The proposals of the employers were formulated principally to secure compliance by the unions and their members with their obligations. 

EFFECT ON PACIFIC COAST SHIPPING 

As a result of strikes, job action and other acts of the unions and their members with complete disregard for their obligations, schedules of vessels, both passenger and freight, have been disrupted and the unreliability of service has caused the diversion of traffic to rail and truck carriers. 

Increased operating costs resulting from these conditions have caused enormous financial losses.

Only three lines remain in service out of twelve lines formerly engaged in the coastwise service of transporting general merchandise. Only one out of seven lines that formerly plied between San Francisco and Los Angeles remains. In the applications of intrastate coastwise lines made before the 

Railroad Commission of this State for permission to discontinue service, unsettled labor conditions was one of the main reasons assigned for discontinuance.

The few lines remaining in the coastwise service of transporting gen- 27

eral merchandise find it impossible, in view of the present unsettled labor conditions and excessive labor costs, to carry cargo at a profit. 

Three Pacific Coast steamship companies have been compelled to seek relief under Section 77b of the Bankruptcy Act. One of those lines has been recently directed by the District Court of the United States in Bankruptcy to discontinue service entirely because of their inability to continue business profitably.

Traffic on foreign trade routes, notably British Columbian cargo, has been entirely lost by the American lines and is now transported exclusively by foreign flag ships. 

If the chaotic condition that has resulted from job action of the unions and their members is prolonged, transportation service by water on the Pacific Coast cannot be maintained and the American Merchant Marine cannot survive.

SUMMARY OF OPERATORS' POSITION 

I wish to summarize the position of the steamship operators on the 

Pacific Coast. 

At the conclusion of the maritime strike of two years ago, after some 83 days of violence and bloodshed requiring the militia to restore order, and after San Francisco had witnessed disgrace of a general strike, all parties were persuaded to submit their differences to arbitration. 

Arbitration proceedings were conducted under the auspices of the National Longshoremen's Board appointed by the President, and in due time awards were rendered.

The Longshoremen's Award established a 6-hour day and the highest hourly wage in the world for longshoremen. 

The Seamen's Award prescribed methods of hiring and established the highest seamen's wage in the world. 

Before these and other awards and agreements expired on Sept. 30th of this year, all parties expressed their wish to secure modifications. 28

The first expression of this desire came from the Waterfront Employers Association on July 29th, more than two months before the expiration of the awards. The employers asked for prompt negotiation and offered to arbitrate any differences on which the parties could not agree. 

The unions delayed meeting for more than four weeks. They then proposed many changes and announced that they would not agree to arbitrate. 

On Sept. 30th, at the request of the Maritime Commission, the awards were temporarily extended to October 15th.

On Oct. 13th, the Maritime Commission requested all parties to continue under existing awards and agreements pending a complete investigation and the announcement of public findings. To this the employers agreed immediately, but the unions refused to agree and the shipping industry on the Pacific Coast is now paralyzed by a general strike. 

The position of the steamship companies has not changed. We are willing to submit to this Commission, or any other impartial body, all disputes in issue. The unions decline to accept the peaceful method of arbitration. They are now on strike and refuse to return unless their demands are granted.

If the Commission proposes to ignore the defiance by the unions of the Commission's request and the public interest by striking, and proposes to proceed nevertheless with a general investigation, the Commission's paramount problem is to secure compliance with awards and agreements. In the absence of some means of assuring performance, all awards and agreements are valueless and it will be impossible to restore the stability necessary for the maintenance and development of the Merchant Marine. 

For two years the unions and their members have deliberately violated awards and agreements, indulging in hundreds of strikes and stoppages of work with ruinous effect on the American Merchant Marine for the purpose of depriving employers of fundamental rights, including: 

1. The right of the employer to select his employees; 29

2. His right to a continuance of work, free from strikes or stoppages; 

3. The right to a fair day's work. 

The proposals of the employers are based upon these fundamental rights of which they have been deprived by job action. 

The longshore unions have seized control of hiring halls that were to be jointly managed under the award. The seafaring unions have established hiring halls by means of which the employment of seamen has been wholly controlled by them. The result is that no employer can select his employees. 

On the contrary, each is compelled to accept the employees designated by the union. The employer has been deprived of any effective right to discharge employees for insubordination, inefficiency or dishonesty through the power of the union to refuse a replacement. The steamship company is thus seriously hampered in complying with the duty imposed by law to properly man, equip and supply its ship and send it to sea in a safe condition. 

The unions seek by their proposals to perpetuate these conditions; the employers, by their proposals, to correct them. 

As a result of job action, the employee has ceased to do a fair day's work. The employers by their proposals seek to restore normal conditions; the unions to perpetuate those that prevail today. 

Appeals by the shipowners to arbitrators and the Department of Labor of the United States have been in vain. 

The employers now place their reliance upon this Commission to secure honest observance of awards and agreements by all parties, to restore the right of the employer to choose his employees, and to enable the employee to perform a fair day's work. 

We appear before this Commission prepared to fully represent these questions, the solution of which is essential to the survival of the American Merchant Marine. We are prepared to cooperate by furnishing all information or evidence that the Commission may desire and to give any assistance that may aid in the solution of our difficulties. 30
