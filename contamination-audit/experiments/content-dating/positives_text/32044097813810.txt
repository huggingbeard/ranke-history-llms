The Commonwealth of Massachusetts REPORT ON ESTABLISHMENT OF HISTORIC DISTRICTS WITHIN THE COMMONWEALTH. 

SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

In line with the directive of the General Court, this report is concerned with the subject matter of House, No. 1688 of 1956 which would authorize cities and towns of Massachusetts to establish local historic districts under prescribed conditions. 

PURPOSE OF HISTORIC DISTRICTS. 

The need for preserving the early structures and areas in this nation which are indicative of our cultural, social and economic development has long concerned scholars and historians. This concern has been shared by the federal government which has set aside a variety of national monuments, historic sites and parks for future generations. Related programs have been undertaken by state and local governments to a limited extent. Of importance, also, has been the effort of private associations to preserve early buildings and sites, although the scope of those activities has been. restricted by financial limitations. 

A comparatively recent approach toward the preservation of buildings and areas of antiquarian value has been the establishment of local historic districts and the application of architectural controls therein. Judging by these developments, this legal device is receiving increasing attention as a means of protecting historic neighborhoods from spoilage and disintegration.

HISTORIC DISTRICTS IN MASSACHUSETTS. 

Popular support for historic districts legislation is in evidence in 

Massachusetts. In Boston, an active Beacon Hill Civic Association obtained passage of an act establishing the "Historic Beacon Hill

HOUSE

No. 2953.

District." Elsewhere in the Commonwealth, public approval has been demonstrated by the respective town meetings of Lexington and Nantucket at which special state statutes authorizing historic districts were adopted by unanimous votes. Unanimity of support in Nantucket was particularly notable since the townspeople have traditionally opposed other forms of public control. 

Formerly, legislation for historic areas was considered quite radical, and its constitutionality was in doubt. The latter question prompted a constitutional amendment in Louisiana some twenty years ago so that New Orleans might regulate its historic Vieux Carré district on aesthetic grounds. 

In Massachusetts, legislative concern with constitutionality has been quieted by opinions expressed by the Justices of the state Supreme Judicial Court in 1955 in connection with passage of historic districts bills for Nantucket and Beacon Hill. While the Court indicated that unreasonable use of this legislation might be declared invalid by the courts, the same possibility holds for any exercise of the police power. Certainly, it is a tenet of constitutional law that legitimate use of a power is not prohibited because of the possibility that it may be abused. These legal findings in Massachusetts are in line with recent decisions in the highest courts of the United 

States, of Wisconsin, and of Louisiana.

PROS AND CONS OF PROPOSED ENABLING ACT. 

On the one hand, the Legislature may feel it to be unwise to pass 

House, No. 1688, the enabling act, and thereby risk too great and too rapid extension of the historic district idea. Under local control, it is also possible that some abuses might arise. 

On the other hand, a general enabling act would delegate to individual communities the intimate determination of what they need and want in the conduct of their own affairs. The precedent for such delegation of responsibility for exercise of the police power in matters of local concern is already well established in the Commonwealth. Finally, if local developments prove unsatisfactory, the General Court can prevent their further spread by quick repeal of the proposed enabling act under consideration in this report.

Chapter I. Introduction. 

LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND. 

The omnibus order House, No. 2791 of 1956, which is printed on the inside of the front cover, referred various problems to the Legislative Research Council for study, including the subject matter of House, No. 2547 relative to the establishment of historical districts within the Commonwealth. The latter bill called for a recess study by the legislative committee on State Administration; it was based on the more detailed House, No. 1688, proposing the Historic Districts Act for the Commonwealth which had been introduced previously by Representative John M. Eaton, Jr., of Concord, on the petition of Charles W. Eliot, John M. Eaton, Jr., and another, at the 1956 session of the General Court. House, No. 1688 proposed general enabling legislation to permit any municipality of the Commonwealth to establish historic districts within its corporate limits, and to control the architectural features of the structures in the areas so designated.

Such districts have been established in the Commonwealth within the past two years in Boston, Nantucket and Lexington on the basis of separate statutes. The three municipalities concerned had to obtain passage of separate statutes from the General Court, despite the similarity of the objectives and provisions of each enactment. Meanwhile, without seeking special authorization from the legislature, Salem established historic districts in 1955 under the provisions of a revised zoning ordinance. 

A score or more of other communities throughout the Commonwealth may become interested in establishing historic districts. 

House, No. 1688 was therefore introduced, proposing a general enabling act which would delegate state authority to set up local historic districts and commissions under standardized provisions, to the individual municipalities of the Commonwealth.

PROVISIONS OF HOUSE, No. 1688 OF 1956. 

The purpose of this bill, reprinted as Appendix A, is "to promote the educational, cultural, economic and general welfare of the public through the preservation and protection of historic buildings, places

and districts" (section 2). For this purpose the establishment of an historic district would be authorized by vote of any city council or town meeting after presentation of such a request, accompanied by the "report either by an authority in the field of historic sites (or architecture) or by a special committee” and after a public hearing by the planning board (section 3). • > Thereupon the mayor and council, or the selectmen, would be required to appoint an historic districts commission of three to five members with the overlapping term of one member expiring each year (section 4). This commission must review all plans and related information and pass upon "the appropriateness of exterior architectural features of buildings and structures hereafter to be erected, reconstructed, altered or restored upon the removal of any building. . and [upon the] . . . display of occupational or other signs. ." It shall only act to prevent "developments obviously incongruous to the historic aspect of the surroundings." Buildings may be razed only after the commission has approved a permit to do so, which it shall refuse to do if the removal would be "detrimental to the public interest of the city or town." The development of vacant land, including that vacated by catastrophe, shall be controlled by the commission. The commission shall state in writing any reasons for disapproval, may make recommendations with respect to "design, arrangement, texture, material, color and the like, of the building or structure involved." If the commission does not act within 60 days on an application for permission to modify a structure, the case shall be deemed approved (section 5 (a)–(h). 

Structures may not be changed or razed without a certificate of appropriateness from the commission (section 6). Similarly, all occupational and other signs over two feet long and six inches wide must be approved in advance by the commission (section 7). 

Within 10 days after receipt of an application to change or demolish a structure, the commission must call a public hearing, after giving due notice to "the estates. . . affected"; within 30 days the commission must then approve or disapprove a certificate of appropriateness before structures may be changed. Even if a change is deemed inappropriate, it shall be permitted in specified cases in order to avoid "a substantial hardship to the applicant". Reasons for disapproval "shall be spread" upon the records (section 8).

The bill does not apply to ordinary maintenance, or to the correction of unsafe conditions (section 9). Persons aggrieved may appeal "to the board of appeals acting under the local zoning ordinance or by-law"; such boards may only reverse decisions of the commission by unanimous votes of those boards with four members, or by the concurring vote of all except one member if the board has more than four members (section 10). Any applicant or commission may appeal a decision within 20 days to a superior court sitting in equity. 

Historic commissions or appellants shall only be assessed appeals costs if the court finds gross negligence, bad faith or malice (section 11). The Superior Court shall enforce, act, and may restrain by injunction (section 12). The doctrine of severability shall be in effect (section 13). Violations of act are punishable as misdemeanors by fines of from $10 to $500; each day that a violation continues shall constitute a separate offence (section 14).

Chapter II. Federal Preservation Activities. 

Preservation activities of the federal government have been instrumental in saving a variety of important sites ranging from natural formations and prehistoric remains to forts and mansions of national interest. The program, now administered by the National 

Park Service, has rapidly developed since the passage by Congress in 1906 of an act for the Preservation of American Antiquities. 

Previously, few sites had been set aside for preservation, and those only by special legislation. The so-called “Antiquities Act,' "how- ever, delegated to the President authority to establish as national monuments, federally owned or controlled lands containing historic landmarks, structures and other objects of historic or scientific interest. Under this Act, almost half of the areas now administered by the National Park Service have been set aside since 1906. 

The Historic Sites Act of 1935, passed by Congress as a supplement to the earlier Antiquities Act, provided a more comprehensive basis for a preservation program. This legislation authorizes the

Chapter III. Historic Districts within Massachusetts. 

The General Court of Massachusetts enacted two statutes in 1955 authorizing the establishment of an historic district for Beacon Hill known as "Historic Beacon Hill District"; and of two historic areas in the town of Nantucket, known as the "Old and Historic Nantucket District" and "Old and Historic Siasconset District." 

In the following year, the Court approved similar legislation for districts in the town of Lexington: "Battle Green District", "Hancock-Clarke District", and "Munroe Tavern District". In all three cases, the respective municipalities have acted on these authorizations with much local support, including appointment of the historic district commissions to administer the historic districts. 

Prior to the request for special legislation, groups in all three communities made extensive surveys to determine the areas which should be protected. Because of varying historical background there are basic differences in the districts selected: those of Nantucket have historic associations which are largely economic in nature and reflect the early fishing and whaling enterprise of the community; the Historic Beacon Hill District reflects the social history of early nineteenth century Boston; while the districts in 

Lexington are closely associated with the history of the American Revolution. Each of these districts has a rather uniform architectural style.

Local groups in the three communities undertook studies of advisable changes in the law. Despite independent approaches to this problem, there was great similarity in the provisions of the statutes which they formulated with respect to the important considerations of (a) the establishment of the commission; (b) the regulation of exterior architectural features visible from a public way, including color, texture and material; (c) general procedures for administration; (d) right of appeal to superior court; (e) enforcement; and (f) penalties. These provisions governing the historic district cities are compared in a later full page tabulation.

The city of Salem did not petition the General Court for special authorization to establish historic districts for that city. Instead, its city council approved a revised zoning ordinance in 1955, prepared by the planning board, which provided for "Historical Districts" as one of the separate types of zones. These "Historical Districts" consist of three separate areas of the community, within which "(no) existing building or structure or part thereof shall be altered or enlarged or reconstructed in any way that will alter the exterior architectural design of the building." No special provisions for supervision of these historic areas are included. 

Chapter IV. Historic Districts outside Massachusetts. 

The establishment of historic areas has been approved by the courts on the basis that such neighborhoods acquire particular status by reason of historic associations, and that any incongruous change would damage other properties concerned. Since the protection of property values is directly related to the general welfare objectives of the Constitution, the use of the police power is justified to control undesirable developments.

The exercise of this police power places limitations upon private property similar to those in the case of zoning regulations. However, such limitations do not constitute a taking, and no compensation has occurred where historic districts have been created. 

Although the preservation of historical or architecturally significant areas is comparatively new to Massachusetts, the practice is in effect in eight communities elsewhere in the United States. The "Old and Historic Charleston" district in the city of Charleston, South Carolina, is the oldest, having been established under local zoning regulations in 1924 or 1931, according to two sources. An ordinance of 1937, supported by an amendment to the Louisiana Constitution, provided special protection for the famous, historical 

French section of New Orleans known as the "Vieux Carré." 

Further application of historic districts did not occur until after the second World War. In the mid 40's, three other southern cities followed suit, in Alexandria, Virginia, in 1946; in Williamsburg, Virginia, in 1947; and in Winston-Salem, North Carolina, in 1948.

The three cities all established historical districts under local zoning ordinances.

All of the replies from persons who were city residents when the law was passed, favored it before passage.

3. What have been the main benefits, and who has benefited?

All replies agreed that the law had been beneficial to the area involved, and to the whole city. The architecture had been preserved; and civic pride had increased.

Chapter V. Legal Background. 

The primary purpose of historic district legislation is the protection and preservation of districts or neighborhoods which reflect elements of our cultural, social, economic or political history. This objective is manifested through control of the exterior appearance of buildings and constitutes æsthetic regulation. 

Until recently the courts generally have considered restrictions on the use of private property invalid on the basis of æsthetic considerations, unless it could be shown that they served the fundamental purposes of health, safety, morals or welfare of the public. 

This attitude that æsthetic objectives alone did not constitute a proper exercise of the police power was attributable to the great reluctance of the courts to infringe upon the traditional rights of private property except where the infringement appeared to be in the interests of society. A further reason was the lack of standards

[Mar. 

of measurement without which it was feared that arbitrary and unconstitutional regulations might occur. 

As the scope and nature of governmental activity have expanded to meet new social demands, the courts have been confronted with an increasing number of cases involving æsthetic regulation, particularly when comprehensive zoning is in effect. 

Concurrently with this development, court interpretation as to what constitutes the public interest has been broadened and certain inroads have been made on the general rule subordinating æsthetic purpose to the more tangible objectives of health, safety or welfare. 

This breach has occurred in isolated decisions correlating the use of æsthetic regulation to the general welfare through the protection of property values. (See Lexington v. Govenar, 295 Mass. 31;

Burlington v. Dunn, 318 Mass. 216.) The policy of the courts, however, has been to base judgment narrowly on the particular merits in such cases while generally adhering to the general rule on æsthetics. Nevertheless, many authorities feel that the law is undergoing change as a result of the increasing frequency of decisions recognizing the relationship of æsthetic regulation to the general welfare.

EARLY LOUISIANA DECISION, 1941. 

Because the constitutional aspects of æsthetic regulation have long been in doubt, it was only natural that the establishment of historic districts would be challenged in the courts. Thus far, however, arbitration on this particular matter has reached the higher courts only in Louisiana, where the state Supreme Court has upheld the validity of the ordinance establishing the Vieux Carré District in New Orleans on at least five occasions. HISTORIC DISTRICTS AND MASSACHUSETTS CONSTITUTION. 

The constitutional basis for historic district legislation is contained in the following provisions of the Constitution of Massachusetts:

Art. LI. (General Court may prescribe for taking Ancient Landmarks, etc.).— 

The preservation and maintenance of ancient landmarks and other property of historical or antiquarian interest is a public use, and the commonwealth and the cities and towns therein may, upon payment of just compensation, take such property or any interest therein under such regulations as the general court may prescribe.

Art. LX. (Building Zones in Cities and Towns). — The general court shall have power to limit buildings according to their use or construction to specified districts of the city.

Both of these amendments to the Constitution were ratified and adopted by the people of this Commonwealth on November 5, 1918. 

While they were intended for specific use, i.e., eminent domain in the former, and zoning in the latter, they seem applicable to the establishment of historic districts. 

Further precedent in the regulation of private property for historic preservation exists as the result of a 1935 Massachusetts 

Supreme Judicial Court decision. (General Outdoor Advertising Co. Inc. v. Department of Public Works, 289 Mass. 149.) The court upheld the constitutionality of restriction on signs and advertising devices as provided for in Article L of the Amendments to the Massachusetts Constitution and as promulgated by the Legislature (G. L., ch. 93, §§ 29–33). With reference to the prohibition of signs in two recognized historic areas, the court said (on pages 187-188):

Even if the rules and regulations of billboards and other advertising devices did not rest upon the safety of public travel and the promotion of the comfort of travellers by exclusion of the undesired intrusion, we think that the preservation of scenic beauty and places of historical interest would be a sufficient support for them .... The Debates of the Constitutional Convention of 1917-1918 . . . 

show a current of discussion in favor of the adoption of Art. 50 as a means for the protection of beautiful landscapes and places of historic interest. . . . Those debates afford proof that one purpose of Art. 50 would fail if the regulations for the exclusion of advertising devices from such places cannot be upheld.

In this study no attempt has been made to cover this question of constitutionality in Massachusetts in detail. Hence, further work might develop other aspects than the important material here presented.

Discussion of Massachusetts Court Opinions re Constitutionality of Two Historic District Bills, in Boston University Law Review of November, 1955. Opinion of the Justices, Mass. 

N. E. 2d 557 (1955); Mass. 128 N. E. 2d 563 (1955). "The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court was asked to answer questions propounded by the Senate of the Massachusetts Legislature concerning two acts then pending before that house.¹ 

The acts, now law, concerned the setting aside of two areas, one on the island of Nantucket and the other on Beacon Hill in Boston, as historical districts. Briefly, the purpose of both acts was to preserve the architectural features of the two areas. The acts provided for commissions which would first consider for their approval any proposed change in exterior architecture before the issuance of a permit allowing the property owners to effect the change. Failure to obtain a permit would make an owner subject to severe fines. The acts were not to be an exercise of eminent domain, therefore there was no provision for compensation. Rather the ordinances were designed to retain historic associations, for example, as in the case of Beacon Hill, 'to maintain said district as a landmark in the history of architecture and as a tangible reminder of old Boston as it existed in the early days of the commonwealth.'2 "The Senate's inquiries were, in substance, directed as to whether the acts were within the constitutional exercise of the state's police powers. The problem facing the legislators was that the acts might be interpreted as being tantamount to confiscation of property, which is of course prohibited by the fifth and fourteenth amendments of the United States Constitution. In order to be constitutional, the zoning ordinances must be based on some ac-

Realizing it was faced with zoning laws which had no relation to public health, morals, or safety, the court came to grips with a long-standing problem, i.e., could the acts be sustained on 'aesthetic' grounds alone? They answered this question in the affirmative. This determination by the court represents the final point in a trend which has been underway both in Massachusetts and in other jurisdictions. To appreciate the significance of this decision, it is necessary to look to prior treatment of aesthetic standards by the courts. "In one of the earlier pronouncements on the use of aesthetic standards in Massachusetts, the Supreme Judicial Court's view was an emphatically negative one. The court said then, 'It has been decided quite generally if not universally, by courts in which the question has been raised, that aesthetic considerations alone or as the main end do not afford sufficient foundation for imposing limitations upon the use of property under the police power.' It was not alone in this feeling. In a much quoted decision the 

Court of Errors and Appeals of New Jersey said, 'Aesthetic considerations are a matter of luxury and indulgence rather than necessity and it is necessity alone which justifies the existence of the police power to take property without compensation.' Both these courts possessed a view which, as the Massachusetts court said, was held 'quite generally if not universally.'5 "Why was there such a reluctance to allow the aesthetic to become a determinant in the use of the police powers?
