MISS GERTRUDE ENNES The House Directress of the Elizabeth Sommer Y. W. C. A. 

Washington, D. C.

CHAPTER I 

Carmen was more than ordinarily pretty and attractive. 

Her eyes were brown and wistful, her hair black and wavy, her complexion brown and smooth. Her form was of exquisite beauty from head to feet. Her waist was slim, her hips curved like the crescent of the moon, her breast full and firm in the bloom of maidenhood.

With her shapely arms and legs, small hands and tapering fingers, and trim ankles she was a living, breathing image of Venus di Milo; a form that might, easily, become the delight and despair of any artist who sought to depict it upon canvas. 

Of Carmen it had been said she was wholly unconscious of her attractions-feminine charms over which a poet might rave and strive to immortalize, an inspiration for any sculptor who sought to mould the glorified image of the divine feminine form.

The perfectly moulded features, brown velvety skin and the striking beauty of her smile were a combination that made a deep impression upon the romantic male. In brief she was a figure and a vision that might have graced the pages of the history of beautiful women and, for whose favor, men would have fought and died.

Carmen was also endowed with wealth which made her, in the eyes of some, more desirable.

Her life had always been peaceful and guarded, ever since her mother had placed her in the Convent where the saintly Sisters had taught her to love God, the Church and her parents. Here she was not allowed to associate with young men, to write a letter without the Sisters reading and approving it, nor to read novels pertaining to love affairs.

The long, weary years in the Convent had developed a strange unrest. Now that she was at home she was still closely guarded and chaperoned on all occasions. Men looked at her,

but from a distance. Her reserved manner and the fact she was always accompanied gave them no opportunity to make an approach.

At seventeen she was bored with it all, and she longed for love, romance and freedom such as the other girls of her acquaintance enjoyed. Yes, she had admitted to herself, she wanted all of these things, but she wanted most the thing for which she starved-love.

Why, she wanted to know, was she always compelled to read books dealing with history and religion and denied the novels of love and thrilling episode.

What did it matter that her father was a man of influence and power in the Philippine Islands, Speaker of the House of the Philippine Assembly, Political Leader and President of the Nationalists' Party! Of what avail the money, servants, cars and other material things if she had to remain miserably unhappy. At 

Young men dared not approach her lest they invite her father's wrath or be subjected to great embarrassment. dances, and at social gatherings, it was just the same. 

Her spirit rebelled, she was bored with parental solicitude. 

Why did they treat her as a child when she was now a woman who wanted to love and be loved.

Often during the long, lonely nights, spent brooding in her rooms, she tried to amuse herself by writing love notes to men of her acquaintance and then had burned them fearing her watchful chaperone might find the written yearnings of her heart, in the morning.

She wrote lyrics, expressive of a woman's heart throbs who wanted love and tender thoughts whispered into her ear, bemoaned the weakness and stupidity of men who were not brave enough or wise enough to understand she was lonely. 

And once she put the tender missives under her pillow, hoping they might invoke a dream that would satisfy the longing of her heart.

Carmen found great delight in her new freedom, and in the attentions paid her by Alberto Torres and the other young, college boys. Secretly, she admired the family doctor's son, who had all the attributes of youth and manliness and who had just returned from a three-year Post Graduate course at John Hopkins University. He was the only one amongst her

male acquaintances whom her parents approved of, but he alone, seemed blind to her charms and paid her little attention. This so provoked her she became disappointed with life, itself, and hated all other men who sought her favor. 

One night, on advice of her maid, who understood and sympathized with her moods, Carmen put her shoes under the bed. To do this for three consecutive nights, it was explained, would bring on the third night, a dream of her future lover.

Faithfully, she followed the instructions and on the third night she dreamed, a beautiful dream. The man was white and smartly clad in an officer's uniform. The vision was disturbing because Carmen realized that any alliance with a man of another race was impossible. Her people, and her parents would not approve. They were against intermarriage, holding their race was superior to all others, and that the daughter of the Speaker of the House should not lower herself by marrying anyone of a different order. She had reasons to know that walking or even riding with white men who had tried to court her would not only be frowned upon by her parents, but would lose her the respect of her own proud people.

Thus restricted in choosing her society Carmen was often lonely and unhappy, and the long days proved empty and uneventful.

Finally there came the great Reception in honor of the departure of the Governor General and Hon. Ramon Gonzales, Carmen's father, was chosen as one of the Reception Committee. It was not only to be a gala event, but an official affair and an order had been issued that all members of both houses, the Senate and House of Representatives and Provincial Governors were expected to attend. 

Carmen eagerly made plans to attend. Following the elaborate banquet there was a dance with American Army and Navy officers and other officials present. Amongst these was 

Captain Nolan, a young and handsome officer of the 4th Artillery of Quartil de Spania, who had recently graduated from 

West Point with the highest honors of his class.

Captain Nolan was a Southerner with a strong antipathy for the colored race and who boasted of ancestors who had come to this country on the Mayflower.

During the dance he found his glance straying often to the

beautiful brown girl, who, when she was not sitting quietly beside her middle-aged chaperone, danced with her father. 

And, as he took pains to observe, danced beautifully and gracefully. He knew her father as President of the Senate but wondered why all the young officers did not dare to ask her for a dance. In Carmen he saw a girl different and more appealing than all other girls he had met, a bit old-fashioned, but charmingly like the women of his own race. 

Requesting an introduction from an Aide-de-Camp he soon found himself bowing before her and asking the pleasure of a dance. Carmen smiled her consent. Once, twice, many times during the evening they encircled the great hall to the romantic strains of the music while her parents looked on disapprovingly and the outraged chaperone sought, vainly, to have her stop.

Carmen was openly rebellious. She admired Captain Nolan because unlike the other young men present, he had the courage to ask her to dance. And besides, she mused, Captain

Nolan's attention would serve to open the eyes of one young, Doctor Velligas, who had been too indifferent. 

Thus did Captain Nolan, with white blood in his veins, come glamorously, into Carmen's life.

When he asked to see her again she was obliged to tell him she was not allowed to leave the house unchaperoned. "My parents and some of my friends, not to mention my chaperone," she told him, "did not approve of my dancing with you.' ""

In response to his pleadings to come with him for a ride some afternoon with her chaperone, Carmen said she would consider it. "If I am able to manage it," she added, "I will come alone, tomorrow afternoon and meet you at the Bay Shore."

Carmen kept the tryst but not without some misgivings. 

She was conscious that Captain Nolan was an officer and a white man, that the fair, blue-eyed women of his race were free to go their way unchaperoned, and that he would find a timid Filipino a bit difficult. Her fears, however, were set at rest when he told her she was different and delightful, and she laughed happily.

Time passed quickly and then Carmen reminded him she must return. It was nearly seven, the hour for the Gonzales'

evening meal, and it was rule and a custom that all members of the family must be present. So, they took leave of one another, regretfully. The afternoon along the shore had satisfied her longing for romance, and romance, she believed, was the greatest of all things in a girl's life.

The thought that thrilled Carmen was that notwithstanding the handicaps of an over-exacting chaperone, Captain Nolan had asked her to go to Bay Shore again-swimming. She laughed when she recalled how amused he was when she told him she could not do as the American girls did, wear a bathing suit that revealed her bare arms and legs. And he had thought it strange because she thought it was a sin. 

The urge to keep the appointment grew until Carmen decided to go to her mother and tell her she wanted to go out alone. Her mother objected until Carmen explained she would drive her own car as she wanted to take Christina, her classmate, to church and confession. 

On her way, Carmen's conscience troubled her. She had told her mother a lie and, perhaps, had taken the name of the Church and the Lord in vain, but she knew there was no other way to get out unchaperoned.

Captain Nolan was waiting for her at Pasay Beach and after finding a place of vantage they sat together watching the sunset pale to gray twilight. As she gazed, dreamily and with half-closed eyes, off to the broad expanse of waters, Captain Nolan waxed eloquent and she gave him rapt, sympathetic attention as he told of the Negro Mammy who had raised him, his plantation, and his loved Virginia, miles and miles beyond the ocean. He likened the blue of the sky to the blue eyes of the women of his native land and expressed his love of his profession.

He explained his love of the sea in all its changing moods; its restful calm and fury and would like nothing better than to sail away to unknown and unchartered waters. 

The sea made him lonely, and he was sorry he had not gone to Annapolis, instead of to West Point. It was his parents who had willed otherwise.

She listened quietly, attentively, but with some apprehension, to this outburst of confidence. "Carmen," he said fervently, "you are different from other girls I have known, even different from the girls of your own race."

There was a passionate longing in the eyes that looked into hers, and alarmed, she stirred uneasily. "Don't be afraid," he assured her. "I want to be frank, even though it does seem so unconventional." "I must remind you, Captain Nolan," she said prettily, "you are the first man whom I have ever met without a chaperone and I am only eighteen. You know the customs to be observed by a Filipina girl and I should have brought along my chaperone."

Both laughed. He moved closer and whispered in her ear. "Seeing as how we are both distinguished persons, we are exceptions to the rule." "Perhaps," she answered, "but mother thinks I am in

Church, confessing my sins. Instead of a priest I meet a man at the Bay Shore-alone! "My people have different ideas about love, marriage and the conventions. They believe a man never marries a girl who is easy to get, or who kisses or flirts, because he will think she is not a good girl and will never be faithful to him. "If a man wants to marry a girl," she added, "he will never ask a kiss from her. And, if a man has good intentions, he will not demand anything, not even to hold her hand." 

Captain Nolan laughed. "Your people," he said, "have queer ideas." "True," she answered, "but we have less divorces." 

He looked at her. "I like you," he said, frankly. “You are truly an interesting girl." As he moved closer, he was conscious of her face turned up to him, her eyes glowing, her lips parted expectantly. Then the soft pressure of her mouth brought him to his senses. He gently pushed her from him. "You said that I am different," she sobbed. "I am. I made you kiss me because I know you would not marry a brown maiden. You made me love you well enough to give myself to you, but I know you will not marry me, you would not lower yourself to marry a girl not of your own people, not even if she be an heiress. I made you kiss me because you are not like my own people, because I wanted the thrill; the romance, and love I have been deprived of; love I could not get from my own people. I wanted to be in love like other girls."

He drove the car home as Carmen sat in silence. He did

not look at her. He never spoke. When at last they were nearing the Quartil de Spania, he stole a glance in her direction. Her features were grim and set; her eyes downcast. "Sorry Carmen," he said, as she stopped the car, "I should not have done it. It was all my fault." 

"It wasn't," she answered curtly. "Why shouldn't you have kissed me?" "Well, it was not right and you are only a child." "I made you do it," she persisted. "No, Carmen, it was the waves, the sea, the ocean. I hope you like me a little." "I like you very much," she answered tremulously, tears welling into her troubled eyes, "but I don't believe I mean any more to you than the man-in-the-moon does to me. I like you because you are not afraid like my own people; not afraid of my father. Our young men are cowards, but you are not." "You only think you do," he said, gently. "I think I understand," she countered. "I love you much, as you must know, but I am not so blind I cannot see you do not care much for me. Our companionship has meant everything to me. I shall always be your debtor, and always remember the happy afternoon spent together." "Carmen, don't," he protested. "It has been that way with me too. But I want you to start out with the whole world to choose from. I want you to be free. I am not good enough for you." "Yes, I understand," she said simply. "I am a brown maiden; you are a white man." "No, it is not so, Carmen!" "Please," he begged, his hand on hers, "let's not make this parting a sad one. I want to remember it, always. If ever you should want me, I will come. I am afraid of and hate your father-sometimes. He thinks he is everything; can run the whole Philippines. I sometimes wonder if the silly ideas of your father and those of your people will last. Ï hate myself for stealing a kiss from you." "So you are another one thatback and slapped him full across the face. 

She threw her arms "You could not steal a kiss from me," she added spiritedly. "You or any man! I kissed you because I wanted to, and

because I thought you were different from the cowardly men of my people. But you are-race prejudiced. I made you kiss me because I wanted you, too. I slapped you because 

I wanted to, but I hate you, now."

Then she stepped into her car, threw in the clutch, and the car shot forward, raising a cloud of dust around Captain Nolan, left standing helplessly in the road. 

Carmen lay sleepless that night, her brain a seething furnace, her soul racked by a tempest of rage-rage at Captain 

Nolan, at herself, at fate.

With the passing hours, reason returned to a penitent Carmen. "It was not his fault," she finally decided as she sat up with a jerk. Oh, what a fool she had been! Love was cruel. 

Hate could have been more kind.

She wakened to a flood of sunshine across the floor. Teresa her maid was preparing her bath. Bathing and dressing, quickly, she went to the dining room but found she could not eat. "Eat your breakfast," urged Teresa. "I am not hungry," said Carmen. "Any healthy girl is hungry in the morning," ventured the maid, "unless she is in love, and you are not, I know." "But I am, Teresa," confessed Carmen, "you know I am. 

Do I show it-that plain?" "Dios mio, no." She drew her down beside her. "Tell me, hija, who is the man?" "Teresa, you are the only one who really sympathizes with me, who really understands. I know that you have loved me ever since I was a tiny baby, so I am going to tell you my secret, but if mother ever knew, she would kill me.

I am getting tired of everything, Teresa; tired of the convent; tired of the Sister who teaches only love of God, Church, and parents and that it is a sin to love men. "I want to be loved like the rest of the girls. I want to have freedom. I cannot go out unless I have a chaperone, and I am getting so tired of Margarita. Everything I do is wrong. I cannot even look at any man without the old buzzard scolding me. "For the past week I have been meeting a man at the Bay Shore pretending to go to church and confession to be alone,

but the truth is I never go to church. I love this man, Teresa, but he is just like the rest who fear my father. I like him like inferno; he is an American officer." "Oh, Dios mio!" exclaimed Teresa, "an Americano!" "Yes, an Americano; but he has not race prejudice, I know that!" "Oh, hija, you must not do that," warned Teresa. "The 

Americano will never marry a brown maiden; you are just a plaything to him.” "How do you know?" exclaimed Carmen, angrily. "You have not seen him." "I know, hija, but your father will never consent. If you marry him, and his intention is good, he will take you to his country far away from your own people, from your parents, from Teresa. You will be alone in a far country; think of that Carmen. You must not do that; esta is culpa de Dios." "Everything is culpa de Dios to you," said Carmen, rebelliously. "Everything is culpa de Dios to all of you." 

Tears overwhelmed her. "Forgive me," said Teresa, "you must not take it too hard. 

I was only giving you a little advice. Of course, if you love him, and he loves you, go ahead, but you must be careful.

All of us have had this experience. Look at me, I should have been very happy if I had not followed all that they told me to do. I was once in love with a very nice young man whom I used to meet by the brook; we lived in the country. 

This boy was our neighbor, but we were not allowed to talk to each other. One night he came serenading under my window and father chased him with a bolo, then at midnight when my father and the rest of the family were sound asleep, I used to sneak from the house and meet him. We talked of eloping, so one night he put a ladder under my window to take me away, but father caught him and beat him with bejoco, then I was sent to the convent. I was not allowed to see anybody; we did not see each other since that night.

I thought I would escape from the convent, so I wrote to him, but the Sister got the letter. When I was able to send him a message, he was sick; badly injured from the beating that father had given him. When he recovered, he came to the convent disguised as my brother but the Sister became suspicious, and would not allow him to see me. Later, he

For months tried to enter as a muchacho, but did not succeed. One night he climbed to the corral of the convent but the guerrilla saw him, and thinking him a burglar shot him dead. I could have thrust a dagger in my heart. I was miserable, unhappy. wanted to die, there seemed nothing to live for. He died because of me, he died for love; his love for me.

I sat, every night, fighting myself, thinking of revenge upon the guerrilla who killed him. I have sat with a dagger in my hand awaiting an opportunity but each time I was restrained by the still, small voice of conscience. I conquered myself! 

Do you understand?" "I guess you are right," answered Carmen, meekly, “but what shall I do?" "Go on as before," was the reply. "Instead of letting disappointment upset you make it strengthen your character; stiffen your will." "But what did you do, finally, Teresa? Tell me please! 

I want to know." "It was not easy," she said, sadly. "I thought the best thing was to forget everything; live a new life, but it has been difficult-very difficult. "I could not forget him, could not make up my mind to love another man, so here I am, old and unhappy. When my parents died I had no place to go. Your mother, looking for a nurse maid, went to the convent and asked the Sister to send her an orphan. The Sister sent me. But your case

Carmen, is different; you are young, have health, beauty, and money, and everything money can buy. I was poor. So try to forget him, and start fresh again. Pretend you have never known him." "I will," said Carmen, jumping to her feet. "I will start afresh, and some day he may come to me, even though it takes years and years."

CHAPTER II 

The summer dragged along and Carmen often recalled the last meeting at the Bay Shore with memories of Captain Nolan always confronting her. What fate awaited her, she wondered. 

Before she fully realized it, the day arrived to move to their summer home in Bagio; the night had become uncomfortable in Manila.

Bagio swarmed with teachers. The white flannels of the men and the multi-colored gowns and parasols of the girls gave it a lovely, festive air. They strolled in pairs and sat in groups. Laughter and happy faces were everywhere. The spirit of it was infectious, but Carmen was miserable and unhappy. She read books in the library. Books from every source dealing with love and romance were devoured with the hunger of an aching heart. Her final resolve was not to be so robbed of love again. She went from luncheons to tea dances at the Pine Hotel, from tea to dinner, and from horseback riding to a long trip in her expensive foreign car her father had given her as a birthday present.

Soon Carmen was allowed to drive her car alone, and to go horse-back riding, unattended. Her mother was busy with bridge parties and the chaperone was ill. She tried hard to keep Captain Nolan out of her thoughts; to forget they had ever met.

Captain Nolan had been transferred to Camp John Hay. 

His thoughts were of Carmen and their last meeting at the Bay Shore. He had taken to gambling and drinking, and hated himself for his racial prejudice. He was associating with private soldiers, gambling and drinking with them. 

One afternoon Carmen took her car and drove around Camp John Hay, but before she reached the camp she saw an officer who looked familiar, walking towards the hotel. stopped, and the car stopped too. It would do no harm, she told herself, to learn if he still liked her, and valued their friendship. 

He had loved her from the first, he told her. Then, "Where have you been hiding since that night at the Bay Shore? I

have been looking all over for you. I went to the Bay Shore every afternoon thinking I might see you there." "I did not know you cared that much," she answered. Fear was beginning to creep into her soul. She loved him and he loved her. She heaved a deep sigh. "Step into the car," 

Carmen invited. "I will take you for a ride." "No, let us go to the hotel-and dance," he countered. 

Carmen drew a deep breath. "Well, step in, and let's go."

They rode in silence, then Captain Nolan spoke. "It is nice to see you again, Carmen. I'd give anything if I could dance with you the whole night." He took her in his arms.

Carmen stopped the car, but did not resist. 

At the hotel they danced and danced again, the music vibrating as each sensed some change in the other; a new strength and quality seemed to endow Captain Nolan and Carmen thrilled to it, as her body relaxed, and came closer to his. Her breath came hotly, her blood coursed feverishly then by mutual consent they ceased dancing, and together, they descended the wide steps of the Pine Hotel into the garden. This beautiful garden is a feature of the famous Pine 

Hotel, with its tropical shrubs and flowers. Beyond, and running parallel was the famous "Zig-Zag," equally well advertised, on souvenir postcards. This avenue of stately trees is a veritable lovers' lane, and it affords an enchanting view of the Camp John Hay. Time has made it even more beautiful and popular amongst those sentimentally inclined. 

Here Carmen and Captain Nolan found themselves, without knowing which one had led the way. As a matter of fact both had moved unconsciously toward the path that led out of the light and into the gloom. Here they paused and faced each other as if by some unspoken signal. Even in the semidarkness each was revealed to the other. 

He took her in his arms, brushing his lips to hers, with ruthless passion overpowering him while the girl responded to his caresses, unreservedly. It was capitulation for both, conquest, the sudden bursting of all restraint. Love's inhibitions, and the ecstasy of it all was keener than a knife thrust; intoxicating, maddening, an outpouring of emotions strong enough to paralyze the very process of thought. 

People passed, voices called, but neither Captain Nolan nor Carmen heard. They were entranced. While they sat in

Carmen's car, time passed. It grew darker in the garden all around them. Then suddenly they realized that the lights had been turned off in the hotel and only the light of the stars remained. "A thousand times, I've dreamed about holding you like this," Captain Nolan murmured. "I seem to experience feelings I've always known. I've thought about you, constantly, ever since that first night we met. Every night I have fallen asleep haunted by your voice. It was like the music of some stringed instrument singing in the moonlight. I have breathed the fragrance of your hair, its scent like the sweet-scented honeysuckle of my native country." "But" Carmen exclaimed. Captain Nolan interposed. "Don't tell me anything darling. What does it matter? 

What does anything matter? Is it about your father again?” "No, Harvey, tonight I am not his daughter. I am just flesh and blood, there is nothing, nothing sane or sensible about me. 

You might be a stranger, an American, it won't make any difference."

When finally the two awoke, Captain Nolan faltered as he peered at his watch. "I had no idea of the time. What will your father say?"

Carmen laughed and drew his arms closer about her waist. "Let us elope," Captain Nolan suggested. "Darling, darling," his voice was low in her ears, she raised her lips again, and he crushed them as if he'd drink the life from her throat, "Will you marry me?" he begged hoarsely. "Yes," she whispered. "Now?" "Yes, we will elope tonight." "Oh, Harvey, I-I want to-" 

He kissed her uncounted times, then threw in the clutch of her car.

On and on they sped in the night; Carmen's father and mother and her chaperone were forgotten. Each was wrapped in the other. A few hours later they were on the pier and then on their way to Hong Kong.

After Carmen's elopement and their honeymoon, Captain Nolan was ordered to report for duty at Fort McKinley, a few miles out of the city limits of Manila. Carmen was considered a disgrace to the Gonzales family. She was the topic of the gossips of the community, for her father was a wealthy

22 and influential man. Hon. Ramen Gonzales had lived and ruled Philippine politics for forty eventful years. He had succeeded his father and had come to his fortune at twentyone, while he was still in the college in Madrid. The urge of necessity had never been his. The death of his father left him political power and he was sole heir of the vast estate, the sugar plantation and other properties inherited from his 

Friar uncle, a priest.

Hon. Ramon Gonzales found himself, for the first time in his life, encumbered with responsibility. He was fond of politics. He had no very definite ideals about his fortune, or the kind of training he needed, for he was no student. He was spoiled and wayward, and ruled the people like his father. 

He had no training, of the kind he needed, other than the inherent ideals of his class-that he should play fair, hit straight in a fight, and have regard for other people. He was an aristocrat in every fibre. The people were as remote as dwellers in Mars; to him they were a part of the ugliness and stupidity of the world. Business he scorned. He left the administration of his estate to men who understood the grimy game, but ruled with an iron hand. He surrounded himself with exquisite things and luxury, and to his inferiors his word was law. His sugar plantation exported a million tons every month. He indulged himself in numerous hobbies, and his hacienda was famous for its size and appointments. The house itself he had filled with treasures; in his garage were nothing but high priced cars. Ramon Gonzales in short, was prodigiously successful and he lived up to this prestige and swore he would never forgive Carmen for marrying an Americano; nor would he ever want to see her again.

In Manila, Carmen's friends avoided her; and even her closest friends and relatives snubbed her. Lowest class of

Filipinos refused to recognize Carmen as belonging to their race. Her mother was prostrated, she knew that Carmen would never be hers again. Her husband would take her away from her and to a strange country. The question of how the girl met the Americano grieved her for she had watched her closely. 

Captain Nolan received a cablegram from his mother complaining of the suddenness of his marriage to a Filipino. 

This anticipation of trouble with Mrs. Nolan Sr. was not the only rift in the perfect bliss of the situation. Captain

Nolan also received an order to leave in the Transport Thomas for Presidio.

It was the first time for Carmen to be away from her native land; away from her people. It was hard for her to leave her parents at first, but she could not bear to see Captain Nolan leave without her.

The Transport Thomas docked at the pier in San Francisco, and Carmen stepped out into a new world; to her an enchanted land. The people were different from her people; white and not brown. Everything was different from where she came. It was all like a Fairy Tale. To her, everything was wonderful. It took a little time for Harvey to give instructions to the expressman about the delivery of the trunk.

They took a taxi and Captain Nolan suggested they should go first to the Golden Gate Park. They were in the business district now, then they began to wind through the park. The trees were different from the trees she had seen; many hundreds of years old. The Eucalyptus trees, lily-lined hedges, gardens, flowers, and shrubs were new to her. They went to 

Twin Peaks and Captain Nolan pointed out the business district, then they descended to the Hotel Whitecomb. She accompanied him to the elevator and into the great open hall and up the wide stairway to a suite of rooms at the end of the second floor. "These are the rooms, sir," said the clerk. "Thank you," said Captain Nolan. "These will do very nicely, Harvey," said Carmen. "There is another room on the top floor if you prefer,” said the clerk. "No," responded Carmen, "I prefer this room, thank you."

The clerk withdrew, Carmen closed the door, then she flew about upon a tour of inspection, while Captain Nolan, amused, watched her.

She drew a deep breath of enjoyment and went to the chair where Harvey sat and kissed him. "Harvey!" she exclaimed, rapturously. Then she laughed. She was excited. She had never seen such nice things; she had never dreamed of such a fairyland as she found herself in. She took off her coat and tossed her hat on the bed. It disclosed a cropped, curly head. She ran a comb through it, dashed some water on her face, brushed her skirt and hurried again to Harvey.

"You look so different, and pretty, today," he exclaimed. "I have to make myself pretty," she answered, "or else I will lose you. There are plenty of nice American girls that you might prefer to me," she added, laughingly.

After a while Harvey left her in the hotel and went to the 

Presidio to report to the Commanding Officer.

CHAPTER III 

Carmen stood at the open window of the Hotel Whitcomb overlooking the Market Street. The fact the window stood open gave her a feeling of freedom which amounted almost to intoxication; she was away from the snobbery of her own people. Soon she became restless and homesick. Something rebellious stirred within her, a thousand mischievous tongues were whispering in her ears; her thoughts were as unruly as wild igorots. She went to the mirror to ask herself: "Was it that my family was right?" She realized she could not go home; her father would not forgive her; her friends would ignore her; her own people snub her. As she sat by the window she could hear voices in the lobby, girls' voices, laughter.

A bell tinkled discreetly, then came the bellboy. "Come in," she said.

He entered the room, bowed and announced that Captain 

Nolan was calling on the telephone.

CHAPTER X 

After a vain search for Carmen, Captain Nolan asked for a transfer to Panama Canal. He was not the same; he had become moody and temperamental; one moment loving and lovable, the next hateful. Towards the Commanding Officer he was by turns obedient and rebellious, humble and domineering, and always restless.

A few months after his arrival, he again asked for a transfer. 

He was discontented, yearning for California, wanted to be there, and even though he could not find Carmen, thoughts of her being near would be soothing.

After her disappearance, he was not content to remain in one place long enough to get acquainted. If ever there was a stormy petrel a pigwidgeon blown upon the wings of a tempest, it was Captain Nolan. His comrades could not make him out; sometimes he gave orders contrary to military regulations, and sometimes he changed all plans without authority. 

He refused to obey the orders of the Commanding General and, as a result, his resignation was demanded. He left without delay, arranged to go direct to New York, then changed his mind and went to California.

He soon became a regular and extravagant patron of San Francisco dance halls. At one dance hall he found white girls in the arms of Chinese, Japanese, Mexican and Filipino men.

This evoked astonishment at first, then resentment. It seemed so abnormal, so deeply repugnant, and yet he could not tell why. The place was not unlike other public dance halls. The men were not all Americans nor white men.

The dances lasted only four to five minutes; the intermission even less. The orchestra stopped playing barely long enough for the floor to be cleared and for more tickets to be collected. These tickets cost ten cents each, the dancing girls received fifty per cent of what they took in and sometimes made five to ten dollars a night, besides the gifts from the 

Asiatic boys.

A month after, Captain Nolan was a physical wreck. Not

being able to find Carmen he started drinking again. He had no serious interests. All he cared about was gambling and drinking; two cravings it was impossible to satisfy. His face became bloated and spotty; there was a strained look around his eyes, and his shoulders stooped as if they supported a weight too heavy for them to carry. CHAPTER XIII 

The funeral over, Carmen and Dr. Villegas returned together from the cemetery. Through it all she had a feeling it was something belonging to Dr. Villegas that had been laid in the earth. When she tried to thank him, he passed it off lightly as something hardly deserving of her gratitude. It was the first time in many years that she had walked with a man of her own people and she was conscious of feeling absurdly young and girlish. A soft flush, the flush of youth tinted her pale cheeks.

Would his presence quiet this strange, new restlessness in her heart; a restlessness which had come upon her after the death of her husband.

Was it true that her youth had fled before she had grasped all the happiness that life offered? CHAPTER XIV 

She had changed, greatly, since that night she eloped with Captain Nolan. Her moods were no longer gay, but languorous, and wistful. There were haunting thoughts that recalled fugitive seasons; what was once immortal and ineffable beauty.

The enchanting crystal had been shattered and she saw life, not imprisoned in magical sunshine, but gray, sordid, and monotonous; as utterly hopeless as the faces that haunted 

Market Street. Yet it was not so long ago that these faces had reflected inward hope; the joy in sadness, the gaiety in disappointment, that had brightened the world for her. She had sensed an invisible current flowing from the crowd to herself, but today when she passed the same street this contact seemed broken and she felt detached, adrift, and distrustful of the future. That bond of understanding friendship for the crowds and the life of the city that had come to her first on the brilliant morning when she had arrived from Manila, and the first day she began to work as a social worker, and which had given her a fresh perception of human responsibility, this had deserted her so utterly that she could barely remember its miraculous visitation. Her personal life had seemed a part of the life of the factory district, part of the life of the street, of the sky, of the mysterious city outlined against the gray background of dusk. 

Today she walked alone, and without sympathy, through the crowd. Her feet dragged, and she felt dully that she had lost her share in both the street and the sky. The faces of the men and women around her, those lethargic foreign faces that crowded out the finer American type, awoke in her a sensation of hopeless revolt which one feels before the impending destruction of higher forms by masses of inert and unfeeling matter. She thought gloomily: 

I have lost the vision, there is for me no hope of happiness unless I go home and never see anybody. There is no hope for me unless I stay home and live like a nun. And, while she thus reflected there passed over her a vague feeling of the

loss of something missing; as if a precious possession had slipped from her grasp.

Her morning work was unusually trying. She went first to the factory district before going to the hospital. As she made the rounds she asked herself, dejectedly: What is the matter? 

These people no longer interest me. If I could only make up my mind to take more interest in my work, I would not feel so depressed. What a wreck, what an inconceivably stupid wreck she had made of her life. As she entered the 

County Hospital, she encountered a nurse; it was Miss Jane

Her figure, in her silk-rayon uniform looked scarcely a day over twenty. The peach-bloom texture of her skin was as unlined by care or pain as if she had spent the last twenty years with the nuns in a convent. In her case, at least, Carmen realized, the retribution which awaits the sinner had been tardy in its fulfillment.

As she moved toward the ward, disregarding the hand Miss Polly held out, Carmen was conscious of an inclination to laugh. Though she felt no bitter resentment against her, for after all she had not been able to hurt her, there struggled in her bosom an indignation that was more profound. This resentment was directed not against Miss Polly, but against some abstract destiny which had permitted her to have her way without paying the price.

Then the memory of Captain Nolan's face, as it looked in death, floated before her vision, and she reflected, grimly, that tradition was not always the mirror of life. In this case, the man, not the woman, had been the victim of natural law, and Miss Polly, fool though she was, had shown herself at the hour of requittal to be stronger than fate. By that instinctive wisdom, which is so much older, so much truer than civilization, she had triumphed over the ordination of life. In refusing to suffer she had blunted every weapon with which nature might have punished her in the end. Not by virtue, since she had none, but by pure insensibility, she had escaped the wages of sin. She was a sensualist whose sensuality, hard and metallic, had encased her like armor. 

The meeting with Miss Polly appeared to Carmen, as she walked to the car line, to be the last touch needed to push her into a state of utter despondency. The oppressive heat

of the day had exhausted her strength. It was still light when she alighted from the street car. As she strolled, listlessly, down Market Street, she felt again that curious sensation of smothering which had come to her after her talk with Dr. 

Villegas. 

"I would like to go back to Manila," mused Carmen. And, as she caught the fugitive sweetness of the hyacinths there rushed over her again the feeling that life was slipping, slipping, and that she was missing something infinitely precious, something infinitely desirable. Was it the panic of fleeting youth, of youth unsatisfied, denied, and still insatiable? 

As she entered her apartment, she felt a contraction in her throat as if she had become suddenly paralyzed and was unable to swallow. I hope he has gone, she thought; everything will be easier. But even to herself she could not explain what it was that would be made easier. 

When Miss Benton met Carmen, she stared at her anxiously. "What on earth is the matter with you, Carmen?” she exclaimed. "Nothing," said Carmen, "I have had a hard day, and I am tired." "Well, come into my room and have supper with me." 

Carmen flung herself down upon the davenport, and burst into tears. "Well, I never," ejaculated Miss Benton. "Something must be wrong, for it is not like you to give away. Are you sure you haven't a pain somewhere?"

Carmen shook her head, and swallowing her sobs with an effort, arose to her feet. "I am just tired out, that's all," she said humbly. "You must have been working hard. It is not right; you have plenty of money, and you don't need to work." "I am going home, Miss Benton," said Carmen. "If I am able to pack up in time I might go tomorrow; there is a boat that leaves in the afternoon-the President Lincoln."

It was midnight when she finished packing her trunks. She felt a girlish thrill of happiness, of restlessness, of uncertainty; a vague yet poignant longing in her happy and exultant heart. 

In obedience to the law of her nature, which decreed she should move swiftly and directly toward the end of her des-

tiny, she was returning to Dr. Villegas as resolutely, as unswervingly, as she had fled from her own country. "It is strange how little I have ever understood," she told 

Miss Benton. "I have gone on in the dark, making mistakes and discoveries from the very beginning, undoing and doing over again, creating illusions and then destroying them, always moving, always changing, always moving in new directions. 

A year ago, I would have laughed at the idea that I could love any man but Captain Nolan, but now of all men I love only Dr. Villegas. He holds the key to my future; he, alone, is capable of renewing my youth."

It was five o'clock when Carmen left her apartment, called a taxi and instructed the driver to take her to the boat landing. On the way she began to wonder if Dr. Villegas had booked passage, if she would meet him and what he would say. Perhaps it might be best to make it appear like a chance meeting. If she missed him they would meet in Manila soon. 

She would devote her time to social work; teaching her people what she had learned in the United States. She would devote her time to charity work, and perhaps meet Dr. Villegas in the hospitals, at social gatherings or elsewhere; when she met him, for meet him she must, everything would be all right. 

Her eyes were on the street where a multitude of children were swarming. From this outpouring of the young and ardent, reflecting life, she gathered new confidence, to make a new beginning, to live, love, create, and conquer.

The taxicab turned swiftly into Golden Gate Park. A sudden fear overtook her, suppose Dr. Villegas would not go back to the Island and decided to live in this country for the rest of his life. He did not tell her where to write him. Suppose he went to Japan or China?

As she pondered these possibilities, apprehensively, and reviewed her past experiences, her pulses drummed in her ears, and her throat contracted until she struggled for breath, yet now, as in every crisis of her life, she was stirred to action. 

She cast aside the mandates of tradition. Tradition, she knew, bade her sit and await destiny or until she had faded like Miss Benton. But something mightier than tradition, something which she shared with the adventurous multitude of children in the street, the will to live, to strive, and to conquer, this had risen superior to the empty rules of the past.

With her hand on the door of the taxicab, she spoke to the driver. "Drive to the nearest drug store as fast as you can. I must telephone."

As the taxicab drew up beside the pavement, she saw Dr. 

O'Brien standing at the drug store, his hands in his pockets, staring into the street. As she alighted, he came forward quickly to ask, "Have you been seeing Dr. Villegas off, too?" "No, Doctor," she answered breathlessly, "has he gone? 

Where is he going?" "To Manila, but he intends to practice medicine in China." "Then I better say good-bye to him," Carmen answered as she hurried back to the waiting cab. As the taxi started forward, she clasped her hands on her knees, her eyes on the street, where some children were playing. They were obliged to drive slowly, and when they were held up by traffic she felt an impulse to scream. Suppose she missed him after all.

Suppose the boat had already gone. She would never see him again. This grim possibility was now added to all the other suffering of her life. But Captain Nolan's desertion, her humiliation, her struggle to make a living while in Washington, the loneliness, poverty, hunger and self-denial; all these seemed merely petty annoyances, now. If we don't go faster, I shall die, she thought. The cab increased its speed and the noise of motor mingled with the innumerable noises of the street. "If we don't make it, I shall die," she was repeating in her mind when the taxicab stopped, and a porter ran forward to take her bag. She thrust the fare into the driver's hand, then she hurried forward to ask directions. "I must catch the boat to Manila," she explained to a surprised official. "Have you your ticket and passport?" he asked.

She stared back at him, blankly. Though she saw his lips moving, and heard his voice she was incapable of realizing what he was saying. "Have you got your ticket?" he repeated, as they reached the gangplank.

Then it dawned upon her, and she opened her purse and handed her ticket to the purser. As they ushered her to her stateroom, her eyes traveled expectantly along the deck. 

Groups were gathered everywhere, bidding fond adieus to the

people on the dock. "Suppose I miss him, after all," she thought, gloomily. "Suppose he has changed his mind. Suppose I never see him again." All that was young in her, all that was vital and alive, strained forward, eagerly. Then a man stepped from a stateroom at the end of the hall, and by his slender figure, she recognized Dr. Villegas. At sight of him, the panic died suddenly in her heart, and the old peace, the old sense of security and protection was hers again. That night Carmen sat alone on the deck gazing at the moon and the flickering stars, with dreaming eyes thinking, pensively, of many things, but, particularly of Dr. Villegas. 

When she looked around Dr. Villegas stood beside her, transfixed as though doubting what he saw. "Why Carmen," he exclaimed, "I could hardly believe it was you! Are you going back to the Island? 

Have you changed your mind? Are you coming back with me?" "Yes," she answered, tenderly, "I followed you because I could not help it; because I wanted you more than anything else."

Then he took her in his arms, tenderly, and brushed her lips with kisses. "Will you marry me, Carmen?" he asked, softly. "Yes," she answered, her voice unquavering, her eyes frankly meeting his questioning gaze. 

It was all so simple at last.
