The title Wider Horizons is meant to denote more than just the physical horizons that broadened as I passed from one continental shore to another.

Living with students of many races at the University of Rangoon caused racial barriers to fade away. Traveling third-class among the natives and visiting them in their homes was a search for the larger life that is consummated in the world brotherhood of man. 

Spiritual values broadened and new depths were fathomed in the experience of living in a leper colony. 

Here, the spirit of Him who walked among the lepers centuries ago was gloriously manifested. Simple, sincere Christians in many lands brought a new and vivid sense of the reality of the Christian ideal. Everywhere was evidence that the world Christian fellowship was working that all may be one.

CHAPTER I LONDON BOUND 

August 7th

Aboard M.V. Britannic 

"It's five and twenty to 'ight, sir!" With proper 

Cockney accent my room steward awakened me to the thrills of my first morning at sea. After hurried ablutions in my cracker-box cabin, I just had time to take a turn about the deck before breakfast. How soon it is that even one who is on his first voyage adopts the sailor's habits, for who would think of breakfasting without first having an exhilarating walk on deck? With lungs filled with tangy salt air, breakfast on shipboard can be an exciting event.

Although the brief walk and the salt air are bound to create an appetite, it seemed to me the dinner menu had been put on the table by mistake. For here was the list of foods that met my eye: Grapefruit, Figs and

Prunes; Choice of Hot and Cold Cereals; Fried Porgies with Lemon and Manx Kippered Herring; Grilled Wiltshire Ham and Crisp Irish Bacon; Eggs; Buckwheat 

Cakes and Maple Syrup; Rolls, Toast and Scones; 

Preserves; Tea and Coffee. The crowning touch, for me at least, was a brief note at the bottom of the menu: "Beef tea will be served at 11 a.m."

This morning after a plunge in the pool, I went up topside to rent a deck chair for the voyage. As I sat there watching the vast expanse of moving water, my thoughts turned to the land I had just left behind.

WIDER HORIZONS 

Scarcely five months have passed since the Student 

Volunteer Movement conference at Wooster College in

Ohio, when the idea of taking such a trip first occurred to me. It seemed like an inspiration. What a wonderful experience it would be, I thought, to visit the lands where missionaries are giving their lives in service. 

For three years I had been attending meetings all over the country, listening to missionaries on furlough, great scholars of foreign missions, and various officials, until I had become saturated with internationalism, world Christian fellowship and brotherhood. Suddenly the desire came upon me to see these people about whom I had heard so much, to live with students of other races in their own environment, to endeavor to understand their religious beliefs and appreciate their culture. 

I lost no time in putting my resolution into effect.

That evening I dispatched two letters, one bound for a leper colony on the northeastern frontier of India, the other to the principal of Judson College, a division of the University of Rangoon in Rangoon, Burma. Within two months word was received that I had been awarded a scholarship at Judson College as an international guest student and that I might visit the leper colony en route to Rangoon. In the enthusiasm of making plans for the trip, I gave little thought to finances, but within six weeks a benefactor made it possible for me to undertake the journey.

I was brought out of my reveries by an invitation to join a foursome for shuffleboard. Aboard the Britannic, as on other oceangoing vessels, shuffleboard is usually the most popular game. The beef tea at eleven, which I had frowned on only two hours before, proved to be very welcome after a few vigorous games on deck.

August 9th 

Somewhere in the Atlantic

After lunch today I took the siesta which seems to be fast becoming a habit. Just before teatime a group of us went swimming in the tank on deck. Theoretically the first-class passengers, with a splendid indoor marble pool, have an advantage over those in third-class who must be content with an open-air tank. Nevertheless, I noticed a bit of envy from our friends on A deck for it is certainly more invigorating to swim in the sea air than in a pool below deck. One of those using our "fishbowl" tank this afternoon was the "Major," an eccentric Englishman who has been in a constant state of inebriation since we left Pier 54 in New York last Saturday. He is to be seen everywhere in a pair of shorts, pipe in mouth, hat atop his head, and with his arms behind his back. He has been so completely intoxicated that he seldom bothers to remove his hat or pipe before plunging into the tank.

Eight bells, and it's time for tea again. This time traditional English tea replaced the beef tea we were served in the morning. It is at teatime that some of the advantages of traveling first-class are made apparent, for those on A deck enjoy the privilege of being served wherever they may be. Second-class passengers usually proceed to the dining room and there partake of their refreshment. But in third-class a few tables are set on deck and the principle of first-come-first-served is in effect. This afternoon a student from first-class visited us at teatime and was astonished at the wild scramble for the best bits of pastry. "This is the first time," he observed, "that I have seen people push and

August 10th

Fifth Day Out 

Naturally, one isn't on a ship very long before even the most unmechanical sort of person begins to wonder about the mechanism that drives a great liner through the water with such magical ease. After tea this afternoon, a party of eight of us were shown through the engine room. Once below decks, the tremendous din of the engines blots out all other sound. It is almost impossible to hear even shouted speech. Gadgets, gauges, and machines are everywhere, churning, ticking, and thrashing. We had been told that the Britannic burns eighty tons of oil a day, and the figure had seemed almost fantastic to me, but after my visit below decks that amount of oil seemed not unreasonable. 

One of the pleasantest memories of all ocean voyagers is the brilliant display of acrobatics staged for their benefit by the porpoises. "Why is it," I asked one of the stewards, "that they jump out of the water to such heights?" He explained: "Small fish attach themselves to the underside of the porpoises which can rid themselves of the pests only by leaping out of the water and attempting to dislodge their tormentors as they crash back against the waves."

Today we chanced on a whale at rather close range. 

At this time of year one can occasionally see whales spouting some distance away, and we considered ourselves fortunate in getting a close-up. Even though we were safely aboard our liner, this biggest of ocean creatures appeared to be alarmingly dangerous as he

August 11th 

Not Seasick Yet

Ever since coming aboard the ship, I have yearned to climb up to the crow's nest. However, several difficulties presented themselves. The first was to find an opportunity to get into the masthead itself, for it is in full view of the captain's bridge, and the officers do not appreciate your apelike endeavors sixty-five feet above the deck. At a moment when the officers' attention was distracted I slipped in and began to climb up through the cobwebs and jutting spikes. A tiny light at the very top of the mast indicated my destination.

But once at the top, a new difficulty arose. The sailor on watch emphatically informed me that no one was allowed out in the nest. Shoving my little movie camera into his hand, I said, "All I want is a picture, sir. 

Will you please take it for me?" Whereupon he weakened and let me climb out into the crow's nest. A halfgale was blowing today, so canvas curtains were placed around the nest to protect the lookout. The curtains, however, served a double purpose, for not only did they protect the watch from the winds but they also provided a screen to protect me from the censure of the captain. After I took a few pictures we chatted awhile. 

I learned that a seaman keeps constant lookout and answers the captain's bell, tolled every half hour, with a bell in the crow's nest. 

The ship's time is counted in watches of four hours each, with each watch changing at eight bells. One bell is added every half hour until the watch is completed

with the eighth bell. Watches begin at 8 a.m. and continue every four hours around the clock. Thus 4 p.m. is eight bells, 4:30 is one bell, and 5 o'clock is two bells. 

Another treat lay in store for me this afternoon, for

I was able to get an interview with two stowaways. 

They were caught on the fourth day out when they attempted to have a seaman bring them water. Unfortunately for the stowaways they were unaware of the rule that any seaman neglecting to report the presence of stowaways not only must pay the stowaways' fare but runs the danger of losing his job. One of the bewhiskered gentry was an unemployed American with Communist ideals. The other was a Greek-American headed for Paris to meet his brother whom he hadn't seen since they parted years before in Greece. "No stowaways are bums! They either have the yen to travel or have a definite purpose in view," said the younger man, with apparent sincerity. After listening to their stories I was inclined to agree with him. These two men suffered the fate reserved for all stowaways who are unlucky enough to be caught, for they were taken to London, held in custody, and shipped back on the next boat.

Tonight I had an interesting chat with the curator of mammals at the Field Museum in Chicago who is en route to London on a Guggenheim Research Fellowship.

His particular interest is the study of bats and he is anticipating an examination of a rare species in a London museum. He told me that a particular service has been arranged by some African missionaries who often send unusual specimens to the museums. One such specimen was so new that it was decided to name the species "missionaris."

August 12th 

Almost to Ireland

Since coming aboard ship I have had many interesting and enlightening conversations, but one this afternoon seems worthy of special mention. I made the acquaintance of a young Welshman who has been spending the last five months in the United States studying the film industry in the interest of world peace. He hopes to promote international understanding and sympathy through a study of music, dancing, and the problems of the people of democratic countries. 

Under Mr. Ambrose's plans, education and cultural appreciation are substituted for amusement in the movies, although entertainment value is not automatically eliminated. In the future he hopes to have his plans in definite shape to present to the governments of the world for their co-operation. Since several films already on hand in the United States and England are suitable for the type of interpretative education which

August 13th 

Land Ahoy! 

This morning, coming on deck, the first thing I saw was the most brilliant, verdant green of my experience. The Emerald Isle at last! No country was ever more truly named. It is difficult to convey in words the almost startling beauty of the Irish countryside. The grey bluffs of the coast silhouetted against the deep green background, and set off by little shining white castles and forts nestled on the cliffs, seem to epitomize the history and the beauty of Ireland. 

August 14th

Last Day Aboard 

We dropped anchor this morning at Cowes on the 

Isle of Wight just off Southampton. Cowes is a beautiful little harbor town which boasts the Royal Yacht Club. Opposite the ship was a lovely old castle, simple but formidable, with sheep grazing near its low walls. 

Above the town could be seen Osborne House, a favorite residence of Queen Victoria, now a public memorial. 

Weighing anchor, we soon passed Portsmouth Harbor, the Empire Naval Center studded with forts and prominent battleships. 

London tomorrow!

CHAPTER II  

August 15th 

Hub of the Empire "Earth has not anything to show more fair." So wrote Wordsworth of London, as he stood early in the morning on Westminster Bridge. Thus in one line the poet epitomized the glories of the great city. They are still to be seen, for although magnificent structures in the modern style have been erected, there are preserved in historic buildings and in many old houses a dignity and tradition which give London an exclusive character. Ancient churches, stately homes, impressive monuments, pleasant open parks and quiet squaresall tell the story of London, and of Britain, too. 

I lost no time in beginning my jaunts around the city. 

Following a visit to the British Museum I made my way to Trafalgar Square. Dominated by the Nelson 

Column, it serves as a reminder of the finer qualities of the last century. It is curious that in erecting a monument to the greatest of its heroes England should have placed his effigy so high in the sky that it is impossible to get a satisfactory view except from an airplane. Nelson was intensely human, yet his countrymen have elevated him to the status of a flagpole sitter 

-a modern Simon Stylites. 

This first day in London has indeed been an exciting one, but perhaps the greatest thrill of all was passing through immigration and having the first stamp put 18

August 16th

The Abbey 

Westminster Abbey is the one supremely beautiful thing that London possesses. Misused, choked with beastly monuments, a showplace far more than a church, it yet stands austere in its beauty, a memorial to the past and a pledge to the future. Originally called the Church of St. Peter at Westminster, the Abbey is rich in tradition and legend. St. Peter, so a legend runs, has bestowed his special favor upon this church. 

On the eve of the day fixed for its consecration, a stranger appeared on the river bank and demanded to be ferried across. When he landed, the ferryman was astounded to see a host of angels surrounding the stranger while he dedicated the church. Afterwards the stranger said to him, "I am Peter, Keeper of the 

Keys of Heaven. I have consecrated my own church here at Westminster. For yourself, go out into the river; you will catch a plentiful supply of fish whereof the larger part will be salmon. This is granted on two conditions-that you never fish on Sunday, and that you pay a tithe of them to the Abbey of Westminster." 

As one walks by the Thames at Westminster, watching the grubby, overloaded barges, it is pleasant to imagine that St. Peter once crossed from Lambeth, and, remembering the days when he himself had fished on the Sea of Galilee, did not forget the ferryman's reward.

It is hard to believe that the now turbid river was once so well stocked with salmon that it was found

Leaving the Abbey this afternoon I walked towards the Houses of Parliament. I was impressed not only with the beauty of the stately buildings but also by the majestic booming of Big Ben. There is an interesting story told about Big Ben's predecessor, Great Tom. A sentry was accused of having slept at his post. As evidence that he was awake, he declared he had heard Great Tom strike thirteen at midnight. It was proved by some other witnesses that this eccentricity had occurred, and the man was acquitted. 

August 17th 

Pilgrimage to Canterbury

Taking advantage of one of the many inexpensive sight-seeing bus tours of this part of England, I set out for Canterbury today. The bus followed the route of the original pilgrims to Canterbury. We passed through the beautiful county of Kent, and visited old 

Rochester before reaching our destination. 

Like Westminster, Canterbury Cathedral is choked with relics and statues, but even these could not spoil this most beautiful of all English cathedrals-nor could the overzealous vergers who rushed about the sanctuary shepherding flocks of tourists.

For me, however, the high light of the day was not the cathedral or the quaint city with its Huguenot weavers' homes, but the picturesque Greyfriary within view of the cathedral. Here one could escape from the crowded streets of the city, with their souvenir shops that rivaled those at our own Niagara Falls. There

were no officious vergers here, but only a quiet, sedate woman who briefly explained the romantic history of the little building which housed the Greyfriars for so many centuries. St. Francis of Assisi sent a missionary to establish the Order here in 1224. 

A tiny brook wends its way through the gardens and grounds. Beside the brook is an open walk where the 

Greyfriars were wont to take their quiet, secluded strolls deep in spiritual meditation. After walking through the gardens we were admitted to the house. 

As proof of the Friars' practical nature we were shown a trap door in the kitchen through which the ancient order caught the morning mess of fish in the brook directly below. The official history of the Friary was brought to a noble close during the first World War, when it served as a place of safety for Belgian refugees. August 18th 

St. Paul's 

Of St. Paul's, Hawthorne said: "The cathedral appears to me unmistakably grand and noble, and the more so because of the throng and hustle continually going on around its base, without in the least disturbing the sublime repose of the great dome. Other edifices may crowd close to its foundation and people may tramp as they like about it; but still the great cathedral is quiet and serene. There cannot be anything else in its way so good in the world as just this effect of St. Paul's in the very heart and densest tumult of London." 

St. Paul's does not suggest the mysticism of some of the other great cathedrals. Rather it is a monument of solid, immovable faith. It suggests a firm grip upon the earth, and the comprehensiveness of an empire.

Aboard the Maloja

Stepping from the boat train to the docks of Tilbury this afternoon, I received my first sight of "India." As I stood at the rail a fine-looking middle-aged man next to me waved good-bye to his wife and family. In reply to my inquiry Mr. Forsythe told me that he was bound for Egypt to join his unit of the Royal Air Force. He had been home on leave for a few months.

August 25th Marseilles

To the tourist plagued by outrageous prices in many parts of the world, Marseilles is a welcome haven. Here prices are well within reason, and the traveler who is a prey to souvenir salesmen can at least escape cheaply. 

One may purchase a cup of coffee at one of the sidewalk cafés, and for hours watch the unceasing flow of people from all quarters of the globe. 

August 27th

CHAPTER III  

September 8th 

Gateway to India 

Bombay, the Gateway to the East, welcomes the tourist to its magnificent harbor. But also welcoming the tourist is the Indian customs official, and because of an insignificant error in valuation, I was delayed for several hours in going through the customs. Even the assistance of one of the members of the Inter-Mission Office who had come to welcome me could not prevail upon the inspector to allow me through. 

Late this afternoon I wandered about the city and visited the University of Bombay campus with its beautiful clock tower. Near by is the Indian High Court, with a great open parkway stretching for several blocks. My first impression of India is an extremely favorable one. If the whole country is up to the standard set here in Bombay, with its lovely buildings, magnificent palms and lazy flow of people, I feel certain that my visit will be an enjoyable one. 

September 9th Bombay

I'm sure I shall always remember my visit to the Parsis Towers of Silence on Malabar Hill, overlooking the bay. It is here that the followers of Zoroaster bring their dead, and reverently place them on high towers to be consumed by vultures in less than an hour. To the 27

WIDER HORIZONS 

members of this sect the earth is sacred, and for this reason they refuse to defile it by the bodies of their dead. Because they believe also in the sacredness of fire they do not practice cremation. Finally, water, the third of the elements, is hallowed, and the Parsis cannot, therefore, observe the custom of watery burial so common in India. Thus the only possible solution is to dispose of the bodies on the Towers of Silence. 

While the vultures are at work the close relatives of the deceased are at prayer in a near-by grove. After the body has been picked clean, the bones soon disintegrate because of the extreme heat and dryness. The rains wash the resulting dust down into the tower where it is filtered by three different processes before returning to mother earth, thus keeping intact its purity. The Parsis bring sandalwood instead of flowers to their funerals. To complete the ceremony an offering of sandalwood is given to the priests of the temple inside the grounds, whose duty it is to replenish the sacred fire which must be kept eternally burning. 

Can any better example be found than this to illustrate the great need for tolerance based on understanding? Outwardly revolting, inwardly this custom has its basis in the mystery which may be found in every religion that has endured through the ages. The emphasis placed on the sacredness of the elements has its counterpart in every religion from the Gulf of Persia to the Japan Sea. How necessary it is to bring an open mind and an impartial viewpoint to the Orient, especially for the tourist who, returning to his home, often spreads information that is incomplete or actually false and misleading, thus creating racial prejudice and erecting barriers of ill will between nations.

The American traveling in India who expects to find nothing but romance and the mystery of the East is destined for disillusionment from the moment he takes his first journey by train. The Indian trains are unlike the European continental type in that each compartment is entered directly from the station platform. 

First-class carriages are reserved for one person or his family and are strictly private. The second-class compartments accommodate eight passengers by day and four by night. For sleeping purposes each person is entitled to a divanlike bunk covered with smooth leather, there being two upper and two lower bunks in each compartment. Even in first-class there are no Pullman accommodations; every passenger is expected to provide his own bedding roll, and make up his own bed. 

There are no porters here. 

But if the unsuspecting traveler, after experiencing all these trials, expects to have his troubles at an end, he will find himself to be sadly mistaken. It is almost impossible to get a complete night's rest, for at every station the vendors and bystanders on the station platforms set up an unearthly din, and the vigor of their shouting seems to increase in the night as the hours go by. To make the rigors of travel even more severe the traveler must always be on his guard lest his every possession be stolen. Many a missionary has arrived in the city only to discover he was minus the pair of shoes which he had removed upon retiring! Someone else has removed them further-and also retired! If one wishes to eat while on a train it is necessary to get off at a convenient station and walk back to the diner, if there happens to be a diner on the train. 

Otherwise the train halts at a station equipped with a restaurant so that passengers may alight for a meal.

September 11th

At Agra and Fatehpur Sikri 

Among the dynasties that have ruled India, one in particular has made an indelible impress upon the culture of the country. The Moghul rulers have left enduring monuments to an abounding energy and varied wisdom. Possessors of an arbitrary power which could use the lives and labors of men for their own purposes, they have made of Agra perhaps the most interesting city in all the wide plains of Hindustan. Their contribution to the life of India has not been entirely materialistic, however, for besides the monuments which they have erected they brought with them their Mohammedan faith--which has since caused grave friction between Hindus and Moslems. One cannot pick up an 

Indian newspaper without reading of a new recurrence of this ancient feud. 

After a hasty breakfast I left early this morning for the great fort of Agra. Here it was that Akbar the 

Great erected the red sandstone fort that spreads its giant contours along the banks of the Jumma. It is without doubt one of the most extraordinary strongholds to be found anywhere. The vigorous and exuberant style of architecture that Akbar introduced into his red sandstone palaces is in strange contrast to the delicacy and finesse of the Taj. Akbar's grandson, Shah 

Jahan, improved upon the work of his predecessor, lavishing the wealth of the empire on intricate inlay

work, low reliefs in white marble, and buildings that are a glory to that period of history when Indian architecture was at its zenith. 

At the fort we were led to a balcony where it was possible to see the Taj more than a mile away. It is related that when Shah Jahan's son usurped the throne, he imprisoned his father in the section of the fort adjacent to this balcony. To torment the old man, the son used to have him led blindfolded onto the balcony, turn his back to the Taj, remove the blindfold and keep him there for hours turned away from his beloved Taj. 

One day the old Shah discovered that by polishing a certain large jewel in one of the pillars supporting the marble and jeweled porch it was possible to see the Taj reflected clearly. So, today, an attendant polishes the jewel that you may see, even as did Shah Jahan, the image of the memorial.

Some twenty-five miles from Agra is Fatehpur Sikri, the deserted court of Akbar the Great. The dead city is an enduring monument to the greatest emperor India has ever known. The story of this rose-red city, whose walls measure six miles in length, is one of the most romantic in Indian history. As I visited there this morning, it needed little imagination to conceive these halls and pavilions, now deserted and still, glowing with silken hangings and gorgeous tapestries, the courts and cloisters and vestibules glittering with resplendent retainers and all the clashing bravery of the court of an eastern emperor who was undoubtedly the most remarkable monarch of his era.

Up to the age of twenty-seven Akbar had no living issue; all his children had died in infancy. To have a son was his great ambition. While returning from a

campaign against the rebellious Uzbek nobles, Akbar halted at Sikri, which in those days (c.1560) was a shabby collection of stonecutters' huts. In Sikri, however, lived the famous saint, Salim Chisti, whose sanctity, asceticism, godliness and miraculous powers had won him wide renown. Akbar fell at the feet of this holy man, and prayed that he might be given a son. His prayers were answered. At the birth of his son, Akbar's joy was unbounded, and he determined to express his gratitude in concrete form. A great city of pomp and gaiety began to rise as if by magic. The strong battlemented walls, the magnificent palaces and courtyards, the spacious mosque and other superb specimens of the skill of the Moghul stonemasons provide to this day a source of endless wonder and admiration.

There is so much one could write about this City of Victory. For example there is the tomb of the saint 

Salim Chisti, the most sacred of all the edifices in the deserted city, a place of pilgrimage honored to this day by Mohammedans. Examining the delicately carved marble screens which surround the cenotaph, one recognizes the same motif seen in the Taj Mahal. At first glance it is hard to believe that these fretwork screens are really carved out of marble-they have the appearance of fine lace. I was interested in the large number of colored threads tied in the interstices of these superb screens. They were placed here by pilgrims who believe that any wish made in the tomb will be fulfilled. 

When their wish is realized they come again and make their offerings to the priest in charge.

In the center is a quaintly carved octagonal pillar, the top constituting a flat platform from which radiate four +4 stone galleries to another gallery encircling the chamber. This curious building is full of memories. Here, it is said, the wise men of many religions gathered, while Akbar sat cross-legged in the center on the isolated platform, listening to their arguments. As I stood on that exalted platform I could see in imagination the white-bearded scholars sitting in Oriental fashion in these strange galleries, while Akbar, clad in regal splendor, called upon first one then another to expound his philosophy. This emperor had an amazing tolerance and breadth of vision for his day. He was an idealist far in advance of his age. 

Reluctantly one turns away from this hall of memories to another relic of Akbar's reign, the Pachisi Court. This court is laid out in sandstone squares to represent a board on which is played the national game of pachisi. Bordering the court are black marble steps, in the midst of which is a raised seat. Here, beneath a canopy of silk, sat the emperor with his courtiers as players, while slave girls moved from square to square at their command-live "pieces" on this great game board.

The well-jumpers are perhaps the one bit of life still active in these secluded walls. Before leaving we were treated to the ancient spectacle. The men who jumped for us were direct descendants (and very proud of it) of the ancient jumpers. This was one of the favorite sports of the Moghuls. Seated at various levels, the emperor and his companions would watch these skilled performers dive some eighty feet into a small well. 

The excitement centers in the fact that each succeeding level protrudes a little farther during the eighty-foot descent. Thus the jumpers have to be astute judges of distance or be crushed on one of the protruding levels. 

Until his death in 1605, Akbar maintained the court at Fatehpur Sikri in all its splendor. After the emperor's death the great court was given over to the wild beasts and the wilder and more dangerous humans, the reason being lack of water. Soon, however, the palaces and towers relapsed into the uninterrupted silence

which endures to this day. The tide of life which once surged so powerfully here has receded, and over the deserted quadrangles and mosques is a desolation and a mysticism that seems to express perfectly the fatalistic philosophy of the Orient. Only silence pervades its stately beauty.

After visiting his city, it seemed only right to make the pilgrimage to Sekundra to see Akbar's tomb. Here, repeated in the motifs and designs of the red sandstone of Sikri, reposes the body of the greatest Moghul. It is an ancient custom of the Indians to place a copy of the tomb high up in the memorial, perhaps to confuse evil spirits.

I reserved a tonga for the entire day, for even my slender resources could afford the few cents an hour rental. The tonga is the national taxi of India, used alike by rich and poor. A two-wheeled vehicle, it is unique in that the driver and the passenger sit back to back. The driver patiently waited whenever I alighted to see some new point of interest. I made my last visit at the Ivory Palace-which is not a palace in the usual sense but a large market where workers in ivory gather and sell their wares. It is world renowned for the excellence of its carvings. With amazing deftness, the carvers produce unbelievably delicate objets d'art before one's very eyes.

I wondered at first why so many people walked in the streets. The reason was soon brought home to me in a forcible manner. Anyone walking close to the buildings runs the risk of having refuse thrown on him from the windows and doors, or of being the recipient of a stream of betel-nut juice from an upper story. I have vowed never to complain again about American drivers. Street scenes in India are fantastically confused. Imagine if you can the difficulties faced by a motorist attempting to force his way through a melee of cows, goats, men and women, slow-moving carts and tongas, hundreds of bicycles, and an occasional elephant or camel. To sound one's horn is to invite a smile of amused contempt; cows and bullocks are wholly oblivious to automobiles, and it would be exceedingly difficult for a light car to prevail against an elephant! Hence, it is not long before every able-bodied

European takes to a bicycle or is content to ride patiently and resignedly in a slow-moving tonga. 

One is not in India for very long before becoming aware of the sacredness of the cow. To eat the flesh of the cow, or to molest the animal in any way, is one of the cardinal sins of Hinduism. Cows and bulls, therefore, are allowed complete freedom, and are permitted to roam wherever and whenever they choose.

Many of the animals wander about like gaunt skeletons, too weak to do any work except to get whatever food they can. Nevertheless, the cow population is increasing continually, taking an ever-larger proportion of the food supply, which at best is woefully

insufficient to provide adequately for the needs of the people. The very animal that could furnish a valuable source of nourishment is grinding the Hindu people into the mire.

I was extremely fortunate this afternoon in having an opportunity for a long conversation with Bishop Brenton Thoburn Badley. Bishop Badley has spent all his life in the missionary field here in India, and undoubtedly is one of the best informed men on the history of Indian missions. It was from the Bishop that 

I received the idea that India is perhaps the greatest field for missionary work. "In the first place," he said, "this is the only country on earth where all the great religions are practised, thus differing from Africa, where Buddhism is not represented, and from China, where Mohammedanism has only a few followers. 

Secondly, India is the only country where the East and the West meet on somewhat equal terms in all the fields of endeavor-education, religion, labor and business. 

Thus we have escaped the problem which usually presents itself in other Oriental countries, where it is almost impossible for the two peoples to meet on common ground."

The British imperial government has just erected an entirely new city called New Delhi. The huge burdens of expense and taxation imposed upon the Indians to finance the construction work were at first bitterly resented by the native population, but their resentment has changed to pride in this new city. In beauty of architectural design its buildings and monuments are surpassed only by those in our capital, Washington.

Today is the first day of fall, back home-but no one seems to know it out here. 

Because of the lateness of the dinner hour the social time of the day follows tea at four o'clock. Receptions, formal teas, concerts, and even church services are held between teatime and the evening dinner hour. This afternoon I attended the Bare Hostel Union Social for the first-year students. The older boys in the hostel or dormitory give a social each year for the new students. 

It was a very enjoyable affair in a campus garden surrounded by palms and banana trees bathed in the multicolored hues of the sunset. Each visitor received a garland of flowers as he entered the garden. Seated at attractively decorated tables, we were served tea and 

Indian "sweets." A program of instrumental music, songs and recitations was brought to a close by the introduction of each new student in the hostel.

India is a country of appalling contrasts. One sees great wealth, as represented by rooms full of gems and treasures and bullion accumulated by the rajahs of independent kingdoms. Against that there are millions of people living in utter destitution, bordering on starvation. Beggars are everywhere! When the train stops at a station one is besieged by dozens of mendicants, many of whom are horribly disfigured. Mothers flaunt their half-starved babies before the car windows, begging alms. But also, one sees the marvelous beauty of the rice fields when the monsoon has just passed, the loveliness of the mountain scenes, and artistry at its height in the shimmering whiteness of the Taj Mahal.

September 30th Benares

It was with great reluctance that I bade good-bye to the students at Lucknow Christian College last night. 

My new destination is Jorhat, Assam, on the Northeastern Frontier. However, I decided to take a stopover in Benares en route as this ancient city is well worth a visit.

Sacred city of India, Benares is the center of Hinduism. At the same time, one may see along the banks of the Ganges great mosques of the Moslems. Finally, less than five miles out of the city is Sarnath, the site on which Buddha first made known his doctrines to the world. What a heritage-three of the world's great religions concentrated, as it were, in one city! One might spend months here as a humble pilgrim-studying the Hindu's way of salvation, the Muslim's way of self-negation, and the Buddhist's way of transmigration of the soul. Really to understand and appreciate a people, certainly we must have a knowledge of their religions, which are the guiding motivations of the Oriental way of life. Is it any wonder that one of the great philosophical universities of the world is located in this ancient religious city? 

It is impossible for anyone to tell how many thousands of years Benares has been looked upon as the centre of Hindu religious life. What is certain is that 61

His prayers consist of pronouncing the deity's name, repeating certain verses which he has learned and which differ according to the god worshipped, and offering some petitions of his own. As his winding sheet is being prepared, passers-by give his naked body no more attention than the dozen like it that lie around the ghat. The funeral pyre is ready and the corpse is lifted on. Behind the attendants a local barber serves a fastidious worshipper.

October 16th Tea Gardens 

I have just returned from one of the grandest auto rides of my life! For three hours I was driven around two vast tea plantations which were refreshingly green and laid out in perfect symmetry. Tea is made from the young shoots of the tea plant, an evergreen. Given a sufficiently high temperature and humid climate, it

WIDER HORIZONS 

grows all year. Assam is famous the world over for its tea and it has reason to be, for it has the best climate for successful tea growing. It is hot and moist with generous rainfall, heavy dews and fogs in the early morning, and no long droughts.

The tea plant will grow almost anywhere, but land recently carrying jungle is the best because it is rich in plant food. It will not grow in swamps, and if planted on badly drained land the bush becomes sickly; for this reason deep ditches surround all the plants to aid in drainage. Tea is generally propagated from the seed. 

However, it can be grown from shoots six inches long, and also from a leaf which has undergone certain treatment. It is put out in the early part of the year and is transplanted after two years. In its natural state, the plant is a shrub or tree, and some varieties, if allowed to grow, reach a height of sixty feet. For plucking, the shrub must be kept at three feet or less. 

About two years after transplanting, the plant (which is now cone-shaped) is cut straight across about twenty inches above the ground. Cutting some of the strong center branches lower than the others tends to make the bush develop the side branches. In this way spread is obtained and a large plucking area is made possible. A topping height is now fixed at, for example, twenty-eight inches. Every two years it is pruned two inches higher until it is either too high for convenience in plucking or else moribund. In Assam, the bush yields 560 pounds per acre. Some plants live from fifty to one hundred years.

Plucking is usually done by hand. One of the gardens we visited had a coolie population of over four thousand. Visualize the bright green plants, thousands

of bushes laid out in perfect symmetry beneath the larger trees. Hundreds of coolies in multicolored clothes, wearing large bright sun hats, with bags slung over their shoulders, are busily picking the leaves. As long as labor is cheap and plentiful, there is little likelihood of mechanical plucking being adopted. 

The larger trees in the gardens are planted to act as shade, because the sun might be disastrous to the young plants. The leaf is converted into black or green tea, the former being called the fermented, and the latter, the unfermented. Green tea is made by simply heating the fresh leaf, rolling and drying it. The highest qualities of the leaf are developed in the preparation of black tea, which includes the rolling and drying before the leaf is even heated.

The tea factories are high, but single-storied, buildings with one or more "withering" houses near by. As the leaf is plucked it is brought to the factory at the end of the morning and again at the end of the day, the plucker being paid according to the day's work. After the leaves are weighed, they are taken to the withering shed. Such a shed consists of a series of ten floors three feet apart. The sheds are open and the withering leaf must be spread thin. Next the leaves are rolled, which cracks and bruises them, exposing the leaf juices to the air. The leaves are then heated in a hot pan, cooled, rolled, and dried several times until finally dried over a charcoal fire on bamboo trays. October 17th 

About Assam

Assam is not generally thought of as possessing a stirring history or an old civilization. The latter is

WIDER HORIZONS 

82 attested, however, by numerous forts, ancient temples and certain old highroads, such as the Kamali Alli, running 350 miles over the length and breadth of the land. 

These remains probably reveal only a small portion of what the archaeologist will find when the jungle and forest covering a great portion of Assam can be removed. That it was a densely populated country in the far-off past is shown by extensive ruins at Kamatapur in the west, said to be upwards of nineteen miles around. This city flourished up until the end of the fifteenth century, when it fell prey to the Moghuls. 

T. T. Cooper, writing of Assam (in 1873), refers to the energy and civilization formerly characteristic of this people, in striking contrast to the lethargic existence of the present-day scanty population. He says: "The contemplation of these ruins surrounded by almost impenetrable jungle which has overgrown the once fertile and well-cultivated fields of a people that has almost passed away, is calculated to strike one with an intense desire to learn more of the history of those terrible events which robbed a fertile land of a vast and industrious population, converting it into a wilderness of swamps and forests."

These facts go to prove that Assam, spoken of by the old Moghul writing as a "land of witchcraft and mystery," does possess an interest which will repay those who take time to read books on this fascinating country. When one realizes to what an extent civilization formerly flourished in Assam, the depth of its religions, the engineering and architectural works left behind, one is bound to wonder what has become of the nation and of its people. What caused decay of the power that ruled its once thriving valleys? The people now seen

in Assam, in contrast to their warlike ancestors, are a peaceful, almost effeminate race, small in numbers, addicted to opium eating, and not disposed to diligent labor. Hence the necessity of importing Indian coolies required to work on the tea plantations. 

It is generally assumed that climatic conditions tended very largely to bring about this state of decay. The climate is distinctly an enervating one, and each race that has settled here has in the course of time lost its vigor and been supplanted by a hardier folk. These in turn succumbed to the love of ease and luxury born of a warm, moist climate in a highly fertile land. In addition, Assam has always suffered not only from earthquakes but from the marvelously rapid growth of vegetation. When unchecked for a few years this spreads, chokes up the valleys, and obliterates all traces of former towns and buildings. The greatest amount of rain in the world falls in this province. Nine hundred inches have been known to fall in one year at Cherrapungi, and most of this actually fell within three months, during the monsoons or rainy season. The average annual rainfall in Assam is 400 to 500 inches. 

Completely surrounded as they were by mountains, among them the Himalayas (highest in the world), it is not to be wondered at that the old Assamese taught their children that Assam was the whole world—if you ventured outside the mountains you would fall or be swallowed up in space. The early stories about Assam being purely legendary, it is difficult to lift the veil lying over the country's history, though a little light comes to us here and there from ancient inscriptions in India. As far back as the fourth century B.C., Assam was recorded on Indian pillars.

Three great tribes have dominated at different periods. The most important of these were the Ahoms, because they possessed a literature of a sort and certainly kept written historical records—“buranjis,” meaning "stores of information (instruction) for the ignorant." These give us a definite history of Assam since about 1220. The Ahoms were non-Buddhist 

Shans of the great Tai race who held sway in the Brahmaputra Valley until the appearance on the scene of the present dominant power, the British, in 1825. 

We have often heard of the conquering hordes which at times have overrun the Indian peninsula from outer Mongolia, but we seldom hear of the exploits of some of the great Indian princes who had dreams of conquering all Asia. In 1337, having learned of the vast wealth of China, Mohomed Tuglak conceived the idea of subduing that empire. He ordered a force of 100,000 men to accomplish this aim. Nobles and state councilors assured him in vain that the troops of India never had advanced or would advance a step into Chinese territory, and that the whole scheme was visionary. 

But the king insisted and the army entered the mountains. Diminished in force, they were met at the border by a great army, and being much inferior to the enemy they were soon defeated. Thus it was that, even in those days, China was invincible. [The indomitable spirit of the Chinese has not changed since that early time, as the Japanese have been discovering.]

October 21st 

Naga Hills

From our porch we can see the famous Naga Hills. 

They appear grim and forbidding, stirring much speculation in an outsider, who is thrilled at their mystery. 

I have been inquiring about the strange folk who inhabit the hills.

It was in 1536 that the Ahom "Buranjis" first mention trouble with any of the wild hill tribes, or Nagas, who inhabit the mountains which hem in upper Assam. 

At that time they had already begun to raid the plains and stand up to the trained Ahoms in battle. 

Contrary to popular impression, the name Naga has nothing to do with the seven-headed cobra, "Naga," 86

which is a favorite Hindu emblem. As applied to the hill tribes, it is a corruption of the word "nanga" (naked). The origin of the tribes is rather doubtful. 

Some archaeologists have tried to trace their past to the Dyaks of Borneo, who in some far-off age may have trekked north through the Straits, southern Burma, and Arrakan until brought to a standstill either by the vast walls of the Himalayas or by the resistance of migrating Mongolian peoples. In matters of counting, names for domestic implements, village architecture, and head-hunting propensities, students recognize a slight resemblance between the Dyaks and the Nagas. Their love for marine shells (with which they rarely part) may seem to point to a bygone home near the sea, though now they reside far inland. 

The Nagas are athletic and by no means bad looking. 

They are spirit worshippers and, for savages, are a moral race.

Their weapons consist mostly of seven-foot spears. 

These are thrown with great skill and ease, often twenty-five yards or more. They have been known to kill a tiger at such a distance. Short-statured and powerfully built, they often resemble muscle-bound athletes. Many are naked-hence "naga." Some wear a crude loincloth and a few wrap a blanket about themselves.

All Nagas are head-hunters. Their women provide the chief incentive to this pursuit-girls will not look with favor upon men who have not taken heads or been in raids. Any heads are considered of value-a man's, a woman's, or a child's. It is curious to note that where some tribes adorn their shields and house fronts with rough emblems of heads taken, sometimes one will see

WIDER HORIZONS 

a head represented upside down, this having been taken in murder. Thus they make a distinction between a head fairly taken and one unfairly taken.

They are a cheery and musical folk, the latter quality being shown in their dances, to which they are devoted, and in which they are graceful performers. 

These dances are of the quick heel-and-toe movement, either in pairs or in quartets of both sexes. Naga singing is curious—it is in the nature of an antiphonal chant, without words, which is very effective. 

Most of the Nagas bury their dead in their own villages. One tribe smokes dry the late lamented and then places him on a high trestle, thatched over, on which they hang his ornaments and before which are placed his weapons. When dry wood is scarce during the rains and smoking a corpse is a long and tedious process, the bodies are often placed on a trestle with the cremation only half completed. When this occurs, the passage through the "avenue of the dead" is not a matter of pleasure for those with delicate nostrils. In another tribe the dead are placed in rough-hewn log coffins, or are carefully wrapped in leaves and then lodged in big trees near the village. The head, in some clans, is wrenched off and laid at the base of the tree. 

As wind and weather work upon the trees and coffins, they are often dislodged and the scene is more gruesome than interesting.

In all these Naga tribes social custom demands that the young unmarried men sleep in a house set apart for them. In some tribes the unmarried girls also sleep together in a small house set apart for them by their families. The young men's residence is known as the "dekha chang." In it are hung weapons and the spoils

of the chase. Among the Nagas are "morangs" which are unlike those found in the Dyak villages in Borneo. 

The morang is a large, substantial timber-and-thatch structure of peculiar shape which stands near the entrance to the village. It is the Naga equivalent of a sentry box. Alongside stand war drums hollowed out of a large tree trunk. They are beaten in times of peril to the community to call the men back from the distant fields. The sound emitted is deep, vibrant, and travels far. All young men have to take their turn on guard at the morang, a requirement which forms a rude sort of military system. When his "enlistment" is ended, the man cuts a slab off a certain tree and sets it up in front of the morang in token that his tour of duty is over. So the Nagas, too, have a sort of conscription. 

Among these tribes, heads and other trophies of war are hung in the morang. Some of the enormous timbers supporting the roof will be found elaborately carved with representations of elephants, lizards, toucan heads (greater hornbills), and nude human figures. I have seen as many as forty-two skulls attached to a sort of trelliswork in front of a headman's house, each skull being embellished with a goat's horn fixed on either side. These people are usually friendly and courteous in their independent way, willing to assist Europeans.

However, it was a relief to see very few skulls upside down, for then we knew that the heads had at least been taken in what the Nagas consider fair play and not by wanton murder.

The missionary movement has not been able to gain much ground in the Naga Hills-it is difficult to get government permission to go there, because of the general wildness of the country and its people. However,

WIDER HORIZONS 

an interesting story is told about the work that is being done in one small part of the hills. A missionary relates that the fierce tribesmen decided they would really try the principles of the Great White Master. 

They came to him one day and said, "You have told us many things about this Jesus of yours. We are going to try his way of peace, and love; we are going to love our enemies. The next time they come to attack us we will lay down our weapons before them and show that we want to be their friends." Imagine the surprise of the missionary when they spoke in such a manner. He commended them for their trust and belief, for we in the West seldom show such strength of faith. Not long after, the drums began to beat out the warning that the enemy tribe was on its way to attack. The village warriors gathered their weapons and laid them in a heap on the ground. The enemy, arriving on the scene, were so overwhelmed by this act that they threw down their weapons and fled. What new Spirit had been found as a protector to enable a tribe to leave themselves undefended in the face of danger? We in the West often wonder if the way of love really works. Such an act on the part of head-hunters puts us to shame. The way of love does work-if we would but try it but our cynical, doubting West prefers to follow the way of hatred, bigotry and intolerance rather than of love, brotherhood and co-operation.

Sibsagar 

I have added another curio to my fast-growing collection-a brick from the ruins of the temples of the kings of Ahom at Sibsagar. The Ahoms were noted for their strong masonry-they erected temples, palaces, gates and elephant bridges out of bricks. Some of these mounds have been opened, and from them has been taken a rich store of bones and ornaments.

October 29th 

Steaming up the Ganges

Again we were forced to make a change in the middle of the night. Only this time, at Chandpur, we had to go aboard a boat which brought us up to Goalunda. 

As we sleepily gazed out the train window we could see coolies scurrying about, and in the distance the dim outline of a boat. Commandeering some coolies to help us with our mal (luggage) we hurried to the boat. Although we have been traveling third and inter-class, I took one look at the mass of humanity piled and huddled together around the lower deck and engines, and decided to change to second-class for the duration of the boat trip. Second consisted of four cabins, each with two bunks on the starboard side aft. At least we had a few square feet to ourselves and a tiny cabin. 

Wearily we turned in for a few hours' sleep. It was still dark and we had begun to pass our second night without much rest.

We breakfasted on deck as the boat slowly made its way up the Ganges. About once each hour we would slide up to a little river village and take on or discharge a few passengers. There is no train service in these parts because of the great extent of the Ganges delta and the frequent floods. Often we stopped out in midstream, and then the native sampans would push out from shore with their human cargo. Earlier the river

resembled a broad lake but now it took on the proportions of a great river. 

We changed to the train again at Goalunda this afternoon in order to take the main line at Poradaha. Arriving there, we thought we'd missed our train from Calcutta to Siliguri (terminal for Darjeeling) but it was on the other side of the station. Thank goodness we didn't have to spend the night at the Poradaha station, because I have never seen such a dark and forebidding place-no lights to speak of in the whole station! If a traveler does spend the night in the waiting room he usually locks himself in to insure the safekeeping of his belongings. For a few annas he hires a coolie to keep the punka going all night. The punka is a crude fan which may be pulled from outside the waiting room, by means of a long rope. As it sways back and forth it creates a gentle circulation of air, which aids greatly in keeping the mosquitoes from tormenting one. The chairs in the station room have elongated arms that stretch way out. It is possible to recline by putting one's feet up on the arms.

For the third successive morning we were awakened at three o'clock. However, we are accustomed to it and are anxious to begin the fifty-mile ascent to the "snows." Siliguri connects the main line with the

Darjeeling-Himalayan Railway, one of the most remarkable engineering feats in the world. Although the length of the railway is only fifty miles, it involves a rise of nearly 7,500 feet. The railroad follows the alignment of the old Siliguri-Darjeeling cart road. The

road itself has been renovated and a number of motor cars now ply between the two stations. To the traveler who seeks a unique experience, the train will more than satisfy him. The railroad passes through tea gardens and over the Mahanadi River, then plunges into dense jungle of tall sal trees, bamboo sixty feet high, cane and grass. From the branches of the trees forming a roof overhead hang masses of orchids, some as streamers, others in bunches, all beautiful. 

Throughout the journey the line twists and turns, sometimes hanging on the edge of a precipice, sometimes hidden in the jungle. Frequently the motor road and the railroad join forces. The lorries then run right on the tracks because there is not enough room on the side. It is common for travelers to become seasick on this road because of the dizzy heights and countless hairpin curves. [On the return trip we descended the "hill" in a bus that had a habit of leaning toward the edge of the road, giving us an unwanted view of space falling away for a couple of thousand feet.]

The motor vehicles report at stations three or four miles apart, so that ascending cars may be appropriately warned how many vehicles they must meet between stations. This is because the road is so narrow and treacherous. The only trouble is, one never knows on just which curve out of a possible hundred one may see another car coming.

Besides, one never knows when one is going to come out on the railroad track. This is all right if a train isn't on the track at the identical moment! Inciden-. tally, the Tibetans seem to be ignorant of any such contrivance as a guard rail along a mountain road. Land-

slides are common, too. One wonders, approaching a curve on the edge of a precipice, "Is the road intact or must we close our eyes again and hope for the best?" 

All this, of course, keeps the trip from becoming monotonous.

If one is really courting excitement there is a third possible method of descending the "hill." (They call anything under ten thousand feet a hill.) At Ghum, seven miles this side of Darjeeling, at an elevation of 7,500 feet, one may take a trolley which travels without current to the foot of the hill-something unique in railroad travel. Two men stand ready to apply the brakes as necessary, gravity does the rest! However, we didn't see a crowd around the trolley station waiting to buy tickets; neither did we see a trolley descend the hill all the time we were there. 

Upon arriving at Darjeeling, we went to the Scottish Mission where we inquired about an inexpensive room. 

We found one at a Mrs. Robertson's. After a hearty breakfast we walked over to the Methodist School, Mount Hermon. Although we attended church we kept gazing out the windows, anxiously hoping for a view of the "snows." It was too cloudy.

We spent the afternoon at the bazaar, because Sunday is bazaar day for all the hill tribes from Tibet, Bhutan, Sikkim, and Nepal. A Tibetan bazaar is the epitome of all Asiatic bazaars. Talk about the county fairs back home! Everybody is selling something. The vendors will sit for hours by their meager wares just to make one sale. Even then they won't let you pay one price for it; you must do some bargaining. If they sit here all day you feel that the least you can do is to bargain with them, for they get rich enjoyment from

WIDER HORIZONS 

the procedure. After considerable trouble, I procured a twelve-inch kukurie (Tibetan knife) in a jeweled leather case.

The bargaining procedure is something like this. As a buyer I approached the particular piece I wanted with little outward sign of interest. After handling several adjacent curios, I picked up the kukurie, saying, "Kemandam ekka?". ("How much is it?") "Dos rupee," he replied. (Dos is ten. Thus $3.50.) "Aht (eight) rupee," said I. "No (nine) rupee," he answered. "Saht (seven) rupee," I returned. We left it there, dickering back and forth. The idea is to keep offering a rupee less than the merchant. If he keeps lowering his price, you know you have him. All that remains is to drive home the bargain at a safe figure. 

When the bargaining reached a stalemate, I did as Ram had advised-switched my attention to another article. 

Returning to the kukurie, I offered soi (six) rupees. "Saht rupee," was the reply.

Summoning my best bargaining efforts I burst forth, "Pas (five) rupee!" Gesticulating, and with expressions beyond my meager vocabulary, the seller gave me to understand that such a price was absurd. Recalling the next step, I left the scene promptly but as casually as possible.

Soon he was after me, begging me to return. For some reason or other, probably superstition, these people believe they should never let a white customer leave without buying something. I got my five-rupee kukurie at just half the original figure. Imagine my chagrin to learn later that even so I had paid twice what it was worth. You can't win!

Among my other purchases this afternoon was a little Tibetan prayer wheel. Being Buddhists, the natives naturally use the Buddhist prayer wheel which is common in this part of Asia. The prayer is on paper inside the wheel. Each time the wheel goes around it repeats a prayer. Thus it is possible to say a good many prayers in a day.

We were particularly interested this afternoon in the 

Tibetans' mode of dress. They never change their clothes, but merely sew new ones on over the old. They present a curious sight, for often you can see where the "old" clothes have worn through several "patches."

November 1st

Darjeeling We decided to stay over today and take some more pictures of the "snows." Ever since returning from seeing Everest yesterday morning I've been finding out all I could about the mountain. 

Everest was discovered in the office of the Trigonometrical Survey of India in 1852. Immediately the officials got busy. Observation from all six peaks whence Everest had been observed were checked and the mean height of 29,002 feet was arrived at. Sir 

Andrew Waugh named the peak after Sir George

Everest, his predecessor as surveyor general of India, under whose direction the triangulations had been started. Later the expeditions learned that the Tibetan name was Chomulungma, which means "Goddess 

Mother of the World."

The Himalayas are the backbone of the world, extending for over two thousand miles, with a hundred peaks each over 24,000 feet high, twenty giants each over 26,000 feet, six at 27,000 feet, Kanchanjunga and 

Everest topping them all.

The early explorers and surveyors were up against a tough problem in getting geographical knowledge of this vast Forbidden Land. Every sort of ingenuity was relied on for bringing back valuable data. The story of how prayer wheels were used is a fascinating one. 

The Himalayan passes were walled, barricaded, and guarded by a hostile Tibetan soldiery. Beyond the passes, the lamas in their monasteries ceaselessly spied out the land for foreigners, capturing and torturing any that they found.

In 1860 the Pundit Explorers set out into the mysterious mountains. They would disappear for years, then suddenly return with geographical knowledge most laboriously collected. They counted every step by the revolution of the prayer wheel. At night they would write their observations inside the wheel on the prayer paper within. They recorded the compass bearings by means of compasses concealed in amulets worn about their necks. They carried boiling-point thermometers inside walking sticks for the measurement of high altitudes. The story of how one member of the party was to trace rivers and throw marked logs in at intervals is most exciting. A year, two years, three years passed and no word came. After four years the logs arrived. As late as 1904, the only knowledge possessed of Mount Everest was from these intrepid explorers.

However, all the remarkable stories of assaults on 

Everest are not confined to the earlier period. If you want thrilling reading, choose some modern account of the continued attempts to scale the Goddess Mother of the World. Perhaps the most gripping story concerns the greatest mountain climbers the world has ever known, Mallory and Irvine, two young Englishmen who lost their lives in 1924 scaling Everest. By means of telescopic cameras it was fairly accurately ascertained that these two men came within 600 feet of reaching the top of the highest mountain in the world. There is actual photographic proof of this feat. But they were never seen or heard of again. At the time they disappeared, Mallory was thirty-six years old, married, and had two children. Irvine was on the Oxford crewand only twenty-two,

Why did these young men attempt such a seemingly impossible feat? It seems to be human nature to want to break records. To debate why men are irresistibly attracted by the chance to do something first or to go somewhere first is a waste of time. They are built that way. The very impulse that rules the world is enormously indebted to them because they frequently lay the foundation for accurate scientific data. 

One of the greatest things these young men did was to discover how high man can live. Grasshoppers go 

18,000 feet. No insect has been found at a greater altitude than the attid spider, at 22,000 feet. If animals and birds (birds' nests have been found at 21,500 feet) can adapt themselves to such high altitudes, so can human beings. By their exploits Mallory and Irvine showed that man can live at over 28,000 feet- -if the telescopic camera's findings are correct. The problem of scaling Everest was and remains one of weather and breathing. One effect of a lack of oxygen is to tire the mind and produce mental debility even to the degree of loss of intelligence.

I should like to mention just a few interesting facts about the Tibetan folk. The hill people, differing greatly from the plains people, wear colorful clothes, and have tangled, loose-flowing hair. One never sees veiled women like those down in the plains. All smokemen, women, and children.

Leading a rough life as they do, in a fearful climate and with scarcity of food, one might expect to find them a sad folk, but they are happy and cheerful. 

Their good-humored laughter is fairly raucous. From the day they are born until they die they will never have a water bath. They wash with oils and butter.

This custom helps the children, especially, to stand the hard weather. Mothers love their children but fathers care little for them. The children do not know who their own fathers are in polyandrous Tibet. To marry, all that a couple do is light a fire together. 

Food consists of yak's meat, mutton, and barley. "Buttered tea"-consisting of tea, butter, and saltis common. To escape having to drink this stuff, one explorer said, "As a penance, I have sworn off Tibetan tea, my life's delight (!), until I reach the top of Everest!"

Many interesting stories are told to show how forbidden Tibet was. I especially like the one about the 

Everest leader who, when asked why he was laying a telegraph wire, replied, "To help my party find its way back." Since the Tibetans were anxious for the strangers to leave, they dared not interfere with the important wire. 

November 2nd

Ushagram Our trip down the mountains to the plain was fairly uneventful. Ram and I part company here at Ushagram, where he is to attend school. Ushagram is two miles east of Asansol, 132 miles west of Calcutta on the 

East Indian Railway.

I have heard about this experiment in educational missions ever since the summer of 1936 when I worked with the Lisle Fellowship near Cornell University. It really is a significant enterprise which deserves explanation. 

Ushagram, meaning "Dawn Village" or "Village of the New Day," because of its pioneering, is a school

community of 750 boys and girls with forty-eight teachers, men and women. The students participate in social control through elected panchayats (councils). 

There is a co-operative society, which operates the bank and general store, a library, farm, gardens, shops, maternity center, and high schools as well as cottages for students and teachers. 

Education proceeds from nursery to matriculation in Calcutta University. Groups are co-ed through the first five classes, branching into several departments each with its building and staff. Students choose a prevocational interest and become somewhat proficient in that work before being allowed to take the University examination. The special subjects offered are: (boys) bookbinding, printing, carpentry, tailoring, agriculture, cloth weaving, basketmaking, picture framing, papermaking, and music; (girls) home science, cookery, sewing, gardening, food industries, fine arts, and music. Manual labor is a part of the curriculum, being engaged in for two hours each day.

Although I have been here only a few hours I am immensely impressed by the spirit pervading the "Dawn Village." Everyone is industrious, creative, happy-all are learning to work while learning facts. 

The attitude of the missionaries, Dr. and Mrs. Fred 

Williams, is unique. They have adopted all possible forms of Indian culture and civilization in their daily life, incorporating them in the religious life of the school. 

Therefore, although it is a Christian school, it is also distinctly Indian throughout. Naturally the Indians are impressed by this and rally around the leaders. 

Ushagram has become so well known in the surrounding country as an aid to the rural life of India that

WIDER HORIZONS 

many tiny villages have sprung up near by. How like the method of the Master of old! He not only went out to the people but they often came to Him, settling near 

Him and observing His methods.

Among Dr. Williams' proudest achievements is the installation of an inexpensive septic tank in many native huts. The natives themselves are so proud of this innovation that when you visit them the first thing they want you to see is their septic tank! This may seem strange to the folks back home, but if you had spent weeks traveling amidst the filth and dirt, dust and disease that is India's, much of it caused by unsanitary conditions, you would be greatly impressed at the sight of one man and his brothers initiating what will someday be a great service to India.

There is so much to write about Ushagram. Take for example the interest in toothbrushes, which has turned into an interest in civic pride. To millions of 

Americans the problem of finding a new toothbrush is simply that of taking time to visit the nearest drugstore or Woolworth's. And an American keeps a brush for several weeks or months. Not so in India! The natives there use a brush only once, as it is contrary to custom to do otherwise. (Actually it is more sanitary than our way.)

The problem set up was where to find 750 new toothbrushes every day. An Indian toothbrush is the twig of a tree cut to the length of about eight inches. The twig is chewed at one end to make a brush. Several hundred young trees were planted here not long ago but they have been having a hard time of it because no matter what methods were used the trees continued to be sacrificed on the altar of the toothbrush.

Finally a solution was reached. It was decided that one of the lads, as his part of daily manual labor, should cut the necessary number of brushes from a near-by jungle and prepare them for distribution. Thus the boys' panchayat not only discovered a way to improve hygiene but also showed their ability to handle civic problems of the first magnitude. 

Everyone lives in mud huts, including the missionaries. When the Governor of Bengal visited here he expressed his surprise at the fine way in which the leaders had oriented themselves. He was even more surprised when he beheld the interior of the homes.

With mud walls eighteen inches thick, they are cool and clean inside. Anyone who cares to do so can make them as pleasant and livable as a wood and iron bungalow.

It is a Hindu custom of long standing to paint simple rice-water designs on the exterior of the homes during certain religious festivals. Taking advantage of this custom, Dr. Williams designated several girls to paint appropriate Easter designs on the various faculty homes and school buildings. They were so well received that it was decided to continue this practice each year, at Christmas as well as Easter. Thus, once again, the Williams' have capitalized on an ancient Indian custom and adapted it to Christianity. 

I have seen many lovely little chapels in the world but never one like the church at Ushagram. Built from native cement and brick, it is distinctly Indian throughout. One of the saddest things to observe on the foreign field is a church designed on Western lines. We wouldn't like to worship in an Oriental temple, although it might be beautiful, simply because it would

WIDER HORIZONS 

not seem familiar. It is only fair that we do not inflict our Western architecture on the indigenous Eastern Church. How beautiful it is to visit the Ushagram chapel, which even the humblest native knows is a 

Christian church.

It is placed in a grove of trees just outside the village, inviting one to draw apart and pray. The worshippers sit in native style on the floor, beneath the heavens. The altar alone is under a glistening white dome. A beautiful white cross, the centerpiece on the rear wall, is surrounded by a maze of Hindu lace design. Over the altar hangs a brass chandelier, adding to the Oriental atmosphere. One enters and leaves with the utmost reverence, for the chapel breathes the simplicity of the Orient and the quietness of the Eternal.

Judson We had an enlightening discussion in one class today on the subject of capital punishment. Burma has a high rate of crime, so this proved to be a lively subject.

Clocks and Eats!

Each dormitory here has a durwan (janitor) whose most interesting duty is that of telling time. The

"clock" consists of a piece of suspended iron rail which is struck with a stout stick each hour. Last night I was amused to hear the clocks striking midnight. By that late hour most of the durwans had fallen asleep. First one woke up and hit the gong. Pretty soon, durwan 

No. 2, awakened by the rather anemic strikes of clock

No. 1, arose and struck his clock between yawns. It was nearly fifteen minutes from the first striking until the last durwan was awakened to beat out a weak and futile midnight!

When I first arrived I was curious about a peculiar noise I heard each afternoon out on the campus. It sounded like someone hitting a hollow stick with a piece of wood. Today I decided to investigate the mysterious knocks, and I found that they really were caused as I had imagined. The device proved to be a portable 

Chinese restaurant. A small table was slung from each end of a bamboo pole which the purveyor balanced gingerly on his shoulders. As he moved about, he hit the sticks to attract customers. When a customer appeared, he put down his restaurant and proceeded to serve rice on a piece of banana leaf, or some other such delicacy. Usually there is a table at one end of the pole and a small stove hanging on the other with live burning coals in it.

November 30th 

Rangoon

Port Said the Suez Canal-Bombay-Calcutta each has its fascination, but none of these rivals Ran-

goon. Here is the real East, full of color, mystery and romance. Burma casts a spell over those who know her and compels them to love her. That spell will remain with me as long as I live.

I think that travel has few experiences to give, anywhere in the world, more striking and memorable than one's first impression of the Shwe Dagon Pagoda here in Rangoon. One sees it standing upon a lofty mound, from almost any point in the city. Just as the pagoda dominates the Rangoon landscape, so it is the most venerated shrine of Buddhism in Burma; for to myriads of devout pilgrims it is a pride, an inspiration and a hope.

There is an approach to the pagoda from each of the four points of the compass. Long stairways climb by an easy ascent from the foot to the top of the mound, between continuous lines of booths. Here good-natured 

Burmese men and pretty Burmese girls in silks of many hues, all smoking long cheroots, stand waiting to sell you candles, gongs, incense or flowers for use in paying homage to the memory of the Master to whose glory the pagoda was erected. When you emerge from this sacred bazaar, at the top of the steps, you find yourself on a high platform in the midst of which rises the great pagoda covered with gold leaf and gleaming in the sun. It is surrounded by a forest of smaller pagodas and shrines, each, however varied in form, carrying a statue of Buddha. Palm trees wave their stately branches overhead against the blue Burmese sky, and thousands of tinkly little bells encircling the pagoda-tops quiver in endless chorus as the soft breeze touches them with its unfailing caress.

Shwe Dagon lacks the qualities which usually dis-

tinguish great religious buildings. It is not specially impressive for its majesty or holiness. But on arriving at the platform you feel that you have suddenly passed into another and a different world. It is as if you were walking in the bazaars of Paradise-one of the Buddhist Paradises, where the souls of the moderately pure rest from the whirl of transmigration, where the lotus flowers are golden, and leaves on the trees are golden bells that tinkle in a perfumed breeze. 

December 1st Cheroots

Whenever I have passed a certain corner along the bus route into the city I have seen people gathered around what seemed to be a large white cigar. Upon inquiry, I learned that this was a specimen of the famous "whacking white cheroot" of Burma. This cheroot-over an inch in diameter and six or more inches long is wrapped in a white cover of maize. The small amount of tobacco in such a cigar is chopped-up leaf stalk. Its bulk is made up of shreds of aromatic woods and other combustible material. This hse-baw-leik (cheroot) is a very popular smoke, especially among the country people. It is often passed from mouth to mouth at friendly gatherings when men, women, and even children take a puff. At the bus corner one of these "community cigars" is suspended on a string and kept burning. All who are waiting for a bus are cordially invited to take a puff!

A rather tall story is told about the long cheroot. 

It is said that in certain sections: upcountry where the natives get to the bazaar only once a week, they can tell time by these cigars. One is lighted after the native

returns from the bazaar, and when it burns out, he knows it is time to start for the bazaar again! 

I went out to visit the new Karen Seminary at Insein this morning. The school is just completing a new men's dormitory and library which will add much to the campus. I attended several of the classes and was much impressed by the attitude of the young men studying for the ministry. They seemed to have a much more serious and devoted sense of mission than we do in the States. 

December 2nd Moulmein 

Deciding to spend the week end with the Rev. Mr.

Hackett and his family at Moulmein, I left today at eleven-thirty for my first Burmese train trip. There are two ways to reach Moulmein-one is by boat across the Gulf of Martaban, the other is by train. It is nearly two hundred miles east. Burma boasts the fastest narrow-gauge railroad in the world. Personally I'd rather ride on the slowest standard-gauge back home.

The compartment classes are about the same as India's with the exception that there is no intermediate class. First-class averages about three cents a mile, second-class about two cents, and third-class is less than a cent. Next to the second-class compartment at either end of the coach is usually a small ten-seater third-class compartment. I hastily made for one of these.

If India is called the Land of the Cow, then in all fairness Burma should be called the Land of the Pagoda. Emphasized by the deep green of the jungle, white pagodas dot every hill on the horizon. The Bud-

WIDER HORIZONS 

dhists (Burma is predominantly Buddhist) believe that they obtain better incarnation in the next life if they can afford to erect a pagoda. As long as a man lives he is responsible for the upkeep of the pagoda he has built, but after his death no one must ever touch it, as this might interfere with the builder's reincarnation. 

Consequently many pagodas fall to ruin; yet it is surprising how many have stood the test of time and weather.

Burma does not receive from the traveling public the attention which it deserves. A country of great and varied natural beauties, it has a charming people who are gay, brightly dressed, and possessed of a refreshingly carefree outlook on life. It offers the observer many characteristics not met with elsewhere in the Orient. Land of sunshine and pagodas, of rich agricultural areas and dense jungles, with vast valuable forests, it has been greatly neglected by travelers from abroad. 

I arrived at Martaban at 7:30 p.m. and transferred to a ferry. Moulmein is separated from western Burma by the magnificently wide Salween River. Bill, a son, is about my age, and I'll enjoy having him as a companion. We had a late dinner and chatted awhile afterward.

Bill showed me around the Judson High School this morning. His father is the principal and Bill is one of the teachers. The buildings are ancient but the spirit prevailing everywhere is one of progressiveness. Especially interesting are the woodworking shops for the boys. During this visit I was taken to the very room in which the first Burmese Bible was printed. 

As we stood in this room with its sacred memories, I could see the grateful smile on Judson's face the day the last page came off the tiny press. For twenty long years and more, he had suffered all manner of privation and pain to translate his beloved Bible into Burmese. The story of that translation is one of the most thrilling in our early missionary history. Once when

Judson was a captive of the king, he was forced to sew the translation inside a pillow. The captors became so harsh that they even took away this pillow. By what seems a miracle, one of the few converts (it took nine years to win the first convert) came across the pillow, recognized its valuable contents, and returned it to the missionary.

On the way to the teakwood factory we stopped to see the Siamese consul regarding our passports and other necessary papers. He told us the boy king is to be crowned this month-perhaps we'll get there in time for the coronation!

Of all the interesting things at Moulmein I believe

WIDER HORIZONS 

the teakwood factory is the most fascinating. Here one may see elephants at work tossing the timbers as directed almost a lost art nowadays. The giant pachyderms handle the great logs with perfect ease and control. The story of a teakwood log is romantic. It takes four or five years to get a teak tree down to the coast. 

In the green state the wood is too heavy to float. It is therefore girdled, a cut several inches deep being made near the ground to let out the sap. When dry (in three years) the tree is cut and dragged by elephants or teams of buffaloes to one of the tributary streams. At the big river it becomes part of a large raft and is floated down to Moulmein or Rangoon. Such rafts add to the picturesqueness of the river with its varied craft. 

When a raft reaches the factory the logs are brought up out of the river by means of a revolving chain. The elephants again take charge-carrying them or pushing them to and from the saws. It is a strange sight to see these great creatures gracefully balancing the logs-a slow motion picture of the majesty and might of the jungle being utilized for industry. 

Teak seldom forms more than ten per cent of the crop in the teak-bearing jungles. Teakwood is not only unsurpassed but unrivaled. Impervious to the weather, it exhibits a unique stability under extremes of heat and cold, damp and drought that makes it a general utility wood. It is naturally resistant to white ants. 

Burma exports 70 per cent of the world's teakwood. 

The Queen Mary, along with her steel hull, carries a thousand tons of Burma teak in her tremendous decks and deckhouses.

After lunch today Bill and I hiked up the hill behind Moulmein to visit the famous pagoda mentioned in

Kipling's "The Road to Mandalay." From the base of the pagoda one is afforded a magnificent panorama of 

Indo-China. Far to the east stretch vast jungles, with strange islands of rock rising out of the deep green sea of foliage. In the distance is Siam. To the north, as far as the eye can see, the mysterious Salween twists and turns on its way to the sea. To the west and south is the great Gulf of Martaban, a part of the Indian 

Ocean. The tinkling of the pagoda bells, accompanied by soft-sighing palms, provides an exquisite background of sound for such perfect scenery.

December 11th 

About the Rioting

For several days I have been preparing an article for the home-town paper (Hornell Evening Tribune) on the Burmese riots.

When one views such rioting one must realize several things. In the first place what seemingly begins as a local religious riot actually has racial, national and even international repercussions. As I was sailing for 

Rangoon last summer, a few short items appeared in the daily papers telling of serious rioting in Rangoon. 

Naturally I was interested, but that was the only information I could get. Since arriving here over a month ago I have not only made inquiries but have even had a little firsthand experience in these riots. 

The riots usually flare up from some local religious incident. For example, last summer some Mohammedans financed the printing of a supposed insult to

Buddha and the Buddhist religion (Burma is 85 per cent Buddhist). To consider action a meeting was called at the Shwe Dagon Pagoda, where the Buddhists receive most inspiration.

Burmese-Indian rioting had been brewing for a long time. When Burma was still politically joined to India, millions of coolies crossed the Bay of Bengal each year during the rice season. After appropriating all avail-

WIDER HORIZONS 

able jobs, the coolies returned to India with their earnings. That was not so bad when the countries were joined, but after Burma became politically independent the picture naturally changed. This economic sore spot bred plenty of trouble. Using the excuse of religious provocation for an attack on the defenseless Mohammedans, the Burmese were soon getting the Hindus, too. 

It's about as hard to tell a Hindu from a Muslim as it is to distinguish between members of two sects in America. When people are worked up to the fury of burning, pillaging, plundering, and rioting they don't stop to ask the next victim "Are you a Mohammedan?"

It would be easy for him to shake his head while praising Allah under his breath.

The Indians, pacifistic though they be, were not going to stand by and see things go entirely the Burman way. Thus a race riot soon developed. However, the 

Burmese mowed down their enemies right and left until several hundred had been dispatched by the "dah" (a 15-inch knife). Hundreds of Indian homes and shops were burned and general hoodlumism prevailed until martial law was declared. A law was passed forbidding four or more persons to congregate on the streets under penalty of being shot on the spot. Unless the Burmans were roving in bands, it was believed, they would not be likely to murder the Indians. [This law only temporarily stemmed the tide. In fact, it was a partial cause of a riot which took place just a few days later.]

Things had pretty well quieted down before I arrived in November. I've already mentioned previous demonstrations, which were actually "feelers" to see how prepared the government was to deal with a new riot.

Several recent incidents indicate that a new riot is in the offing. Since early fall over five thousand hypoongyis (monks) have been converging on Rangoon until they have swelled the hypoongyi chaung (monastery) to overflowing. Since they have been arriving in threes and fours scant attention has been paid to them. Lately one has been able to see the unrest caused even among the people by the influx of the priests, who usually mean business. In September, twenty thousand oil workers from upcountry began an announced march on Rangoon to protest wages, which amounts to a protest against foreignism. Several thousand dropped off along the way, because of lack of food or because of repeated warnings by the government that they would not be tolerated. Yet several thousand have arrived, and are at present encamped in temporary shelters around Shwe Dagon. A plague is feared because of the lack of sanitation. 

A third force has reared its ugly head in the guise of student demonstrations and attempted strikes. Soon after I arrived I was amazed to find several students at Judson paying little or no attention to studying. (Not all the students here are Christian, although it is the only Christian college in Burma.) I asked them why they didn't study. They replied, "What's the use?

There is sure to be another riot. This inevitably means student strikes, which would interfere with midyear exams." At first I was amused at them, but they seem to have sensed the approaching riots. Several students who are political pawns have been fomenting strikes all over the country and daring the government to fire upon them as decreed by the new law. They gather in groups of four or more just to taunt the military.

The other day one of these nominal students was arrested on the charge of stirring up unrest. Immediately great placards were erected all over the campus. 

Students began shouting, "Do-be-ma! "Do-be-ma! Do-be-ma!" ("Burma for the Burmans!"). In ensuing weeks they made us think of the Nazi legions with arms upraised, crying "Heil Hitler!" Great mass meetings of students were held all over the country. They declared that they were striking until their demands were met. At once every school was forced to close. Our students at Judson attempted to run the picket lines and for a time carry on classes, but it was useless. Those picketing were only incited to more ruthless deeds.

Even high school students hastily climbed aboard the strike wagon as a vehicle of escape from classes. Along with the older students they rode Burmese buses all over the city, drumming on the bus sides and chanting, "Do-be-ma! Do-be-ma!" The regular R.E.T. bus service was forced to discontinue. Not only did the smaller 

Burmese buses attempt collisions with the larger, bulkier buses, but the passengers were often injured by flying bricks and stones. The Burmese habit of throwing things at moving vehicles is often disastrous not only to the vehicle aimed at but to the occupants. One of the boys on my floor was hospitalized because he happened to be on an R.E.T. bus when it was the recipient of a barrage of stones. The R.E.T. buses are run on foreign capital and—adding insult to injurythey are used to carry British troops whenever martial law is invoked. Thus they are regarded as property of a belligerent, and open to the consequences.

While marching on the Secretariat, one of our own Judson boys was killed. This unfortunate occurrence

did not help the school. The Buddhists seized the opportunity to propagandize and ordered a state funeral for the boy. At first we were perturbed because he was from our school. Upon learning of a remark made by the student to one of the faculty professors, just a day before he was killed, we felt better. He said that he was looking forward to the day when the English and all whites would be swimming in their own blood; that every time he saw one of us he felt like spitting on us. 

This sounds a bit crude but it portrays the ultranationalistic temperament which has caught hold of some of the Burmans (and other Orientals, too). When asked why he had come to the Christian college, he said it was in order to make all the trouble he could. Yet the college had no right to assume such a motive or refuse him entrance when he applied.

Now what does this local situation in Rangoon have to do with the international situation? We have already seen how it provokes a strong sense of nationalism, concentrated first against the Indian but doubtless aimed ultimately at the English or white man. At a time when the Orient is everywhere manifesting its dislike for Western civilization and trying to throw off the white man's oppression, this sort of rioting may easily lead to a war for independence. At any rate it has potentialities for all kinds of trouble. If England meets force with force she gets force or what often seems worse-civil disobedience.

Especially noticeable during the rioting is the reluctance of even the peaceful natives to use English in any form of conversation. This is most inconvenient to a white man. Trivial? Yes. But effective-for this is non-violence or civil disobedience, a force more

Personally I feel that, given the right sort of government and a bit more understanding than the West has yet wanted to share, these people have great possibilities. They have a right to live. December 12th Rangoon

The Judson students make the same use of the leisure-time period for sports, every day, as did the Indian students. They use the track and play tennis, badminton, volleyball, field hockey, and various native games. The game of chinlon requires considerable skill. About half a dozen players stand in a ring. They attempt to toss a ball of plaited cane (four or five inches in diameter) from one to another, without letting it touch their hands or fall to the ground. Expert players often keep the ball going for nearly an hour from feet to shoulders, head, elbow, knee, etc. 

Exhibitions are sometimes given by expert players.

The population of Rangoon seems to embrace every race and every faith. A Rangoon crowd is one of many colors blended by the Eastern sun, from rich Indian

dresses to the lovely silks of Burma. Every morning yellow-robed monks issue from the hypoongyi chaung, carrying black begging bowls to collect the rice offerings of the faithful. On festival days happiness abounds and colors rival the glories of the sunset. 

Persons who have been privileged to see the Royal

Lakes illuminated by myriads of lamps on the water, on the islands, and in the trees along the shore will understand the abiding enchantment of Oriental life and story.

Sir George Scott writes of the Burman, "He is selfconfident without being supercilious; combative without being warlike; conspicuously lazy but capable of great feats of endurance." The Burman is really that.

The Burman might well be called the "Irishman of the East." He is excitable and subject to gusts of passion, and yet he is the most calm and contented of mortals. 

The charge of laziness has often been laid at his door, but the Burman will tell you that there are people so hopelessly ignorant that they don't know the difference between being lazy and taking life easily. The Burman can work as well as the rest when occasion arises, but he doesn't want to get rich. When he makes extra money he spends it on some pious work and rejoices in the thought that this will bring him rich reward in his next existence. After reaping his rice crop he has nothing to do for the rest of the year but to attend the village boat races or the pwes (village theatricals), or make a pilgrimage to some pagoda. The Burmese are so fond of drama that they often sit up all night at their pwes. I attended one not long ago. At midnight the audience were still going strong and the first act was not finished!

December 13th 

Port of Rangoon

In spite of the differences between India and Burma in matters of race, religion, language, temperament and tradition, Burma is an integral part of the Indian economic unit. It was an accident of administrative convenience which first joined Burma to India, but that accident had a decisive effect on their mutual trade. 

Burma lives on her exports, of which rice, oil and timber constitute 80 per cent. Rice is normally over 

40 per cent of the total, which means that cultivation of paddy is the main occupation of the people. Although a beginning has been made in industrial development, Burma as yet has no major industries apart from those by which her paddy, teak, and petroleum are converted into marketable products. The economic structure of the country depends on the rice exports. 

In 1931 it was recorded that in Burma there were over one million Indians. The great majority of them are laborers who come to work in the paddy fields, returning to India for a few months' holiday about once in two years. So considerable is this flow of Indian labor to and from Burma that a commission recently found that Rangoon was handling more immigrants and emigrants than any other port in the world.

This morning we paid a visit to the Pasteur Institute in Bangkok. It is one of three such laboratories in the world devoted exclusively to the extraction of venom from poisonous snakes. Cobras, banded kraits, and numerous varieties of Oriental reptiles are gathered

December 24th 

Ruins of Angkor

Picture to yourself one of the finest architectural productions of all the ages hidden in the depths of the jungle, in one of the remotest countries of the world. Here, in a wild, deserted area, the tracks of wild beasts have long since blotted out those of man.

The man who discovered the ruins in 1861 described them thus: "At the sight of Angkor Wat, the mind feels crushed, the imagination staggered. One can but gaze admiringly and in respectful silence, for who can justly describe a marvel of architecture that has never been equalled in the world? True, the gilding, the coloring, have practically disappeared from the edifice. Only the stones remain, but how eloquent their language. How loudly they proclaim the genius, the force, the patience, the wealth and power of the Khmerdom, or ancient Cambodians!"

The Khmers were an ancient race who built this city of over a million souls. Angkor Thom, the city, was in its day probably the largest city in the world. When it was begun, in the ninth century, Rome had collapsed; 

Paris was little more than a village; London was no metropolis. In extent and population it rivaled Carthage; in intellectual status, Athens; in might, Babylon. 

Yet it perished!

Its people and its civilization utterly vanished. All that we know is that the jungle swallowed it up and that the Khmer kingdom became as forgotten as if it never had existed. Amazing as this seems, the wondrous ruins are here-silent, indisputable testimony to a sumptuousness and glory that must have been the

We do know that they were a race of builders and conquerors, for this is attested by a hundred temples, a thousand towers, and by projects so colossal that only slaves at the point of a sword could possibly have been induced to undertake them.

They built their temples of stone-they endure.

The bas-reliefs alone are worth coming around the world to see. Nothing like them exists anywhere.

They have something of the inspiration, composition, and symmetrical perfection of Greece, Rome, Arabia and the Renaissance combined, yet they have been influenced by none save the Hindu and Chinese.

The Naga, met everywhere at Angkor, especially at the ends of the balustrades, is the most imaginative and finest creation of the Khmer sculptor. It is the cobra,

Yet the most spectacular of all the amazing creations of the Khmers are huge four-faced towers, such as surmount the gateways to Angkor Thom. There is much speculation as to which deity these towers resemble, but the contention of many experts is that they represent the four-faced god Siva.

All the Khmer temples were designed to make the deepest possible impression upon the visitor to cause him to feel the might of the divinities enshrined therein. One must remember that a traveler in the days when the Khmers flourished was not a sight-seer but a reverential pilgrim, sometimes from a faraway land. 

He was made to believe that the temples reproduce on earth the divine majesty of heaven. We have a similar feeling about our cathedrals.

Angkor Wat, in its beauty and state of preservation, is unrivaled. Its mightiness and magnificence bespeak a pomp and luxury surpassing that of a Pharaoh or a Moghul. It is not really a ruin. The roofs still turn the rains.

Rice Terrace 

I thought the Himalayan bus roads couldn't be beat! 

I was wrong. Immediately upon leaving Baguio this morning, we began another climb-three thousand feet this time-on a one-lane road which hung precariously on the edge of mile-deep canyons. 

After two hours of bumping, bouncing, and swaying around sickening curves, most of the nineteen passengers had become ill. At a point where we could see straight down several thousand feet, I ventured to ask if any of the buses ever went off the cliffs. "Oh, yes," replied the driver, apparently immune to the dizzy heights. "Not long ago a bus carrying twenty-five passengers failed to take one of the curves."

We have had to stop every few miles at a sort of gate. This is a safety measure on the narrow road. 

The gatekeepers phone ahead to report how many vehicles have passed through.

As we left a certain gate the bus driver said to me, "Do you remember that gasoline truck we passed just after leaving the last gate?" I replied that I did. "It went over the side a few minutes ago."

Mountains usually look much alike the world over, except of course that some peaks are higher, more rugged or snow-covered. Not only are the very shapes of the Philippine mountains seemingly different, but one of the wonders of the world awaits the traveler who visits them-the famous rice terraces! They are an awe-inspiring spectacle.

The terraces were constructed by the Igorots, the primitive natives that inhabit this region, without any engineering knowledge whatsoever. Begun many centuries ago, they have been improved and enlarged by each generation until they stretch from mountain to mountain, up and down the valley. It took centuries to grade and fill in the mountainsides enough to support the terraces. Each one is rectangular, ranging from a few square feet to an acre or more.

The terraces are built on a graduated scale, from tiny plots at elevations of several thousand feet to broad ones at the base of the mountains. Nowhere else in the world are the natives ingenious enough to attempt to grow rice on the very side of steep mountains. 

The irrigation system is an intricate one. The flow of water is directed by means of tiny troughs which are laid in zigzag fashion. After one terrace has used the water, it seeps through a tiny fissure onto the terrace immediately below. In other cases it is carried by the troughs to terraces farther down the mountain. 

I learned later that the chief crime in these parts is to delay the flow of water onto the lower terraces.

While out walking I visited the village council ring where the elders of the town gather to pass the time.

Many hundreds of years ago the people who lived in the plains of central Luzon were driven back to the mountains by repeated waves of invasion from the mainland of Asia. Unable to hold their own, these lowlanders retreated into the wild, precipitous, pine-clad, cloud-covered hill country. Year after year they pressed farther north up the river valleys. Here they settled in groups where sufficient water could be found for their rice fields. They built their peaked grass-covered huts, sometimes high up under a mountain ridge, sometimes in a narrow valley, but always in places difficult of access. Thus they found peace from their enemies at the expense of complete isolation, strenuous labor to raise sufficient food, and growth of deadly feuds between town and town. Few ever traveled beyond the

Even as they worked in their rice fields, the head axe by their side and the spear stuck in the mud close by proclaimed constant fear of a raid from some neighboring enemy town. Thus each town or group of towns evolved its own dialect and customs and lived its own life in a world which to these people was bounded by the mountain peaks which overhung them. 

The only relief from the almost endless labor of food production was the canau (pronounced can-yow) or religious feast with which every important event was celebrated; the only recreation, that of head-hunting.

China Sea

February 21st

Kobe A day out of Shanghai put us at Moji, Japan's southern portal at the entrance to the beautiful Inland Sea.

It was good to see the fresh green of the pine-clad hills, for we have not seen any verdure since leaving the Philippines.

Today we landed at Kobe, the port city of entry for travelers coming from India, Australia, and Europe. 

It is the largest trade port in the Orient and the fifth largest city in the Japanese Empire, with a population equal to that of our own Cleveland. 

The N.Y.K. Line offers its passengers optional railway tickets if they prefer to go overland to Yokohama, instead of traveling by boat to that port. Since one can see many things in Japan in three days, with its rapid transportation lines, we gladly accepted the opportunity.

In order to include the most important sights in a few days, we must run on a schedule. We allowed ourselves two hours in Kobe. We were met at the boat by a Boy Scout delegation. Ram's guardian, Mr. Tunstall, who is an International Scouting executive, had written the Japanese movement of our arrival. They took us immediately to the Chamber of Commerce, where we received souvenir cards and all sorts of materials. We explained about our short stay here, hoping to eliminate a lot of formality.

We visited several important shrines. At one point we left the taxi and walked about the bazaars, where we saw our first chin-don-ya or advertising procession -a small band with a clown and several banners calling attention to a particular shop. 

The Japanese wear getas (pronounced gay-tas), shoes with two wooden pieces across the sole to elevate the foot. Getas make a sound like a shod horse. The crowded bazaar sounds like a cavalry parade.

I asked our guides please to take us to Shinkawa, the slums of Kobe. I wanted to visit the scene of Kagawa's work among the poor. Ever since reading his little book of poems entitled Songs from the Slums I have yearned to see Shinkawa.

Songs from the Slums was written during his first years in Shinkawa, when he was in the very midst of the sacrificial life which first set him apart from his fellow men as "one who takes Jesus in earnest." Born a son of the samurai, educated in a Christian seminary, he looked until he found the place where his services were most needed. Renting a room six feet square, without bed, table, stove, or chair, he lived and loved and served the outcasts of society, disregarding the advice of friends. In time he became tubercular, and he was all but blinded with trachoma. 

Bullied, persecuted, tormented by criminals and traffickers in vice, hounded by police who grew suspicious of his efforts in behalf of labor, he never hesitated in the prosecution of the work he believed God had called him to do in Shinkawa. His prayer was: "O God, make me like Christ."

As we walked about, I recalled his lines: "But here there are slippery streets, which never are dry; they are lined with open sewers, where rats come out to die." How thankful we should be that he snatched brief intervals in his drab days and fearsome nights to write down his heart's deepest emotions, rare poems sketched in brush letters on sheets from old magazines. 

His verses on the slums have blossomed with the beauty of the Japanese magnolia, "gleaming boughs alight with white against the gloom" of indescribable wretchedness. Although he depicts abject squalor and misery,

at the same time he lifts one's eyes to the Hope of the 

World, through his manifest love and tireless ministrations.

One of the Scouts graciously accompanied us to Osaka, forty-five minutes distant by train. After lunch we again set out sight-seeing. Boasting a population of three million, second largest city in Japan, Osaka has a remarkable castle, built in 1585. 

In the southern part of the city, among many temples, is an ancient and famous Buddhist shrine, founded thirteen hundred years ago. Tennoji Temple is especially noteworthy because of its huge bronze bell, the largest in the world. Thousands of pious worshippers passed to and fro, stopping at one temple to turn the "Wheel of Life," at another to light candles, or to attach paper strips to the wall of their favorite temple. 

When one approaches a famous Japanese shrine, it is difficult to tell whether one is near a bazaar or temple, for both sides of the walks are lined with booths. 

Here one can buy toys; nicknacks of all sorts (it's a kind of Oriental Woolworth's, housed in booths); pipes; character paper; and sheaves of wood, almost like shingles, which worshippers use to appease the gods. One is impressed with the fact that the people are very loyal to their religion. We returned to the station, this time to entrain for Kyoto.

Capital of Japan for more than a thousand years, Kyoto is a historic city, rich in classical associations and legendary lore. Its artistic customs give it charm and it is a mecca for tourists.

February 22nd

Fujiyama 

We traveled all night on the train so as to reach Odawara this morning at six. The temperature was just 30° Fahrenheit as we changed to the electric train for Miyanoshita. There we visited the world-famous 

Fujiya Hotel before starting for Fujiyama.

After a hearty breakfast at the hotel we took a bus to Nagao Pass. With eight inches of snow on the ground and the bus failing to return for two hours, it was lucky for us that there was a small teahouse near the pass.

We spent two hours alternately gazing at the majestic Fujiyama and warming ourselves around a little earthen jar containing anemic coals. 

Known throughout the world for its flawless beauty, Fujiyama rises well over twelve thousand feet above sea level. It has a circumference of sixty-five miles around its base. We had intended to take the motor drive around Fuji and its five lakes, but snow and landslides made the roads impassable. 

On returning to the hotel, we were invited to see the Onaga-Dori (long-tailed fowls) which this celebrated hotel exhibits. In the eighteenth century, after a cer-

tain feudal lord changed his family's emblem to that of a spear decorated with long feathers, one of his retainers succeeded in raising birds with surprisingly beautiful tail feathers. These he presented to his lord. It is supposed that the birds were a cross between green pheasants and common barnyard fowl. 

After many decades birds were bred that would produce purewhite long tails. The tail feathers, fifteen to twentyfour in number, are never moulted. Ordinarily they measure about eleven feet in length.

After lunch we returned to Odawara and entrained once more, this time for Yokohama. There I collected twenty-five letters (the first mail I have had since leaving India) before proceeding to Tokyo. 

Two things have impressed me most in our journeys through Nippon-industrialization, and the innate ability of the Japanese to assimilate what they desire from other cultures and still retain what is characteristic in their own.

Tokyo epitomizes New Japan with its numerous fine buildings, its wide clean highways, its parks and transit facilities for the third largest city in the world—one having a population of nearly seven million. 

Had it not been for the great earthquake of September, 1923, Tokyo would have assumed a modernized but queer aspect, with narrow streets and labyrinths of crooked alleys, discreditable to the capital city and a hindrance to the flow of traffic. The disastrous fire following the earthquake reduced about half the city to ashes in a couple of days. Reconstruction was started upon a gigantic scale, and was brought to completion in less than seven years.

Tonight, as we sat down to supper, we were each given a plate with a wadded-up damp cloth on it. We wondered what on earth it was for. Looking about us, we discovered that it is the Japanese custom to make sure the guests' hands are clean before they eat a meal. 

Smilingly we performed our belated ablutions.

Nikko has the most lovely setting in Japan. The gorgeous shrines and temples, the mountains, lakes and waterfalls, have no equal in the Orient. Although it is still winter, with snow several inches thick on the ground, winter cannot chill the scenery that awaits the pilgrim.

Standing by the ancient Red Lacquer Bridge over the Daiya River, we found ourselves absorbed in an inspiring scene, combining natural and man-made symbols of religious worship. It was a strange mixture of the 

Gothic pointing of the giant cryptomeria trees and the

ornate yet mystic temples of Shinto. Beyond the sacred bridge, amid the groves of godlike firs, stands the world-famed mortuary temple of the shoguns. In spite of the snow and cold, hundreds of devout pilgrims had arrived.

Observing the Oriental requirement that the Westerner remove his shoes (the Orientals of course must take off their sandals), we did so upon entering each temple. Br-r-r! The bamboo mats surely were cold today. It was bad enough to have to remove our shoes, but a little later we were told that our coats must be left outside when we entered the “Holy of Holies." It didn't take us long to see that temple!

I thought the most interesting temple was the one called the "Temple of the Laughing Dragon." It is the 

Buddhist custom to bow and clap the hands twice in front of the altar. In this particular temple a huge dragon is painted on the ceiling. Whenever anyone claps his hands, there is a faint echo as if the dragon were laughing-hence the name.

For sheer color, Nikko outdoes any shrines or temples in the world. Bright reds and golden yellows, supported by a host of brilliant colors which only a Japanese artisan could perfect, characterize all the sanctuaries. Leaving Nikko we returned southward to the land of industry.

March 2nd

Sukiyaki Tonight was the night! We had our long-awaited sukiyaki dinner party on the promenade deck. It is an event which those who have sailed the Seven Seas agree is unique. 

The guests remove their shoes and sit on straw mats on the deck. The preparation of the dish takes place before the dinner.

Each "table" is provided with its own "kitchen" in the shape of an electric fire, and a frying pan in which the meal is prepared. As the various foods which, when blended, form the sukiyaki, cook before the eyes, the aroma forecasts the delight to come. Most impor-

tant is the beef or gyu, which is the mainstay of the meal. The beef must be tender and cut into very thin slices. Next in importance come cut leeks, onions, and the nutritious bean curd tofu. Then one adds a Japanese version of spaghetti. Ajinomoto is a dash of seasoning. For sauces there are shoyu (soya-bean) and, if desired, sake (rice wine).

First the pan is greased with suet held by chopsticks (hashi). The steward usually acts as host in preparing the meal. After the sauces, the vegetables are added, followed by the beef. By this time the guest discovers a hunger he didn't know existed. He begins eating the vegetables while the meat is still cooking. 

Rice bowls are brought forth-rice acts as a base for all dishes and a sort of fish soup. To cool the food Our sukiyaki dinner on shipboard-a novel experience.

and add another flavor to the sukiyaki, it is dipped in a bowl containing a beaten raw egg. It's a good idea to practice with chopsticks in advance or you're likely just to smell the delicious dish. 

It is said that years ago Japanese farmers found that meat fried on a plowshare was the most appetizing. 

Sukiyaki means "to fry on a plow," and the present elaborate dish, we are told, has developed from this humble beginning. 

March 3rd Honolulu 

Honolulu! Paradise of the Pacific! The Royal Hawaiian Band met our boat, as is the custom. There was singing, too. It was a musical welcome. I shall never forget the strains of "Aloha" as we drew near the pier. Stepping from the boat a few minutes later we were given garlands called leis (lays). Almost to Frisco 

Tomorrow we land at San Francisco.

The horizons have certainly widened for me in these past months. Shore lines have appeared, only to melt away. Racial colors have blended into one. Political conceptions have expanded. Ideals have broadened into a more inclusive philosophy of life. 

As I sat on the deck this afternoon, my thoughts turned to the Christian laborers I had left behind on many fields. Can anyone read or hear about the works of missionaries and then say, "Why should we send our Christian workers over there?" We ourselves have no right to go out to the ends of the earth unless we are impelled by the unmistakable urging of God to become His ambassadors. But if we have faith that our Christian way is the way to more abundant living, even though our religious experience be meager, there will be no sense of inferiority, of having little to offer. 

The fact that men from one part of the world, in spite of all that makes their message hard to understand, can help men in another part of the world to meet Christ, is inexplicable unless God Himself is the real agent in the process of witnessing. 

Before I went out to our Church Abroad, I believed that there was no hope for the world except in men and missions, and no way of building character adequate for the task save through Jesus Christ. I believed that before, I am sure of it now!
