I. 

STUDY ON TRIBAL CAPABILITY TO ASSUME REGULATORY PRIMACY Introduction A. Purpose 

The purpose of this report is to provide (1) a comprehensive evaluation of the current capabilities of the three coal-producing tribes (Crow, Hopi, and Navajo) to implement 

Title V of the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 

1977 (SMCRA, or the Act, P.L. 95-87, 30 U.S.C. 1201 et seq.), (2) feedback to the tribes regarding their performance under Title V funding cooperative agreements, and (3) direction for future Title V funding cooperative agreements. OSMRE has previously conducted internal evaluations of the three tribes and has considered the information from those studies in the preparation of this report.

In evaluating the tribes, OSMRE has reviewed work products prepared by or for the tribes pursuant to the tasks specified in their Title V cooperative agreements and has also referred to the Council of Energy Resource Tribes' (CERT) draft report entitled "Tribal Capability and Experience in Regulation of 

Surface Coal Mining.

" The report has also been produced, in part, in response to the General Accounting Office's (GAO) report of October 1986 entitled "Surface Mining: Regulatory Capability of Indian Tribes Should Be Assessed. The GAO 

report concluded that while OSMRE has actively funded both 

Title IV and Title V tribal activities for a number of years, the agency has not comprehensively assessed overall tribal readiness to assume regulatory jurisdiction for coal mining and reclamation activities on their lands. Furthermore, GAO concluded that such an evaluation 'would enable OSMRE to determine whether future agreements are needed and, if they are needed, the tasks that should be included." 

GAO also recommended that in conducting the evaluation, OSMRE should first establish criteria upon which to assess tribal capability. In evaluating the regulatory capability of the three tribes, OSMRE utilized the same criteria as those used for the evaluation of proposed State programs, recognizing that it is not clear what criteria will actually apply once Federal legislation pursuant to section 710 of SMCRA is enacted. For example, the Congress might apply the criteria that pertain to State programs, in some modified form, or provide for only partial primacy, or for partial primacy as one option which the tribes might select. -1-

In responding to GAO's July 1986 draft of the report, the Department of the Interior expressed its agreement with GAO's recommendations and stated that a study of tribal regulatory capabilities would be initiated by OSMRE in Fiscal Year (FY) 1987. The Department's response was published as part of 

GAO's final report released in October 1986 (GAO/RCED-87-34).

B. Background 1. SMCRA Provisions For Indian Lands Regulation 

SMCRA establishes minimum environmental protection and reclamation standards for the regulation of surface coal mining and the surface effects of underground mining on all lands within the United States. Those standards, contained in Title V (Control of the Environmental 

Impacts of Surface Coal Mining) of SMCRA, are intended to minimize the adverse environmental impacts of mining and to ensure adequate reclamation upon completion of mining. Title IV (Abandoned Mine Reclamation) of SMCRA 

addresses the problem of abandoned mine lands mined prior to August 3, 1977, and establishes an Abandoned Mine Reclamation Fund to reclaim and restore land and water resources adversely affected by such mining. 

Ultimate responsibility for the administration and enforcement of SMCRA lies with the Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement (OSMRE) within the 

Department of the Interior. However, pursuant to section 503 of SMCRA, the individual States may apply for and receive regulatory jurisdiction, or primacy, over the non-Federal and non-Indian lands within their boundaries by submitting for the Secretary of the

Interior's approval a Title V regulatory program which demonstrates the State's ability to carry out the provisions of the Act. Any State with an approved Title 

V program may also seek the Secretary's approval to implement a Title IV program for reclamation of abandoned mine lands.

SMCRA contains no such mechanism for tribal assumption of primacy on Indian lands, which until recently also precluded the possibility of tribal Title IV authority as well, inasmuch as primacy was a prerequisite to Title IV program approval. However, the Congress recently passed legislation authorizing the Crow, Hopi, and Navajo tribal access to Title IV monies without an approved Title V program. P.L. 100-71 was signed into law by President Reagan on July 11, 1987. -2-

2. 

Section 710(a) of SMCRA directs the Secretary to study the question of surface coal mining regulation on Indian lands and to submit to the Congress a report containing proposed legislation designed to allow the Indian tribes to elect to assume full regulatory authority. Although the required report was submitted to the Congress in February 1984, very little progress has been made toward enactment of section 710 legislation. Pending passage of such legislation and the tribes' subsequent assumption of primacy, the Secretary, through OSMRE, will continue to exercise regulatory jurisdiction over

Indian lands with respect to surface coal mining and reclamation operations.

Tribal-Federal Intergovernmental Agreements 

Although OSMRE currently serves as the regulatory authority for surface coal mining and reclamation operations on Indian lands, this responsibility could ultimately be assumed by the tribes themselves. In anticipation of eventual tribal primacy, OSMRE initiated a program of Tribal-Federal intergovernmental agreements, commonly referred to as funding cooperative agreements, in the late 1970's and early 1980's to assist the coal-producing tribes in developing their regulatory capabilities. Under this program, Title V 

funding agreements with the Crow, Hopi, and Navajo Tribes were established in FY 1982 and have been subsequently renewed on a regular basis. The agreements for all three tribes have been renewed through FY 1987, although the Crow received only 9 months of funding under the current agreement because of the timing of the tribe's submission of an adequate funding application.

The Title V funding cooperative agreements with the tribes encompass tribal participation and assistance in a full range of regulatory activities on Indian lands, including the review of permit application packages, permit revisions and renewals, transfers and sales of permits, mid-term permit reviews, inspection and enforcement activities, and abandonment, bond release or forfeiture proceedings. Regulatory decisionmaking remains with OSMRE, however. Funds are also authorized for development of tribal surface mining and reclamation codes and regulations; tribal review of proposed section 

710 legislation; technical, administrative, and -3-

regulatory training of tribal staff members and development of management capabilities; land use planning activities; and attendance at joint 

OSMRE/tribal conferences regarding Title V program status.

Title V agreements typically authorize payment for tribal work products, such as detailed work plans and proposed training for tribal staff; tribal comments on 

OSMRE-prepared environmental assessments, technical analyses, and environmental impact statements for existing and proposed mining operations on Indian lands; formal letters of recommendation on proposed permit application package submittals; and proposed tribal surface mining and reclamation codes and regulations. 

The agreements also fund tribal attendance at meetings with OSMRE, other Federal agencies, and the applicants to discuss their technical concerns related to proposed actions. Tribal staff members also routinely accompany 

OSMRE Field Office inspectors on monthly inspections of surface mines located on their lands and file follow-up inspection reports, thus providing OSMRE personnel the opportunity to evaluate the tribes' performance in the field and their ability to carry out associated administrative functions.

As the funding agency, OSMRE is responsible for monitoring tribal performance under the cooperative agreements. To date this has generally been done informally, primarily by OSMRE's Western Field Operations Office in Denver and the Casper and 

Albuquerque Field Offices, where the majority of the interaction with the tribes takes place. Western Field

Operations Office in Denver functions as OSMRE's permitting office for the Western United States and, as such, consults with all three tribes on technical, environmental, and socioeconomic issues associated with the review and processing of permit application packages, permit revisions and renewals, and mid-term permit reviews. Tribal work products generated during the consultation process are reviewed by Western Field

Operations personnel in Denver for adequacy, quality, and timeliness and are utilized as appropriate. -4-

The field offices coordinate with the tribes in relation to program administration and inspection and enforcement and monitor the associated field and administrative duties performed by the tribe or tribes located within their geographic area of responsibility. The Casper 

Field Office deals exclusively with the Crow Tribe, whereas the Albuquerque Field Office works with both the 

Hopi and Navajo Tribes. OSMKE's Denver and Headquarters offices have both previously conducted formal evaluations of the proposed Crow Reclamation Code in 1980 and 1982, respectively. These evaluations were provided to the tribe.

As stated earlier, funding cooperative agreements were originally established as a means of developing tribal regulatory proficiency to its fullest potential. 

However, OSMRE has determined that consideration of individual tribal work products and activities provides only a cursory indication as to the overall success or failure of the agreements in achieving this goal, and that this comprehensive study of tribal capabilities is necessary.

C. Scope and Methodology This report examines the Title V regulatory capabilities of the Crow, Hopi, and Navajo Tribes, presently the only coal-producing tribes and, thus, the most likely to seek primacy if section 710 legislation is enacted. (Tribal abandoned mine reclamation capabilities are not evaluated in this report because, as noted above, abandoned mine reclamation is not part of a Title V regulatory program under 

SMCRA.) Evaluations of other coal-owning tribes would be premature until those tribes demonstrate an interest in developing and assuming regulatory responsibilities for surface coal mining activities on their lands. 

Part II contains summaries of three types of evaluation: (1) 

OSMRE compared each tribe's proposed reclamation code or ordinance with SMCRA; (2) OSMRE compared tribal laws and regulations, performance, procedures, organization, and staffing with 30 CFR 731.14 (Content Requirements for Program 

Submissions) and 30 CFR 732.15 (Criteria for Approval of

State Programs); and (3) OSMRE conducted a comprehensive review of tribe-specific performance under the cooperative agreements. In the absence of section 710 legislation, OSMRE has chosen to compare the proposed codes or ordinances with -5-

the requirements of SMCRA assuming that such legislation, when enacted, would require that any Indian program contain provisions closely paralleling those of SMCRA. 

Part III is an analysis of associated regulatory and legal issues generic to all tribes seeking Title V primacy. 

Part IV summarizes the findings in Parts II and III. 

Appendices A through C contain detailed comparisons of the proposed tribal reclamation codes and regulatory capabilities with the statutory and regulatory requirements. The analyses of tribal reclamation codes are not formal findings of consistency with SMCRA or with Federal regulations. A final evaluation of consistency can only occur if, pursuant to section 710 legislation, OSMRE is called on to review a formal tribal regulatory program submission. 

Appendix D contains copies of the comments received in response to distribution of the preliminary report to the Congress, Indian tribes, industry, and other interested parties for review and comment.

Organizational Structure a.

Tribal Government 

The Crow Tribal Government consists of four elected officials: Chairman, Vice-Chairman, Secretary, and 

Vice-Secretary. Election of these officials is held on a biennial basis in May with installation in July, or more frequently if a special election is held. The next election is scheduled for May 

1988. No primary is held, and elections are based on a majority of the popular vote. A recent amendment to the Constitution and Bylaws of the Crow Tribal Council provides that a two-thirds vote -8-

b. of eligible voters present is required for impeachment of elected officials. This new requirement may reduce the turnover rate among elected officials, since impeachment previously required only a simple majority vote.

Each adult member of the Crow Tribe has a voting right at all tribal council meetings. All tribal governmental actions are conducted through this council at quarterly meetings. A majority vote is required to act on all motions, 100 voting members must be present for a quorum, and business is conducted according to Robert's Rules of Order.

(2) History and Current Status 

The first Title V funding for the Crow Tribe was in the form of a contract for the period 

September 1980 through February 1983. The original contract included three full-time employees; however, various contract modifications allowed the staffing level to fluctuate according to projects. A maximum of seven employees was funded at one time during the contract duration.

The Crow Tribe's first Title V cooperative agreement overlapped with the contract funding described above and covered the time period 

May 1982 through September 1983. Seven full-time positions were funded. -9-

Beginning in 1984, OSMRE provided joint funding to the Crow Tribe for Title IV and Title V projects. Prior to this time, funding was received separately for Title IV and Title 

V. The 1984 cooperative agreement provided funding for six full-time employees. The 1985 and 1986 cooperative agreements (again combining both Title IV and Title V programs) 

funded five full-time employees and three full-time employees, respectively.

The Crow FY 1986 cooperative agreement expired on September 30, 1986, and FY 1987 funding was approved in March 1987. The Crow submitted a satisfactorily complete application for FY 

1987 funding in January 1987, prepared jointly with OSMRE's Casper Field Office; however, approval was delayed pending resolution of disallowed audit costs from a previous cooperative agreement. The FY 1987 Crow Tribe funding is approved through September 1987.

Upon expiration of the 1986 cooperative agreement, one staff member continued to report to work, apparently without pay, and the other two were placed on indefinite furloughs beginning in October 1986. The Crow notified OSMRE that they had hired new staff members in January 1987, even though they had not yet received OSMRE funding for their program.

The Casper Field Office recommended staffing three full-time positions for FY 1987 and requested that the Crow retain at least one of the previous staff members for continuity. As approved, the 1987 cooperative agreement allows for three full-time technical employees and one part-time clerical employee. The Crow voluntarily reduced the salaries of the staff and other expenses in order for their budget to accommodate the additional part-time position.

At this time, the exact qualifications and disciplines of the new staff are not known to OSMRE; however, one technical employee does have previous experience working with the -10-

program and the Crow have assured OSMRE that the other employees will be sufficiently qualified.

Management courses and grants training have been provided to the Crow staff as well as technical training in such disciplines as soils, revegetation, aerial photography, engineering practices, mine inspection, and hydrology. Some Crow personnel have also received informal on-the-job training from OSMRE in reviewing permit application packages and conducting inspections. The Crow Office of Reclamation has not requested funding for use of supplemental expertise from other Crow agencies and has not indicated that such expertise is or will be utilized. 

Potential for turnover within the Crow Office of Reclamation is relatively high, owing to frequent changes in Crow tribal leadership, which generally result in corresponding wholesale personnel changes in other areas of tribal government. Most recently, the Crow 

Office of Reclamation underwent a staffing change following the 1986 general tribal election, when two of the three FY 1986 staff members were replaced. This situation tends to create an unstable working environment which may adversely affect employee morale and productivity. It is also costly in terms of time, money, and retraining efforts, and severely limits the tribe's ability to develop a solid base of experience in program administration. 

In the absence of SMCRA section 710 legislation and because of declining workload projections and the limited potential for expanded mining activity in the near future (see discussion under "Workload" below), OSMRE has reduced funding for the Crow Office of 

Reclamation under the FY 1987 cooperative agreement.

III. Associated Regulatory and Legal Issues 

In order to assess the capability of the tribes to assume regulatory authority, it is necessary to make assumptions as to -41-

what areas the tribes will regulate. The Act does define Indian lands, but that definition serves to establish OSMRE's regulatory jurisdiction over those lands under SMCRA. It is not clear whether or to what extent the Congress would retain that definition as the basis for establishing tribal jurisdiction under SMCRA. The current definition has generated considerable confusion as to what is included for purposes of OSMRE's jurisdiction. There is no dispute that the definition reaches all Indian lands on the reservation. It is also clear that some off-reservation lands are included. Both off-reservation and on-reservation lands are included in the SMCRA definition. 

However, the exact extent of tribal authority over various types of Indian lands outside the reservation is not clear under existing law. For example, tribes may have different authority over off-reservation tribal trust interests than over off-reservation tribal fee interests, and there may be differences in tribal authority over off-reservation tribal surface and mineral estates. Thus, although it may be relatively clear what is OSMRE's jurisdiction over Indian lands, it is not at all clear that tribes could exercise similar authority over all lands included in the definition. 

Because of the uncertainty as to what authority tribes might ultimately exercise over off-reservation lands, for the purposes of this study OSMRE has assessed capabilities only with respect to on-reservation lands. Where appropriate, OSMRE has reviewed off-reservation regulatory issues.

A. Tribal Authority to Regulate Land Use by Individual Indians and Non-Indians Within the Reservation. 

There is no question that Indian tribes have the general authority to regulate activities of Indians within reservations. Tribal authority to regulate the use of non-Indian fee lands within the reservation by non-Indians has been the subject of considerable litigation. The test for tribal authority to regulate non-Indian activity on fee lands enunciated by the Supreme Court in Montana v. United 

States, 450 U.S. 544, 565-566 (1981) has been applied by the 

Tower Federal courts to uphold tribal authority to regulate land use and other activities of non-Indians on non-Indian fee land within reservations. Knight v. Shoshone and 

Arapahoe Tribes, 670 F. 2d 900 (10th c. 1982); Cardin v. 

DeLaCruz, 671 F. 2d 363, (9th c. 1982), cert. denied 103 S. 

Ct. 293 (1982); Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes v. 

Namen, 665 F. 2d 951 (9th c. 1982), cert. denied 103 S. Ct. 

314 (1982). -42-

B. 

The Montana test, particularly as applied by the lower courts, recognizes tribal authority to regulate non-Indian use of fee land which "threatens or has some direct effect on the political integrity, the economic security, or the health or welfare of the tribe." Under this test, it seems clear that Indian tribes would (after enactment of section 710 legislation and Title V program approval) have authority to regulate surface coal mining operations on reservation non-Indian fee lands because those operations would have potential effects on the health and welfare of the tribe. 

The Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit has recently upheld tribal court jurisdiction to award civil damages against non-Indians who violated substantive civil regulations of the tribe. Babbit Ford, Inc. v. Navajo Indian 

Tribe, 710 F.2d 587 (9th c. 1983) petition for cert. filed, 52 U.S.L.W. 3324 (No. 83-610, Oct. 11, 1983). Under present law, Indian tribes have no criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, Oliphant v. Suquamish Tribe, 435 U.S. 191 (1978), and the criminal penalties they may impose upon Indians are limited by the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. 

1301 et seq., to 1 year's imprisonment and/or a fine of $5,000. Penalties normally criminal in nature have been held unenforceable against non-Indians when imposed for violations of a civil ordinance. Quechan Tribe v. Row, 531 F 2d 408, (9th c. 1976); United States v. Montana, 604 F. 2d 1162 (9th c. 1979).

Off-Reservation Authority 

The issue of tribal authority off-reservation is a key element in determining the relationships among State, Federal and tribal regulatory authorities. This is important because of the extensive amount of off-reservation Indian lands. 

Each of the three coal-producing tribes analyzed in this report owns coal, or land which is underlain with possible coal deposits, outside their reservation boundaries. The mineral rights covering approximately 150,000 acres outside the reservation are held in trust by the United States for the Crow Tribe, while individual Crow trust allotments total approximately 17,000 acres. The Navajo Tribe's off-reservation landholdings include the fee title to approximately 495,000 acres of surface and minerals, and an additional 588,000 acres of surface estates which are held in trust by the United States. Individual Navajo trust allotments cover 595,181 acres of surface estates. addition, the Navajo and Hopi jointly own the minerals underlying the 1.8-million-acre former Navajo-Hopi Joint Use

Area. In this case, each tribe has an interest in minerals on the other tribe's reservation.

In -43-

C. it Indian tribes are not generally recognized as possessing independent authority to regulate land use outside their reservations. However, if the Congress elects to do so, may extend tribal authority to clearly reach off-reservation areas. Tribal authority derives from the tribes' power to control the use of their lands, and activities on their lands, which significantly involve the tribe or its members, as well as from the power to exclude non-Indians. 

Two recent Supreme Court cases address these powers in the context of tribal power to tax non-Indian activities within reservations. Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the 

Colville Indian Reservation, 447 U.S. 134, 152 (1980); 

Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe, 455 U.S. 130, 144-148 (1982). A decision of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth 

Circuit has upheld the authority of the Northern Cheyenne

Tribe, in conjunction with authority delegated by the 

Environmental Protection Agency under the Clean Air Act, to redesignate air quality for its reservation even though, as the court recognized, the tribe's action affected land use outside the reservation. Nance v. Environmental Protection 

Agency, 645 F. 2nd 701, (9th Cir 1981), cert. denied sub nom.

Crow Tribe v. Environmental Protection Agency, 454 U.S. 1081 (1981). 

The limits of authority of tribes to engage in regulatory activities on Indian lands outside the boundaries of reservations have not been established. Should the Congress transfer to the tribes the regulatory authority over such lands, tribal technical and administrative capability to regulate are likely to be comparable to their ability on the reservation.

D. Tribal Imposition of Penalties 

Sections 503 (a) (2) and 518(i) of the Act require State regulatory authorities to have civil and criminal jurisdiction to carry out the provisions of the Act. Under existing law, Indian tribes may exercise only civil jurisdiction over non-Indians within their reservations, with criminal offenses regarding nonmembers being subject to 

Federal jurisdiction. 

The Supreme Court has held that the retained sovereignty of Indian tribes over tribal reservations does not include the power to punish non-Indians who commit offenses against tribal law. Oliphant v. Suquamish Tribe, 435 U.S. 191 

(1978); Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544 (1981). The court reasoned that retained criminal jurisdiction to try and punish non-Indians is inconsistent with the dependent status of tribes under the Federal system and, therefore, was implicitly surrendered. Although the court's decision does not limit the Indian tribes' authority to impose civil penalties over non-Indians, it does create obstacles with respect to the ability of tribes to enforce otherwise permissible regulations through criminal sanctions. This stands as a direct bar to the ability of any tribe to achieve primacy. Specific legislative language would be needed to authorize the tribes to impose criminal sanctions against non-Indians and to increase the maximum penalties tribes can impose (or to specify some other workable enforcement process).

With regard to criminal penalties, section 518(e) of the Act requires that upon conviction for a willful and knowing violation of a permit condition or various other orders issued pursuant to the Act, a fine of not more than $10,000 and/or imprisonment for not more than 1 year be imposed. 

Presently, the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 (ICRA) limits the criminal penalties a tribal court may impose for such offenses to no more than 1 year's imprisonment and/or a fine of $5,000. 

E. Citizen Suits

Section 520 of the Act provides that citizens having an interest which could be adversely affected may commence a civil action against the States, their agencies or any person alleged to be violating the Act, or the State regulatory authority where there is an alleged failure to perform any -48-

nondiscretionary duty or act to compel compliance with the Act. The Congress believed that citizen participation in the regulatory process was important to the success or failure of the surface coal mining program. Indian tribes acting in the capacity of a sovereign regulatory authority would be immune from suit. States are required to waive any immunity to citizen suit in order to obtain State program approval. 

Indian tribes have similar immunity and should also be required to waive immunity upon becoming regulatory authorities. A tribe which participates in a joint venture to develop its coal reserves is arguably a "person" under section 701 (10) of the Act and may be subject to suit in that capacity. However, all tribal immunity from suit should be waived for all issues arising under the Act.

In its 1984 legislative report, the Department recommended that the Congress authorize civil suits against an Indian tribe for violating the Act, or against a tribal regulatory authority for failing to perform its duties, in the same manner that such suits are authorized against States. We believe it is likely that the tribes would waive sovereign immunity in order to obtain primacy, although such waiver should be similar to that required for States. 

F. Unsuitability 

Section 522 of the Act sets forth two approaches for the designation of areas as unsuitable for surface coal mining. 

First, a State is required to establish a planning and designation process for determining the unsuitability of non-Federal and non-Indian lands for all or certain types of surface coal mining operations. This process must include the opportunity for citizen petition for designation. 

Designation must be based on the criteria set forth in the 

Act. 

Second, for Federal lands, the Secretary is required to conduct a review of such lands based on the statutory criteria, to determine which Federal lands are unsuitable for all or certain types of surface coal mining operations. Upon such a determination (which may also be initiated by citizen petition), the Secretary is authorized to withdraw affected areas from leasing or mineral entries to limit surface coal mining operations.

In its legislative report, the Department recommended Indian lands be treated like the first category of non-Federal and non-Indian lands. They should be subject to a tribal planning and designation process (including a petition process) and designated unsuitable (if appropriate). The -49-

Department recommended that if mineral interests are owned by or held in trust for an Indian tribe but Congress deletes them from the definition of "Indian lands" for tribal jurisdictional purposes, the tribe or tribal regulatory authority should be able to review and, if appropriate, withdraw such lands from coal leasing.

G. Applicability of Other Federal Laws to Indian Lands 

H. 

If Indian tribes assume regulation of Indian lands, one area of interest is what will happen to other Federal responsibilities. When a State program is approved, OSMRE's approval of the program is subject to consideration of compliance with the Endangered Species Act, the National Historic Preservation Act, and similar laws. The States and 

Indian tribes are not responsible for many of the study requirements of these laws. Generally, OSMRE satisfies its obligations with regard to these laws by ensuring that the State will accord the protected value the required consideration. OSMRE could take a similar approach with regard to tribal primacy.

In delegating regulatory authority to an Indian tribe, OSMRE would have to ensure that the tribal program contains provisions which ensure the protection of endangered or otherwise protected species, such as bald and golden eagles, as well as consideration of historic properties. 

Conflict of Interest 

In addition to having coal lands on their reservations, each of the three coal-producing tribes owns significant coal reserves. Thus, in addition to each tribe's potential role as a regulatory authority, it also stands to profit from successful coal development. The percentages of tribal income generated from coal are as follows: Navajo, 19 percent; Hopi, 57 percent; and Crow, 61 percent. Further, each tribal member owns a share of tribal resources. Section 517 (g) of SMCRA prohibits State employees from having direct or indirect financial interests in coal mines. 

Presumably, this would also apply to employees of Indian regulatory authorities or the authorities themselves. Thus, section 517 (g) would appear to bar any Indian regulatory authority. This appears to be a significant issue. However, Congress may develop some alternative financial interest provision in section 710 legislation if it is enacted. -50-

IV. Summary and Conclusions A. Crow Tribe 

The Crow Tribe presently does not have the capability to assume full or partial primacy for the regulation of surface coal mining and reclamation activities at the Absaloka mine, the only existing mine located on Crow lands. The Crow 

Office of Reclamation, the tribal-designated regulatory authority, has participated in various aspects of the regulatory process since 1982, but has not developed the necessary level of administrative support or technical expertise to independently review and process permit application packages, permit revisions, or permit renewals, and prepare the required technical analyses and environmental compliance documents, or to conduct mid-term permit reviews. 

Also, the tribe has not demonstrated the abilities necessary to implement and manage an effective inspection and enforcement program for the Absaloka mine. 

While the Crow Tribe has prepared a draft reclamation code generally analogous to SMCRA, no implementing regulations have been or are being developed. In addition, the Crow 

Reclamation Code contains a number of provisions or omissions which are potentially less stringent than SMCRA. However, in the absence of any section 710 legislation to establish standards which the tribe must meet to achieve primacy, there are no content requirements for such documents and thus no tribal statutes or regulations can be finalized or implemented. 

B. Hopi Tribe

The Hopi Tribe has demonstrated limited capabilities to administer the Title V program for the Black Mesa-Kayenta mine, the only existing mine located on lands in which the tribe has an interest. However, the tribe is not yet ready to assume full or partial primacy for regulation of surface coal mining and reclamation activities at the Black

Mesa-Kayenta mine. The Division of Mining and Reclamation 

Enforcement, the tribe's designated regulatory authority, has been participating in the regulatory process for approximately 5 years and, during that time, has developed an adequate administrative support structure. However, the staff currently consists only of the Program Director, an Administrative Assistant, and a Secretary, and as such, lacks the technical expertise and range of disciplines necessary to effectively implement and manage a Title V program. At a 

-51-

minimum, the Division of Mining and Reclamation Enforcement would require additional technical personnel, with the appropriate combination of qualifications and experience, to effectively analyze the adequacy of mining and reclamation plan proposals and deal with enforcement issues.

During the past several years, the tribe seemed to have acquired the basic knowledge necessary to carry out inspection activities at the Black Mesa-Kayenta mine. was accomplished primarily through the participation of the Reclamation Specialist employed by the Division of Mining and Reclamation Enforcement, the only individual on the staff involved in the inspection process on a monthly basis and thus perhaps the most knowledgeable on the subject. 

Recently, however, this individual left the employment of the tribe. The only technical employee presently available to participate in inspection activities is the Director of the 

Division of Mining and Reclamation Enforcement. However, this individual has not been involved in the inspection process on a regular basis and it may be difficult for the tribe to continue to effectively participate in the immediate future. In addition, the lack of staff continuity renders the previous training in inspection and enforcement received by Division of Mining and Reclamation Enforcement personnel of little benefit.

With initial close supervision, the tribe may eventually be able to perform the inspection and enforcement duties incumbent upon a regulatory authority. However, further experience would be needed before the tribe could gain a thorough working knowledge of applicable regulations, and the ability to enforce tribal regulations, once adopted, in a consistent and effective manner. 

While the Hopi Tribe has prepared a draft reclamation ordinance generally analogous to SMCRA, no implementing regulations have been or are being developed. In addition, the Hopi ordinance contains a number of provisions or omissions which are potentially less stringent than SMCRA.

However, in the absence of any section 710 legislation to establish standards which the tribe must meet to achieve primacy, there are no content requirements for such documents and thus no tribal statutes or regulations can be finalized or implemented.

The role of the Hopi Tribe in the regulatory process must be more precisely defined before attempting to establish specific goals under future cooperative agreements, particularly since the Black Mesa-Kayenta mine does not now involve any Hopi surface, nor will it in the foreseeable future. -52-

C. Navajo Tribe 

The Navajo Tribe has exhibited the potential to assume regulatory primacy for surface coal mining and reclamation operations on its lands. The Department of Surface Mining, the tribal-designated regulatory authority, has demonstrated the ability to function as a regulatory agency. The staff has participated in both the permitting and inspection and enforcement aspects of the regulatory process for almost 5 years, thus obtaining field experience and on-the-job training in Title V regulatory activities, supplemented by OSMRE-sponsored courses in various technical disciplines. 

The Navajo Department of Surface Mining has demonstrated overall stability and the ability to attract and retain employees for extended periods of time as a result of professional program management and the large number of existing and proposed mines located on Navajo lands. In addition, the Department of Surface 

Mining technical staff is supported by the network of tribal agencies comprising the Navajo Division of Resources, and by other divisions and departments of the tribal government, such as 

Water Resources and Department of Justice. 

The tribe has acquired a general knowledge of the inspection and enforcement process, but needs to develop a more precise knowledge of the regulations and their specific applicability to inspection and enforcement problems. The Department of Surface 

Mining staff also requires further experience in preparing inspection reports, as the reports submitted by the tribe do not always correlate to specific regulatory requirements or contain the necessary level of accuracy or detail. 

Up to this point, the Navajo Department of Surface Mining has not actually prepared the technical analyses, NEPA compliance documents (environmental assessments and environmental impact statements), or other analytical documents required for permitting actions; the tribe has functioned only in a review capacity, evaluating and commenting on the adequacy and content of permit application packages, permit revisions, and OSMRE-prepared documents. This aspect of the regulatory program could be explored through future cooperative agreements in which the tribe would develop the necessary documentation, with OSMRE in an oversight position. While the Navajo Tribe has prepared a draft reclamation code generally analogous to SMCRA, no implementing regulations have been or are being developed. In addition, the Navajo Reclamation 

Code contains a number of provisions or omissions which are potentially less stringent than SMCRA. However, in the absence -53-

NARRATIVE COMPARISON OF TRIBAL CAPABILITY WITH REQUIREMENTS OF 30 CFR 731.14 and 732.15 APPENDIX C

Regulatory Capability of Indian Tribes 

Since no legislation authorizing Indian primacy has been enacted, the Secretary of the Interior has promulgated no regulations prescribing requirements for permanent regulatory program submissions from Indian tribes. The Federal regulations at 30 CFR 

731.14 and 732.15, which set forth the content and approval requirements for program submissions, apply to States alone. 

However, the three coal-producing tribes, the Crow, the Hopi and the Navajo, have developed codes or ordinances corresponding to the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 and have entered into intergovernmental agreements (funding cooperative agreements) to work with and assist OSMRE in the regulation of surface coal mining and reclamation operations on their lands. In completing this study of the regulatory capability of the three tribes, OSMRE evaluated their proposed codes or ordinances to determine whether they were in accordance with the requirements of SMCRA, using the standards for non-Federal and non-Indian lands. 

The following analyses examine the current capability of the Crow, Hopi and Navajo Tribes, using the criteria of 30 CFR 731.14 and 

30 CFR 732.15. Permanent regulatory program content requirements are set forth at 30 CFR 731.14, whereas 30 CFR 732.15 establishes the criteria for program approval. In general, the materials required to document the specific findings required by 30 CFR 732.15 are included in the submission requirements of 30 CFR 731.14; hence the analyses address only those findings of 30 CFR 732.15 which lack a counterpart in 30 CFR 731.14. - 1 -

Westmoreland Resources, Inc. ("Westmoreland") owns and operates the Absaloka Mine, which is located in the Crow ceded area of Big Horn County, Montana. The Absaloka Mine is located outside the exterior boundary of the Crow Indian reservation and the majority of the coal reserve is owned in trust by the United States of America for the Crow tribe.

We have reviewed the preliminary draft "Study On Tribal Capability 

To Assume Regulatory Primacy", and offer the following comments: 

1. 

Tribal Authority The study correctly recognizes (Section I.B.1) that Indian tribes may not assume primacy and regulation of coal mining under SMCRA until and unless authorizing legislation is enacted by Congress under Section 

710, notwithstanding Tribal-Federal Intergovernmental Agreements which have sought to include Indian tribes in the regulatory process. The importance of the prerequisite legislation cannot be over emphasized. 2. 

Jurisdictional status of off-reservation lands On page 42, the study states that the definition of Indian lands in SMCRA "serves to establish OSMRE's regulatory jurisdiction Also on page 42, the study states that "it may be relatively clear what is OSMRE's jurisdiction over Indian lands. 

On page 3, the study states that, pending passage of legislation, OSMRE "will continue to exercise regulatory jurisdiction over Indian lands 11 "1 Westmoreland does not agree that the definition of Indian lands in SMCRA confers jurisdiction to OSM over such lands. Instead, the only jurisdiction granted to OSM over Indian lands is as provided in and limited by the provisions of Section 710. With respect to off-reservation lands, Congress made it clear in subsection 710(h) that, pending completion of the Secretary's study of the jurisdictional status of offreservation lands and the enactment of legislation, "nothing in (SMCRA) shall change the existing jurisdictional status of Indian lands."

Mr. Arthur W. Abbs 10/14/87 Page 2 Both Westmoreland and the State of Montana have consistently maintained that the Crow ceded area is not "Indian lands" as defined in SMCRA, and further, that nothing in SMCRA was intended by Congress to give OSM exclusive regulatory authority over the ceded area. 

Westmoreland and the State of Montana sued the Secretary in 1984 to challenge OSM's assertion of exclusive regulatory jurisdiction over Westmoreland's Absaloka Mine. That litigation was settled, in part, through the adoption of a Memorandum of Understanding between OSM and the Montana Department of State Lands, setting out the procedures for coordinating joint regulation of the Absaloka Mine. 

For the same reasons, Westmoreland believes that it is not fully accurate to describe the Absaloka Mine as being located on "Crow tribal lands," as is done on pages 12 and 51. While the description on page 12 of the history of the Crow ceded area is accurate as far as it goes, Westmoreland believes that off-hand references to that area as "tribal lands," could, unless qualified, create a misunderstanding of the complicated jurisdictional questions attendant to these lands. 

3. Associated regulatory and legal issues

Westmoreland believes that this section of the study correctly identifies at least some of the issues that must be addressed by any Indian lands legislation enacted under Section 710. We are uncertain how to comment on this section of the study, however, because the treatment of these subjects is neither exhaustive, nor does it offer any conclusions or recommendations. Westmoreland recommends that thorough examination of these issues is necessary before any legislation could be considered. In particular, we note that there is no discussion of de novo review of tribal court decisions by the federal courts. 

Westmoreland believes that any legislation conferring jurisdiction over mining activities to Indian tribes must ensure that non-Indians affected by decisions of a tribal court will have a full opportunity for review of such decisions.

The Hopi Tribe informed OSMRE of potential scheduling conflicts early in the process.

The Hopi governmental system involves a lengthly process of review for matters considered to be policy policy in nature. With limited staff resources, the Hopi DMRE did manage to keep pace with the permitting/EIS schedule. The schedule does change periodically to accomodate requests for time extensions. Because of the concurrent scheduling, each time extension directly affects the completion of tasks either in the permitting or EIS agenda.
