UNIVERSITY LIBRARY To the Student and the Teacher 

Three definite objectives were kept in mind in preparing this workbook: (1) To help the student test his knowledge of the text; (2) to provide the student with opportunities for applying his knowledge of facts and principles as he learns them; and (3) to facilitate the task of grading the completed exercises. 

In the workbook there is a set of exercises for each chapter in the text. In each set, or chapter, of exercises there are four sections. 

These sections are as follows: 

1. Study Questions. The purpose of these questions is to help the student find out how well he knows what is in the particular chapter in the text. Before undertaking this, or any other part of the exercises in the workbook, the student should read and study carefully the chapter in the text. 

2. Meanings of Expressions. The purpose of this section of the exercises is to help the student to become more familiar with the meanings of certain expressions as the expressions are used in the chapter. It is highly important that the student understand the exact shade of meaning that is intended to attach to each of the expressions. 

3. Selective Summary. The purpose here is to enable the student to see whether or not he has grasped the essentials of the chapter in the text. If the student has merely memorized definitions, he will not be able to read the selective summary correctly. 

4. Applications of the Chapter. The purpose of the applications of the chapter is to give the student an opportunity to apply the facts and principles that he has gained from a study of the chapter in the text. The exercises in this section vary in number.

If the teacher so desires, the students may be asked to exchange papers and to check the answers as the teacher calls out the correct answers. Or the teacher may attend to all the work of checking and grading the papers.

DIRECTIONS: Chapter I in the textbook is designed to give you a general understanding and an appreciation of the nature of economics. A study of the chapter indicates that we are largely concerned with man's efforts to make a living. By making a living we mean producing goods and services necessary to life. Practically every adult considers himself a wage earner or one who contributes to production. In return for the part one plays in production in modern society, one receives an income. The value of income is usually stated in terms of dollars and cents. 

We know, however, that from year to year the cost of living varies. We speak of the cost of living "going up" when, in general, prices are higher than they have been. So our income at any given time is not always worth the same as it was at some former time. For example, the value of a dollar of the workingman's income from 1914 to 1936* is shown by the following table. AN EARLY MORNING IN THE Life of MR. REYNOLDS 

Mr. Reynolds is a fairly prosperous merchant in a fair-sized American city. He is not much given to analyzing the kinds of goods and services that he consumes. If he gave the matter any thought, he would be surprised to find that services play so prominent a part in his daily life. Let us observe some of the events of an early morning in his rather routine life and note some of the services he often uses. 

Let us begin while he is still asleep. While he slumbers, his safety is relatively assured by reason of the fact that there is a fire station five blocks down the street. Although the windows of his house are of glass, he is little disturbed, because an armed policeman would respond to his call on the telephone within a very few minutes. Moreover, he has no haunting fear that an invading force-on land or in the air-may suddenly arouse him, for a standing army, a large number of warships, and thousands of airplanes make invasion a remote possibility.

At breakfast, he eats somewhat hurriedly, but he takes time to remark to Bessie, the cook, that the coffee is very good. On his way to his store he drives carefully as he passes the graded-school, which his two children attend, and speaks to the friendly officer who is regulating traffic as children cross the street. Suddenly remembering his wife's admonition not to forget to mail the letter she had given him, he drives out of his way two blocks to mail the letter at the post office. As he enters the building, he encounters Dr. Fisher, his dentist, and asks if he could give him an examination at three o'clock the next afternoon. Dr. Fisher assures him that he will have his secretary call him later and let him know if it is possible to make an appointment for that hour. 

Mr. Reynolds then drives his car to the garage and parks it. He buys a newspaper from the newsboy at the corner, who is urging every one to read about the "big robbery." As he passes the barber shop, he sees that no customers are there as yet and, since he is a few minutes earlier than usual, he decides to get a haircut and a shoe-shine.

Within a few minutes he was entering his store. And here we shall have to take our leave of Mr. Reynolds, although it would be interesting to notice the number of services that he will use during the remainder of the day.

When a company has a simple and finite common stock capitalization, there is no leverage. But when a company has bonds or preferred stock outstanding, there is that much senior capital working for the stockholders. That senior capital provides leverage. True, interest must be paid on the bonds, dividends on the preferred stock, but everything earned over and above those prior charges inures to the common stockholders. And in good times, when earnings are expanding, senior capital serves as a multiplier of earnings on the common. 

Here is an example in leverage: Suppose a company is capitalized at $300,000-$100,000 in 5 per cent bonds; $100,000 in 6 per cent preferred stock; $100,000 in common stock. For every $1 of common stock there are $2 of senior securities; and suppose the company earns 7 per cent on its total capitalization. That would be $21,000 a year. But $5,000 is required for bond interest; $6,000 is required for preferred stock dividends. Total senior requirements are $11,000. That leaves $10,000 in earnings for the common. Thus, instead of earning only 7 per cent, the common stock earnings are 10 per cent.

ORDINANCES OF THE WHITE-TAWYER'S GUILD 

In honor of God, of Our Lady, and of all Saints, and for the nurture of tranquility and peace among the good folks the megucers, called white-tawyers, the folks of the same trade have, by assent of Richard Lacer, mayor, and of the alderman, ordained the points underwritten. 

In the first place, they have ordained that they will find a wax candle, to burn before Our Lady in the church of Allhallows, near London wall.

Also that each person of the said trade shall put in the box such sums as he shall think fit, in aid of maintaining the said candle.

Also, if by chance anyone of the said trade shall fall into poverty, whether through old age or because he cannot labor or work, and have nothing with which to keep himself, he shall have every week from the said box 7d for his support, if he be a man of good repute. And after his decease, if he have a wife, a woman of good repute, she shall have weekly for her support 7d from the said box, so long as she shall behave herself well and keep single. 

And that no stranger shall work in the said trade or keep house for the same in the city, if he be not an apprentice, or a man admitted to the franchise of the said city. 

And that no one shall take the serving man of another to work with him, during his term, unless it be with the permission of his master.

And if anyone of the said trade shall have work in his house that he cannot complete, or if for want of assistance such work shall be in danger of being lost, those of the said trade shall aid him so that the said work be not lost.

And if anyone of the said trade shall depart this life, and have not wherewithal to be buried, he shall be buried at the expense of their common box. And when anyone of the said trade shall die, all those of the said trade shall go to the vigil and make offering on the morrow. 

And if any serving-man [apprentice] shall conduct himself in any other manner than properly toward his master, and act rebelliously toward him, no one of the said trade shall set him to work, until he shall have made amends before the mayor and the alderman and before them such misprision shall be redressed. 

Also, that the good folks of the same trade shall once in the year be assembled in a certain place, convenient thereto, there to choose two men of the most loyal and befitting of the said trade to be overseers of work and all other things touching the trade for that year; which persons shall be presented to the mayor and alderman for the time being, and sworn before them diligently to inquire and make search, and loyally to present to the said mayor and alderman such defaults as they shall find touching the said trade without sparing anyone for friendship or for hatred or for any other manner.

Also that skins falsely and deceitfully wrought in their trade which the said overseers shall find in the hands of any person, citizen or foreigner, within the franchise shall be forfeited to the said chamber and the worker thereof amerced in the manner aforesaid.

Also, that no one shall take for working in the said trade more than they were wont heretofore, on the pain of the aforesaid; that is to say for the dyker [package of ten] of Scotch stags, half a mark; for the hundred of goatskins, 20 s.; the hundred of roe leather, 16 s.; for the hundred skins of young deer, 8 s.. and for the hundred of kid skins, 8 s.
