HATTERS' FUR Fourth Report to the President Under Paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401 of Developments with Regard to Hatters' Fur Since Modifica tion of the Trade-Agreement Concession on February 9, 1952 

United States Tariff Commission, 

Washington, February 4, 1957

To the President: 

This is a report made pursuant to paragraph 1 of Executive Order 

10401 of October 14, 1952 (17 F. R. 9125), 1/ of developments with regard to hatters' fur. This is the fourth report under paragraph 1 of the order with respect to hatters' fur since the modification, effective February 9, 1952, of the concession that was granted on imports of that commodity in the General Agreement on Tariffs and 

Trade (Proclamation 2960; 17 F. R. 188). The third report was submitted to you on February 6, 1956.

The Tariff Act of 1930 (par. 1520) originally specified a rate of duty of 35 percent ad valorem on imports of hatters' fur. This rate was reduced to 27 percent ad valorem (effective May 1, 1935) pursuant to a concession granted in the trade agreement with the Belgo-Luxembourg Economic Union, and was further reduced to 15 percent ad valorem (effective January 1, 1948) pursuant to a concession granted in the General 

Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. 

Prescribing procedures for periodic review of escape-clause modifications of trade-agreement concessions.

Hatters' fur was the subject of an "escape clause" investigation by the Tariff Commission in 1951. The report on this investigation was made pursuant to section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 and contained data regarding conditions of the trade through the first 6 months of 1951. In its investigation the Commission found that the domestic hatters' fur industry was being seriously injured by increased imports of low-grade hatters' fur. The Commission also found that the least restrictive and yet effective remedy for the injury was a change in the form of duty that would take into account the differences in grades and the changes in prices of hatters' fur. 

In accordance with the recommendation made by the Commission in the investigation, the trade-agreement concession with respect to hatters' fur was modified by Presidential proclamation, and the present rate of duty of 473 cents per pound, but not less than 15 percent nor more than 35 percent ad valorem, was made effective on February 9, 1952. Under the modified duty the 35 percent ad valorem rate specified in the Tariff Act of 1930 applies to very low-grade and low-priced hatters' fur (that valued at $1.36 or less per pound); the trade agreement concession rate of 15 percent ad valorem applies to higher priced hatters' fur (that valued at $3.16-2/3 or more per pound); and the specific rate of 47 cents per pound applies to hatters' fur at the intermediate prices (between $1.36 per pound and $3.16-2/3 per pound).

Hatters' fur is the soft underfur obtained from certain animal skins and used principally as a raw material in the production of fur felt hats. 

It is cut chiefly from the skins of rabbits and hares, and to a small extent from skins (or pieces of skins) of beavers and other animals. The 

United States industry producing hatters' fur sells almost all of its output of cut fur to the domestic fur felt hat industry as United States exports of hatters' fur are negligible, and sales of cut fur to domestic consumers other than hat manufacturers--such as the domestic textile industry--are small.

Domestic hat manufacturers have ordinarily bought from independent cutters most of their supplies of hatters fur. Those hat manufacturers who cut fur for their own use have probably accounted for less than 10 percent of total domestic consumption of hatters' fur in recent years.

Throughout the 1930's and until 1948, United States imports of hatters' fur were small, and in terms of quantity supplied less than 1 percent of domestic consumption. Imports during this period often consisted of special qualities or kinds not regularly produced in the United States or not immediately available from domestic sources. Imports increased in 1948 and continued upward in 1949 and 1950. In 1951 imports were slightly less than in 1950, but even so they were substantially larger than in any year before 1948.

From 1948 through 1952 imports consisted in large part of one lowpriced grade of fur obtained from European rabbit skins and commercially known as Petit Bon. This fur is closely comparable and competitive with

the domestic grade Grey Entire, also cut from European rabbit skins; to a less extent it is competitive with the domestic grade BCB Unpulled, which is cut from Australian rabbit skins. 

Beginning in 1953 and continuing through 1956, a larger proportion of imports consisted of white and mottled fur and a smaller proportion of Petit Bon (a grey fur). White and light-colored furs sell at substantially higher prices than Petit Bon or darker colored furs. Moreover, since 

1952 the prices of rabbit skins and the prices of all grades of hatters' fur have increased materially. The very great increase in the foreign unit value of imports in recent years, therefore, has been caused by the shift to higher priced grades of hatters' fur, as well as by the overall increase in prices of all hatters' fur. 

Imports declined in 1952 after the trade-agreement concession was modified (effective February 9, 1952); they increased sharply in the early months of 1953 to about the same level as in the corresponding periods in 1950 and 1951, but declined again in late 1953 and have remained at a low level since. Imports amounted to 282,000 pounds in 1950, to 247,000 pounds in 1951, to 133,000 pounds in 1952, to 248,000 pounds in 1953, to 140,000 pounds in 1954, to 159,000 pounds in 1955, and to 109,000 pounds in the first 

10 months of 1956 (see table 1). Imports supplied approximately 5 percent of domestic consumption in 1950, 4 percent in 1951, 2 percent in 1952, 4 percent in 1953, and 4 percent in 1954, 1955, and 1956 (see table 2). 

United States consumption of hatters' fur was slightly lower in 1952 than in the 2 previous years, owing to the decline in the production of

fur felt hats. As a result of the increase in the consumption of men's fur felt hats in 1953, the consumption of hatters' fur increased also. 

In 1954 and since, however, consumption of hatters' fur has been much lower than in earlier years--amounting to approximately 3.4 million pounds to 3.8 million pounds annually compared with more than 5 million pounds in 1953 and earlier (see table 1). 1/ Trends in the volume of domestic production of hatters' fur have closely followed the trends in domestic consumption. That is, in 1952 production dropped somewhat, it increased slightly in 1953, but was materially lower in 1954, 1955, and 1956. ?

Based on data for reporting firms which account for roughly two-thirds of the total domestic production, the financial position of the companies producing hatters' fur showed some small improvement in 1955 but deteriorated again in 1956. In 1955 these concerns made a profit of $210,000, but in the first 6 months of 1956 the profit was only $20,000. The profit in the corresponding period of 1955 was $140,000. 

In 1951 the group experienced a loss of more than $250,000; in 1952 they made a profit of $32,000, but in 1953 they had a loss of about $80,000, and in 1954 a loss of about $75,000. 

The combined sales of the reporting firms have been about 8 million dollars annually except in 1952, when they amounted to 4.5 million dollars, and in 1954, when they amounted to 6.7 million dollars. 

As pointed out in the original report to the President on the escape-clause investigation, changes in the price of hatters' fur closely follow changes in the price of the rabbit skins from which the fur is cut. Prices of tame-rabbit skins and of hatters' fur, which were low in 1952 and 1953, declined even further in 1954 but increased sharply in 1955 and remained high throughout 1956. The price of one important grade of hatters' fur (Grey Entire) was quoted at $2.50 a pound throughout most of 1952 and from $2.60 to $2.75 a pound in 1953, but had declined to $2.35 a pound by late 1954. The price of this same grade, however, had increased to $4.00 per pound by the end of 1955, to $4.50 a pound in

early 1956, and was $3.75 a pound in December 1956 (see table 3). Price quotations for the domestic grade BCB Unpulled and the imported grade Petit Bon in the last few years have followed the same general trend as those for Grey Entire--a substantial decline in 1952 with a partial recovery in 1953, and a further decline in 1954, followed by a very sharp increase in 1955 and continuing high prices in 1956.

United States fur cutters are almost wholly dependent on imports for their supply of rabbit skins, and for years had relied on Australia and New Zealand as important sources of supply. With the decline in the quantities available in those two countries, domestic fur cutters in recent years have used proportionately larger supplies of European skins. In 1955, however, Italy placed restrictions on the exports of rabbit skins that have had the effect of reducing imports of such skins by United States producers of hatters' fur. Effective January 

1, 1957, France also placed restrictions on exports of rabbit skins by fixing export quotas at 50 percent of the monthly average in the period 

January 1, 1954, to November 30, 1955. 1/ These export restrictions together with the reduced world supply of rabbit skins because of the spread of myxomatosis, greatly limit the ability of domestic cutters It is understood by the Commission that officials in the United States Embassies in Rome and Paris are currently discussing these restrictive measures with officials of Italy and France, respectively.

to obtain sufficient supplies of skins to compete effectively with imports of cut fur.

Table in the appendix shows that since the trade-agreement concession was modified, effective February 9, 1952, almost all of the imports of hatters' fur have been dutiable either at the specific rate of 47 cents per pound or at the minimum ad valorem rate of 15 percent ad valorem, and that there have been only small imports dutiable at the maximum rate of 35 percent ad valorem. These data indicate that the modification of the duty substantially reduced imports of the very lowpriced fur, but has had almost no effect on the imports of the higher priced fur. At most recent prices (December 1956) imports of many grades of fur would be dutiable at the minimum rate established under the escape-clause procedure, which is the same as the concession rate granted in the General Agreement. 

From February 9, 1952, through October 31, 1956, 54.1 percent of the total imports were dutiable at 47 cents per pound, 45.1 percent at the minimum rate of 15 percent ad valorem, and only 0.8 percent at the maximum rate of 35 percent ad valorem. From February 9, 1952, through

October 31, 1956, the average ad valorem equivalent of the duty on all imports of hatters' fur was 17.4 percent.
