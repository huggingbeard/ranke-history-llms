The Reign Of The Nightriders CHAPTER ONE A Glimpse of Kentucky 

Kentucky is not an oyster although one looking at the map, remarking its topography, would naturally think so. Citizens who rest against her mountains and slumber in her valleys are wont to put a smirch upon her reputation but Nature sweeps by and transforms the darkness to crystal light, to blooming flowers, to greening trees.

It has been truthfully said that Kentuckians have the peculiar characteristic of singing their conversation with long drawn-out sounds, and a reputation of possessing a trigger finger and an accurate eye for measuring the distance of a foe. Kentucky also has the reputation for beautiful women, brilliant Statesmen, silver-tongued orators and fast horses. 

Kentucky's citizenry is compiled from all points of the compass, having a little of every country flowing in their bloodfrom taint to royalty, from obscurity to renown, from abject poverty and ignorance to intelligence and affluence. Like her sister states she possesses a large share of everything—virtue, vice, health, happiness. There are family homes and altars, according to the Master's ordinance, and then there are the other kind-the intolerance of social standards. It is the point from which the plow boy goes forth to wrestle recognition from Big Business; it is the point from which the mountain goes to the profession, after maturity has shown the world beyond the shadow of the hills.

Kentucky's wealth comes from fertile lands, where the blue grass creeps over hill and valley into every crevice, where pure bred stock swirl the flies under shady trees and rest in security with their blooded forebears; where the range of

IO 

knobs shed their holdings beneath the soil that man may enrich his life with the spoils from shale and limestone. The knobs that hem Kentucky to the north and the mountains that bound her on the east give to her at eventide a topaz twilight that is seen never again in all the glories of the glorious states of the Union-a topaz twilight against a tinted sky, where gathers the gold and azure, the platinum and the purple to crown her people with sun gleamlets, to sing them to sleep with the gorgeous song of the mocking bird, which swings and sings from a lofty limb its message of hope and cheer ere its song is hushed in the dark of the night, when day is done. 

In the archives of Kentucky are enshrined the names of famous men, whose heroic deeds are oft told in song and story to eager ears around the family fireside, or in public halls where the glory of race and pride of ancestors is reflected in the hearts of men. Kentucky has her faults, perhaps no greater than other states, but, then, she has her compensations, too. Up in the mountains there live at this very day men who rival the great Joshua who commanded the sun to stand still, the only difference is that the Joshua of the hills makes the still moonshine.

Overlooking and surrounding the Capitol lives an element that holds to the standard of the unwritten law regardless of their proximity to the shadow of the dome of law. They go their way of unrighteousness, knowing naught of law and abiding citizenship. It is true, that in their effort to lift the younger generation from the scourge of illiteracy, philanthropists clamor for schools so that these children of superstition and ignorance may take their rightful place among the citizenship of the world.

Kentucky has a system of education about the equal of her surrounding sister states. There are universities, colleges and public schools and her citizenry have the same opportunity as those of other states of the Southland. Presidents have risen from her ranks, historians, biographers, scientists, men of letters, poets, writers, teachers of theology and famous statesmen have blazoned the name of Kentucky upon the Hall of 

Fame. 

Kentucky has a history. It does not all lie upon the shelves of libraries, but in humble cabins-wherever a yearning heart dwells. Its pages are full of glory, glory that was attained

by blood and tears and broken hearts, for Kentucky means "dark and bloody ground," and her spirit has never known defeat, when the hearts of her people fought for what they conceived to be right.

Kentucky has beautiful women, beautiful in their courage and forbearance, beautiful in their adherence to their puritanic religious convictions, beautiful in their understanding of virtue, of motherhood, of homemaking, beautiful of face and form and character and from this beauty great men have come forth to better the world and blazon the trail to earthly glory.

Kentucky has her troubles, her blemishes, her spots that are charred and scarred, burnt in the crucible of passion, corroded with the bitterness of soul, broken against the issues that take and break a people against the walls of tribulation; issues that tear asunder the soul tenets like a hungry beast tears at the vitals of his victim; issues that drive manhood to tragedy of beastial instincts and blasts the hope of civilization. 

The trouble of 1906 and 1907 that arose in the attempt to organize the Dark Tobacco Protective Association in the

States of Kentucky, Tennessee and Virginia, was an issue that bruised and burned and pillaged ere it went into oblivion and the silence of death as all issues must do, leaving a trail of broken homes and moss-grown graves, of empty places and deserted farms.

The story of the Nightriders, although of so recent date as history goes, is fast dying out in the memory of this generation, as the leaders of both factions have practically all died with lips sealed, leaving the real history of this dark and bloody period unwritten save as it has been handed down from word of mouth like the early folk tales and the true issues are difficult to determine.

There is a certain class of citizens scattered throughout the United States that are not amenable to laws or to leadership. They are never submissive even after a law has been written upon the statute book of our country. For instance, a larger per cent of the population revolted against the prohibition of liquor. It is a revolt that has not been checked in all the years since the Volstead law went into effect. Breaking the prohibition law to a certain element has taught them to break other laws, they having the wrong impression of what liberty means, and feeling that they have been denied liberty

they have resorted to license, forgetting that others have to suffer for their transgressions. 

Politics play an important part in the life and liberties of the population and politics have made some black smudges upon the white pages of Kentucky's history. It was a detour from the broad highway of human sympathy and understanding; an error that time must efface. Mistakes lead us to better attempts; to redeeming efforts, to sympathy for a weaker brother. To right a wrong, to outlive a mistake, to correct an error, are all indicative of esthetic living and esthetic life is the aim of civilization. 

What constitutes civilization is a much mooted question. 

One of the foremost writers of this era has proclaimed that to be a connoisseur of fine wines, a driver of fine cars, a person who is able to distinguish fine arts and a man adroit in love, exhibiting skill in the art of artless women, is the pinnacle of civilization. Another dominant character, a minister of the 

Gospel, takes exception to the writer's definition and tells the world that until all nations adhere to the old picture of the Garden of Eden, the crucifixtion of the beloved Christ and accept all theology from the puritanical conception of religion, that civilization will never be in the form of standardization. 

Neither the writer nor the minister were taken seriously although the argument very cleverly shows the extremes to which theory may go, but civilization has more than two sides. 

Stern scientists and serious philosophers have compiled books that now stand upon dusty shelves, telling of the early efforts in the struggle of the human race to become civilized, and though we think that the dawn of civilization is at hand, the controversy still goes on. Is this the era of civilization? 

With a world bowed in grief and pursued by the phantom of

God's destruction of the universe, for the unstinted spilling of blood from every point of the globe? Is this an era of civilization for the United States of America with law-breakers, bootleggers, racketeers and the men who buy, thereby upholding the crime against the constitution of law and order, which is the fundamental law of civilization? Is this an era of civilization when men, living unhonored under our flag, tie a rag over their faces and rob and kill for the blood-stained gold they squander in self indulgences? Is this an era of civilization when man and woman shatter the marriage vows, leaving

CHAPTER TWO Selling, Buying and the Devil 

Colonel Frank Arnold, pompous and proud, stood before the doors of his barn which hung full in every available space with dark tobacco. This entire crop of tobacco had been cultivated, cut, hung and fired exactly as he had dictated for the old Colonel prided himself upon the fine quality of his dark tobacco.

The barn was hung to the topmost point with long, dark, brown leaves that almost touched a man's head from the bottom tier. This was a sample of all the other barns and grades of the full crop grown on the fourteen hundred acres of Kentucky soil owned by Colonel Arnold. There were twenty barns that were filled with this splendid crop and it represented the maintenance of twenty families. 

Colonel Arnold had heretofore been able to care for and support his croppers, to maintain them in comfort, but for the past two seasons the price of tobacco had fallen to such a low level that he had to go in debt to see his people safely through their needs until another crop could be made. He was feeling greatly encouraged at the fine quality of his tobacco. 

On this morning of early fall, the old Colonel had made an engagement to meet a tobacco buyer, as at this season in the dark tobacco district, it was customary for buyers to visit the barns and make bids on the weed. So he walked and waited before his barn, a grand old man, his snow white hair and broad erect shoulders, his immaculate appearance, with the goatee and inevitable quid of tobacco in his jaw, marking him as belonging to a type that is all but a memory. When he had occasion to expectorate, he carefully parted his mustache, turned his head and spat unobtrusively. Colonel Arnold looked after the fast disappearing Wethers as he slashed the unoffending cuckleburr bush with his goldheaded cane. Sombre thoughts filled his mind which grew distressing, maddening.

It was too true that the tobacco buyers had the growers by the throat. Last year it had been a bad market and his men came out in debt and now this year the prices promised to be lower and if Wethers was right then he would soon be bankrupt. 

He had pledged his honor to pay for the keep of his croppers, to pay the grocer, the dry-goods merchant, the doctor and the druggist and to do that he would have to put a mortgage upon his beloved home, "Woodstock," with its hallowed memories, with its sacred hillside where sleeps his illustrious and sainted

dead, with the glorious tradition of never having passed out of the ownership of the Arnolds and never wearing a plaster of mortgages.

These last two years were the first of the tobacco buyer's intolerance. The second year had been like the first and another the landowners would be reduced to poverty, walled in with distress and mortgages. Good farmers, who had heretofore been as honorable as men could be were being denied longer credit and the banks shied at the farmer who brought only a sorrowfully long tale to deposit.

Mortgages and poverty, poverty and mortgages-something had to be done! Something must be done! Hope had gone out of the farm owners' hearts! Hope had gone out of the grubbing croppers' hearts. No wonder, with these lean years and hard labor, their women and children in rags and undernourished—one year of want and denial following another year of desperation and all hopeless and forlorn.

To chronicle the tale of the Reign of the Nightriders and to do the fair thing by the parties involved, it is necessary to start at the beginning. There were a mere handful of men who represented the foreign markets, and it was easy to get them to agree on a certain price to the growers. There was a vast difference in the purchase price of the dollar in foreign countries, and what seemed a good price in Italy and Russia, was not a living wage to the workers according to the standard of living in the United States. Thus when prices were set by foreign buyers it not only bred poverty and mortgages, but want and destitution for those who must depend upon tobacco for maintenance.

The American buyers pledged to the foreign markets tobacco at a certain price agreeable to the purchasing powers. To get this tobacco to fill the contract, the price had to be met and the grubbers and landowners alike were reduced to meet the requirements. The buyers had to have a price for their labor and they priced their labor abnormally high when compared to what they proposed to pay the farmers. One year's earnings of a tobacco buyer was more than all the earnings of a life time of the tenant farmer and the knowledge was galling to his aching back.

It was a condition like this that drove the tobacco growers to revolt and, taking a lesson from the buyers themselves,

they undertook to better their marketing conditions in combining as the buyers had done. Long the farmers grumbled and murmured against the oppression, complaining against the robber barons and then when some brave spirit started a campaign for their betterment, it was found that each farmer wanted a platform of his own. This was natural for heretofore the farmer had been monarch of his own small world, granting favors and dictating terms, mastering and controlling his lands and his people. It was not a small condescension to submit to a dictator.

Colonel Frank Arnold was of the old school of Southern cultivation, a more polished gentleman could not be found, an aristocrat in whose veins flowed the blood of royalty, and with these attributes, he was a man whose word was as good as a United States bank note. He was thoroughly honest and upright from the crown of his white hair to the sole of his aged feet.

CHAPTER THREE War of the Night 

Out of the gray of sullen clouds came the better class of farmers to the aid of the hard working individuals who tilled and toiled and starved that mankind might have the indulgence of fragrant smoke. Not one in the thousands of consumers of tobacco in the great cities and towns of American or the foreign countries ever gave a thought to the sweat and blood and privation of the tobacco growers. Of their troubles they never knew as they smoked in hotels, in cafes, in stations or by their own firesides.

The great cry for relief was answered by the forming of the Association. The better class of farmers gave unstintingly, and millions of dollars were contributed by them to better conditions in the Dark Tobacco districts, which took in parts of three states, Kentucky, Tennessee, and Virginia. 

The plan upon which the Association was founded, was that every tobacco grower, white or black, rich or poor, should become a member. They were to establish warehouses in the principal tobacco markets of these states, and those who were not able to await the sale of their crop, would, upon delivery, be advanced a certain percent of the value of their tobacco. 

The Association would hold all tobacco off the market until their demand for a higher price was granted. They held it for a living wage, so that their long hours of toil, their privation and poverty might be vindicated. The Association was hailed as the savior of the people, of the soil, of their homes, of their farms, of their happiness.

The men who drew up the charter for the Association were those who were interested in the welfare of their more unfortunate brothers-the small farmers and the tenants-and their plan met with unanimous acclamation. Committees were formed to explain the marketing system which the Association would inaugurate, and to bring the growers into line, pledged to carry out the plan offered by the thinking men of the dark

tobacco belt as their only salvation. They selected one of the foremost and wealthiest men of Tennessee as President of the Dark Tobacco Protective Association and incorporated in the states of Kentucky, Tennessee and Virginia, these being the three states of the Union in which dark tobacco was largely grown. CHAPTER FOUR 

The Deacon and His Gloves

The middle of April found the farmers of the black patch facing two weeks of rain, wet ground, and no general planting. 

Fall plowing was too wet to disc and harrow, but on the hillsides, where the spring rains had not tarried, the laborers were frantically at work. The sun was beaming hot on bending backs in the fields, perspiration was also streaming down the bodies of the human machines. Women and small girls carried large baskets of plants, along rows that had been plowed and checked four feet apart and dropped the plants in the corner or hills. 

Men and boys punched holes in the ground with round pegs and put the plants in, being careful not to bruise the tender growth. Down at the plant bed, elderly men, who were unable to stand the hard labor of setting plants, crouched on low stools and drew out the plants, filling the baskets for the women and girls. Although the ground was still damp and cold, the mothers who toiled in the fields, had their young offsprings wrapped in coarse clothes and lying in the field on improvised beds of hay or straw. 

The laborers worked feverishly, for they knew that the plants must go into the ground in April, else they would die and have to be replanted, thus entailing a double amount of labor. 

They felt that they were lucky, indeed, when the beds yielded enough plants and the weather was propitious enough for them to prepare the ground. To get an early start meant an early cutting, when the weather would be dry and the dews heavy enough to ripen the tobacco; then the firing could be done under better conditions, and the danger of "black fire "-a death blow to the weed, if the late summer rains caught the nearly matured plants in the field, could be avoided. Thus the story of tobacco is a long struggle to the growers, from the burning of the plant beds to the firing and curing in the barns. 

As the plants begin to grow, they must be hoed, weeded and

plowed; then they must be wormed and suckered and topped; then plowed and harrowed and leveled. First the earth must be taken away from the plant and piled in the middle of the rows; then this earth must at a later stage of development be thrown back on the plants and leveled once more. 

The grass must be hand-picked away from the growing plant as the season advances all this cultivation, which is not done by machinery, furnishes work for every member of the family from the five year old child to the white haired grandfather, during the season of planting and cultivation. When the tobacco is ripe it is cut and put on sticks; then it is loaded on wagons and taken to the barns where it is hung on tiers to be smoked and fired until it takes on a rich brown hue, which gives the name of "Black Patch to the districts where the tobacco is cured in this way.

Firing and curing comprise the third chapter in the story of tobacco raising. After the barn is hung full of plants, fires are put at intervals over the dirt floor and the process of curing and smoking begins. The fires must be kept going night and day for a period of many weeks. This is a long and arduous task and a great hardship upon the small farmer where there are not other men in the family to relieve him of his vigil. The man who is firing must be constantly on the watch to keep the drying weed from catching fire. Often a stray spark will ignite a leaf on the lowest tier in the barn, and then the man's work through the long, hot season avails him nothing. 

When tobacco is cured in the barn, the grower must wait for the proper season-a damp atmosphere, not too damp-to take the tobacco from the tiers and sticks and lay it upon clean straw or hay where it is sorted as it is stripped from the stalk. 

There are three grades of tobacco-leaf, seconds and lugs. 

These grades are packed separately and placed in tiers upon planks against the side of the barn. When the buyer has purchased the tobacco, and after it has been properly prepared for market, it is loaded on wagons and drawn by mule teams to the nearest market. Sometimes the grower is so far from market and the roads are in such a condition that he has to travel for days to reach the warehouse where he is to deliver his tobacco and then he has to take his turn in the long line of waiting teamsters for the chance of unloading.

In stripping tobacco and grading, it is tied into hands with

the tie leaf taken from one of the lower grades. The large farmers, who hire this labor done by the tenant class, pay twenty cents per hundred pounds for stripping, tying and sorting the tobacco. A family of four, working from early morning until late at night can usually handle five hundred pounds, thus earning about a dollar for their labor. The long hours and poor pay speak for the abject poverty of the tenant farmer. 

Destitution lurks in every home and pauperism and privation loom large in the immediate future. Winter often finds the poor farmer with his tobacco unsold, the market depending upon unreasonable buyers and the caprice of foreign consumers.

The landowners had the brunt of inconvenience to bear, for the tenant had to be fed, however so scantily, and furnished with medicine, shelter and fuel. If the crop did not pay out then the landowner stood to lose the greater part, as very few of the tenant farmers were ever able to recuperate from a loss and their debts to the landowner were never paid-there was never anything more, available toward the indebtedness. The landowner as well as the tenant became worse off financially each year under this system.

Mortgages had gathered on the land of Kentucky, Tennessee and Virginia like fungus on crumbling walls. Men who had been in affluence, whose credit was good and who represented the substantial citizenship of the community, in a few years had become ridden with poverty; debt had blighted their homes and lands and loved ones. In vain did they turn to wheat but being in the soft wheat belt, they were soon crowded from the commercial world by the hard wheat growers. 

Then they began to raise corn to fatten hogs and make whiskey, but hogs were over produced and did not pay, and prohibition and local option forced corn to the lowest price ever known on the grain market. The farmers turned to hay, legumes, lespedeza, clover-red and crimson-timothy and red top covered the fields for a short time, when the automobiles came along and mules and horses went to the can leaving the demand for any sort of hay quite dull and infrequent. Mortgages grew rapidly. Work mules and milch cows were sold to meet the burden of taxation. Tobacco was the only sure crop by which the farmer could get a little ready money, and when it went up in flames or sold below the cost of production,

there was want, starvation, distress, destitution, pestilence and death.

The laws of every country are the results of the best brains of that country and are enacted into statutes for the weal and happiness of its people. There is small excuse for any number of citizens banding themselves together outside the laws of their country. Southtown could not refuse to harbor the Hillbillies' tobacco. 

The citizenry preferred to be neutral, however, they had to live and the merchants wanted the farmers' trade-wanted what money they had to spend for necessities. 

Hillbillies rented warehouses in Southtown for their convenience, and as in Kentucky, they protected the highways so that the wagons of tobacco could reach them without any interference from Nightriders.

The Nightriders had already boycotted Southtown and every road leading into the place was picketed by their men. No one could enter without their permission, and the strictest censorship was kept on what was taken into the town and what was brought out. Pickets stood about the doors of shops and stores, both wholesale and retail, and when a prospective customer attempted to enter to make a purchase, a few whispered words spoken to him by a man, who looked as if he were ready to suit his action to his words, made the customer take to his heels.

Merchants saw ruin staring them in the face, but nothing could be done. Wholesale hardware houses closed their doors and their owners took a vacation. Buggies and harness men, farming implement and supply merchants closed their doors and some were never in business again. The town people were afraid to venture out and what they had to buy was delivered to their doors. There were no public gatherings. Conditions did not reach the pitch of a reign of terror as they did in the Woodstock district, but there was a reign of despair. 

Southtown had always been a tobacco town. Its wealthy citizens were retired farmers who had made their money and moved there to enjoy the privileges which the rather exclusive old town offered. Every country has its standard of law both civic and religious. 

The laws of any country determine its claim to civilization. It is, indeed, a sad thing when men so ignore the laws of their country as to hide their faces behind a mask in order to take the law in their own hands. Few are the excuses and fatal is the venture, for the laws of a country are builded upon the foundation of happiness to the majority and when men live outside the constitution of their country, they become bandits and breakers of all that civilization stands for. There is a law that governs every branch of crime, and should there not be one to fit a misdemeanor, then the legislators make a law to fit the crime. No set of men has a right to control the rights of others, contrary to the laws of his country. There is frail excuse for an act of that dimension. Instead of establishing a law of the survival of the fittest, they should give their strength to their country, giving the best of themselves instead of the worst form of citizenship. Blessed, indeed, is the man who gives his best to his country, his earnest efforts to his family, his heart to his religion. To be able to keep the law, to build his moral and mental status is to build his own happiness. 

To keep the laws of civilization brings only happiness, yea, it brings honor, it courts education, culture, social activity, and makes a man a lover of good deeds. On the other hand, a law-breaker darkens his life with shadows of fear-fear that is eternal, fear that blights his loved ones or those who love him.

To break the law, ostracises a man from the society of lawabiding citizens and starts him on the downward path. To break the law makes any man, the common kind, he takes his place in the lower strata of society and puts out of his life things aesthetic and divine, living in the realm of materiality. 

To break the law leaves its tarnish on the lives of a man's children, a tarnish that gnaws and blights and forbids growth.

To be civilized in any country, one must keep the laws of that country.

For two years the Association flourished and then it met its Waterloo. The officers, men who had never worked a hill of tobacco or knew one grade from another, piled up an enormous indebtedness on the farmers by buying huge warehouses that were not needed for storing the tobacco and taking for themselves salaries that were disproportionate with the work they did.

The farmers, seeing that their crops were going to pay the expenses of the organization and that the percent they received was getting smaller each year, began selling outside the Association and things went from bad to worse. The farmers were almost beggared once more. Then the curse of black fire came upon the crop and there was a scant supply for marketing. 

The 1922 crops were held for the payment of the Association debts, and not until eight years later, when the law forced the officers to straighten up the balance on hand were the farmers paid a scant two per cent on the deliveries of tobacco they had made to the organization. 

Every farmer in the Woodstock district lost his land through mortgages. Farm land could not be sold at any price and the banks that advanced money on land in that district were out of cash and out of luck. The old negroes who still survive the 

Reign of the Nightriders tell their descendants that the land is cursed by Duncan and that it is a curse of blood that will not be wiped out for many generations. 

Ancestral lands have passed out of the hands of families that had owed them for generations. To eke out a meagre existence, timber was cut, and the resources of the farms destroyed so that the old aristocracy of the soil could not survive and in the twenty years since the Reign of the Nightriders, few of the families that once flourished in that district remain.
