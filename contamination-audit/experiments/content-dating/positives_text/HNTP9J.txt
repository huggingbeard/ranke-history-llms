FINANCING MICHIGAN'S GOVERNMENT 

1930-1938* INCE 1930 a number of significant changes have occurred in the system of governmental finance in Michigan. Federal, state, and local governmental relationships have undergone a transformation. The tax system has been altered substantially. The relief function has developed into a major governmental activity. So rapidly have the changes occurred that the average citizen is confused and bewildered as to what is taking place in the field of government finance. 

It is the purpose in this pamphlet to analyze briefly some of the major developments in the financing of Michigan's government during the last nine years and to explain the chief features of the present fiscal system. The analysis and explanation will be developed along the following lines: (1) fiscal policy; (2) how the money is spent; (3) how the money is raised; (4) the deficit; and (5) the combined cost of state and local government. 

FISCAL POLICY 

The changes since 1930 are traceable in general to the economic depression starting in 1929, and in particular to the strain on the general property tax which has been the mainstay of state and local finance for many years. The sharp decline in prices, real-estate values, employment, and incomes

As the depression became more severe, the strain on the property tax became acute. The years 1930 to 1934 were the most critical. Assessed valuations-the base of the general property tax-declined 30 per cent between 1930 and 1934, property-tax levies declined 40 per cent, and property-tax delinquency soared to unprecedented heights. The large volume of tax delinquency constituted one of the most striking features of the depression, not only in Michigan but in other states as well, because of the fact that the property tax had been a fairly stable source of revenue for many decades. 

FINANCIAL STRAIN ON LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

The decline in property-tax revenues was primarily responsible for the serious financial condition of the local units, because about 90 per cent of their tax revenues are derived from this source. State aid and state-collected locally-shared taxes represent the only other major source of local revenues. In 

1930 these grants from the state amounted to $33 million as compared with a total local levy on property of $235 million. 

While the local units were struggling to finance their operations with rapidly dwindling revenues from the property tax, property owners were complaining of their heavy tax burden. These complaints culminated in the adoption in November, 1932, of a constitutional amendment prohibiting the imposition of a property tax rate in excess of fifteen mills, or 

1.5 per cent, per dollar of assessed valuation. Although this granted some tax relief, it also added to the difficulties of local finance. The strain on local revenues, however, was reduced by the decision of the Michigan Supreme Court in which it was held that the fifteen-mill amendment did not apply to incorporated cities and villages unless those units voted to amend their charters to conform with the requirements of the amend- 2

Michigan have adopted the limitation.

Local officials were faced with the problem of how to obtain sufficient revenues for operating purposes. The imposition of additional fees or taxes was not practicable. Borrowing was practically prohibited because the fifteen-mill limit was applicable not only to taxes levied for operating purposes, but also to any levy for the payment of principal on debt incurred after the effective date of the amendment. It was under these circumstances that the localities sought to obtain additional financial assistance from the state. 

STATE FINANCES CRITICAL 

The state was not in a position, however, to render assistance, for it was also on the verge of a fiscal crisis at the time of the adoption of the fifteen-mill limitation. Prior to 1933 little had been done to deal with the situation, except for the adoption of the malt and aviation gasoline taxes in 1929 and a reduction of 15 per cent in the state appropriation for 1932. The expenditures for state government and for state aid had exceeded revenues available for these purposes in 1931 and 1932, and in the fiscal year ending on June 30, 1933 this deficit was more than $7 million. In the face of this condition in state and local finance it was imperative that steps be taken immediately to deal with the situation.

ADOPTION OF A NEW POLICY 

Saddled with the double responsibility of dealing with the state problem and of assisting the local units, a new fiscal policy was initiated which had the effect of increasing revenues, of granting property-tax relief, and of increasing the amount of state aid. 3

Three new tax measures were enacted in 1933. The first, and most productive, of these measures was the retail sales tax. 

In the first year it produced $34 million, and by 1938 it yielded $53 million.

Shortly after the sales tax was put into effect the chain-store tax was adopted, but it has never produced much revenue. In 

December, 1933, the liquor control act was passed, providing for the imposition of licenses, beer and liquor taxes, and the operation of state-owned liquor stores. In 1938 the liquor taxes, licenses, and gross profits from operation amounted to approximately $18 million.

Property-Tax Relief 

Property-tax relief has occupied an important place in recent fiscal policy as indicated by the fact that the annual tax levy on property declined 41 per cent from $265 million in 1930 to $155 million in 1938. Not all of this decrease was due to the fifteen-mill limitation, however, since there was a 29 per cent decline in assessed valuations which was due to the depression, as compared with a 17 per cent decline in the tax rate. 

The adoption of the fifteen-mill limitation necessitated a considerable reduction in the levy on property in the calendar year 1933-this tax was payable in the fiscal year 1934. In order to make more revenue from the property tax available to local units, the state levy on property was reduced in 1933 from $29 million to $3.5 million, and in 1935 it was dropped entirely. This step could not have been taken by the state, however, had it not been for the adoption of the retail sales tax, which more than made up for the loss in state revenue from the property tax. The retail sales tax was adopted for the purpose of "relieving real estate from the state levy, and also for the purpose of providing funds for the emergency welfare relief.” In addition, it made possible an increase in the amount of state aid for the public schools.

The legislature devoted considerable attention to the problem of tax delinquency, and a number of measures were

enacted to provide relief for distressed property owners. Penalties and interest on taxes for certain years were canceled.

Increased Assistance for Local Units 

In discussing state financial assistance to the local units, a distinction should be made between shared taxes and state aid, although the term state aid is used rather loosely in this pamphlet to denote both of these items. Shared taxes are those taxes such as the automobile weight, gasoline, and severance (oil and gas) taxes—which are collected by the state and shared with the local units according to some definite percentage. For example, 40 per cent of the proceeds of the severance tax is retained by the state and 60 per cent is returned to the local units; of this, 20 per cent goes to the county and 40 per cent to the township or city in which the oil or gas, on which the tax was imposed, was produced. Prior to 1934 the weight tax was shared on a fifty-fifty basis between the state and counties, but the law was changed in that year to authorize the return of the entire proceeds to the local units. Seven-eighths are distributed according to weight tax collections in each county, and one-eighth is divided equally among the eightythree counties.

In the case of state aid, the proceeds of certain taxes are apportioned among the local units according to some index or standard of need. This can be illustrated by reference to the primary-school interest fund. This fund is apportioned among the school districts according to the number of school children between the ages of five and eighteen in the district, and it is made up of the proceeds of the specific tax on railroads and certain other public utilities, the inheritance tax, the corporate organization fee, and the tax on foreign insurance companies. 

During the last nine years the amount of state aid showed a decrease in only one year, namely, 1933. Even when declining state revenues necessitated a reduction in state expenditures, the amount returned to local units increased. Between 1930 and 

1933 the expenditures for state government alone declined

per cent, and they remained below the 1930 level in 1934 and 1935.

In the case of state aid and shared taxes, however, there was a reduction of only 6 per cent between 1930 and 1933. 

The state responded to the demand for increased financial assistance with a large increase in the grants to local units in 1934, and this was followed by successive annual increases. 

By 1938 shared taxes and state aid amounted to $91 million as compared with $33 million in 1930. 

Not all of this increase of $58 million in state aid was financed by new taxation. The expansion in the grants for education and welfare was made possible by the adoption of the sales tax, but the increase in highway grants represented merely a return to the local units of a larger share of the proceeds of the automobile weight and gasoline taxes. In fact, the rate of the weight tax was reduced from fifty-five cents to thirty-five cents per one hundred pounds of weight in 1934. This change in policy for highway grants was made to facilitate the consolidation of the county and township road systems, and to assist the local units in meeting payments of principal and interest on highway debt. Ordinarily, the debt service was financed by a local levy on property, but this source of revenue was restricted at that time by the large volume of tax delinquency. 

FEDERAL AID 

Of outstanding significance during this period was the increase in federal aid after 1933. Michigan received only about $3 million in federal aid in 1930, practically all of which was spent by the state government for highways. With the sharp increase in expenditures for relief, the amount of federal aid rose to $30 million in 1934, and the peak was reached in 1935 with a total of $61 million. In 1938 federal funds spent by the state government amounted to about $17 million. In addition, a large amount of money was spent by the federal Works 

Progress Administration and the Public Works Administration.

HOW THE MONEY IS SPENT TOTAL DISBURSEMENTS 

The last nine years have been marked by a great increase in state expenditures. Total disbursements, which amounted to $118 million in 1930, had climbed to $199 million in 1938. 

The increase was not continuous, however, as total disbursements had also amounted to $199 million in 1935. 

Not all of this money was spent by the state government, because large amounts were returned to the local units, particularly in the three years 1934 to 1936 when federal aid for relief was very large. In these three years the local units actually received and spent more state and federal funds than were spent by the state government. With the adoption of state legislation for participation in the federal social security program in 1936 and 1937, the state was made the spending agency for old-age pensions, aid to dependent children, and relief administration; the federal funds now returned by the state to the local units are practically negligible. Of the $199 million disbursed in 1938, $108 million were spent by the state and $91 million were returned to the local units. 

FINANCING THE STATE GOVERNMENT 

The state government is the focal point in an analysis of the fiscal changes since 1930. We have seen that it assumed a large part of the responsibility of financing local government services after the shrinkage in the property-tax base. The state treasury received the bulk of the federal aid, even though a large part of it was actually spent by the local units in 1934 and 1935. 

Thus, in order to develop the nature of the fiscal problem, total disbursements by the state should be analyzed to show the following: (1) the costs of state government to Michigan taxpayers; (2) the amount of state aid and of shared taxes that is returned to local units; and (3) the expenditures that are financed by federal funds.

The Increase in Costs of State Government 

It is not easy to isolate the cause of this increase of $8 mil- 10

lion in the cost of state government since 1930, because expenditures for some state functions increased while others decreased. 

The amount spent for education and highways in 1938 was $5 million less than in 1930. Expenditures for state enterprises such as prison industries, the state ferry, and the Chelsea cement plant-showed a decrease of about $4 million. 

On the other hand, the new function of welfare and relief accounted for nearly $11 million of state expenditures, excluding the amount returned to local units for relief, in 1938 as compared with $65,000 in 1930. Another new function, liquor control, required slightly more than $2 million in 1938, but this was paid for from the profits of the state liquor stores. 

About $5 million more was spent in 1938 than in 1930 for general government (the judiciary, legislative, executive, and other general administrative items) and protection to person and property (state police, inspectional, and regulatory items). 

Because of the relatively large amount spent for welfare and relief, it would seem that the major part of the net increase of $8 million in the actual cost of state government could be attributed to this activity.

Chief Functions of State Government 

The activities of state government are numerous and of wide variety, although the bulk of the expenditures are for a few functions. General government and protection to person and property embrace a large number of items, but from a relative standpoint they account for a small part of the total. The four chief objects of state expenditure are (1) highways, (2) education, (3) welfare and relief, and (4) charities, hospitals, and corrections.

In 1930 three functions-highways, education, and charities, hospitals, and corrections-accounted for $56 million, or 75 per cent of the total costs of state government. By 1938 the relative importance of these three functions had declined to 60 per cent, but welfare and relief added another 13 per cent. 

Thus, these four functions now account for $60 million, or 73 per cent of the costs of state government. 11

STATE AID AND SHARED TAXES 

It has been shown previously that the amount of state grants to the local units increased by 178 per cent from the figure of $33 million in 1930 to $91 million in 1938. 

Relative Importance of Major Functions

The same functions predominate in the state grants to local units as in the expenditures of state government. Most of the state aid to local units is spent on the three functions of highways, education, and welfare and relief. Very little is returned by the state for charities, hospitals, and corrections.

Of the state aid returned in 1938, $42 million (47 per cent) was for education; $26 million (29 per cent) was for highways; and $16 million (17 per cent) was for welfare and relief. These three functions accounted for 93 per cent of the state aid in that year.

EXPENDITUREs from FederAL AID 

Federal aid played a significant role in Michigan during the depression. In 1930 practically all of the federal aid, amounting to slightly less than $3 million, was spent for highways. The amount spent for highways in 1935 and 1936 amounted to approximately $11 million, but in 1938 it had declined to $4.5 million.

Welfare and relief has been the most important item to be financed with federal funds. Direct relief amounted to $5 million in 1933, $29 million in 1934, $61 million in 1935, and $20 million in 1936. In addition, expenditures made directly by the federal government amounted to $45 million (Civil Works Administration) in 1934 and to $48 million (Works

In 1938 federal aid amounted to about $17 million, of which $11 million represented grants under the social security act. In the same year almost $66 million was spent in Michigan on projects of the Works Progress Administration and Public 12

SUMMARY OF DISBURSEMENTS 

An analysis of state disbursements for the last nine years shows that four functions-(1) highways, (2) education, (3) welfare and relief, and (4) charities, hospitals, and corrections -accounted for 89 per cent of the increase of $81 million. Of this increase, state aid to local units accounted for $58 million, federal aid for $14 million, and state government for $8 million. 16

HOW THE MONEY IS RAISED 

The Constitution of this state provides for two general types of taxes, namely, ad valorem and specific. The general property tax represents the first type and involves an assessment based upon "cash value." Specific taxes are levied according to some unit of measurement-such as the weight of an automobile, per dollar of retail sales, and amount of capital stock issued by a corporation—and they do not require an 'assessment beyond a listing and classification of the subject to be taxed." The class of specific taxes is broad and includes several different kinds of taxes, of which the following are representative: (1) the taxes on certain types of property that have been removed from the scope of the general property tax, such as mortgages, certain bonds, and the operative property of the railroads, telephone and telegraph companies, and certain other public utilities; and (2) the taxes on various sorts of privileges such as the inheritance tax, which is based upon the privilege of transferring property at time of death, the corporate organization fee, and the annual privilege fee, which is based upon the privilege of engaging in business as a corporation. With the abandonment of the levy on property for state purposes, the state government is now relying exclusively upon about twenty "specific" taxes.

CHIEF TAXES 

From 1930 to 1932, four state taxes accounted for 78 per cent of total taxes, licenses and fees: the general property tax, the specific tax on public utilities, the automobile weight tax, and the gasoline tax. An additional 10 per cent was produced by the corporation and inheritance taxes. 

The relative importance of these taxes changed considerably after 1933. In the following year these same four taxes produced only 52 per cent of total taxes, licenses, and fees. A substantial part of this reduction was brought about by the decline in property tax revenues from 25 per cent in 1932 to 8 per cent in 1934. However, this decline was offset by the adoption of the sales tax, which accounted for an additional 33 per cent. These five taxes then represented 85 per cent of the total.

In 1938 the four taxes mentioned above had declined to 40 per cent of total tax revenue, but the sales tax proceeds brought the figure up to 75 per cent.

THE DEFICIT 

It has been shown previously that total disbursements increased $81 million, from $118 million in 1930 to $199 million in 1938. Total receipts, however, increased only $61 million, from $126 million in 1930 to $187 million in 1938. 

While expenditures and revenues will not be exactly equal in any year, such a wide discrepancy over a period of nine years will ordinarily indicate a deficit. This was the result in Michigan, and the state budget director has announced that the state deficit on June 30, 1938, amounted to slightly more than $8 million. 18

There was also a deficit in the four years from 1931 to 1934. In the next three years there was a substantial increase in revenues due to the adoption of the sales tax in 1933, the increase in the yield of several other taxes, and the increase in federal aid. The deficit reappeared in 1938 largely on account of the increased expenditures for welfare and relief, hospitals and corrections, and education, and it has continued to increase since then. The state budget director announced that the deficit as of December 31, 1938, amounted to slightly more than $10 million, and he has estimated that it will amount to about $25 million by the close of the present fiscal year on June 30, 1939. 

The reduction of this deficit and the balancing of the budget constitute the most pressing problem in state finance at the present time.

ARE NEW TAXES NECESSARY? 

It is clear that if the deficit is to be reduced a choice must be made between curtailed appropriations and increased taxes. 

It is the desire of the present administration to avoid any increase in taxes. In this, the administration is supported by the report issued recently by the Governor's Tax Study Commission. In that report, the commission stated that although "some additional revenues are needed to meet the pressing demands upon the state government, . . . no new tax sources are required; that the most immediate source of additional revenue is the thorough collection of taxes already provided." It will be difficult to forestall some increase in expenditures because the various state departments have requested appropriations for the next two years that exceed estimated revenues by $50 million.

METHODS OF REDUCING THE DEFICIT 

Expenditures

If expenditures are to be reduced it will be necessary to direct attention to those items that bulk largest in the budget. 

It has been shown in this pamphlet that the major portion of the increase in the cost of government to Michigan taxpayers since 1930 has been due to the increase in (a) expenditures by the state for relief administration and participation in the 19

federal social security program, and (b) state grants to local units for education, highways, and welfare and relief. Obviously, very little can be accomplished by an economy program that ignores these items. Some retrenchment could undoubtedly be effected in payroll and the functions of general government and protection to person and property, but it would not be a substantial amount. Any material reduction will have to be confined to education and welfare, because highway expenditures are protected by the constitutional amendment adopted in November, 1938, prohibiting expenditure of proceeds of the weight and gas taxes for any purpose other than highways. It should be noted that a bill has been introduced in the legislature suspending for two years the continuing appropriation of $5 million from the general fund for highway construction purposes, and it seems quite likely that it will be adopted. Taxation

The method of reducing the deficit will vary according to the length of time in which it is liquidated. Naturally, if it is eliminated in one year a more drastic policy will have to be followed than if the retirement is spread out over two or three years. For example, in the year 1934 the state of New York eliminated about $85 million of an accumulated deficit of $100 million. The deficit-retirement plan was very broad and included a reduction in income-tax exemptions, continuation of emergency rates of income taxation, an increase in the inheritance tax, a tax on retail sales (excluding food) of 1 per cent, a gross income tax, and new revenue from liquor taxes that had been adopted following repeal of the Volstead act. While most states probably would not adopt such a drastic policy, the New York case is an illustration of what can be done. 

To meet the situation in Michigan, it has been proposed that the retail sales tax be increased to 4 per cent, with the additional 1 per cent being used to finance the deficit. The present rate of 3 per cent is relatively high, and the sales tax now accounts for 35 per cent of total taxes, licenses, and fees collected by the state. Consequently, legitimate objections can be advanced to a rate increase. Nevertheless, an improvement in 20

the administration of this tax might be instrumental in reducing the deficit. In its report, the Tax Study Commission pointed to the possibilities along this line, and among other things recommended that the State Board of Tax Administration, which is in charge of the administration of the sales tax, be abolished and its functions transferred to a new Department of Finance. The commission stated that "delay in sales tax administrative reorganization will cost the state millions of dollars in uncollected taxes." Presumably, the commission had in mind the results that would follow the application of more efficient methods of tax collection.

PROSPECTS FOR INCREASING REVENUES 

There are several possibilities for an increase in revenue during the next few years. In the first place, there has been some increase in sales tax collections recently as compared with last year, and these should increase substantially with an improvement in business conditions. Second, a substantial increase in revenue may be expected if the legislature adopts a bill which has been introduced for the creation of a new Department of Revenue, along the lines suggested by the Tax Study Commission. The proponents of this measure claim that state revenues may be increased by as much as $15 or $25 million if an efficient system of tax collection is adopted to replace the present decentralized and antiquated methods. Third, it is probable that there will be an increase in liquor revenues because the Liquor Control Commission has adopted the policy of selling all liquors at an average retail markup of 55 per cent. Although this policy was authorized by the legislature in 

July, 1937, it was impossible to take advantage of it at that time because the commission was attempting to clear a rather large supply of slow-moving inventory at reduced prices. Finally, some increase in revenue may be expected if the legislature adopts a bill which has been introduced for a low-rate tax on intangible personal property-stocks and bonds. This tax is designed primarily as a substitute for the present local property tax on intangibles, but the present bill provides for a 21

retention by the state of one-third of the proceeds of the tax with the other two-thirds being returned to the local units of government.

COMBINED COST OF STATE AND LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

The analysis thus far has been confined to state finance, including state aid and shared taxes for local units, and federal aid. It is the purpose in this section to make a rough approximation of the combined cost of state and local government by bringing into consideration the local levies on property. Data are not available for a precise determination of the change in the cost of local government during the period 1930 to 1938 because the local units do not report their expenditures and revenues to any central agency. The Bureau of Government has compiled the cost of local government in Michigan for the year 1937 for all units except the townships and villages, and cities with populations of less than 25,000. This was a very tedious task, and a similar undertaking for the year 1930 would require much time and effort. Furthermore, it is doubtful if data comparable with those for 1937 could be obtained for the earlier year.

The figures used here represent the sum of (1) the actual costs of state government, (2) state aid, (3) local levies on property, and (4) federal aid. The sum of these items amounted to approximately $346 million in both 1930 and 1938. Although such a compilation excludes local expenditures made in connection with public service enterprises and some other items not financed by the property tax or state aid, it is doubtful if this weakens the comparison to any substantial degree. Public service enterprises, the largest item excluded, are relatively unimportant except in cities with populations in excess of 25,000. In 1937, according to the compilation of the

The $22 million difference between these two figures may be designated as the amount by which the cost of local government declined during the period. Part of this reduction of $80 million is traceable to a decrease in the tax levy on property for debt service; this was made possible by the adoption of new methods of financing local debt, such as (a) the return to the local units of a larger share of the proceeds of the weight and gas taxes to assist in the retirement of local highway debt, and (b) the issuance of refunding bonds. In 1938 the local levy on property amounted to $155 million, of which $123 million was for current purposes, and $32 million for service on debt incurred prior to the adoption of the fifteen-mill limitation in 1932. Thus, 25 per cent of the 1938 property-tax levy was for debt service and 75 per cent was for current purposes. Further reduction of the property-tax levy may be anticipated when these debts are retired, unless the levy for current purposes is increased or new borrowing is authorized by the voters. 23

The combined cost of state and local government amounted to $343 million in 1930 and to $329 million in 1938, which is a decrease of $14 million. This decrease was offset by the $14 million increase in federal aid during the period. On the basis of these figures the combined cost of state and local government has not changed during the period, although significant changes have occurred in federal, state, and local relationships and in the actual method of financing governmental activities within the state.

SUMMARY 

The most significant changes that have occurred in the Michigan system of governmental finance during the last nine years may be summarized as follows: 

1. The actual cost of state government to Michigan taxpayers has increased by $8 million, or 9 per cent. 

2. State grants to local units in the form of state aid or shared taxes have increased by $58 million, or 178 per cent. 

It should be borne in mind that the increase in highway grants did not require new taxation, as the state was merely returning a larger share of the proceeds of the automobile weight and gasoline taxes. 

3. In 1938 the actual costs of state government to Michigan taxpayers amounted to $83 million, whereas the amount returned to local units was $91 million. Thus, the state is now contributing more to the financing of local government services than is actually spent for state government. 

4. If one considers the combined costs of state and local government, the increase of $58 million in state aid to local units is more than offset by the decrease of $80 million in the local levy on property. This indicates a decline of $22 million in the cost of local government. 

5. The analysis indicates a decrease of $14 million in the combined cost of state and local government to Michigan taxpayers. This is counteracted, however, by a net increase of $14 million in federal aid. Thus, the conclusion may be drawn that the cost of state and local government has changed little, if any, during the period. 24

6. Although the total cost of government has not changed, the distribution of the tax burden in supporting these costs has changed considerably. In 1938 the state and local tax levy on property was $110 million below that of 1930. How has such a substantial reduction been financed? In the first place, $14 million of this reduction has been offset by the increase in federal aid. Second, the sales tax produced $53 million in 1938. 

Third, liquor taxes, licenses, and gross profits from operation of the state liquor stores amounted to approximately $18 million. 

Fourth, there was a $10 million increase in the proceeds of the gasoline tax ($7 million) and the inheritance tax ($3 million). 

Fifth, the yield of various state licenses and miscellaneous revenue increased by $7 million. Sixth, $8 million of this reduction in property-tax burden has not been assigned to any particular group of taxpayers as indicated by the existence of a state deficit of approximately this amount on June 30, 1938.

Just how this deficit, which is increasing at the present time, will be financed is uncertain. 

7. The financing of welfare and relief has become a major problem in governmental finance. In the last six fiscal years, $418 million have been spent for this purpose in Michigan, exclusive of local expenditures for relief purposes, which amounted to about $12 million in 1937 and probably somewhat more in 1938. Of this amount, $64 million were state funds and $354 million were federal funds-including ex penditures by the Civil Works Administration, Works Progress Administration, and Public Works Administration. 

In 1938 relief expenditures that were made through the state treasury amounted to $37.5 million, of which $26.5 million represented state funds and $11 million were federal funds. Local expenditures for relief were probably about $12 million, assuming that they were the same as in 1937. In addition, the federal government spent $66 million for projects of the Public Works Administration and Works Progress Admin istration. This group of items represented a combined expenditure of $115 million. 

8. Finally, reference should be made once more to the 25

increase in state aid, which is one of the most significant factors during the period 1930 to 1938. In the last nine years the state has turned over to the local units $632 million, of which $517 million were state funds and $115 million were federal funds, and the amount returned annually has shown a continuous increase since 1933. Although several departments of the state exercise some supervision of the expenditure of these funds, no state agency is charged with the responsibility of analyzing the fiscal and economic effects of such large grants. This is a financial and an economic problem, and it should be studied from the standpoint of (a) the extent to which state and local-financial and administrative relationships should be readjusted, and (b) the ability of the local units receiving such grants to levy taxes and to perform governmental services efficiently. This is strictly a research problem that is closely related to the annual budget, and if such a study is made it probably should be carried out by a co-ordinating agency, such as the budget division, rather than by a department whose duties are more specialized. 

State aid is one of the most important problems of state finance at the present time. Its significance should be recognized and the problem studied in order that the legislature may be in a position to formulate a long-run program. 26
