FOREWORD 

Res ipsa loquitur is an ancient and proper principle in the law of negligence. It is so recognized in Pennsylvania, although there are but few situations to which it has been permitted to apply. Apparently our courts have preferred to cultivate a somewhat analogous doctrine, that of allowing juries to infer negligence, not from the mere happening of an accident, but from the circumstances under which it happened. The underlying theory is that when all elements of the occurrence are within the defendant's control and the event ordinarily does not happen when care is used, lack of care may fairly be inferred.

In this volume are collected all of the principal Pennsylvania decisions having to do with either doctrine. The author has not undertaken to write a textbook, nor even to formulate statements of legal principles. He has simply classified the cases according to the more important relationships in human activities, and then, in terse and lucid language, has presented the salient facts in each, and the court's conclusion as to whether negligence rightly might be inferred therefrom. The result is a book invaluable to those concerned in the trial of negligence cases.

No matter what our opinion may be as to whether the doctrines of res ipsa loquitur and inferable negligence are essential to legal salvation, the fact remains that the active practitioner has need of a compilation which will insure instant reference to the latest decisions on those subjects. The present volume supplies that need. By its preparation, the author has made the profession his debtor.

THE MAXIM  CHAPTER I 

The study of the maxim res ipsa loquitur brings us at once into the realm of definition. 

Bouvier Law Dictionary (3d Revision), page 2908, gives a definition as follows: "Res Ipsa Loquitur (Lat., the transaction speaks for itself). A phrase often used in actions for injury by negligence where no proof of negligence is required beyond the accident itself, which is such as necessarily to involve negligence. See 5 Ex. 787.

It is thus defined by Erie, J., in Scott v. London 

Docks Co., 2 H. & C. 596. ‘When the thing is shown to be under the management of the defendant or his servants, and the accident is such as in the ordinary course of things does not happen if those who have the management use proper care, it affords reasonable evidence, in the absence of explanation by the defendant, that the accident arose from want of care.' 

This definition has been termed a legal classic: 

1 Thomp. Negl., sec. 15, with a note collecting numerous cases. *** "" 

Zahniser Oil Company v. Penna. Torpedo Co., 190 Pa. 

350, is concerned with this maxim, and because it is one of the leading cases we quote from it: "In other words, appellant claims that under the circumstances it was entitled to have the jury draw the inference of negligence from the happening of the injury alone. "That such inference may sometimes be drawn is true, but the cases are exceptional. The maxim res ipsa loquitur is itself the expression of an exception to the general rule that negligence is not to be inferred but to be affirmatively proved. The ordinary application of the maxim is limited to cases of an 2

absolute duty, or an obligation practically amounting to that of an insurer. Cases not coming under one or both of these heads must be those in which the circumstances are free from dispute and show, not only that they were under the exclusive control of the defendant, but that in the ordinary course of experience no such result follows as that complained of. It is sometimes said that the mere happening of an accident in this class of cases raises a presumption of negligence, but this is hardly accurate. Negligence is never presumed. If it were, it would be the duty of the court, in the absence of exculpatory evidence by the defendant, to direct a verdict for the plaintiff, whereas in these cases the question is for the jury. The accurate statement of the law is not that negligence is presumed but that the circumstances amount to evidence from which it may be inferred by the jury. "In cases where the duty is not absolute, like that of a common carrier to exercise the highest care and skill in regard to the safety of a passenger who has committed himself to its charge, but arises in the ordinary course of business, it is essential that it shall appear that the transaction in which the accident occurred was in the exclusive management of the defendant, and all the elements of the occurrence within his control, and that the result was so far out of the usual course that there is no fair inference that it could have been produced by any other cause than negligence. If there is any other cause apparent to which the injury may with equal fairness be attributed, the inference of negligence cannot be drawn."

In one of our late cases, Fitzpatrick v. Penfield, 267 

Pa. 564, the Court reëxamined the question and speaks of the maxim in the following language at page 577: "It is urged that the court applied the doctrine of res ipso loquitur and that in his instruction to the jury he practically placed defendant in the position of an insurer. It is a well-known principle that negligence is never presumed and generally speaking it must be affirmatively proven, except where there is an absolute duty or an obligation amounting prac-

tically to that of an insurer, when the maxim expresses the law and permits an inference of negligence through the law: Johns v. Penna. R. R. Co., 226 Pa. 320; Zahniser v. Penna. Torpedo Co., 190 

Pa. 350. "There is another class of cases coming close to the line of the class just mentioned, but entirely distinct therefrom, where the circumstances are free from dispute and were under the exclusive control of the defendant-cases where the accident is such as, in the ordinary course of things, would not have occurred if due care had been exercised, and in the absence of explanation by defendant the happening of the accident affords an inference that it was from want of due care. The leading case on the subject is Scott v. London & St. Katherine Dock Co., 3 Hurlstone and Coltman, 596. For illustration of the doctrine see St. Clair v. Edison Electric Light Co., 38 

Pa. Superior Ct. 228; Lanning v. Pittsburgh Rys. 

Co., 229 Pa. 575; Hauer v. Erie County Electric Co., 51 Pa. Superior Ct. 613; Johns v. Penna. R. R. Co., supra; Kahn v. Kittanning Electric Light Co., 238 

Pa. 70; Zahniser v. Penna. Torpedo Co., supra. In all such cases it must be made to appear affirmatively that the appliances or circumstances which caused the injury were under the control of the injuring party, and that by the use of ordinary care an accident could not happen. In other words, the failure to provide proper care being the negligent act, when the accident occurs it can only come through one channel, that is, the negligent act. It is in effect the accident proving its own cause. It stands as its own accuser. It applies chiefly to the series of accidents where the rights of the parties injured are closely allied to the rights of the public generally, and the information, or means of information, as to the occurrence itself, is or was, wholly within the control of the injuring parties. But this doctrine of exclusive control has not been established in this State in its entirety; what has been determined here is this, that the evidence of negligence appears when the detailed explantion of the circumstances immediately preceding the accident, the description of the injuring appliance and the testimony

from experience, show that the thing which happened would not have happened had the injuring appliance or thing been securely in place or properly cared for. The causal connection is supplied, as is done in many other instances, by affirmative proof that the accident would not have happened had the thing been reasonably cared for, so that in reality it is affirmative proof, the degree of proof required being slight because of the duty imposed and the care required. 

The phraseology used to describe the legal result, where the maxim is applied, varies; in some cases it is spoken of as a presumption; in others a prima facie case; in others an inference of negligence.

As pointed out in the Zahniser Oil Company case, it can not amount to a presumption in law because "if it were, it would be the duty of the Court, in the absence of exculpatory evidence by the defendant, to direct a verdict for the plaintiff, whereas in these cases the question is for the jury."

In Pennsylvania it is a presumption of fact and of course for the jury, and not for the Court. 

Therefore, we may take it for granted that, no matter how often in the various cases hereinafter cited, the word "presumption" is used, it is used in the sense of a presumption of fact, that is to say an inference of negligence.

Accurate diction is a matter of the utmost importance, in respect of this phase of the law, because, amongst other things, of confusion concerning the burden of proof. The Supreme Court of the United States has illustrated this view of the maxim in Sweeney v. Erving, 228 U. S. 233: "The general rule in actions of negligence is that the mere proof of an 'accident' (using the word in the loose and popular sense) does not raise any presumption of negligence; but in the application of this rule, it is recognized that there is a class of cases where the circumstances of the occurrence that has caused the injury are of a character to give ground for a reasonable inference that if due care had been employed by the party charged with care

in the premises, the thing that happened amiss would not have happened. In such cases it is said, res ipsa loquitur,-the thing speaks for itself; that is to say, if there is nothing to explain or rebut the inference that arises from the way in which the thing happened it may fairly be found to have been occasioned by negligence. "The doctrine has been so often invoked to sustain the refusal by Trial Courts, to nonsuit the plaintiff or direct a verdict in favor of the defendant, that the application of the rule, where it does apply, in raising a question for the jury, and thus making it incumbent upon the defendant to adduce proof if he desires to do so, has sometimes been erroneously confused with the question of the burden of proof. 

"In our opinion, res ipsa loquitur means that the facts of the occurrence warrant the inference of negligence, not that they compel such an inference. "Res ipsa loquitur, where it applies, does not convert the defendant's general issue into an affirmative defense. When all the evidence is in, the question for the jury is whether the preponderance is with the plaintiff." 

Sweeney v. Erving is not followed by Pennsylvania

Courts in its entirety, although in certain cases the "inference rule" has been applied; whilst in others it is frequently said that the burden was on the defendant. 

The nature of the maxim is discussed and analyzed in an exhaustive note (53 A. L. R. 1494), which, after setting forth the basis of the rule cites the authorities holding to the view that "res ipsa loquitur" is a presumption, and other authorities holding that it is not a presumption but a "permissible inference."

CHAPTER III PASSENGERS INJURED BY JERKING, JOLTING AND JARRING 

Some jolting, jerking and jarring is incident to travel even when a common carrier acts with the utmost care. 

Therefore recovery can not be had in this class of cases unless negligence is shown; it is not enough to describe the jerking, jolting and jarring by some adjective such as "awful" or "unusual," as will be seen in the following cases:

Zieger v. Phila. Rapid Transit Co., 84 Superior Court 

541, is a typical illustration; here the plaintiff alleged that while walking to a seat he was thrown to the floor by a violent jolt; he also described it as a sudden jerk; two of his witnesses described it as an awful jerk; it was not shown to have affected other passengers. In approving the entry of judgment non obstante veredicto for defendant the Superior Court said: "We are of opinion that even the term "awful jerk" when not accompanied by testimony inherently establishing its extraordinary character or by evidence of its effect on other passengers, who must have been disturbed to some extent by an unusual or extraordinary jolt of the car, is too vague to sustain a finding in negligence."

In Holutin v. Phila. Rapid Transit Company, 89 Superior Court 326, the car was alleged to have stopped so suddenly as to throw the plaintiff off her feet. This plaintiff described as a "hard stop" and the court said: "Her testimony that it was a hard stop did not measure up to the standard which has been fixed by the decisions on this subject." 

Delaney v. Buffalo, Rochester & Pgh. R. Company, 266 Pa. 122, illustrates the principle. Plaintiff was just resuming her seat in the observation car when a sudden lurch caused her to fall on her knees and strike her head and shoulders against the revolving chair on the opposite 11

side of the car. The plaintiff described the lurch or jolt as "terrific." The lower court was correct in entering judgment for defendant, for "There was no evidence of defendant's negligence, except the happening of the accident, which of itself does not justify recovery. "That a passenger fell from her chair from the mere movement of the car of a fast train does not make out a prima facie case of negligence; * * "It is a matter of common knowledge that cars will lurch in rounding curves and to recover for an injury to a passenger resulting therefrom it must appear that such lurch was caused by some negligent act or omission on the part of the defendant." 

The evidence in Young v. Penna. Railroad Co., 283 Pa. 

234, was sufficient to allow recovery; the plaintiff while moving from her seat in a dining car was thrown by what was called "a terrible crash" against the corner of a table. That there was some unusual and extraordinary violence appeared in the testimony showing the effect of the crash on other passengers and movable objects in the car. 

Where the jerk occurred in the starting of a car before the plaintiff had a reasonable opportunity to be seated, throwing her down and causing the injuries, the case is for the jury: White v. Columbia & Montour Electric 

Railway Co., 215 Pa. 462.

A violent coupling caused the injury in Gould v. Atlantic City Railroad Co., 68 Superior Court 431, of such violence that other passengers were affected. It was described as having happened with "terrific force" and also as a "crash." "It was incumbent on the plaintiff to show affirmatively that this jerk was not only unexpected, but was unusual and violent, and that the car was carelessly and negligently started with such sudden force and violence as to produce his injuries." 

Judgment for plaintiff affirmed.

CHAPTER VIII DAMAGE BY COMMON CARRIERS TO PROPERTY 

The question of whether there is an inference of negligence frequently arises in cases involving damage to property, particularly where the contract of carriage purports to limit the liability of the carrier. 

The contract in Empire Transportation Company v. 

Wamsutta Oil Refining & Mining Company, 63 Pa. 14, provided that the defendant railroad should not be liable for loss by fire in transit; plaintiff shipped refined oil on defendant's road, and immediately in front of it the defendant attached in the car a load of crude oil, which later was discovered to be on fire; refined oil will not ignite by flame at lower than 110 deg., but crude oil will ignite at 65 deg.; the fire which was of unknown origin spread to the car containing plaintiff's oil and destroyed it, it being impossible to uncouple the refined oil car from the crude oil car because the coupling was defective. Because combustible substances were carried, it was "the special duty of the carrier to take every available precaution against the communication and spreading of a fire, if it should occur. An evident and simple measure is to have the coupling of the cars in such perfect order that any one or more of them can be easily detached from the others, in time to be saved from the consequences."

The court held that the proof of the defective coupling shifted the burden to the carrier, and judgment for plaintiff was affirmed.

A distinction exists in the cases between animate and inanimate objects and between property liable because of its character or quality to deterioration, and that which is not. Where the injury may have been the result of natural causes, not involving the carrier's default, something more than the mere fact of the loss must be shown (Blackburn v. Adams Express Co., 43 Superior Court 

276). There the court said: 30

CHAPTER X ELEVATORS AND ESCALATORS 

The doctrine of res ipsa loquitur applied for the protection of passengers in the hands of common carriers has been extended to passengers in elevators, it being held that the owner and operator who undertakes to carry passengers in an elevator, undertakes to carry them safely. He is bound to use the greatest care, not only in providing safe and suitable cars, appliances and machinery for motion and control, but also in managing these means of transportation. The law holds him to the utmost care and diligence, and makes him responsible for the slightest neglect. The happening of an accident raises a presumption of neglect and throws upon the carrier the burden of showing that such neglect did not exist. Fox v. 

Philadelphia, 208 Pa. 127, is the leading case on the subject and the plaintiff's action was nonsuited in the court below for the reason that the evidence disclosed no negligence. The judgment was reversed, the Supreme Court saying: "Whether this evidence was sufficient for the purpose for which it was offered we need not decide, for the plaintiffs were not called upon for specific proof that the city had been negligent. Their case was for the jury when they showed that their father had been crushed to death by the elevator through no fault or negligence on his part, and it was for the defendant to rebut the presumption that it had been negligent. *** In case of injury without fault or negligence by the one injured, the presumption is that such protection had not been afforded, and that there had been negligence on the part of those operating the elevator.”

The facts in the Fox case were that plaintiffs' decedent was in an invalid's chair and when the elevator reached the second floor, the door was opened and the chair was being pushed out; the elevator suddenly started downward and he was crushed to death. 40

Joyce v. Black, 226 Pa. 408, is one of the leading cases in Pennsylvania on the question of falling objects. A pedestrian was injured by reason of the fall of an ornamental bracket from the front of a building. It was held that in such a case the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur does not apply. The plaintiff had attempted to show negligence by the offer of certain evidence, which was refused; this was held error and the case sent back for another trial.

Nevertheless, persons who are in control of the building have a duty of keeping it and its attachments in such condition as to prevent probable injury to anyone lawfully upon the street: Fitzgerald v. Sherwood, 239 Pa. 

298. The accident here was caused by a loose board which fell from the fourth floor fire escape injuring a teamster; there was a storm at the time, both wind and rain. "The defendants were in control of the building, and the duty rested upon them of keeping it and its attachments, including the fire escape in such a condition as to prevent probable injury to any one upon the street. If they deposited loose material upon the floor of the fire escape in such a way as to make it probable that winds or storms incident to this region might blow the material off, endangering persons upon the street, such conduct would fairly be regarded as negligence.'

Hot pitch fell upon a child lawfully on a public street, being discharged from a bucket which had been taken by a workman to the roof of a building: Cutler v. Morrison, 43 Superior Court 55. Two methods were testified to as being in general use in applying pitch to the roof of a building; neither was used. 

A judgment for plaintiff was affirmed upon the theory that, "the jury could have found that by the adoption of precautions in general use in that business, the accident in all probability would not have occurred.' 44

A porch used in common by tenants fell, injuring one of the tenants, and an action to recover damages was brought: Prager v. Gordon, 78 Superior Court 76. A falling tree injured a boy, Mardo v. Valley Smokeless Coal Co., 279 Pa. 209, playing in the yard. Suit was brought against the landlord. CHAPTER XII HORSES AND ANIMALS 

The leaving of a horse unhitched and unattended in a public street is prima facie evidence of negligence, and puts the burden of proof upon the defendant to show that there were circumstances which excused or justified his conduct.

In Henry v. Klopfer, 147 Pa. 178, the lower court was asked to charge that the leaving of a horse unhitched in a public street was, in law, negligence, which instruction the court refused to give, but the Court did charge that it was such evidence that "would justify you in finding that it was an act of negligence." In affirming this instruction the Supreme Court said: "What the Judge did say was, that he was not disposed to say, as a matter of law, that the act of the defendant, in leaving his horse unhitched in the street was negligence; but that it was evidence, prima facie, of negligence, and put the burden of proof upon the defendant. If there were circumstances which excused or justified his conduct, it was for him to show them. If none such were shown, the fact, unexplained, that he had left his horse, unattended and untied, in a public street of a city, was enough to justify a verdict against him."

In affirming this rule, in a later case, it is spoken of as "a presumption of negligence," and the strength of the presumption there discussed: Stevenson v. United States 

Express Co., 221 Pa. 59. "One who leaves a horse unhitched and unattended on a city street takes the risk of what the horse may do. It was held in Henry v. Klopfer, 147 

Pa. 178, that such an act raises a presumption of negligence and puts on the party doing it the burden of showing circumstances which justified or excused it. How strong the presumption will be must depend largely on the circumstances. If the horse is young, skittish, nervous or unused to the sights and 51

sounds of a city street, the presumption would be strong, while if he is old, staid and accustomed to city life, it might be very slight. But even a staid and veteran horse may be liable to sudden fright, or as in this case to sudden pain which may induce dangerous behavior. It is, therefore, a matter for the jury." 

In Jordan v. Eisele, 273 Pa. 95, a young and skittish horse was left untied while the driver went over to its mate, who had fallen; the fallen horse in getting up kicked the untied young and skittish horse, causing it to run three blocks and injure the plaintiff. The court held that a presumption of negligence arose, placing the burden on the defendant, and the question whether that burden was met was a question for the jury. 

It is not necessarily negligence to leave a horse untied and unattended in a private lane: Coller v. Knox, 222 

Pa. 362.

The mere fact that a horse has run away is not sufficient to establish negligence. The Court so held in Quicksall v. Abbotts Alderney Dairies, 89 Superior Court 420; in addition to the fact of a runaway horse, "there must be evidence from which it may be inferred that the owner was negligent in so caring for the horse that he was suffered to run away."

In this case there being no such evidence a judgment for plaintiff was reversed. 

In Erickson v. Sutherland, 69 Superior Court 516, plaintiff's husband was killed in a runaway of the team which he was driving. It was charged that his employer furnished him with two unbroken, wild and unroadworthy horses, but there was no proof that they ran away because they were wild and unbroken, and a recovery was denied.

If, however, the runaway is the result of some negligent act of the driver the case may be for the jury. In 

Luks v. American Ice Company, 267 Pa. 337, it was shown that one of a team was a young horse, not city broke, and it was whipped by the driver, causing it to

53 become uncontrollable, or was whipped by the driver while it was in the act of running away, and in consequence the wagon crashed into a pole, whereby plaintiff was hurt by the horses or by falling backwards over a step. While the verdict for plaintiff was reversed because of errors in the charge, it was reversed with a venire, the case being for the jury.

Proof that a horse, which was unattended and unhitched, snapped at a child which was playing on the sidewalk, injuring him, is not sufficient to establish liability: Barshay v. American Ice Company, 84 Superior

Court 538; this because there was no cause or connection between the failure to hitch the horse or attend it, and the injury to the child, the accident could have occurred just as well whether the horse was hitched or not, or whether a driver was on the wagon or not, or even if both measures had been resorted to. 

Another illustration of the lack of causal connection is found in Chachkin v. Accomodation Ice and Coal 

Company, 92 Superior Court 416, where, in the delivery of ice, a horse was left unhitched and unattended close to the curb, and the plaintiff leaned against the rear wheel; the horse took a step or two forward, catching the plaintiff's arm between the wheel and the brake. Recovery was denied because the failure to hitch or attend the horse did not cause the injury.

That the failure to hitch or attend a horse in a public street gives rise not to a presumption of law but to a presumption of fact only is emphasized in two cases. In 

Weikel v. Pullman Taxicab Company, 59 Superior Court 

595, a taxicab on a well-lighted street, ran into the rear of a milk wagon which had a light at the back. The horse and wagon were unattended and were proceeding slowly along the street. The court held, that while there is a presumption of negligence in leaving a horse unattended or unhitched in such circumstances, the presumption is for the jury, and it should determine whether the driver's negligence did or did not contribute to or cause the accident. A similar state of facts is found in Supplee-Wills- 

Jones Milk Company v. Southern Penna. Traction Com pany, 98 Superior Court 550, where it was held that the

failure of the plaintiff's employee to hitch or attend the horse was not conclusive of contributory negligence; the entry of a nonsuit was reversed. 

As applied to an employee the rule is that negligence must be shown. Pittston Coal Company v. McNulty, 120 

Pa. 414, involves the injury to a minor employee while driving a mule. After the mule was stopped to detach a stretcher, the mule "started out again" throwing the boy on the track. There was no evidence that the mule was vicious or that he ran away. Judgment for plaintiff was reversed.

Rennie v. Schepps, 297 Pa. 39, is a case of a runaway steer. Plaintiff was driving his automobile to Philadelphia when this steer lunged against the car two or three times, knocking it over on one side. It appeared that while some steers were being taken from a truck a short distance along a street, fifty feet, one of them bolted and ran away and before it could be caught, the accident occurred. In affirming the nonsuit the court, commenting on the fact that there was no evidence to show carelessness on the part of those in charge of the steers, said: “The burden of proving negligence in a case of this sort is no different from that of any other negligence case, except (in cases where) the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur is applied, and this is not one of them; plaintiff can not recover on the mere happening of the occurrence; he must present some proofs from which negligence can fairly be inferred."

CHAPTER XIII PHYSICIANS, SURGEONS, AND DENTISTS 

The standard of care which the law requires of a physician or dentist is stated in English v. Free, 205 Pa. 

624, where it is said: "It is settled law in this class of cases, that 'a surgeon undertakes to possess and in the treatment of a case to employ such reasonable skill and diligence as is ordinarily exercised in his profession; and in judging of the degree of skill, regard is to be had to the advanced state of the profession at the time': McCandless v. McWha, 22 Pa. 261. Taking this to be the law, the burden was on plaintiff to prove at least by the weight of the evidence, that defendant failed to treat plaintiff with reasonable professional skill.’

The plaintiff brought trespass in this case against the defendant surgeon to recover damages for alleged negligence in the diagnosis and treatment of an injury received. It appeared from the evidence that the plaintiff was hurt by a car body which fell on him; he was examined by defendant, a practicing surgeon, who treated him for four months without discovering that his hip was dislocated. During this time two other surgeons were called in to see the plaintiff. As plaintiff was not responding to defendant's treatment he went to another city, and was there examined by another surgeon who diagnosed the injury as a dislocation of the hip joint and practically effected a recovery. The court held that a surgeon is not an insurer of his patients and there was no evidence that the defendant surgeon did not bring to the treatment of the case reasonable skill and diligence, although he may have made a mistaken diagnosis in that he did not discover the exact nature of the injury. The entry of a nonsuit by the lower court was affirmed. 

In another case we find an attempt to recover for a mistaken diagnosis. (See Wohlert v. Seibert, 23 Superior 55

Court 213.) Here the defendant, a general practitioner, failed to diagnose glaucoma, a rare disease of the eye, the symptoms of which are frequently confused with conjunctivitis and other diseases. While the result of the disease was blindness, the appellate court held that the defendant was not liable for a failure to cure or diagnosis correctly, and a judgment for plaintiff was reversed.

In Delong v. Delaney, 206 Pa. 226, the plaintiff's decedent had his leg badly crushed in attempting to board a railroad train and the defendant, a physician, rendered first aid including bandaging the leg; the defendant then relinquished the case to the family physician who took the patient to the hospital where he died a few hours later from loss of blood. The plaintiff offered evidence that a tourniquet was an instrument in common use among physicians for stopping the flow of blood, and that it had not been used by the defendant. The lower court held no negligence was shown, and entered a nonsuit which judgment, on appeal, was affirmed. The court said, "the jury could only have made an uninformed guess. Negligence can not be found in that way."

An attempt was made in a later case, (Stemons v. 

Turner, 274 Pa. 228), where the plaintiff received an

X-ray burn to impose upon the defendant physician a different standard of care, and the lower court instructed the jury it was the duty of defendant to use a high degree of care; this instruction upon appeal was held error. 

An attempt was also made to apply the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur. Holding that this doctrine should not be applied in such a case, the court said, after referring to a note in Runyan v. Goodrum, 13 American Law Reports, 1403, 1413 (1921): "The note to this case covers all the decided cases, points out the conflict of authority among them on the res ipsa loquitur doctrine, and deduces the generally recognized conclusion that a person undertaking to use X-rays is held only to the same measure of responsibility as in administering other forms of

medical treatment. (See 21 Ruling Case Law 386, to the same effect.) This is the rule which wisdom dictates, but the court below did not apply it." 

The refusal to apply the res ipsa loquitur doctrine also appears in Nixon v. Pfahler, 279 Pa. 377; here the plaintiff's wife was having X-ray photographs taken of her teeth when she claimed to have been injured by an electric spark which struck her right knee and passed down and out her foot. No evidence of negligence was offered, the plaintiffs resting their case solely on the proof of the accident.

The Supreme Court held that to apply this doctrine would make a physician practically an insurer of the safety of his patients, although it is necessary for those engaged in the medical profession to constantly employ dangerous agencies, like electricity, radium, surgical instruments, poisons, and anesthetics. The court said that if prima facie liability attaches for an accident resulting from the use of one, logically it should from the use of any other. We quote: "Plaintiffs make no claim of having shown any negligence, but on their behalf it is urged that negligence should be inferred from the happening of the accident. In other words that the rule of res ipsa loquitur should be applied. We have applied that rule against carriers of passengers, and in other rare instances, but never against a medical practitioner, who is within the general rule that negligence will not be presumed from the mere happening of an accident. **** The X-ray machine is indispensable to the healing art and the mere happening of an accident from its use creates no presumption against the instrument or its operation."

It is still open to the plaintiff however, to show negligence in the use of an X-ray machine and this was done in a case where it was contended by the plaintiff and found as a fact by the jury, that the defendant had failed to insert the proper filters in the machine, as a result of which certain rays which will burn were not eliminated as was admitted by all the expert witnesses called on

both sides. The Supreme Court said, Frederick v. 

Strouse, 299 Pa. 268: "Here there is positive evidence of improper treatment. There is no question under the evidence submitted that plaintiff's injuries were due to an X-ray burn and that defendant is the only one who administered this form of treatment to her, and there is direct evidence of a failure on his part to use the proper safeguarding instrumentalities to protect her from being burned. * * *''

Where a gauze pad or sponge has been left in the body of a patient following an operation in which the pad was used, a presumption of negligence arises against the operating surgeon and in an action by the patient the burden of proof is upon the defendant surgeon. In announcing this rule the Supreme Court said, Davis v. Kerr, 239 Pa. 351, at page 358: "Here the surgeon had reached the conclusion, that he had removed all the sponges, a mistaken conclusion, but verified by the nurses' count. In reaching his conclusion, did he exercise ordinary skill? 

We see nothing in the evidence to warrant the inference that he did not; but, on the other hand, we find nothing to warrant the inference that he did, which is far more important, since the burden of showing care was upon him. Why was a foreign substance left in the parts which the operating surgeon should have removed? It was for him to acquit himself of negligence with respect to it."

In this case the court held that the defendant did not entirely discharge the burden of proof upon him by showing a custom for operating surgeons to rely upon the count of the nurses for assurance that all pads or sponges had been removed, the proof being that the defendant surgeon had been told by the nurses that all the pads or sponges had been removed; there still remained a duty upon the surgeon to exercise care in determining whether any foreign substance had been left in the wound.

In Barnard v. Schell, 85 Superior Court 329, a portion of a drainage tube broke off and was left in the patient's body and damages were claimed for this injury; it appeared that the use of the drain was proper and that the breaking of it was not due to the negligence of the defendant. Even if the defendant had been aware of the fact that a piece was broken off the question remained under the evidence whether he would have been negligent in failing to attempt to remove it. Expert evidence on both sides showed that it would have been dangerous to the patient to attempt to remove it, and there was a difference of opinion as to when the attempt should be made. In commenting upon the Davis case the court said, Barnard v. Schell, 85 Superior Court 329, at p. 335: "This defendant was called upon to show that he used reasonable skill, care and prudence. Under the rule declared in Davis v. Kerr, cited, it was a sufficient answer to plaintiff's case to establish that allowing the piece of rubber tube to remain in the incision in the circumstances, if defendant knew it was there, was justified by the practice of good modern surgeons in the community, and that an attempt to remove it would have endangered the safety of the patient. If there was mistake or error it was one of judgment for which defendant is not responsible in damages.” 

Stewart v. Manasses, 244 Pa. 221, where the plaintiff claimed to have been burnt, after an operation in a hospital, by a hot water bottle, placed in the bed by a nurse,

acting under the direction of the hospital, and not under the direction of the defendant, holds that the defendant was not liable, and a nonsuit was properly entered; the defendant had agreed to perform the operation for a stipulated sum but the hospital expenses were to be paid by the plaintiff and the nurse who failed to remove the hot water bottle acted under the direction of the Directress of Nurses.

Where a patient died under an anesthetic, it was sought to impose liability upon the attending physician. 

Two physicians said that a local, instead of a general, anesthetic should have been used; that if ether or chloroform were to be used a heart stimulant should have been given; that it was not good surgery to switch from ether to chloroform when there was an accumulation of mucus, and that the operation should have been abandoned: 

Remley v. Plummer, 79 Superior Court 117. Seven sur geons said in testifying for the defendant that everything was done in accord with the best practice. 

In the lower court the plaintiff recovered a judgment but this was reversed because the jury could not be permitted to decide a disputed scientific medical and surgical question, where the doctors and surgeons could not agree.

Premature discharge from a hospital has been the basis in two cases of an attempt to impose liability upon a physician or surgeon, the first case being Veit v. Hinchclife, 90 Superior Court 280. The evidence showed in this case that plaintiff's wife was taken to defendant's private hospital to be confined; one of the allegations averred certain of her illness was due to improper and premature discharge from the hospital, but no evidence was produced showing that her discharge was improper or that injurious consequences were traceable thereto. 

Substantial verdicts for the plaintiffs were reduced by the lower court to nominal damages, and upon plaintiffs' appeal affirmed without discussion of defendant's liability for such damages; if defendant had appealed, the judgments would doubtless have been reversed. 

The second case is that of Coots v. Lauffer, 99 Superior 

Court 214, where the plaintiff's wife was permitted to be

61 taken home twenty hours after the removal of her tonsils and when her throat was in a greatly swollen condition. 

A specialist testified that he did not send his patients home until thirty hours after the operation, and that he did not think a doctor who knew her condition should have permitted her to leave. Subsequent operations which she underwent were not shown to have been caused by her early discharge, her own physician testifying they were due to an infection of the tonsils. Judgment for defendant non obstante veredicto was affirmed. 

No distinction appears between the standard of care which the law imposes upon dentists and that required of physicians. The rule is the same in either case. An illustration of negligence on the part of a dentist is found in Krompoltz v. Hyman, 70 Superior Court 581. Here the evidence was on the part of the plaintiff that he desired a tooth treated, or if it could not be, then to have it extracted. Defendant carelessly extracted a sound tooth, and then took out the tooth of which plaintiff was complaining. Because the testimony as to the latter tooth was interwoven with the other injury the Superior Court held that the lower court did not commit error in submitting the entire case to the jury. As to the pulling of the sound tooth the Appellate Court said: "A dentist, who without justification pulls a perfectly sound tooth, might be said to be guilty not only of carelessness but of wanton recklessness." 

Gentile v. DeVirgilis, 290 Pa. 50, is a case charging a dentist with malpractice. Plaintiff claims that defendant dentist extracted two teeth, one of which he broke off, and allowed the root of it to remain. All the doctors agreed that the mere fact the tooth was fractured in the extraction and that part of the root remained in the jaw was not of itself any evidence of lack of skill on the part of the defendant, and this left the failure to extract the root as the only basis of possible recovery. The plaintiff admitted that the defendant instructed her to go home, use salt solution and an ice bag and to return to him the next day for further examination and treatment; she did

not return for three or four days, at which time a serious condition had arisen. The court held that it was the duty of the patient to coöperate with his professional adviser, and if he will not or can not, there can be no recovery.

CHAPTER XIV WATER, OIL, GAS, VAPORS, ETC. 

Unless the facts amount to a nuisance, damage caused by water ordinarily can not be recovered unless negligence is shown.

In Killion v. Power, 51 Pa. 429, the facts were Power occupied the first story of a storehouse, and Killion the stories above; on occasion the water was turned off and 

Hughes, a customer of Killion, desiring to get a drink, went to the third story where there was a stopcock and was told there was no water there; whether he turned the stop was not known. Hughes was frequently at Killion's premises, having at least implied permission to use them. When the water was turned on, because the stop was open, the first story was flooded, and the court held Killion was under a duty to see that the stopcock was not left open so as to flood the store downstairs, and affirmed a judgment for the plaintiff: "It would be such negligence as to entitle plaintiff to recover."

A similar situation is found in Levinson v. Myers, 24 

Superior Court 481; here a millinery store was on the first floor, over which was an occupied flat, under defendant's exclusive control, which contained water pipes and a hot water boiler. Zero weather caused the exposed boiler to crack and leak through the ceiling, resulting in the destruction of goods in the first floor store. Quoting Killion v. Power, 51 Pa. 429 and Shafer v. Lacock, 168 Pa. 497, the court held, an inference of negligence arose casting the burden on defendant of explanation. 

In Silver Costume Company v. Passant, 71 Superior

Court 252, plaintiff was an occupant of a third floor; defendant, with another tenant, had possession of the fifth. The son of the defendant had worked on the fifth floor on Sunday; no one else had been there except that the night watchman had gone through the building. On 

Monday morning water came through the ceiling, damag- 63

ing plaintiff's stock and it was discovered that the spigot on the fifth floor had been left open, causing the overflow. Upon the authority of Shafer v. Lacock, 168 Pa. 

497, the court held a presumption or inference of negligence arose. 

The same conclusion is reached in Majestic Amusement Company v. Standard Cigar Company, 79 Superior 

Court 309, where it was shown a tenant had complete and exclusive possession of leased premises and the entire water system froze, and burst, causing damage, for which the landlord brought suit. A judgment for plaintiff was affirmed on the ground that a presumption or inference of negligence had arisen.

Cook v. Davis, 7 D. & C. 45, is another case where the water leaked to the lower floor, causing damage, and it was held a prima facie case had been made out, calling upon the defendant for an explanation. 

In all these cases the inference of negligence, or presumption thereof arose from the proof of facts and were cases where the defendant was in exclusive control or possession.

In Householder v. Quemahoning Coal Company, 272 

Pa. 78, the water supply of the surface owner was destroyed; the lower court put a higher burden on defendant and was reversed for charging that the defendant's duty was other than refraining from negligent acts. 

Morgan v. Duquesne Borough, 29 Superior Court 100, is frequently cited; here the lower court instructed the jury that if the water pipes broke or leaked and injured plaintiff, recovery could be had. Judgment for plaintiff was reversed with a venire, because the instruction withdrew from the jury the element of negligence. 

In Schwindt v. Lehigh Water Company, 33 Superior 

Court 23, the plaintiff undertook, because of the ruling in the Morgan case, to prove negligence, and it is shown that the water main, whose breaking or leaking caused the damage, had been installed for fifty years; originally it had been subject to a sixty pound pressure, which was later increased to a hundred and ten pounds; for two years the surface in front of plaintiff's premises had from time to time settled, indicating trouble beneath, and

65 it was alleged that the defendant did not make proper inspection. This evidence was sufficient to warrant a verdict for plaintiff, which was affirmed on appeal. 

Similar evidence showed negligence sufficiently in Zimmer v. Philadelphia, 57 Superior Court 20, where a house was damaged through a break in the water pipe. There was evidence of settling and of depression in the street, and that the street was set; the break when examined by an expert was said to be an old one; all this should have put the defendant on notice. 

The opinion in Clouse v. Crow, 68 Superior Court 248, contains an extensive analysis of applicable cases and holds that the discharge of mine water, either by drift or from a lower level by a shaft, does not impose liability for injury to a neighbor's property if the same is done without negligence or malice.

The two following cases, which were the result of the breaking of the same water main, are conspicuous examples of what evidence will not, and what evidence will prove negligence in this class of cases. 

Allied Realty Company v. Philadelphia, 95 Superior

Court 62, the first case to be tried, was one in which it was shown that the high pressure main broke, causing a flood which damaged plaintiff's property. The Superior 

Court held: "The mere fact that water from the ruptured main injured the plaintiff's property did not create a prima facie liability. Defendant's liability depends on whether or not it was negligent." 

The court then analyzed and came to the conclusion that negligence had not been shown. 

The companion case, later tried, Riegel & Company, Inc. v. Philadelphia, 296 Pa. 256, concerned the same accident to a different plaintiff, and additional evidence was offered, including evidence of water for months before in other properties, and notification to the city that that water came from the main in question, and evidence that the city's repair crew did not properly continue their efforts to find the leak; because of the additional evidence offered the Supreme Court distinguished the

Riegel case from the Realty Company case and held that the negligence of the defendant was for the jury. 

Where the injury amounts to a nuisance, the question ceases to be one of negligence. For example, in Welli ver v. Irondale Electric Light, Heat and Power Com pany, 38 Superior Court 26, it was shown that the defendant constructed an artificial watercourse or race, for the purpose of supplying power to the manufacture of electricity for the town of Bloomsburg, the water being brought from a distance. Plaintiff claimed and recovered damages for injury to his land caused by seepage and percolation of water through the embankment of defendant's artificial watercourse or race. 

It was contended that defendant was not liable in the absence of negligence, but the Superior Court held that the facts shown, "do not absolve the company from the obligation so to conduct it that a private nuisance shall not be created by percolation of water brought by artificial means upon its premises.' 

The judgment for plaintiff was affirmed. 

On the other hand in Abraham v. Yardum, 64 Superior 

Court 225, plaintiff having failed to show negligence, tried to sustain his case upon the theory of a nuisance, but failed. Defendant was the owner of a lot and without objection on the part of the city, constructed a water line under a street in order to connect the city water main with his own property. Water escaped from the pipe and caused damage. The court held the defendant was not liable unless negligence, in construction or maintenance, was shown. case 

Stokes v. Penna. Railroad Co., 214 Pa. 415, is a ca where there was a recovery upon the nuisance theory. 

Here defendant's employees discharged the contents of vessels containing acid from the tower house, at times within six years; the case was brought upon the theory of the maintenance of a nuisance, and the appellate court held the entry of a nonsuit was improper. 

Where a pipe line company carries oil from a distance, allows it to escape from the pipes, and percolate

Bros. & Co., 145 Pa. 324), even without proof of negligence. Robb v. Carnegie, was a case in which a company manufacturing coke from coal not mined by it, but purchased at mines of other persons, was said to be liable in damages for injuries to crops and soil caused by smoke and vapors emitted from the coke oven. 

The evidence of negligence can not consist in what was done after the damage was inflicted; in Matteson v. 

New York Central & Hudson River Railroad Co., 218

Pa. 527, where the plaintiff's land was flooded due, as alleged, by construction of defendant's bridge, it was error to permit a witness to testify to certain directions given by the defendant's supervisor of bridge work, for the removal of the pile which it is claimed caused the obstruction in the stream and the flooding of plaintiff's land; it was error because the jury would most likely have drawn an inference to-wit, an implied admission of negligence in having the piles where they had been placed.

CHAPTER XV NEGLIGENCE, PROOF OF-STATUTORY VIOLATIONS 

Illustrations of this phase of proof of negligence are to be found growing out of statutes relating to manufacturing, sale, and operation of chattels and property. 

The shotgun cases are conspicuous; the Act of June 

10, 1881, P. L. 111, provides that it shall be a misdemeanor to sell to any person under sixteen years of age, knowingly and willfully, any cartridge, gunpowder or explosive substance. In Shaffer v. Mowery, 265 Pa. 300, the defendant sold a cartridge to a minor under the age of fourteen years and with it the minor accidentally shot his younger brother. The lower court entered a nonsuit which was reversed, the court saying: "When defendant sold the shell to Samuel, he violated this statute, thereby making himself liable for any natural or probably harmful result which might follow in the wake of his wrongful act. ***"'

A violation of the same statute was charged in Wassel v. Ludwig, 92 Superior Court 341; in this case a rifle was sold by defendant to a minor and was used by another boy, causing the injury, resulting in the death of plaintiff's daughter. It was for the jury to say whether the defendant ought not to have anticipated the use of the gun by playmates, and that acts of injurious character would likely be committed. This case illustrates the rule that actionable negligence may be the proximate, without being the sole or immediate, cause. 

In McMillen v. Steele, 275 Pa. 584, defendant's clerk sold the gun to a minor and the defendant was held liable for the action of his servant upon the theory that a principal is liable in damage because of the criminal acts of the agent, when such acts are within the scope of the agents' employment. 

Fritz v. Elk Tanning Co., 258 Pa. 180, relied upon the statute of 1905 under which "it was defendant's duty to know the character of the fumes and gases arising in its bleach room, and, 68

NEGLIGENCE, PROOF OF-STATUTORY VIOLATIONS 

69 if poisonous, to provide for their elimination by exhaust fans or other sufficient devices.' 

Sulphuric acid was used in the tannery, causing the plaintiff to suffer from sulphuric acid poisoning; the defendant did not comply with the statute; the court said: "If the fumes were poisonous and plaintiff was injured thereby, without negligence on his part, he was entitled to recover, as the provisions of the statute are mandatory. ***" 

The purchase of intoxicating liquor, which was poison, resulted in the case of Dolan v. Burke, 89 Superior Court 

295. It was held that plaintiff's violation of law in purchasing, did not bar recovery unless such violation of law was an efficient cause of the injury. Plaintiff can recover for the unintentional drinking of poison furnished either "knowingly or negligently by one purporting to sell him a drink fit for beverage purposes and harmful only because of its alcoholic content."

The judgment for defendant was reversed and recovery allowed.

This case illustrates the doctrine set forth in many cases, that there must be a causal connection, between the statute violated and the injury. 

In Snyder v. Penna. Railroad Co., 285 Pa. 619, the defendant had failed to equip its train with airbrakes as required by statute; the engineer saw cattle along the track but did not slow down, the train was wrecked and the fireman killed therein. It was held that there was no causal relationship between the violation of the statute and plaintiff damage in that the absence of the airbrake equipment in no way contributed to the accident. 

Under the rule that the violation of a statute must be the proximate cause in order to warrant the inference of negligence, driving to the left of the center of the road when making a left turn at an intersection did not in Hayes v. Schomaker, 302 Pa. 72, contribute to the accident, and therefore did not bar plaintiff from recovering, even if the statute was violated.

Judgment for plaintiff was affirmed in the case of 

Laubach v. Colley, 283 Pa. 366, where a father permitted his son under sixteen years of age to operate an automobile in violation of the Act of 1919; the father was held liable for the injury caused by the negligence of his son irrespective of whether the son was at the time engaged in his personal business; the court said, however: "The injury must, of course, have some causal connection with the improper conduct encouraged," 

quoting Scorsoni v. Pittsburgh Provision and Packing 

Co., 272 Pa. 253. The Scorsoni case emphasizes the importance of this rule because there, a collision occurred between two trucks one of which was driven by plaintiff's son who was under 18 years of age and without a license. As a result of the collision the father was killed; plaintiff claimed the accident was the result of the negligence of defendant's driver; the jury having found that it was caused by negligence of defendant's driver, it followed the minority of plaintiff's son bore no causal relation to the occurrence.

If automobile lights do not comply with the law, such failure is not necessarily a bar to recovery: Lane v. 

Mullen E. A., Inc., 285 Pa. 161. Here plaintiff said he was driving an automobile with dimmers which carried but twenty-eight to thirty feet ahead, while the law required clear illumination of substantial objects two hundred feet ahead. Defendant's steam shovel was parked without lights and plaintiff saw it; he turned to avoid it but struck a projecting coal box on the side of the shovel, which was fifty inches above the ground; "therefore, the state of his lights as to their disclosure ability did not cause him to strike the projection." and plaintiff was entitled to recover.

In Lloyd v. Noakes, 96 Superior Court 164, the plaintiff was a guest, injured by defendant's negligence in driving an automobile; defendant only had a learner's permit and the trip was not for instruction. The court held plaintiff was not barred from recovery because of

NEGLIGENCE, PROOF OF-STATUTORY VIOLATIONS 

71 defendant's violation of law but that plaintiff was barred if he knew, and it was for the jury to find, that defendant was an incompetent driver.

Driving on the left side of a road, in violation of law, does not establish negligence, unless the position on this side was the efficient cause of the collision: Siglin v. 

Haiges, 95 Superior Court 588; here a child was coasting on an alley and approached a highway where it was struck by a truck traveling on the left side of the highway at a speed of from twenty to thirty-five miles per hour; the driver had no reason to anticipate the child's presence; the defendant was held not liable. 

Unlawful speed of an automobile, unless the excessive speed is the proximate cause of the injury, does not impose liability: Stubbs v. Edwards, 260 Pa. 75.

As to mining companies, the same, rule has been applied. In Christner v. Cumberland & Elk Lick Coal Co., 146 Pa. 67, where a coal miner was injured by a fall of roof slate, the liability alleged was from the failure to have a certified mine boss, and also a stretcher, at the mine. It was held that these violations of the law did not warrant a recovery because the injury was not occasioned by them.

The principle barred the plaintiff, in Floyd v. Paulton 

Coal Mining Co., 94 Superior Court 1; here the plaintiff, a fire boss, in making his mine examination entered the mine, in violation of the Act of 1911, carrying an open carbide lamp instead of an improved safety lamp. There could be no recovery because of this violation of law and it only need be proved by the preponderance of evidence, and not beyond a reasonable doubt. 

The various laws relating to an employer and an employee have brought into the court cases involving a violation of the statutes and a consequent injury to an employee: Stehle v. Jaeger Automatic Machine Co., 220 

Pa. 617. Trespass was brought for personal injuries to a boy under fourteen years of age employed contrary to the Act of 1925 to clean and oil machinery while in motion; his employment in violation of law was held to be evidence of negligence and there was liability on the

employer for an injury which resulted by reason of the prohibited employment. 

In Jones v. American Caramel Co., 225 Pa. 644, there was an unguarded fan. The Act of 1925 requires that "machinery of every description shall be properly guarded."

A workman was injured and a verdict for plaintiff was affirmed. 

An unguarded shaft appears in Lanahan v. Arasapha 

Manufacturing Co., 240 Pa. 292; the entry of a nonsuit was reversed. 

In Kelliher v. Brown & Co., 242 Pa. 499, there was a failure to provide a belt shifter; this resulted in an injury to a workman and a verdict for plaintiff was affirmed.

Krutlies v. Bulls Head Coal Co., 249 Pa. 162, is an illustration of the employment of a miner, which was illegal because he was under age; he was injured and it was held that the illegal employment was sufficient evidence of defendant's negligence, and if the injury was occasioned in the course of plaintiff's service under such unlawful employment, that was enough to show causal connection; even when the minor has made a false statement as to his age and although the defendant had used reasonable diligence to ascertain his age. 

Where a father admits knowledge of his son's employment in violation of the Act of 1915, the father is not entitled to recover: Pinter v. James Barker Inc., 272 

Pa. 541.

The illegal employment, so the court finally says in 

Faiola v. Calderone, 275 Pa. 303, constitutes negligence per se.

The Safety Appliance Act of Congress was before our courts in Sims v. Penna. Railroad Co., 279 Pa. 111, where a brakeman was injured because an automatic coupler failed to work. The Act of Congress required the railroad to equip its cars with safety appliances and to

Our Appellate Courts have construed various acts for the purpose of carrying out the intention of the Legislature. The Child Labor Act of 1915, prohibits minors under 18 years of age from operating hoisting machines.

In Williams v. Rheas, Inc., 99 Superior Court 438, this prohibition was held to include elevators, and since such employment was prohibited the minor plaintiff could maintain trespass for personal injuries caused by a defect in the elevator he was running, and was not concluded by the Workmen's Compensation Act. 

A crane was held to be a hoisting machine in Lincoln v. 

National Tube Co., 268 Pa. 504, and since employment of a minor for that purpose was prohibited by statute, a contract of employment was illegal, and trespass was an available remedy. 

In King v. Darlington Brick & Mining Co., 284 Pa. 277, the Workmen's Compensation Act did not control because the minor was expressly directed by defendant's foreman to work at unguarded machinery; the defendant was liable in trespass for violation of the statute. 

A contrary conclusion was reached in Johnson v. Endura Mfg. Co., 282 Pa. 322; the general employment of the minor was proper and legal, some incidental services which he rendered were illegal, to-wit, the oiling of machinery, but the accident did not occur while he was so engaged. Applying the rule that the violation of a penal statute may constitute negligence per se, but that no right of action arises therefrom, unless such wrongdoing is the proximate cause of the injury, the court reached the conclusion that the defendant was not liable. 

The most recent case on the subject is Welsch v. Pittsburgh Terminal Coal Co., 303 Pa. 405; here the plaintiff, an adult employee, brought trespass to recover for injury received when he was caught in the unguarded gears of an electric pump, which gears by law were required to be guarded. It was contended that this violation by the

employer took plaintiff out of the Workmen's Compensation Law upon the theory that the contract of hiring was void, or partly void, being contrary to statute. But the court held that the Workmen's Compensation Act applies to all accidents, and affirmed judgment for the defendant.

Illustrating the rule that the violation of a statute is at least evidence of negligence, and where its violation is the proximate cause of the injury, nothing but the contributory negligence of the plaintiff will relieve from liability, we find Drake v. Fenton, 237 Pa. 8, in which a city fireman, for the purpose of extinguishing a fire, in the performance of his duty, entered a storage warehouse at night, owned and occupied by the defendant, and while on the third floor fell through an elevator shaft and was injured; it appeared there was no guard or gate at the elevator shaft, which was required by law. 

The court in affirming judgment for plaintiff said: "It is a reasonable construction to hold that it (the act) was passed for the benefit of all persons lawfully on the premises, and that as to them it creates a duty, the breach of which may be actionable negligence."

The fire escape provision of the Act of 1897, came before the Supreme Court in Kohn v. Clark, 236 Pa. 18.

Here there was a fire escape but whether it was adequate provision considering the whole situation, was a question for the jury. It was claimed by defendant that plaintiff's failure to escape was due to his want of familiarity with the means of escape. The court held that it was not the lack of familiarity with the means of escape, but the inadequacy of the means, and reversed the judgment for defendant.

Plaintiff's husband having died from eating diseased pork, which was originally sold by defendant to a dealer and by him to plaintiff's family, an action was brought to recover damages for his death: Catani v. Swift & Co., 251 Pa. 52; the Act of 1889 provides there “shall be an implied contract or undertaking that the goods or mer chandise are sound and fit for household consumption,

75 where the same are sold. The entry of a nonsuit was reversed, the court holding there was a statutory as well as a common law liability. 

This case was followed by Tavani v. Swift & Co., 262 

Pa. 184, where the plaintiff ate diseased pork, containing trichinæ. Defendant showed there was no negligence by proving there was no trustworthy test, and that it had used due care. In discussing the difference between the two cases, the Supreme Court said, in the Catani case plaintiff made out a prima facie case by proving that the pork sold by defendant was diseased, and caused the injury, without showing that the defendant was aware that the pork was unfit for food; such case not overcome by the showing by defendant that the meat had been inspected by the United States Government. In the Tavani case the evidence was sufficient to sustain a verdict of the jury that the defendant was not negligent, the action being trespass for negligence and not for breach of warranty.

CHAPTER XVI STORES, THEATERS, PLACES FOR BUSINESS, AMUSEMENT AND PUBLIC RESORTS 

While the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur does not apply, and there must be affirmative proof of negligence, the proprietor of a grocery store who permits fat meat and vegetables to remain on the steps of his store, although his attention is frequently called to this without result, and a customer slips on the refuse and is injured, may be liable for negligence; these were the facts in Markman v. Bell Stores Co., 285 Pa. 378, where a verdict for plaintiff was affirmed.

Again, the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur does not apply where there was a fall upon steps inside a grocery store; this fall was caused by wet and crushed spinach on the upper step, and it was shown that an hour before, men carrying open baskets of spinach went up the stairs; there was no proof that any spinach dropped from the baskets; the steps were poorly lighted and the treads were smooth; these facts did not warrant an inference of negligence and the entry of a nonsuit is affirmed: 

Gorman v. Brahms Sons, 298 Pa. 142. 

Another store case, where a customer slipped on a step at a store entrance, in which it is asserted that the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur does not apply, is Bell v. 

A. & P. Tea Co., 288 Pa. 160; here a judgment for plaintiff was reversed, since it was not shown the proprietor had notice, actual or constructive, of the alleged negligent or dangerous condition.

Oil on a store floor, can not successfully invoke the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur: DiMarco v. Cupp Grocery 

Co., 88 Superior Court 449: "The doctrine of res ipsa loquitur does not apply to such a case. No liability attached to the defendant from the mere happening of the accident; it was necessary to prove some specific default or negligence on its part causing plaintiff's fall and injury, 76

or the existence of conditions so obviously dangerous."

The same rule was applied in our Supreme Court, see 

Spickernagle v. Woolworth, 236 Pa. 496, where plaintiff slipped on an oiled store floor, and it was held this did not raise a presumption of negligence even if plaintiff was lawfully on the premises; also Diver v. Singer Manufacturing Co., 205 Pa. 170, to the same effect. 

In Robb v. Niles-Bement-Pond Company, 269 Pa. 298, sufficient negligence was shown; here a man delivering ice to a factory fell, this being caused by slipping into a pool of oil about two feet in diameter, which had been there five hours; the light was not sufficient to enable the plaintiff to see the oil; judgment for plaintiff was affirmed.

In McLure v. New Castle Dry Goods Company, 93 

Superior Court 606, an allegation that the steps were in "a damp, slippery condition" did not establish negligence even though a scrubwoman was alleged to be cleaning on the second floor, there being no direct testimony that the woman had been cleaning the steps where the plaintiff fell.

Sufficient evidence of negligence appeared in Dalgleish v. Oppenheim, Collins & Co., 302 Pa. 88, where it appeared the floor of the aisle was sloppy and slippery, covered by two rubber mats, whose underside was smooth and covered with a slippery substance; that as plaintiff stepped from the first mat onto the second, the latter slipped and caused her to fall violently to the floor. 

Falling glass injured a customer in a store (Lineweaver v. Wanamaker, John, Phila., 299 Pa. 45), and slight evidence of negligence was all the law required. 

Certain work was being done on and over a skylight and the method of protecting customers was discussed and agreed upon between defendant and defendant's contractor, but no tarpaulin or other guard was placed under the skylight to catch glass if it should fall.

A workman fell from a board which had been so negligently placed that it tipped with his weight, resulting in certain glass being broken, which fell upon plaintiff.

"While we have held that the mere falling of an object from a building does not raise a presumption of negligence against the owner we have also held that in such case only slight evidence is required to shift the burden of proof."

In Chapman v. Clothier, 274 Pa. 394, a worn place in a step in a store caused the fall of a customer; the testimony as to the depth or extent of the defect was not clear, the court referring to it as being one-sixteenth inch deep; the steps were less than three feet in height without a handrail; no inference of negligence could be drawn from these facts.

One of the older cases frequently cited is Huey v. 

Gahlenbeck, 121 Pa. 238, where the plaintiff was visiting a warehouse and something fell and struck him, causing him to fall down the elevator shaft; there was no evidence of negligence and no proof of the exact cause of the injury. The defendant was not liable. 

In Haddon v. Snellenburg & Co., 293 Pa. 333, a customer fell while walking along a balcony leading to a washroom; there was but one step about six inches high. "It is not negligence per se or negligent construction in a store or other public place to have one floor at a lower level by a few inches than another." 

Visitors on an inspection trip to the defendant company's property were entitled to the exercise of ordinary care on the part of the defendant; and where they were being conveyed across a river on a ferry owned by the company, which was used to transport the employees, and the ferry was overcrowded, as a result of which plaintiff's husband was drowned, a recovery was permitted based on the testimony of an experienced river man that the boat could not hold with safety more than six persons whereas there were eleven on it at the time of the accident: Corbin v. Haws Refractories Co., 277 

Pa. 126.

Circumstances warranting the inference of negligence are found in Kapuscianski v. Philadelphia & Reading 

Coal & Iron Company, 289 Pa. 388; defendant operated a colliery and to enlarge its business placed appliances

on its land to encourage sale, providing a road for coal haulers to a building containing coal chutes; plaintiff's son went with a truck along this road and, while waiting, was struck by a coupling hook, an instrument used about the colliery to clear the breaker of rubbish; a moment before, defendant's employee had it, and another employee saw it passing through the air, hitting plaintiff's son; no one saw it thrown, but "it could be inferred from the circumstances related, that an employee carelessly threw the iron too far."

Strawberries with tacks in them do not warrant the inference of negligence, apparently, as set forth in Lieber v. Horn & Hardart Baking Co., Inc., 10 D. & C. 362. Here the defendant maintained a public restaurant into which the plaintiff went and ordered strawberries. There was a tack in them such as used in making berry baskets. As to whether "the presence of the tack, in and of itself (is) evidence of negligence," the court said: "Therefore, it seems to us the fact that the plaintiff got this tack in her jaw when eating the strawberries, which at this season of the year, May, must have come from a distance, would not in and of itself be evidence of negligence on the part of the defendant." 

A close case is Sidwell v. Gimbel Bros., 52 Superior

Court 286; here the plaintiff, a head saleswoman, in the upholstering department, while at a checking desk, was suddenly struck by a roll of linoleum, which toppled over; there was a row of these linoleum rolls, each weighing from twenty-five to two hundred pounds, without any support by rail or guard and along an aisle frequently used. "Was this due care under the circumstances, was it a reasonable protection to persons who were rightfully using this aisle to allow these heavy rolls with uneven bases, and subject to jar of passers-by or trucks, or the vibration of the floor, to remain without superadded support to their weight? In other words, was the upsetting of such a roll a fact ‘reasonably to be anticipated' from the general knowl-

edge of natural law. This is not a question of law, but purely one of fact, and the evidence adduced comes up to the standard required by the above decisions."

Judgment for plaintiff was affirmed. 

The proprietor of a hair shop was sued by a customer, alleging that she, while receiving a permanent wave, suffered serious electric burns. 

The lower court nonsuited; this was error, and in reversing the Supreme Court applied the rule of very slight proof of negligence, due to the fact that defendant had control of the entire process and found the evidence of negligence in the fact that one of the wires was accidentally moved and almost immediately the plaintiff felt the burn, that the attendant should have remained constantly in the room, which was not done, that the current should not have been applied for a longer period than fifteen minutes, and the attendant was unable to state how long it was applied: Lesick et ux. v. Proctor, 300 

Pa. 347, 350.¦ "Not the mere fact of the accident, but the circumstances under which it occurred may give rise to a presumption of negligence. The circumstances here were sufficient to send the case to a jury, and hence, it is unnecessary to decide whether the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur is applicable."

In McSorley v. Katz, 53 Superior Court 243, a customer purchased at a grocery store, operated by defendant, a can of molasses and was assured that "it's perfectly safe and all right." On opening it at home there was an explosion, as plaintiff termed it, injuring plaintiff. In holding that the defendant, who said he handled the can of molasses as an article of merchandise, and having no knowledge or reason to suspect that it was anything other than indicated by the manufacturer's label, was not liable, the court said: "It was not the happening of an accident with the means of prevention under control of the defendant, but under the evidence it was due to a latent, un-

suspected cause, in the handling of a trade article, and as capable of inspection and examination by the one party as the other.' 

The same rule is applied to the proprietor of a drug store in West v. Emanuel, 198 Pa. 180; a girl twenty years old, plaintiff's daughter, purchased from defendant a "Kohler's Headache Powder" and five hours after taking the powder died apparently from the effects of it; this powder was a proprietary medicine, sold in packages to drug stores. The court held that in the sales of patent or proprietary medicines the druggist is not required to analyze the contents of each bottle of package he receives, and if he delivers to the consumer the article called for, with the label of the proprietary or patentee upon it, he can not be justly charged with negligence in so doing. 

Defendants were liable in Butterfield v. Snellenburg & 

Co., 231 Pa. 88, where the plaintiff asked the drug clerk in defendant's general store for some aromatic spirits of ammonia and upon drinking the dose was immediately poisoned and burned. There was no direct evidence that what she took was anything other than what she had ordered, but a physician did testify that the injuries could have been produced if the dose of aromatic spirits of ammonia was not sufficiently diluted. "Certainly this gave rise to a presumption of negligence on the part of the clerk who prepared and administered the dose; or, speaking more correctly, it was a circumstance amounting to evidence, from which the jury would be warranted in inferring such negligence."

The Supreme Court has declined to apply the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur to a case where a minor purchased a ticket to a movie and upon taking her seat the left side gave way causing her to be thrown to the floor; her testimony being that the seat "broke," Durning v. Hyman, 286 Pa. 376. The court saying: 

"Though the facts here disclosed do not permit the application of the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur, yet, if it is shown a dangerous condition was per-

mitted to exist, the one responsible is liable for the natural and probable consequences resulting, and but slight evidence is sufficient to meet the burden of proof placed on the person injured. * * *"'

In this case, the plaintiff entered the moving picture house and placed herself upon a seat provided for the use of patrons. It broke and caused the fall. The duty devolved upon the proprietor to show that reasonable diligence had been used to provide safe accommodations, and that a proper inspection was made to assure their suitable condition. 

Sellmer v. Ringling, 62 Superior Court 410, is a case where a woman was hurt in leaving her seat in a circus by having her foot caught between two boards as she was descending; one gave way and the other overlapped, catching her foot; there was no stringer to support the boards. The court held that while the facts did not give rise to a presumption of negligence, in the absence of an explanation by defendant they were sufficient to show negligence; that the proprietor of the circus while not an insurer, nevertheless impliedly warranted the stand safe for the purposes intended, save only as to defects unseen, unknown and undiscoverable; despite defendant's general evidence of care the question did not become one of law but was for the jury. 

The injury to plaintiff in Hardy v. Phila. National 

League Club, 99 Superior Court 326, was caused by the collapse of a grandstand, in a baseball park owned by the defendant, during a game. It was shown that the stand fell because a jointed spruce beam had rotted at a point over a post, the rotting being caused by seeping water. The stand had been erected fifteen years and the life of spruce timber was claimed to be from two to four years; the negligence alleged was the lack of inspection. 

There was sufficient evidence of negligence to go to the jury.

Another moving picture theater case is found in Rutherford v. Academy of Music, 87 Superior Court 355, in which the plaintiff was injured by falling down a flight of steps which she reached after going to a third floor

where she was directed by an usher to go to a certain corridor and in so doing she fell down a short flight of steps, which, because of insufficient lighting, she could not see. The case was for the jury upon the negligence of the defendant in failing to provide sufficient light. 

A step tread, broken off for the entire width, so that it was only half its usual depth, caused a child to fall in the case of Leckstein v. Morris, 80 Superior Court 352, the defendant being a proprietor of a moving picture theater. The break had existed for six days, sufficient time to give constructive notice; judgment for plaintiff was affirmed.

Employees of a theater must not throw a patron over a gallery rail to the first floor to the injury of another patron there, under peril of having the theater proprietor held liable, because the injury to the plaintiff "was the natural and probable consequence of such an act and one which the servant should have foreseen." Rice v. Gibson, 94 Superior Court 541.

In James v. Smith, 93 Superior Court 485, it was shown plaintiff had bought a ticket to a moving picture theater and asked to be directed to a toilet room. In following the directions plaintiff went to a door which opened into a dimly lighted stairway, took one step and fell into the cellar. Judgment for plaintiff was affirmed. 

While in a public bathing establishment, called a "Russian Bathroom," plaintiff got up from the bench and in so doing a piece of splinter went under his thumbnail. 

No evidence was offered as to the condition of the bench either before or after the alleged accident, and in reversing judgment for the plaintiff, Kline v. Kratchman, 87 Superior Court 463, the court said: "The burden was upon the plaintiff to show not only that he had been injured but that the injury had resulted from the negligence of the defendant. Did the fact that when plaintiff went to get up and supported himself with his thumb a splinter of wood ran under the thumbnail, warrant a finding of negligence upon the part of the defendant? One who conducts a business establishment, while required to use

ordinary care to keep his place in a reasonably safe condition so as not to expose his customers to unnecessary dangers, is not liable for accidents they may therein sustain, except upon proof of his default."

Liability of hotel proprietors is discussed in Lyttle v. 

Denny, 222 Pa. 395, where the top of a heavy folding bed fell forward and down upon a guest while he was lying quietly upon the bed; under the doctrine of exclusive control, etc., it was held the burden of proof was on the defendant.

Benjamin v. Colonial Hotel Co., 268 Pa. 459, was an action by a guest against the hotelkeeper for the loss of money alleged to be due to defendant's negligence. The money had been put in the hotel vault and was missing when the plaintiff went to get it. The court said, if the jury found plaintiff had put his money in a vault provided by defendant for that purpose which was protected by a double lock, that the box was locked, and that it could not be unlocked except by the help of a master key, which was in defendant's possession, and that it was found unlocked later, that would make out a prima facie case. Reference was made in the opinion to Hoyt v. 

Clinton Hotel Co., 35 Superior Court 297. 

Rose v. Adelphia Hotel, 300 Pa. 1, is a case where a guest fell while stepping upon a platform in a dining room. There was no proof of what caused the plaintiff to fall, and the circumstances were not of a character to justify an inference of negligence.

A man, guest of a hotel, was awakened at night by the hotel being on fire and rushed to the window leading to the fire escape, which window could not be opened because of a rusty latch; he then went back to his room, where, in attempting to descend a fire ladder which had been run up to his room, he fell and was killed. Judgment for plaintiff was affirmed, it being held the negligence of defendant in not affording an unobstructed access to the fire escape was for the jury: Ritchey v. 

Cassone, 296 Pa. 249.

The liability of a bank to its customers is discussed and applied in Sinn v. Farmers Deposit Savings Bank, 300 Pa. 85, where a depositor entered a bank on business and was injured by the explosion of a bomb; the case was for the jury because it was shown the bomber had threatened to explode a bomb if he was not given money, of which threat the officers of the bank knew, but gave the plaintiff no warning, athough, according to plaintiff, twenty minutes elapsed. Plaintiff was an invitee and the bank owed him the same duty as a shopkeeper owes his customer, which is to use reasonable care for his protection.

CHAPTER XVII EXPLOSIONS 

In Derry Coal & Coke Co. v. Kerbaugh, 222 Pa. 448, houses were damaged by the explosion of dynamite and blasting powder. It appeared several tons of dynamite were kept by defendant in a small building in which there was also a stove used to thaw out the frozen dynamite. 

Shortly before the explosion the stove was shown to have been red hot; the dynamite was from ten inches to two feet away from the stove. A few seconds before the explosion the fireman in charge of the building was seen running as though to warn workmen. The court held that the highest degree of care was incumbent upon defendant, but the case was not left to the jury to find negligence "from the mere fact of the explosion. A condition, entirely under the control of the defendant, was shown, to which as a producing cause the explosion might be attributed."

The evidence showed that the red hot stove being close to the dynamite and to explosive caps might have caused the explosion. 

Sigmund v. McAbee Powder & Oil Co., 39 Superior

Court 559, is an explosion case where there was evidence of negligence in that boxes of dynamite cartridges were carelessly nailed, undue force being used, and then they were thrown into a pile which involved unnecessary concussion. It was shown that dynamite was scattered over the floor over which workmen walked in shoes which had iron nails; these methods were dangerous. 

Kiser v. Kerbaugh, H. S., Inc., 40 Superior Court 163, is another case where dynamite was stored in a small building; a hot stove was close by and there were percussion caps lying on the floor. 

Sowers v. McManus, 214 Pa. 244, specifically rules that the mere fact of an explosion does not impose liability; the court said: "The maxim res ipsa loquitur is itself an expression of the exception to the general rule, that negli- 86

In Forster v. There was a stove three feet away. The court held the storage of high explosives in a populated district was not necessarily a nuisance, but when kept or stored in a negligent manner, which created an unnecessary danger, amounting to a menace to life and property, then the jury could determine that the defendants had maintained a nuisance which resulted in destruction of plaintiff's property, even though no negligence was shown. 

Rafferty v. Davis, 260 Pa. 563, is a very interesting explosion case. Here a quarry blasting was carried on by dynamite explosion which cast a rock through the window in plaintiff's house, injuring her. The house was a square and a half away. The violence of the explosion was shown by proving its effect on persons and property. 

The negligence averred was the overcharge of powder. 

The court held the case was within the rule that the result was so far out of the usual course that no fair inference could be drawn of any other cause than negligence, and no other cause was apparent. The court went farther however and held: 

"Another element * * * arises out of the duty which rests upon one, conducting blasting operations upon his own property, to do so in such a manner as not to injure others, in person or property."

It was held that a trespass had occurred and that liability arose without regard to negligence.

One of the older explosion cases, where lamp oil exploded is Elkins Bly & Co. v. McKean, 79 Pa. 493. Suit was brought for the death of plaintiff's husband against a refiner and vendor of lamp oil, which was put in a lamp and was said to have exploded in the husband's hand as he was walking. An explosion occurred injuring plaintiff while he was operating a cement kiln for defendant (See Leonard v.

A gas explosion is the basis of liability in Hartman v.

There was evidence of odor for a long period of time and this fact, and other items, tended to show a leak in existence for a long time and therefore called for an investigation at the hands of a jury. 

A coffee urn exploded and injured plaintiff: Muller v.

Plaintiff on appeal argued that the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur applied, but the opinion, although affirming judgment for plaintiff, bases liability on proven negligence.

CHAPTER XVIII ELECTRICITY-INJURIES FROM 

Injuries from electricity give apt illustrations of the application and the nonapplication of the maxim res ipsa loquitur. 

Devlin v. Beacon Light Co., 198 Pa. 583, applies the maxim in favor of the plaintiff, who stepped on a live electric wire which defendant's workmen had permitted to lie upon the pavement of a main city street. This established a prima facie case which was for the jury until overcome by countervailing proof and whether it was so overcome, was likewise for the jury, and not for the court, even though defendant contended the wire had been disconnected at both ends and could not have been charged with electricity.

A patron of an electric light company was injured as shown in Alexander v. Nanticoke Light Co., 209 Pa. 571, by handling an electric lamp furnished by defendant, whereby he was severely shocked. The presumption was that defendant was negligent and this presumption placed upon defendant the burden of showing the contrary. 

The electric current from a telephone on the premises in which deceased lived killed him; the rule of res ipsa loquitur was held to apply even though the deadly current was not generated by defendant telephone company: 

Delahunt v. United Telephone & Telegraph Company, 215 Pa. 241. 

In Long v. St. Clair Borough, 253 Pa. 92, it was shown that the electrical current was furnished by the municipal lighting plant of defendant to plaintiff's house. Plaintiff's husband went to the cellar of his home, came in contact with a wire or other electrical appliance and was electrocuted. Two days later it was discovered that the high and low tension wires of the borough had come into contact due to the rotting of some pins, and of this condition the Borough had no notice. Nevertheless a presumption of negligence arose and was not so clearly over- 91

come by defendant's evidence as to require the case to be taken from the jury.

Where the electrical appliances in the house are shown to have been furnished by the plaintiff and not by the defendant, and are not under its control, nevertheless the maxim still applies; but in this case the jury was instructed that if there were defects in the house appliances which contributed to plaintiff's injury, the verdict should be for defendant. Lynch v. Meyersdale Electric 

Light, Heat & Power Co., 268 Pa. 337.

Plaintiff's building was burnt (Derrick v. Harwood 

Electric Co., 268 Pa. 136), by an excessive current entering his premises on the defendant's wire; while the electric company is not an insurer the burden is on the company to offer such explanation as will relieve it of responsibility. Under such circumstances, the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur applies, but only as based on a finding that the fire resulted from a high voltage current. 

See also Moyer v. Blue Mountain Electric Co., 294 Pa. 265.

Circumstances giving rise to an inference of negligence appear in two cases. In Sorrell v. Titusville Electric 

Traction Co., 23 Superior Court 425, a derrick blew down throwing uninsulated trolley and telephone wires to the ground; the current was not turned off for more than an hour after notice, whereby horses were killed; the case was for the jury. 

In St. Clair v. Edison Electric Light Co., 38 Superior

Court 228, an explosion lifted up the surface of a public street, on which plaintiff was walking, and injured him. 

The explosion took place in an electric conduit of defendant, in which a fire had been burning for four hours, and the electric current had not been turned off. Moreover a manhole was open, admitting air, contrary to the practice in smothering a fire. The defendant being in exclusive control, and the result being so far out of the usual course in such circumstances, an inference of negligence arose.

The defendant bank, in Whaley v. Citizens National 

Bank, 28 Superior Court 531, undertook to drive people away from the front of its building, by charging a brass rail, which projected into the street, with electricity. This

93 was done by a battery and a generator, located in a banking room, which was at all times under the control of defendant's servant. The plaintiff alleged that he stopped in front of the bank to speak to a friend, and without any notice of danger placed his hand on the rail and was seriously injured. The janitor immediately before the accident had sprinkled water on the pavement and then entered the building; the wires leading from the battery to the railing had not been disconnected. A presumption of negligence arose and judgment for plaintiff was affirmed.

The defendant's negligence is established by circumstantial evidence in Morris v. Jefferson Electric Co., 278

Pa. 361, where a boy was shocked by coming into contact with a broken wire.

The fact that a wire was installed close to a building permitting the insulation material to wear off, and without fastening the wire to prevent it sagging, was enough to take the case to the jury: Fitzgerald v. Edison Electric Illuminating Co., 207 Pa. 118.

For a case where negligence was shown prima facie, but the plaintiff barred from recovery because of decedent's contributory negligence, see Haertel v. Penna. Light & Power Company, 219 Pa. 640.

A man using a telephone during a thunderstorm was shocked and injured and it was held the rule of res ipsa loquitur did not apply: Rocap v. Bell Telephone Co., 230 Pa. 597. Failure to place warnings against the use of a telephone during a storm is not evidence of negligence, and proof that the best known devices had been used, required the court to give binding instructions for the defendant.

Lightning, claimed to have been carried along connected telephone wires, killed plaintiff's husband: Sinkovich v. Bell Telephone Co., 286 Pa. 427. Affirmative proof must be given, in order to warrant a recovery, that the negligence of the defendant concurred with the act of God, and effectively contributed to the accident. The fact that an arrestor was not provided, where it appears that the bolt of lightning left the wires before it would have reached the arrestor, which therefore would not have been a safeguard, is not a ground of liability.

The duty of care resting upon manufacturers brings up the maxim res ipsa loquitur. 

The late case on the subject in Pennsylvania is Rozumailski v. Phila. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 296 Pa. 114.

Here a manufacturer of Coca-Cola, who did the bottling, and then sold a bottle to a restaurant keeper, who, in turn, opened it and immediately served it to a customer, was held liable to the customer who was injured by broken glass which was within the bottle: "It is a case where the accident proves its own negligent cause, and the jury would be permitted to infer negligence, as the court below instructed it, from the fact that ground glass had been found in the bottom of the bottle.”

A manufacturer of ice cream was held liable to the customer of a restaurant for an injury to him occasioned by glass in the ice cream in Minutilla v. Providence Ice

Supreme Court), and thoroughly annotated, covering drugs and medicines, foods, beverages and tobacco, oils, explosive substances, automobiles, and weapons; the note also deals with the theory of liability based upon false representations, and also of implied warranty. 94

Where a pedestrian steps upon the lid of a coal hole which tilts under her weight, the evidence being that immediately before an employee of a coal dealer, defendant, had delivered a load of coal through this hole, although negligence will not be inferred, from the mere happening of the accident, the fact that the lid tilted is a circumstance corroborating the evidence that it had not been properly replaced: Martin v. Letter, 282 Pa. 286: 

"The evidence warranted a finding that the movement of the lid resulted from its improper replacement, caused by leaving particles of fine coal and coal dust in the flange, thereby keeping the lid from its normal condition. The accident happened in less than one minute after defendant's servant had left the place, and the evidence of his failure to safely replace the lid, which he had removed, justified the jury's finding that defendant was negligent." 

The Martin case was affirmed as to the liability of the coal dealer, in Vendig v. Union League of Philadelphia, 291 Pa. 536, where it is said: "When a coal dealer delivers coal through an opening in the footway of a public street, he is liable to persons injured by his negligence, if he replaces the lid so that it is unsafe, or does not replace it at all."

In the Vendig case the property owner was held to be in a different position: "He has a right to suppose that the employees of the coal dealer will not negligently replace the lid. 

If he knows of the dangerous condition of the footway, following the delivery of the coal, he must be swift to make it safe; but he can not be held liable if he has had neither actual nor constructive notice of that fact." 95

In an earlier case, Strohm v. Haverstick, 44 Superior 

Court 166, the suit was against the owner to recover for personal injuries sustained by a fall into a coal hole on the sidewalk, and from the proof by defendant that on account of the construction no misplacement of it could happen by reason of a footpassenger stepping on it, and the proof of the plaintiff that when he stepped upon it it did slip and left the hole uncovered, "The jury might fairly infer that, at the moment of the accident, some foreign substance had found its way to the shoulder of the rim thus preventing the iron covering from resting on its accustomed bed. Such an inference was not only invited but was almost impelled by the facts proven. It could not be fairly classified as a mere conjecture or speculation unsupported by evidence."

An employee of a brewing company delivered beer by taking off the lid of a hole in the sidewalk and dropping the barrels in the cellar, after which, for a short time he went into the hotel, to complete the delivery, during which time the plaintiff in walking on the sidewalk fell through the hole; the Brewing Company was held liable: Powell v. Wilhelm, 49 Superior Court 268.

The Supreme Court has quite definitely held that with regard to this class of cases the principle of res ipsa loquitur does not apply. (Bryan v. Barber Asphalt Co., 289 Pa. 123.) The plaintiff was injured by reason of a defect in a plank laid by a municipal contractor at a public crossing. Other than stating that the plank looked like an "old board" and that other planks had cracked but with no evidence that the split was an old one, there was not sufficient positive evidence of negligence, and commenting upon that situation the court said: "Apparently recognizing that, upon the basis stated, a recovery could not be had, plaintiff sought, in effect, to apply the principle of res ipsa loquitur. It is without relevancy to the present controversy. ***""

CHAPTER XXI BRIDGES 

The rule of res ipsa loquitur does not apply to a case where the driver of a truck was injured while passing over a highway bridge in Pittsburgh, and an iron beam fell through the top of the truck, striking the plaintiff: 

Zehner v. Pittsburgh, 279 Pa. 168. There was no evidence as to where the iron came from or that it was part of the bridge or used in connection therewith. "We have held over and over that a municipality does not insure the personal safety of those who use its highways. To sustain an action for damages, the testimony must show affirmatively the municipality has been guilty of some neglect, so connected with the accident as to make it the proximate cause of the injury. This burden was on appellant, as the rule res ipsa loquitur does not apply."

Negligence need not be established by direct proof, but may be shown by circumstantial evidence, but the inference must be one which can reasonably be drawn and which excludes other causes. In Sumey v. Fayette 

County, 298 Pa. 93, where a county bridge collapsed under a heavy threshing machine a compulsory nonsuit was properly entered, no latent or patent defect having been shown, and where it appeared there was no fault in the inspection, care or maintenance of the bridge.

Negligence can not be predicated upon the failure of an owner to inspect a structure, such as a bridge, where an inspection would have disclosed no defects or dangers, except those which were plainly known and appreciated by the persons using the structure or bridge: Freedom 

Oil Works Co. v. Beaver County, 298 Pa. 174.

The "proper inspection," required by law, does not go so far as to require the removal of the flooring of a bridge unless there is something to arouse suspicion of a latent defect: Knopf v. Delaware County, 292 Pa. 237.

In this case the bridge collapsed, killing plaintiff's hus- 98

This defect was not reported to the county but was concealed in the course of the repairs. The county was not liable, knowledge of the employees of an independent contractor not being knowledge of the municipality. 

A defect, in Gehringer v. Lehigh County, 231 Pa. 497, caused the collapse of a bridge precipitating a truck into the stream below, whereby plaintiff's husband was killed, although this defect was not visible from the outside. 

There was evidence the Commissioners had been informed the bridge was out of repair, and also that the inspection made by the Commissioners was most casual. 

A verdict for plaintiff was affirmed; the evidence "was adequate to sustain the conclusion that the use of the roads by vehicles of the character of the motor truck in question had become so usual in the vicinity that the Commissioners must have had knowledge of that fact for a sufficient length of time to have acted thereon and made the necessary repairs properly to strengthen the bridge.

A projecting beam of a bridge caused personal injuries to the plaintiff while driving a wagon with a high front seat across it: Levine v. Pittsburgh, 252 Pa. 181. The plaintiff was unfamiliar with the locality. On seeing the beam he bent forward as far as he could, but was caught by it. There was evidence that the beam at this place had caught a number of wagons before this time.

Where the trial court placed upon the County Commissioners "a measure of accountability so strict as practically to make the County an insurer against accident happening to persons using the County bridges," a reversal was ordered: Childs v. Crawford County, 176 Pa. 139. 

A railroad bridge collapsed causing the death of a brakeman employed by the company: McGuire v. Lehigh

There was nothing to go to the jury and a verdict for plaintiff was reversed.
