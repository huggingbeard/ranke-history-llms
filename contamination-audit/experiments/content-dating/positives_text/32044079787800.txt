Universities and the Public Service By Fritz Morstein Marx 

N ATIONAL institutions, if they be durable, must meet both material and ideological needs. Their purpose must stand out in the practical experience of man. It is equally important that they harmonize with the fundamental tenets underlying the constitutional order. Pragmatic utility alone is not sufficient. Allegiance does not spring from utility. 

To enlist the loyalties of the people, political institutions must be able to serve as community symbols. On this point, the past affords us rich illustration. We cannot safely discard it. 

During the past fifty years, American public opinion has wrestled with the organizational problems posed by the natural decline of amateur government and the increasing complexity of social control. The adjustments worked out in these five decades were not saluted with popular enthusiasm. They were concessions to the demands of a new era, grudgingly made. The passage in 1883 of the 

Pendleton Act establishing a federal civil service exemplified the mixture of bewilderment and hesitant experimentation. The law produced a torso. Abroad constructive effort centered on effective recruitment for the ranking administrative positions. The Pendleton Act left the top structure of the federal hierarchy outside its scope. So it has remained. 

In Europe, the university man is attracted into the government service by the promise of a career that carries with it considerable prestige. With us there is no similar opportunity. The paradox points to an unresolved ideological dilemma. Slogans such as "economy and efficiency" do not penetrate to the core of this dilemma. We must reach deeper.

In its formative period, modern democracy rested its case on the belief that progress could be achieved only under a government fully responsive to the interplay of social forces. Democracy was to be a cooperative endeavor. Man's value was to be measured in terms of his cooperative contribution. Freedom was to release his cooperative energies. Democracy's dedication to cooperation would give direction to policy, temper individual pursuit, and condition the use of liberty. 

A way of life rather than a set of devices, a dynamic orientation rather than a static formula, democracy depends for its success on a constant revitalization of its cooperative purpose in the outlook, attitude, and conduct of man. Freedom invoked for any other purpose becomes obstruction. Disorganized government, kept ineffectual in the name of liberty, is a denial of democracy. Frustration would be a more serious threat to democracy than any willful act of office. holders. 

It would seem to follow that no believer in democracy can permit himself the inconsistency of indifference toward the operative aspects of democracy. Obviously, this involves today a far broader sector of community action than it did in the earlier stages of our constitutional development. No other nation has seen. more sprawling growth and more rapid change during the past five generations. 

Let us admit, then, that our present mode of national existence has little in common with the "American way" of 

1787 or that of 1829 or even the "American way" of the eighties and nineties. 

Let us admit also that the "expansion of government activities" has increasingly transformed the United States into a vast service establishment. Shall we bemoan it? Does not this evolution testify to the strength of democracy's creative energies? Does it not evince that sensitiveness of our political system to popular demands which is the very essence of representative government? Ever since 

Alexander Hamilton's initial steps toward making the republic safe for business, regulatory power has been exercised to provide security for investment. There are many reasons, among them the negative attitude of labor leaders, to explain the fact that we were the last among the great democracies to adopt a social security program for the common man, whose purchasing power, after all, is the basis of our domestic market. Having reached this point on the road toward a still "more perfect union," we are not in a position to leave the promise of social security unredeemed. A breakdown of our administrative machinery might well have fateful consequences. Too much of the substance of democracy, as it affects the man in the street, would be at stake. 

Too much of it is intimately bound up with the administrative competency of our system of government. 

Few will deny that administratively our system of government is underequipped. Many feel that the degree of its under-equipment is alarming. Surely,

there is ground for grave concern when legislative decision is being invalidated, wholly or partly, by administrative weakness. There was a time when the American people could take such incidents of administrative collapse in their stride.

The time is no longer. It is perhaps the most pressing issue of our era to reconstruct the cadres of our administrative services so as to make them fully effective instruments of government. In proceeding along this line we cannot confine ourselves to "keeping the spoilsmen out" or selecting the bulk of our minor officials in accordance with merit standards. 

The vitally significant task is to create within the public service a body of broadly trained administrators, men of social grasp and intellectual alertness, capable of seeking out the best methods of transforming a legislative mandate into administrative practice.

Specifically, what would be the function of these administrators? It may be assumed that their function would not be fundamentally different from that of the higher administrative staffs abroad. It would be a dual function, then. On the one hand, theirs would be an organizational task: to supply the ignition effect within the administrative unit, to outline the objectives of administrative action, to allocate responsibilities, to exercise general supervision and control, and to inspire team work by providing administrative leadership. Closely connected herewith would be, on the other hand, their contribution to the formulation of policy. Their administrative knowledge and experience, their first-hand observation of statutory inadequacies and deficiencies, their familiarity with the social and economic problems bearing upon their branch of administration-all this would place them in a key position to present for political decision defined alternatives for carrying out the government's program. As such, they would act as advisers and administrative planners, evolving rational formulae in an attempt to synthesize, within the confines indicated by political direction, the one-sided claims of different group interests. Largescale industrial management in its best examples avails itself of essentially the same technique in moderating between its investors, its workers, its customers, and the public. One should suppose that government-having at its disposal the facilities of political representation, which industrial management lacks-might do even better. It has done better in other countries. 

It is clear that in speaking of administrators we do not mean technicians. It is the task of the administrator to correlate and coordinate the work of the technicians: the scientists, lawyers, physicians, engineers, and the rest. The recruitment of government technicians presents no particular difficulties. Their professional training provides a reasonable test of selection. But in the absence of a higher administrative career we usually rely upon the technician to substitute for the administrator-as far as the position does not go to an outsider whose title may rest on nothing but "political coverage."

Instead of being himself the master of a specialized skill, the administrator must be able to explore and map avenues of advance and combine the skilled technicians in interrelated projects. He must be able to anticipate developments, to adapt himself to the given framework of general policy, and to maintain administrative momentum.

nized to serve any social purpose at all, is it revolutionary to suggest that as a rule career administrators be recruited from the university level? Fortunately, we have approximated in this country more closely than has England Thomas Jefferson's ideal of "culling from every condition of our people the natural aristocracy of talents and virtue, and of preparing it by education, at the public expense, for the care of the public concerns." Almost exactly a century before Jefferson wrote these lines (in a letter to Joseph C. Cabell, dated January 5, 1815), King Frederick 

William I of Prussia had begun to systematize the merit principle by filling the higher administrative posts, despite the grim resentment of the nobility and the urban patriciate, with men of of "low station," provided that they had "intelligence and could think" (von Verstand und guten Kop). Nor was Jefferson aware of the fact that he narrowly missed plagiarizing Plato-Plato, whom he called "one of the race of genuine sophists" and a "foggy mind" (letter to John 

Adams, of July 5, 1814). Still more remote to him must have been the thought that in formulating the ways and means of "culling the natural aristocracy from every condition of the people" in his 

Virginia Public Education Bill (sec. 42) 

he unwittingly followed the scheme outlined by Confucius in his Li Chi. But for the scholar-bureaucracy of ancient China both higher learning and government office were naturally allied; officials. ("public parents") ruled by the propaganda of their personal example.

Jefferson's plan envisaged a regularized transfer of pupils "of the most sound and promising understanding and character" from one level of instruction to the next higher, beginning with the "ward schools" (county subdivisions) and ending with the university. Confucius held out the same procedure as a tested model, thus linking the lowest level of education, the village schools, with intermediate training centers and the Imperial Academy as the apex (Li Chi, Bk. XVI). The cardinal feature was in either case that talent was drafted in the truest sense; it was not merely accommodated. There was much of the same spirit in the educational innovations of early Prussia. The cost of such drafting had to be met from the public purse. With due tribute to our great sister institutions, the state universities, we cannot say as yet that we have mastered this problem. It is true low family income does not present insurmountable obstacles to academic training. But there is a difference between a general opportunity and an assurance measuring up to real expectancy. I consider the strengthening of this expectancy the paramount issue confronting our educational system. 

Without it, the life lines of democracy are in danger. Recognition of the implications has lately become more widespread, and the general movement of educational policy seems to be in this direction. There are many factors which will accelerate the development. We are bound to progress much further. 

Students of the rise and decline of political elites such as Gaetano Mosca, whose main work will soon be available in an English translation, have made it eminently patent that effective social "circulation" is indispensable for the preservation of any political order. In the long run it must prove disastrous to forego the enrichment of our society by inadequate drafting of the "natural aristocracy of talents and virtue." Of this aristocracy, the graduates of the

Imperial Academy, Confucius said (the translation given here differs somewhat from that by James Legge):

An educated man, familiar both with the appropriate steps toward the attainment of learning and the standards of sound discrimination, is capable of insight. Having acquired insight he can indeed be a teacher. When he can be a teacher, he is qualified for high government office. Being qualified for high government office he is able to be the ruler. Hence it is from the teacher that one learns to be a ruler, and the choice of a teacher must be considered of greatest importance. As it is said in the Record, "The three kings and the four dynasties were what they were by their teachers" (Li Chi., Bk. XVI, 16). 

To design a course of study with the thought of perfecting analytical discipline and broadening intellectual horizons is no unduly perplexing task. We want forward-looking men, not "backwardlooking conquerors of progress." Above all, we want men of social responsiveness, men who do not assume a defeatist attitude in the face of ruthless pressures, men who can sense future social growth. 

From my own experience as an administrator, I should say that I valued most those comrades-in-arms who had at their command the basic data and the methods of the social sciences. I include here the skill and the art of both historical interpretation and scientific inquiry, but not that history which is merely a nostalgic flight into the past or a quest for precedent. Public service is applied patriotism, but I set myself off from notions which I have nowhere found better expressed than in an early report (1891) of a good county superintendent of public instruction in Pennsylvania:

In addition to that which we already taught in our schools, I have recommended vocal and physical training, marching and calisthenics and have emphasized the importance of a thorough knowledge of American history, have endeavored to clinch upon the minds of both pupils and teachers, the self evident proposition, that patriotism feeds upon historical traditions, and have asked that the national airs be sung in all the school rooms of our county and have been largely obeyed in this my modest demand. 

One sometimes encounters allusions to an alleged contrast between learning and action. Can it mean that action must be unwise? Or does it mean that conscientious weighing of all pros and cons inevitably produces deadlock? No doubt the administrator must orient himself toward action. But in my years of administrative work I have rarely observed situations where pressure for action was such that calm inquiry was out of the question. With few exceptions, the administrative process worked differently. 

Ideas would crystallize in consultative gatherings of administrators and their political superiors of cabinet rank; prominent men of the community from different camps would be called in for informal discussion; and slowly there would emerge a tentative program, to be analyzed in its individual aspects by nuclei of experts and to be put into concrete language by the draftsmen. Throughout, the consciousness of controversy was sustained. 

The affirmative of the program had to be shaped so as to disarm, as far as possible,

any conceivable negative. I fail to see the distinction between this procedure and scientific method. 

The role of the universities in offering their share to the public service is not impaired because of the nature and content of academic instruction. It is impaired because as a nation we refuse their offer. There is a new earnestness among our youth-an earnestness that reflects the serious problems to be met by our people in the near future. We must lay a breach into those statutory provisions which virtually bar the coming administrative profession. In the meantime, many institutions of higher learning have attempted to adjust their curricula so as to provide graduates aspiring to an administrative career with a semi-professional certificate attesting to their training in the technological aspects of public administration. This is an inadequate substitute for what elsewhere is more fruitfully achieved by a probationary service with the government lasting several years-a probationary service that actually tests administrative capacities. When last year the vice-chancellors of Oxford, Cambridge, and Bristol made a public appeal for a more positive attitude on the part of local authorities toward university men (London Times, April 22, 1937), the accompanying editorial closed with the following citation from the 

Hadow Report: "Either the entire educational system of the country is mistaken, or local authorities ought to be drawing

systematically on the universities." For the national service, Great Britain does. 

We do neither.

In view of the placement problem, Harvard's new Graduate School of Public 

Administration has practically limited its official enrollment to men with some government experience intent upon returning to their desks. The Dodds Report of 1936 advised restraint in admitting students. 

So far, the School has considerably undercut the Dodds Report. In evolving its program, the School has on the whole confined itself to pooling departmental graduate instruction. But being able to bring in consultants from various spheres of governmental activity, the School can provide itself with the stimulus of practical reality. We may assume that this relationship will work both ways: for the faculty it will combat the perils of our proverbial ivory-tower existence, and for the government officer it will suggest yardsticks of theoretical soundness which tend to disappear at times in the dust of policy battles. One other thing: the

School affords that rare opportunity which we all cherish, the opportunity for reasoning with men usually out of our sight because they happen to belong to another department. 

If this be little, let us fight for more!

We are moving, but we may well move faster. To do so requires that we seek the understanding of others to make better use of the resources freely available.
