THE UNCONSCIOUS SINNER

SHE CHAPTER I 

HE lay in a long chair in a shady corner of the lawn that swept, cool and green, far round and about the house, bearing the shadows of cedar trees on its level surface, as a lake bears reflections. 

She was all happiness, happiness personified; she wanted nothing to complete that dangerous state in which mortals excite the envy of the Gods and Fate brushes round angrily, sharpening all her knives and daggers to stab us with.

She was just twenty-two and so far in her life had never had an hour's illness or unhappiness. Her trim, well-shaped head covered with its thick glossy black hair had never ached, her large dark eyes, so warmly capable of reflecting every emotion, had never known tears, her strong supple graceful body was perfect and fit for any demand its owner might make upon it. 

With these conditions life, of course, should be a happy thing, but it is not always. With all its perfections and powers, youth is sometimes so thwarted and imprisoned by circumstances, so suppressed and starved that it can be the most wretched period of a human being's life, but in her se, no. Everything had smiled upon her. 

She had come out at eighteen. Graceful, attractive,

clever, immensely vital, she had been run after, loved, petted, adored, "spoiled" as grim relations averred, had loved a little in return, had gayly danced and laughed and dressed through three cloudless years and then last year the great thing had happened. She had met the man she really could love, he had loved her and now they were engaged. The great flashing diamond marquise ring, with its little guard, on her finger, attested the fact. In three months more they would be married and then-travel! Travel, travel, travel, she would see the world! See everything in life! There was the world inviting her. They would go by aeroplane, by motor, by seaplane, by steamer, by train, by camel or car in the desert, on foot through the jungle. They would do everything, see everything, have everything. What a prospect calling to the rushing youth in her veins! Youth that thirsts for effort, for movement, for change, excite ment, novelty! The man was a millionaire, and she adored him! That was the funny and fortunate part of it all. For, had she not adored him, she would not have accepted him. His millions meant nothing to her. Millionaires are never supposed to be adored: they are always supposed to be married for their money. By some mysterious process of reasoning the possession of money by either man or woman is held to be proof that they cannot be loved, and so in this case it was commonly said that Rosa Deane was marrying for money. But she did not care. She knew she was not. She knew that if the man had stood be-

fore her without one penny, she would still have said that enthusiastic yes, when he asked her to marry him. And the man knew very well, too, that impetuous rush to his arms, that wild pressure of her lips on his, could not be bought by his millions. There is something in the real fire of passion that cannot be imitated by the cleverest woman, or the most cunning man. Something that absolutely defies efforts to simulate it.

But Rosa was pleased that he had money, for she knew it pleased her parents. For their sakes she would have hated to make what they would consider a bad match. As it was, they were pleased, everybody was pleased. There was no opposition: no worry, no difficulty. Every circumstance smiled upon her-assisted in the happiness that their glorious love for each other gave her. Really it was a wonderful combination for this usually troublesome world to offer to anyone. She was thinking of this, as she lay there; it was too good to be true. Too good to last. Things hardly ever did go like that in this life. Look at all the novels that had ever been written, which after all do portray life more or less. Keen lovers in them were always poor and had unheard of difficulties to surmount, parents were always opposed to them. Millionaires were always ill-bred and insupportable; apparently, too, they were always bald and fat, not at all like her Nicolas, straight and slim and tall, with his fine features and most distinguished air. Oh, she was sorry she had let him go from her now! A glow of

worshiping love ran through her as she called up his vision. Why had she set the marriage so far? 

Three months! Surely it would be better to marry now, to make the dream true that seemed too good to be true! But he had been invited on a big-game shoot and had asked her permission to go. And she had given it as the result of a pact between them. This was his last shooting trip. He wanted her to go with him, but she hated shooting. She had asked if he would give it up for her, and he had promised. 

Never after this would he kill for pleasure again. She had been softened by his concession, then she also must concede something: so it had come about-this temporary separation-and not until the last, when they had to part, did either realize what an agony it was and how foolish they had been to inflict it on themselves. But it had seemed too late then to alter all the arrangements and plans, and he had gone, unpleased, and she had remained regretful and disquiet. Only the ardent, tender letters exchanged made the waiting for his return endurable. 

A slight furrow dug itself in the velvet brow as she raised herself into a sitting position, to watch more carefully the progress of a little fly on a cedar log that lay near her chair. 

How exquisite the little creature was! Her eyes followed its every movement. She did not know its name. It was pure white and so delicate that it seemed almost transparent. Tiny, dainty white feet marched joyously over the rugged tree trunk, dainty trans-

parent wings, perfectly shaped, rose from its back, large black eyes looked gayly round it. Tiny waving antennæ moved above them as it advanced. The girl's eyes followed it. So snowy white, so fresh, so gay, so confident, so alert though fragile, so perfectly created, it seemed to symbolize joyous youth, out on its first excursion to see the world. Where was it going? What lively plans were in that tiny, tiny head? Rosa bent forward suddenly. Before the little creature, straight in its path, ran a streak of black resin, melted by the heat, oozing from the wood. 

The fly in all its beauty advanced serenely. The sticky glue-like resin waited in its path. Anxiety seized the watcher. If it touched that deadly stream, it must be death to the angelic little being. Yet how could she interfere? Her human finger would be too rough and big to touch it safely. Surely in an instant it would spread its gossamer wings and float into safety! No, before the girl could decide what to do or how to save it one tiny foot had touched the black stream. It was caught, another also, then all. Then the fairy wings went down. All the beauty, all the transparent fragile whiteness was nothing now but a struggling anguished tortured atom. With lips white and compressed with pain, Rosa extinguished with one touch the struggling life. It was all she could do, the only help, the only mercy. 

She started up to her feet.

The incident was small but it had hurt her horribly. 

Anything more exquisitely beautiful in its own way,

more joyous, more gay and free than that little fly could not be imagined and, like herself, it had seemed, on this, perhaps its first excursion, its first adventure, so favored by surrounding circumstances. The calm evening, the soft falling light, the prospering zephyrs round it, the smooth cedar trunk along which it moved. Had it not seen the dark stream ahead? Had nothing warned it? 

Rosa felt a shade had come over her joyous mood. 

Was this a bad omen? How ridiculous! She shook her shoulders impatiently. She was not superstitious. 

Superstition only added to the petty little worries, anxieties and fears of life, already quite troublesome enough.

She swept her hand down her white summer frock to smooth its creases and walked slowly across the lawn, the sunlight falling softly over her in its reddening evening rays.

There were no guests here just then at her home, Moor Park, but two were coming this evening, a Major and Mrs. Hope, and she wondered idly what they would be like. She had heard her father and mother talking of them last evening. The man was a friend of her father's-had been in the same regiment as Colonel Deane; a peculiar fellow, he had called him, and her mother had said she never knew whether she liked him or not, but she was sorry for his wife. 

That was all she had heard. Sorry for his wife. Why? she wondered. Soon, she too would be a wife. How funny that seemed! But, oh, on this particular eve-

ning how she longed for her fiancé's presence! How she wished he were there beside her now, and she thought she would say, "Bother these three months' waiting, let's motor up to town and be married at once and then off to see the world! I am tired of everything! I want something new."

She wandered on over the lawn, then paused suddenly as she heard the sound of a motor coming up the drive. That was doubtless the Hopes arriving.

She continued her walk, annoyed that a thrush, which had been singing beautifully, suddenly ceased and flew away frightened by the sound of the engine. 

Fifteen minutes perhaps went by and then she saw her mother coming out of the house with the two guests and turned her steps to meet them. "My daughter Rosa," and she shook hands first with a slight pale woman, who seemed to have funny white eyes, then with the man and had a feeling of pain as he took her hand. The grip was too strong. 

Looking up, she had the impression of a tall, powerful frame, very muscular; of a pale, smooth face, stamped with an expression of pain and bitterness, which immediately gave way to a charming smile as he greeted her, and two very large and brilliant eyes. 

Inevitable talk about the journey, the weather, the heat. Then Mrs. Deane to the wife: "I am sure you are dying for tea. It's ready in the library, that's really the coolest place.-Will you take tea, Basil, or walk round with Rosa?"

"Certainly the last, Mrs. Deane, please. I see you remember I don't like tea!" "Very good. Come with me, Mary; don't go too far, Rosa, and forget that dinner's at eight.”

Mrs. Deane turned with the wife back to the house. 

Rosa and Basil Hope stood for a moment looking at each other. The singing of all the birds had ceased, the brilliance of the sunlight vanished suddenly as the sun dropped behind the hills. A sudden chill came into the air.

She gazed up at him and seemed unable to look away. It was he, first, who with a faint smile turned and put himself beside her for their walk. "I am very, very pleased to be here," he said, with a sort of emphasis, for which there seemed no reason. "Is it your first visit?" Rosa asked in a mechanical way as they walked to the edge of the lawn. How oddly his eyes had seemed to suck something out of her as they rested on her! She felt now as if she hardly knew what she was saying or where they were going. "I was here a long while ago, but the place is much finer now, the trees have grown so." 

Silence. She felt strangely tongue-tied. She, generally the gay chatterer on every subject. And now she would like to entertain brightly this man, her father's guest. But suddenly she found she had nothing to say. She glanced up at him. He had a good profile she noted, as he kept pace beside her in a heavy careless walk. Generally the men with her looked at her frequently with warm friendly looks.

He did not. He was gazing about him, his face cold, pale, indifferent. It was only just as they had started that he had stared down on her with that keen fixity that had seemed to draw something out of her. Something, apparently her power of speech and her usual intelligence, she thought angrily, for she could think of nothing interesting to say and, rather than just make a stupid banal conversation, she kept silence. "Which way now?" he asked abruptly. They had left lawns and spreading cedars, rose gardens and flower beds behind. Passing beyond a line of towering elms they had come out on a silent country road. 

On the other side of it stretched the moor, wild, vast, going away to dim purple distance, its gorgeous heather covered flats gray-purple also in the falling light. "This way, to the left I think. The moor is fine but it's too late to go onto it now. We will go round by the lake and so back to the house in time to dress for dinner." 

To the left they went in silence. "Jolly interesting walk I'm having!" she thought grimly to herself. And yet there was something she liked in his build and certainly in his eyes, if he would only let her see them more. "You are very silent," she remarked at last. "Well, are you talking a great deal?" he replied. "No, I can't. You numb me. I am catching your silence. Generally I talk a great deal.” "Talking is a silly habit."

The girl stopped in her walk. "Really," she began indignantly. Then laughed. "It's your pose, I suppose, to be uncomplimentary," she said. "I only made a general statement." "Never mind! Here's the lake. It's beautiful, isn't it?"

They had reached a gentle depression in the grounds. Under a belt of trees, firs growing down to its edge, lay the lake, tranquil, gleaming. A soft light from the evening sky touching its surface in the center. All the borders in green shadowy mystery. "Very," he answered. "Do you swim in it?” "Yes, I have swum there with Nikko." "Who is Nikko? A dog?" "No! My fiancé!" "You are engaged?" "Yes. Anything very funny in that?" resenting his tone of surprise. "No. Only sad." "Sad? Why?" "It's such a mistake to marry." "You are married." "Unfortunately." "You are a man; it may be different. But for a girl, it's best for her to begin her life that way, don't you think?" "Perhaps," he agreed. "But look round on all your married friends. Are any of them happy?"

"But look round on the un-married ones, are any of them happy?" she protested. "No one is, that's humanity."

They sat down on a bench near them. At their feet stretched the lake, limpid, shadowy and cool. "Tell me about your fiancé," he said after a minute, and she was surprised how gentle and musical his voice could be, as now when he left the hard cynicism out of it. "Well," she said simply, "I adore him. He is wonderful and I think I am just the luckiest girl in the world." "What is he like?" "Tall, very distinguished. A very grave kind face, dark hair, dark eyes; awfully, awfully clever." "When are you going to marry him and where do you go?" "In three months. He has a yacht. We'll go a bit to the South Seas first perhaps. I don't know yet. 

Nothing is settled." "He is very rich then?" "Oh, yes! Millions."

There was a pause while Major Hope thrust the point of his stick down many times into the rich black earth at their feet. "You certainly do seem a fortunate person," he said after a minute, and she noted the sarcastic hard tones had come back. "I hope you do not become seasick?" "No, not a bit. I have been out on the roughest

seas. I love the water-being on it or in it. I should like now to swim across to that island opposite us." "Probably you would go down with cramp half way over!" 

A little angry color came into her face. What a horrid sneering, jeering way he had! Why should he suggest she couldn't swim when he knew nothing about it. She sprang to her feet. "What nonsense!" she answered. "I swim to the island and back every morning! Come, it's time to be going back to the house.' 99 He rose and they walked back almost in silence. 

At the door she gave him a little nod and ran up to her room. It would be some time before she took another walk with him! What a disagreeable man!

She opened her armoire and looked in at the various rows of dresses hanging there. She wanted to look particularly nice to-night. Which should she choose?

At last she selected a green. Some women thought it was very unlucky to wear green, but she was not superstitious. It made life so troublesome if you were and had to remember so many prohibitions. She put on the green. It was a lovely gown, what there was of it, and what there was not—the bare arms, the bare back, the bare neck-was lovelier still. 

Well pleased, she gazed in the glass. Now for a little rouge and that specially brilliant tube of sticky red paint for her lips; a little powder on her already snow-like neck and then, having made herself look like a very attractive courtesan, she went downstairs.

At dinner she sat between Major Hope and the curate of St. John's-on-the-Moor, but the latter gave most of his attention to Mrs. Hope, who, a pallid figure in black, sat on the other side of him. She was free to talk to Hope, but he was not much more use than he had been in the walk. He had gazed all over her with that curious absorbing stare of his as they sat down and after that did not appear to look at her again or seem interested in anything but his dinner. She wondered if he had admired her glorious toilet, her toored lips and her shining hair, at which he glanced sometimes, and then immediately glanced away. Perhaps not. Perhaps he only admired blondes. Towards the end of dinner he did turn to her and said: "Will you bathe with me in the lake to-morrow?" "Of course, if if you like. What time? Before breakfast?" "Oh, no! Say ten o'clock."

The night was so hot they had their coffee outside on the lawn. The Hopes took theirs sitting together, and the curate came and put himself beside her and talked sick poor to her annoyed ears. When he rose to take his leave a great big moon, blood red, was coming up over the edge of the moor and Hope came across to her. "Let's go out on the moor for just a moment, Miss 

Deane, shall we?" and without any question, although she had thought she would not walk again with him, she left her chair and they went towards the moor. They did not talk, but she was accustomed to

this now and she did not trouble herself to try to break the silence.

They passed beyond the trees to the edge of the level plain and there stood still, looking about them. 

The moor stretched immense, silent, shadowy on every side and in front of them the great red moon seemed scrambling hastily up the sky, over some long straight narrow clouds like five-barred gates in it.

There seemed nothing to say and nothing to do, so after a time of stolid staring at the swollen, footballshaped moon, they turned and went back and into the house. In the hall they said, "Good-night! Ten o'clock to-morrow morning at the lake!" and Rosa went up to her room. She rather discontentedly took off her really lovely gown and put it away. Not a single compliment nor admiring glance had it won that evening. "What funny light hair he has, but a lot of it, very thick, and tremendous eyes. His teeth are very good and regular and even, but when he smiles somehow I always think of a wolf."

As she slipped into the soft, gauzy night-wrap she slept in, she heard a knock at the door and went to open it. To her surprise it was Mrs. Hope who stood there outside. "May I come in for a moment?" she said. "I would like to have a minute's talk with you." 99 "Oh, do," Rosa assented politely, and drew up a big chair as her visitor entered the room. She looked curiously at her. She was quite a youngish woman,

not over thirty-five, and not bad looking. But she seemed singularly dead and crushed. "I came, Miss Deane I expect it's rather a thankless errand but I thought I ought to—I came to say it's a pity for you to run after my husband." 

Rosa's face grew crimson. "Really, Mrs. Hope," she exclaimed in indignant surprise. "I don't know what you mean! I have only been civil to Major Hope as a guest." "I don't think being civil to a guest necessitates going on the moor with him in the moonlight, nor bathing in the lake at ten o'clock in the morning," the other answered. "However, don't think I'm jealous in the ordinary sense nor speaking for my own sake. It is for yours. I have quite given up caring what Basil does now. If he's not with you he's with someone else, never with me, so it does not matter who it may be, but I have just been talking with your mother and she tells me of your engagement, of the splendid marriage you are going to make. You are just on the outset of your life: you have simply everything in the world to look forward to. Don't be so foolish as to wreck it all." "What are you talking about?" Rosa returned, angrily. "I can't understand you. How am I going to wreck anything?" "By falling in love with Basil," returned Mrs. 

Hope calmly. Rosa's eyes flamed with exasperation. "Rubbish! You say you know of my engagement! 

I am as much in love with Nikko as I can possibly be.

We're going to be married directly. What is your stupid husband to me?" "Basil is a very peculiar man, and he does attract some women enormously. I see he attracts you, though perhaps you are unconscious of it. I asked your mother what you usually wore for the evening; she said a little black dress; why were you in that gorgeous dress to-night? I asked her if you always painted. She said no. Why did you paint your face to-night? I asked her if you bathed in the lake. She said you had not for some time. Why, then, to-morrow? Is it not all to attract Basil? And he is attracted to you, too." "Is he?" interjected Rosa. "He has an odd way showing it then!" of "He does not show it ever, but it is there, the attraction, and I warn you against him. Your mother and I have been friends ever since we were children. 

I know what she will feel if all that happens to women who love Basil happens to you. The man is a rock, a stone without feeling, without pity, without any conception of love. Women throw themselves against him and get smashed. That's all. And it's a pity for you. Your life is full to the brim. You want nothing more. You have no need of Basil and, remember, you can never have him. For whatever he does I shall never divorce him."

Struggling with annoyance and anger, Rosa sat on the side of her bed, impatiently listening while this pale-eyed woman talked on in this outrageous way.

"I can't see why you should say all this. Of course it's natural to dress more when you have guests than when quite alone; also to try and amuse them. Major 

Hope suggested the bathing. Of course I don't want your husband. Why should I?" "Then go away now somewhere, while you can, before you are in love with him." "How can I go away? Where to? Why don't you go and take your husband with you, if afraid?" you are so "Take Basil away now? He wouldn't come! He won't go away from here till he has had you!" 

Rosa sprang from the bed. "Really, Mrs. Hope, do go out of my room if you are going to say such things! What can you think of me? It's abominable!" 

Mrs. Hope got up too. "Now remember, whatever happens in the future, which I see very clearly, I have done what I could, I have warned you," and she went out. 

Rosa turned the key in the door after she had gone and then sat down panting with anger and annoyance. 

What an unheard-of woman, to talk like that! 

Was there anything at all in what she had said? 

Was her husband really as she made out? A sudden smile came to her lips as she recalled how she had said he was attracted. And had he truly any influence on her? It was odd certainly how she had walked to the moor with him against her will! But in love! It was absurd! How she wisked Nikko were here and they

could go off at once! He was somewhere near Nairobi now, but she could write and ask him to start home as soon as he got her letter. Yes, she would do that, and she rose to find her writing materials. It was odd, but her letter to Nikko did not come out as nicely as usual. 

She repeated over and over that she wanted him to come back, yet, reading it, it did not seem to have the force and fresh impetus her letters usually had. 

However, she did not know what to add to make it better. She closed it up and then got into bed, her thoughts immediately slipping back to a hand that hurt her in its grasp and beautiful, even teeth that somehow reminded her of a wolf. 

In his own bedroom, far from his wife's, which the thoughtful hostess had given her on the other side of the house, Basil sat at his window and looked out over the moonlit moor. 

So he had met her at last! And it was too late! He was married and tied. He had given away the first place in his life to another and now he had nothing to offer to this one, for whom he felt he would cheerfully give up the very blood in his veins. 

How delicious she was! He felt the hot currents of electricity that ran through her! Her superb health! Her look of life, of animation! The tone of her voice, the velvet look of her skin were delightful! 

The bright smile in her clever eyes! Oh, the pain of being bound and helpless and voiceless! That she was engaged he did not care; that her heart had been already given, did not matter either. He could soon

get it back. But his own position made things hopeless. Married and poor! A wife to keep and only his 

Army pay! If the girl were there in his arms, what could he do with her? His face was black and his brows drawn into a knot as he sat there. All his life -and he was now forty-five-he had longed to find a woman just like this one: a female personality that seemed to have the grit and strength and vitality of a young man; a girl whose neat dress fell round her just that way, whose trim dark head turned just as hers did, whose hand had that same dry heat in it which shows the electricity of its owner. Someone who had the gay, bright, challenging look in her eyes that she had. How he loved that look, which seemed to say to a man: "Come, see if you can conquer me!" 

How he had searched for this! What numberless relations and liaisons and affaires with women he had entered into, and been disappointed with all of them. 

He had never been satisfied, never found just what he had wanted. The affaires had always ended in boredom, disgust and contempt. Women had been too nice to him, gone down too easily before his good looks, for he was good looking, and in a peculiar way, which always adds its charm to beauty. He had given way to every sort of appetite and fancy that had come to him, hoping, always hoping, in dark hours spent in dark places, to find that particular sensation of pleasure this girl's hand had given him, meeting her as hostess on her father's lawn. But it was too late! If it had only happened three years ago; before his sense-

less marriage! He had rushed into that purely out of rage against a faithless mistress. One of the few women who had stood up to him, who, resenting his abominable treatment of her, had flouted him and openly replaced him with another lover. Unable to strangle her, as he would have liked to have done, in his violent, jealous rage, in order to hurt her in another way, he deliberately sacrificed an innocent woman who loved him, and married her without a spark of love for her, simply to punish the first woman who had dared to defy him. Almost before the ceremony was over he had madly regretted his folly, and had lived in a sort of silent, sullen fury with himself and his unfortunate wife ever since. Everybody pitied Mrs. Hope for the obvious contempt and disregard with which he treated her, but such was his attraction for women that, for the sake of those occasional times when from physical inclination he sought her, she put up with everything, accepted her life and refused any thought or plan of changing it. 

Basil sat long at that window and Rosa was beside him all the time, in all the various attitudes and poses he had seen her body fall into up to now. Sometimes, sitting beside him, brightly painted in her gorgeous green dinner dress, sometimes standing in white under the cedar, sometimes without concealing raiment at all, and always with arms widely outstretched, calling, calling to him. "Come and take me, win me, conquer me"; and he was bound hard, tight, to the rock -like Tantalus-and could not move.

THE CHAPTER II 

HEY met at the lake next morning at ten. He had been there sitting on the bench since ninethirty. Punctually at ten she appeared through the bushes, by a little side path he had not noticed. 

She saw his face before he saw her and noted it was dark with a savage gloom. But when he sprang up to greet her it was suddenly illumined by a wonderful smile and a look of intense pleasure. For a woman to see how she can so transform a countenance just by her presence, is a flattery greater than any contained in words.

She had a rough white cloak round her and so had he. She sat down demurely on the seat beside him. "Shall we go in at once?" she asked, looking up at him.

His face was pale with deep shadows round the eyes. He showed very plainly his sleepless night. "Are you ready to rescue me when I get cramp and go down half-way over to the island?" she added. “Quite,” he said merely, annoyed that she should have remembered his ungracious remark of last night.

She rose and, with a movement that he thought was the most graceful he had ever seen, put one arm up, unfastened her cloak and let it fall on the seat. She 27

showed herself now in a skin-tight yellow silk stockingette bathing-suit. She had a nearly perfect figure of nearly perfect lines-granted that you liked that type of figure the essentially female one-which Basil did. The shoulders were narrow and sloped sweetly, the breasts small and firm, the waist very slim, and below it perhaps an over-development; the legs perhaps too round and fat but the ankles exquisitely fine.

The sun, falling full on her, showed her skin singularly fair. Basil's eyes saw all this in one glance, then he looked away, taking off his cloak and leaving it by hers on the seat.

Without any words they stepped side by side down into the water and started swimming gently to the island.

The lake was really beautiful, a clear sheet of water, quite gold now in the soft, hot sunlight, with deep green shadows from the overhanging trees all round its margin and about the island, which had a small glistening beach of pebbles and sand for a border. "It's not too cold but just right, isn't it?" she said, turning to him with a smile and swimming on her side, and he smiled back and agreed, looking at her small dark head close down now to the water and the round arm she lifted with every stroke from the limpid surface.

Arrived at the island, they drew out on its small, glistening white beach and sat down there in the sun,

some distance from each other. He looked away over the water and this gave her the opportunity of studying him more closely than she had done yet. She liked his hair, it was so thick, grew in such a nice way round a well-shaped head and rose in such soft waves from a good forehead. It was a sort of reddish color. His skin was very white and he looked well in his dark blue bathing-suit, very powerful and muscular and well put together. He looked only about thirty-five, and she wondered what his age really was. He turned after a minute, and saw her eyes fixed on him; as he turned she noticed a little pocket, fastened with a flap over it, on the left side of his suit. "Do you know what this is for?" he said, seeing she was looking at it.

He unfastened the flap and drew out a knife in a waterproof case and handed it to her. "Be careful! It's awfully sharp," he warned her as she drew it from its sheath. It was a beautiful little two-edged dagger and knife combined, about seven inches long and of strong finely tempered steel. In spite of his warning, as she turned it in her hands to examine, the sharp point of it caught a finger and drew a bead of blood. "How stupid of me," she exclaimed laughing, "but it's wonderfully keen! What is it for?" "I always have it when bathing in a lake,” he answered, "to cut the weeds in case one should get entangled in them. I was very nearly drowned in a lake once. You can't believe how strong these water plants

are nor how they twist and twine round your feet and ankles and seem deliberately to pull you down. 

But this will cut anything in a second." "It's a lovely knife," she agreed, sucking the tip of her wounded finger. "Where did you get it?" Honor would restrain her, but she would desire the kiss and the embrace from Basil all the same, however little she yielded to it. It was really a bother, all this loving business. This valley had once been a lake and on the opposite side to the forest was still a half-dried swamp; here along its soft edge the track of an elephant's foot had been seen. 

The sun was not yet up. CHAPTER VIII 

FTER the Hopes had gone, the time seemed to pass heavily and slowly for Rosa, as if it were a great river flowing over her and she was buried somewhere beneath it.

Various parties of people came and went but she really seemed hardly conscious of either their arrival or their departure, and if anyone had asked her their names afterwards, she would have had difficulty in recalling them. She lived wholly engrossed in her thoughts and was divided between a terrible straining longing for Basil and a fierce hunger for the arrival of Nikko, which she hoped would deliver her from that longing.

But somehow she felt that in that raging pain her love for Basil was dying. "It is like the death of a nerve," she thought. "What awful agony and then a nothingness, no feeling at all; presently I am sure I shall feel nothing, not want him back, not wish for him, not even like it if he came. How strange all these things are, but I will get free from all this before Nikko comes. I must. It is only fair to him, and 

I think I shall."

At first when Basil had left she had longed to hear from him. Although they had agreed not to write to 119

each other, there was some faint anticipation, some hope even, that he might do so. The afternoon following his departure had brought a letter from him to her mother, thanking her for her hospitality, saying how much he had enjoyed his visit and other society nothings. How the advent of that letter had moved her, and how for many days after she had trembled with a sort of desperate longing to see his writing when the posts came in, but now it was so no longer. She was like a man in her efforts and wish to get rid quickly of a useless love affair. It was over and done with. They had decided they must part and for always. Therefore there was no use in worrying over it. One might as well worry for the moon. It was his fault that they were separated, his fault that he was married with a millstone tied round his neck.

How could a man be such an unutterable fool as to marry a woman he did not love, and so forever shut himself off from having one that he did love?

She could hardly understand anyone being such an idiot, and it was her intense realization of his folly and her resentment at it that helped her to push him out of her mind.

CHAPTER XIV 

OSA was on trial for her life. Yes, it had come to that. Incredible as it still seemed to the dazed family, Rosa had been taken away from them and formally committed for trial on the charge of having murdered Basil Hope.

The exceedingly stupid coroner, and still more obstinate than stupid, when Rosa had, as he put it, "interfered" with his and the jury's finding against Mrs. Hope, had caused the inquest to be re-opened and Rosa to be dragged from her bed to stand examination at it, with the result that he obtained from a confused and unwilling jury another of his beloved definitive verdicts, this time against Rosa, and committed her for trial on the charge of willful murder. "As thy day, so shall thy strength be." How wonderfully true that promise is! Written thousands of years ago, the common experience of mankind then, it has maintained its shining truth down the ages. Where does that wonderful strength in affliction come from? Is it lent to us from the Divinity or does it emerge from some half-divine spring within the human being himself? 

Wherever that strength comes from, it is there in 236

moments of great need, just as a staff might be pushed suddenly into the hand of a falling man. 

Had the Deanes been told, in those bright years at Moor Park, that their only daughter would be convicted of murder and have to stand in the dock with the shadow of the scaffold falling on her, they would have certainly thought they would die under the anguish of it all.

But now, here, plunged suddenly into all this agonized suspense and suffering, this curious strength came to them and they rose, forceful, energetic, undismayed, to meet first one frightful pain and then another, without flinching.

The trial was in London and there they all moved from Mallard House; Rosa under arrest. She was not allowed bail and as her condition made her quite unfit for prison she was sent to the infirmary and there lay, helpless and silent on her bed, uncomplaining, unfrightened, only deeply distressed at all the misery she had brought on the others. To her also had come that nameless strength in her day of blackness. She was patient, confident, hopeful, giving every assistance she could to her counsel, the very best man in his profession, whom Nikko had engaged for her. At the first sight of the girl, this man, a barrister of middle age, had fallen in love with her. In her beauty, in her youth, and now crushed under this load of suffering, she appealed to him enormously. 

She was to him a martyr rather than a criminal, and every instant of his time, every atom of force

in his brain he devotedly gave to her. She seemed to him a Joan of Arc, that he would give his own life for rather than see go to the stake.

Nikko came constantly to see her in the infirmary, and in him too, the love and passion seemed to grow as he worked and toiled unceasingly for her and watched in all this time her great unselfishness of mind. For her mother, for her father, for himself, she suffered. To her own fate she was apparently indifferent. With her parents she kept up the idea that she was certain to be acquitted, and they always went from their visits to her cheered and comforted, but Nikko knew that in her own heart she had no such assurance. Her real spirit longed to escape as one longs to escape from a confined space that one is obliged to share with a repulsive companion. 

Her body, so beautifully formed, so perfectly healthy, so full of life, now seemed to her detestable.

HE yacht steamed on, swift, silent, tranquil over the starlit water, through the calm beauty of a night in May.

Nikko walked slowly up and down the deck alone with his thoughts. Everything had been accomplished as they had arranged. 

They had been married that day, simply, quietly, in her drawing-room, everyone had been said goodby to and he had driven her, alone with him, to 

Southampton, and now on the yacht he was taking her away alone with him. And an immense sense of joy filled him to realize she was irrevocably his at last and whatever happened now to interfere between them would be his fault, if he allowed it. How little we know our own Fate! Some years back if anyone had prophesied to him that he, with all his various exceptional gifts and, by reason of them, such a wide choice of women, would choose to marry a girl who had already had a lover and had been a mother and was a murderess, he would have laughed at the joke.

Yet now the joke had become grim fact. She was all these, yet he loved her and wanted her to distraction and knew that there was no other that could take her place for him.

Yes, because our hearts are torn out of our breasts 248

and locked up in another's keeping not by Beauty, not by Virtue or Worth, but by that subtle something that draws and compels us by intangible blows towards the other.

As she had stood facing him through all those four dreary days of the trial, and her soul had been dragged out of her and slowly tortured in public for all to see and greedily gaze upon and point at, the fire of his love had mounted higher and higher. That she was there, in this horrible position by her own will, seemed to him to lift her above all the charges made against her. His thought of the murder-even if she had committed it--was lost in the admiration of her courage in offering herself for the extreme penalty. The problem of this girl, betrayed by her unconscious self yet always struggling to do right in her conscious self, interested and stirred him profoundly.

His love had mounted through his senses to his brain and gripped it. He loved her now entirely differently from when he had first seen her. His passion for her now seemed to flare like a great light in his brain, showing her to him as something incomparable, invaluable, because unique and irreplaceable. 

A timid man would doubtless have felt alarmed, worried at the idea of being responsible for a being above the common laws, but to Nikko the challenge to his courage immediately set it all on fire. He was

not the least bit afraid of her. The unusual, the exceptional and, above all, the dangerous in anything attracted him enormously. 

A horse that no one else could ride was the one he most liked to be mounted on; a room, so ghost-filled that no one else would sleep in it, rather attracted him; a sea or inlet reputed shark-filled always tempted him to bathe in it.

He loved conquest. To subdue, conquer, dominate life and every circumstance it flung against him, to oppose him, that was his joy and his pleasure. In his hunting and shooting expeditions he would never pursue a tame or ordinary animal and in the same way he would never take a tame ordinary wife. Love itself is not a tame ordinary thing. It is a savage instinct in the brain and in the highest natures, so complicated, so mysterious that the victim of its sudden attack never knows himself exactly what has happened to him.

Nikko now realized he had a most strange, unusual, dangerous and, therefore, to him, most alluring companion and his whole being glowed with delight at the thought. 

Down below in the beautiful cabin he had had prepared for them, the girl sat before the oval mirror of her dressing-table, waiting for him. When the yacht's bells above her chimed in their curious way at the hour, he would come to her, she knew. 

In the glass opposite her she saw her lips bright scarlet, half open to let her nervous breath pass

through them. She saw she was beautiful still, her face looked back at her clear white, lighted by its glorious eyes; her body was beautiful too under the loose silken wrap she wore, but she felt a wild rage of anger seethe in her that the gift of herself was now not perfect, untouched as it once had been. And that she loved Nikko as she did made this anger burn in her furiously.

All his wonderful devotion to her through the storm that had broken on her, all his tenderness, his sympathy, his forgiveness, his understanding of her had filled her with a passion of gratitude and of love for him which was painful in its intensity. 

She would have given him her life without hesitation had he asked for it and it was this feeling of adoration for him, this longing to repay him, that filled her with fury to think that much of what she had had to give was taken away from her. She heard his step suddenly, as the bells chimed overhead, and full of this feeling of worship, of the intense desire to give, she sprang to her feet and ran to meet him as the door opened. Some of those passionate, raging thoughts, all the fire of her intense love for him, poured through her lips as he approached her and the man threw his arms round her in a passion of excited longing, that only her contact could appease. "Darling, my own, why regret anything? We have bought this moment by all that misery! Did we ever love before as we do now? You know we did not.

What we have lost is nothing to what we have

gained," and he kissed away from her eyelids the hot salt tears burning there. "Be content, my dearest, as I am. We want nothing more. We are together."
