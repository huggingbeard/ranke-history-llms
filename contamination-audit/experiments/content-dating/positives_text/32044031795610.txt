PREFACE 

People look to their physicians, surgeons and dentists for relief in physical pain, and for the restoration of their health; and likewise they almost invariably consult their attorneys for relief in mental pain-worry over legal difficulties, enforcement of their rights, and rescue from domestic infelicities. 

And, just as physicians, surgeons and dentists may guard their patients, if consulted in time, against physical ailments, so attorneys may frequently guard their clients against domestic difficulties if consulted before marriage. 

Although marriage is the most important and solemn contract into which two human beings can enter, very few people have clear ideas of what the consequences may be. 

The heartaches that have come to the author as an attorney in actual experience in handling divorce cases and in consultation with hundreds of men and women who have suffered the agonies of mental pain and misery in attempting to make marriage a success, have prompted the writing of these four major and eight minor considerations in contemplation of marriage.

The four major considerations are treated rather elaborately, in the order of their importance, in the hope that they will be somewhat instructive and interesting to the average man and woman, and in the belief that there is a general lack of knowledge on the points covered. Many books may be found in libraries which tend to arouse the sentiments and passions of men and women, but very few offer any suggestions for the interpretation of these sentiments and passions in abundant living.

Marriage CHAPTER I MAJOR CONSIDERATIONS RECIPROCAL FONDNESS 

Sentimental fondness has been called "love" all down through the ages,-in poetry, fiction, drama, and in the parlance of men and women of all stations in life. But to call any kind of fondness "love" has been a matter of convenience, rather than of good diction. Love comes, if at all, after marriage, rather than before. The formula or prescription is: Intense reciprocal fondness, marriage, at least one youngster, five or more years in time, and each still very fond of the other. That is love, and there is no other formula. 

From these premises it must be concluded that unmarried people can not possibly know what love is. Love, when it comes, will embrace all the considerations in contemplation of marriage. Love means to love the wife, or love the husband, and not to love one's self through the wife or husband and the pleasures that come to them through that companionship.

This definition of love does not mean, of course, that all loveless marriages are unhappy ones. Although an overwhelming majority of weddings are prompted by selfishness, the frequent result is mutual and complete happiness, and 18

both parties never know that they have not been in love. In all cases of loveless, but happy marriages, sexual compatibility has overshadowed every other element in life. The interest of each is centered solely on sensual pleasure. Fortunately, few children come to these unions because children take up time and spoil the game. Neither man nor woman of this type will ever contribute anything worth while to humanity, and "the world will little note nor long remember what they did here, but can never forget" the kindness they bestowed on the world by not bringing more of their kind into it.

Conceding, then, that love comes only after marriage, we may safely say that we have arrived at a point in our modern society when it becomes necessary to formulate rules and regulations in contemplation of marriage the same as in the formation of other contracts of lesser importance, and that a vast majority of men and women of all ages who are plunging precipitately into the throes of matrimony without seriously considering what the consequences may be, will want to know what the guide-posts are.

Reciprocal sentimental fondness is offered as the first consideration because it is of the greatest importance. A man would be eminently foolish to think about marrying a woman of whom he is not fond-even to the point of nauseating his friends who do not, and can not, understand. Fondness of any kind or nature is most inexplicable even by the parties afflicted by the malady. He himself does not know why he is. fond of this particular young lady. He can not explain to his own mother whether he is doing homage to the young lady's beauty, purity, brains, irresistible smile, sparkling wit, eyes, complexion, or amicable disposition. He simply soliloquizes that she is his type of girl. He closes his eyes, and visualizes a beautiful soul inextricably intertwined with the personal pulchritude of a Venus. The young lady is equally unable to explain her intense partiality for him. counterpart of the man she has seen in her dreams. Intense

An ideal exemplification in fiction of this intense reciprocal fondness for a peculiar type of personality is to be found in 

Charles Major's "When Knighthood Was In Flower"-the story of Charles Brandon and Mary Tudor, sister to King Henry VIII. The whole story is of great interest, full and complete, in its sweet, fierce manifestation of the one thing insoluble by time-love-which, after all, is only sentimental fondness which has met the acid test of due consideration, and proved its permanency by the lapse of time.

CHARLES BRANDON 

Charles Brandon was a young man twenty-four years of age, who had just returned to England from the continental wars, where he had gained great honor and considerable profit. 

He had also created a great sensation by his success in a terrible duel down in Suffolk with a Scotch gentleman named Adam Judson, who was counted the most deadly and most cruel duelist of the time. Brandon came to court and was installed as one of the captains of the King's guard, with no particular duties except such as should be assigned him from time to time.

In personal appearance he was of such noble mien and comely face as to attract the gaze of every one away from even the glittering person of his Majesty, the King. His hair, worn in its natural length, fell in brown curls back from his forehead almost to the shoulder, a style just then new, even in France. His eyes were a deep blue, and his complexion, though browned by exposure, held a tinge of beauty which the sun could not mar and a girl might envy. He wore neither mustachio nor beard, as men now disfigure their faces since Francis I took a scar on his chin, and his clear cut profile, dilating nostrils and mobile, though firm-set, mouth,

gave pleasing assurance of tenderness, gentleness, daring and strength.

As to height, he was full six feet, with ample evidence of muscle, though no great bulk. He was grace itself, and the 

King afterwards said he had never seen such strength of arm and skill in the use of the lance, a sure harbinger of favor, if not of fortune, for the possessor. 

Although at that time less than twenty-five years old, he was really a learned man, having studied at Barcelona, Salamanca and Paris. While there had been no system in his education, his mind was a sort of knowledge junkshop, wherein he could find almost anything he wanted. He spoke 

German, French and Spanish, and seemed to know the literature of all these languages.

THE PRINCESS MARY 

Now, at that time, Mary, the king's sister, was just ripening into her greatest womanly perfection. Her skin was like velvet; a rich, clear, rosy snow, with the hot young blood glowing through it like the faint red tinge we sometimes see on the inner side of a white rose leaf. Her hair was a very light brown, almost golden, and fluffy, soft and fine as a skein of Arras silk. She was of medium height, with a figure that 

Venus might have envied. Her feet and hands were small, and apparently made for the sole purpose of driving mankind distracted. In fact, that seemed to be the paramount object in her creation, for she had the world of men at her feet. Her greatest beauty was her glowing dark brown eyes, which shone with an ever-changing lustre from beneath the shade of the longest, blackest up-curving lashes ever seen.

Her voice was soft and full, and, except when angry, which, alas, was not infrequent, had a low and coaxing little note that made it irresistible; she was a most adroit coaxer, and knew her power full well, although she did not always plead,

having the Tudor temper and preferring to command when she could.

She had been made love to by so many men, who had lost their senses in the dazzling rays of her thousand perfections, that love had grown to be a sort of joke with her, and man, a poor, contemptible creature, made to grovel at her feet. Not that she liked or encouraged it; for, never having been moved herself, she held love and its sufferings in utter scorn. Man's love was so cheap and plentiful that it had no value in her eyes, and it looked as if she would lose the best thing in life by having too much of it. 

Such was the royal maid to whose tender mercies Charles 

Brandon was soon to be turned over.

LOVE'S FIERCE SWEETNESS 

"After we had all returned to Greenwich the princess and Brandon were together frequently. Upon several occasions he was invited, with others, to her parlor for card playing. But we spent two evenings, with only four of us present, prior to the disastrous events which changed everything, and of which I am soon to tell you. During these two evenings the "Sailor 

Lass" was in constant demand.

This pair, who should have remained apart, met constantly in and about the palace, and every glance added fuel to the flame. Part of the time it was the princess with her troublesome dignity, and part of the time it was Mary-simply girl. 

Notwithstanding these haughty moods, anyone with half an eye could see that the princess was gradually succumbing to the budding woman; that Brandon's stronger nature had dominated her with that half fear which every woman feels who loves a strong man-stronger than herself. 

One day the rumor spread through the court that the old French king, Louis XII, whose wife, Anna of Brittany, had just died, had asked Mary's hand in marriage. It was this, probably, which opened Brandon's eyes to the fact that he had been playing with the very worst sort of fire; and first made him see that in spite of himself, and almost without his knowl-

edge, the girl had grown wonderfully sweet and dear to him.

He now saw his danger, and struggled to keep himself beyond the spell of her perilous glances and siren song. This modern 

Ulysses made a masterful effort, but alas! had no ships to carry him away, and no wax with which to fill his ears. Wax is a good thing, and no one should enter the siren country without it. Ships, too, are good, with masts to tie one's self to, and sails and rudder, and a gust of wind to waft one quickly past the island. In fact, one can not take too many precautions when in those enchanted waters. 

Matters began to look dark to me. Love had dawned in 

Mary's breast, that was sure, and for the first time, with all its fierce sweetness. Not that it had reached its noon, or anything like it. In truth, it might, I hoped, die in the dawning, for my lady was as capricious as a May day; but it was love love as plain as the sun at rising. She sought Brandon upon all occasions, and made opportunities to meet him; not openly-at any rate, not with Brandon's knowledge, nor with any connivance on his part, but apparently caring little what he or any one else might see. Love lying in her heart had made her a little more shy than formerly in seeking him, but her straightforward way of taking whatever she wanted made her transparent little attempts at concealment very pathetic. 

As for Brandon, the shaft had entered his heart, too, poor fellow, as surely as love had dawned in Mary's, but there was this difference: With our princess-at least I so thought at the time-the sun of love might dawn and lift itself to midheaven and glow with the fervent ardor of high noon-for her blood was warm with the spark of her grandfather's fireand then sink into the west and make room for another sun tomorrow. But with Brandon's stronger nature the sun would go till noon and there would burn for life. The sun, however, had not reached its noon with Brandon, either; since he had set his brain against his heart, and had done what he could to stay the all-consuming orb at its dawning. He knew the hopeless misery such a passion would bring him, and helped the good Lord, in so far as he could, to answer his prayer, and lead him not into temptation. As soon as he saw the truth, he avoided Mary as much as possible.

As I said, we had spent several evenings with Mary after

we came home from Windsor, at all of which her preference was shown in every movement. Some women are so expressive under strong emotion that every gesture, a turn of the head, a glance of the eyes, the lifting of a hand or poise of the body, speaks with a tongue of eloquence, and such was Mary. 

Her eyes would glow with a soft fire when they rested upon him, and her whole person told all too plainly what, in truth, it seemed she did not care to hide. When others were present she would restrain herself somewhat, but with only Jane and myself, she could hardly maintain a seemly reserve. During all this time Brandon remained cool and really seemed unconscious of his wonderful attraction for her. It is hard to understand why he did not see it, but I really believe he did not. Although he was quite at ease in her presence, too much so, Mary sometimes thought, and strangely enough sometimes told him in a fit of short-lived, quickly repented anger that always set him laughing, yet there was never word or gesture that could hint of undue familiarity. It would probably have met a rebuff from the princess part of her; for with a perversity, both royal and feminine, she wanted all the freedom for herself. In short, like any other woman, she would rather love than be loved, that is, until surrender day should come; then of course. After these last two meetings, although the invitations came frequently, none was accepted. Brandon had contrived to have his duties, ostensibly at least, occupy his evenings, and did honestly what his judgment told him was the one thing to do; that is, remained away from a fire that could give no genial warmth, but was sure to burn him to the quick. I saw this only too plainly, but never a word of it was spoken between us.

The more I saw of this man, the more I respected him, and this curbing of his affections added to my already high esteem. 

The effort was doubly wise in Brandon's case. Should love with his intense nature reach its height, his recklessness would in turn assert itself, and these two would inevitably try to span the impassable gulf between them, when Brandon, at least, would go down in the attempt. His trouble, however, did not make a mope of him, and he retained a great deal of his brightness and sparkle undimmed by what must have been

an ache in his heart. Though he tried, without making it too marked, to see as little of Mary as possible, their meeting once in a while could not be avoided, especially when one of them was always seeking to bring it about. After a time, Mary began to suspect his attempts to avoid her, and she grew cold and distant through pique. Her manner, however, had no effect upon Brandon, who did not, or at least appeared not, to notice it. This the girl could not endure, and lacking strength to resist her heart, soon returned to the attack. 

Mary had not seen Brandon for nearly two weeks, and was growing anxious, when one day she and Jane met him in a forest walk near the river. Brandon was sauntering along reading when they overtook him. Jane told me afterwards that Mary's conduct upon coming up to him was pretty and curious beyond the naming. At first she was inclined to be distant, and say cutting things, but when Brandon began to grow restive under them and showed signs of turning back, she changed front in the twinkling of an eye and was all sweetness. She laughed and smiled and dimpled, as only she could, and was full of bright glances and gracious words. 

She tried a hundred little schemes to get him to herself for a moment-the hunting of a wild flower or a four-leaved clover, or the exploration of some little nook in the forest toward which she would lead him-but Jane did not at first take the hint and kept close at her heels. Mary's impulsive nature was not much given to hinting-she usually nodded and most emphatically at that-so after a few failures to rid herself of her waiting lady she said impatiently, "Jane, in the name of heaven don't keep so close to us. You won't move out of reach of my hand, and you know how often it inclines to box your ears.' 

Jane did know, I am sorry for Mary's sake to say, how often the fair hand was given to such spasms; so with this emphasized hint she walked on ahead, half sulky at the indignity put upon her, and half amused at her whimsical mistress.

Mary lost no time, but began the attack at once. "Now, sir, I want you to tell me the truth; why do you refuse my invitations and so persistently keep away from me?

She gave a fluttering little laugh, and, with hanging head, continued: "Tell me, is not the king's sister of quality sufficient to suit you? Perhaps you must have the queen or the Blessed Virgin? 

Tell me now?" And she looked up at him, half in banter, half in doubt. "My duties" began Brandon. "Oh! bother your duties. Tell me the truth.” "I will, if you let me," returned Brandon, who had no intention whatever of doing anything of the sort. "My duties now occupy my time in the evening-" "That will not do," interrupted Mary, who knew enough of a guardsman's duty to be sure it was not onerous. "You might as well come to it and tell the truth; that you do not like our society." And she gave him a vicious little glance without a shadow of a smile. Brandon, who was on the rack. "Please do not think it. I can not bear to have you say such a thing when it is so far from the real truth." "Then tell me the real truth."

He had been so perilously near speaking words which would probably have lighted, to their destruction to his, certainly -the smoldering flames within their breast that it frightened him, and the manner in which he spoke was but a tone giving utterance to the pain in his heart.

Mary took it as it sounded, and, in unfeigned surprise exclaimed angrily: "Leave you? Do I hear aright? I never thought that I, the daughter and sister of a king, would live to be dismissed by a-by a-anyone." "Your highness" began Brandon; but she was gone before he could speak.

He did not follow her to explain, knowing how dangerous

such an explanation would be, but felt that it was best for them both that she should remain offended, painful as the thought was to him.

Of course, Mary's womanly self-esteem, to say nothing of her royal pride, was wounded to the quick, and no wonder.

Poor Brandon sat down upon a stone, and, as he longingly watched her retiring form, wished in his heart he were dead.

This was the first time he really knew how much he loved the girl, and he saw that, with him at least, it was a matter of bad to worse; and at that rate would soon be worst. 

Now that he had unintentionally offended her, and had permitted her to go without an explanation, she was dearer to him than ever, and, as he sat there with his face in his hands, he knew that if matters went on as they were going, the time would soon come when he would throw caution to the dogs and would try the impossible-win her for his own. Caution and judgment still sat enthroned, and they told him now what he knew full well they would not tell him after a short timethat failure was certain to follow the attempt, and disaster sure to follow failure. First, the king would, in all probability, cut off his head upon an intimation of Mary's possible fondness for him; and, second, if he should be so fortunate as to keep his head, Mary could not, and certainly would not, marry him, even if she loved him with all her heart. The distance between them was too great, and she knew too well what she owed to her position. There was but one thing left ---New Spain; and he determined while sitting there to sail with the next ship.

The real cause of Brandon's manner had never occurred to Mary. Although she knew her beauty and power, as she could not help but know it--not as a matter of vanity, but as a matter of fact-yet love had blinded her where Brandon was concerned, and that knowledge failed to give her light as to his motives, however brightly it might illumine the conduct of other men toward whom she was indifferent. 

So Mary was angry this time; angry in earnest, and Jane felt the irritable palm more than once. I, too, came in for my share of her ill temper, as most certainly would Brandon, had he allowed himself to come within reach of her tongue, which he was careful not to do. An angry porcupine would

have been pleasant company compared with Mary during this time. There was no living with her in peace. Even the king fought shy of her, and the queen was almost afraid to speak. 

Probably so much general disturbance was never before or since collected within one small body as in that young Tartar- Venus, Mary. She did not tell Jane the cause of her vexation, but only said she "verily hated Brandon," and that, of course, was the key to the whole situation. 

After a fortnight, this ill-humor began to soften in the glowing warmth of her heart, which was striving to reassert itself, and the desire to see Brandon began to get the better of her sense of injury. 

Brandon, tired of this everlasting watchfulness to keep himself out of temptation, and, dreading at any moment that lapse from strength which is apt to come to the strongest of us, had resolved to quit his place at court and go to New Spain at once. He had learned, upon inquiry, that a ship would sail from Bristol in about twenty days, and another six weeks later. So he chose the former and was making his arrangements to leave as soon as possible.

He told me of his plans and spoke of his situation: "You know the reason for my going," he said, "even if I have never spoken of it. I am not much of a Joseph, and am very little given to running away from a beautiful woman, but in this case I am fleeing from death itself. And to think what a heaven it would be. You are right, Caskoden; no man can withstand the light of that girl's smile. I am unable to tell how I feel toward her. It sometimes seems that I can not live another hour without seeing her; yet, thank God, I have reason enough left to know that every sight of her only adds to an already incurable malady. What will it be when she is the wife of the king of France? Does it not look as if wild life in New Spain is my only chance?"

I assented as we joined hands, and our eyes were moist as I told him how I should miss him more than anyone else in all the earth-excepting Jane, in mental reservation. 

I told Jane what Brandon was about to do, knowing full well she would tell Mary; which she did at once. 

Poor Mary! The sighs began to come now, and such small vestiges of her ill-humor toward Brandon as still remained

were frightened off in a hurry by the fear that she had seen the last of him.

She had not before fully known that she loved him. She knew he was the most delightful companion she had ever met, and that there was an exhilaration about his presence which almost intoxicated her and made life an ecstasy, yet she did not know it was love. It needed but the thought that she was about to lose him to make her know her malady, and meet it face to face.

Upon the evening when Mary learned all this, she went into her chamber very early and closed the door. No one interrupted her until Jane went in to robe her for the night, and to retire. She then found that Mary had robed herself and was lying in bed with her head covered, apparently asleep. Jane quietly prepared to retire, and lay down in her own bed. The girls usually shared one couch, but during Mary's ill-temper she had forced Jane to sleep alone.

After a short silence Jane heard a sob from the other bed, then another, and another. "Mary, are you weeping?" she asked. "Yes." "What is the matter, dear?" "Nothing," with a sigh. "Do-you wish me to come to your bed?" "Yes, I do." So Jane went over and lay beside Mary, who gently put her arms about her neck. "When will he leave?" whispered Mary, shyly confessing all by her question. "I do not know," responded Jane, "but he will see you before he goes." "Do you believe he will?" "I know it;" and with this consolation Mary softly wept herself to sleep.

After this, for a few days, Mary was quiet enough. Her irritable mood had vanished, but Jane could see that she was on the lookout for some one all the time, although she made the most pathetic little efforts to conceal her watchfulness. 

At last a meeting came about in this way: Next to the king's bed-chamber was a luxuriously furnished little apartment with a well-selected library. Here Brandon and I often

went, afternoons, to read, as we were sure to be undisturbed. 

Late one day Brandon had gone over to this quiet retreat, and having selected a volume, took his place in a secluded little alcove half hidden in arras draperies. There was a cushioned seat along the wall and a small diamond-shaped window to furnish light.

He had not been there long when in came Mary. I can not say whether she knew Brandon was there or not, but she was there and he was there, which is the only thing to the point, and finding him, she stepped into the alcove before he was aware of her presence.

Brandon was on his feet in an instant, and with a low bow was backing himself out most deferentially, to leave her in sole possession if she wished to rest. "Master Brandon, you need not go. I will not hurt you.

Besides, if this place is not large enough for us both, I will go. I would not disturb you." She spoke with a tremulous voice and a quick, uneasy glance, and started to move backward out of the alcove. "Lady Mary, how can you speak so? You know-you must know-oh! I beg you-" but she interrupted him by taking his arm and drawing him to a seat beside her on the cushion. 

She could have drawn down the Colossus of Rhodes with the look she gave Brandon, so full was it of command, entreaty and promise. "That's it; I don't know, but I want to know; and I want you to sit here beside me and tell me. I am going to be reconciled with you, despite the way you treated me when last we met. I am going to be friends with you whether you will or not. Now what do you say to that, sir?" And now let us settle back into a cold-blooded discussion of the realities and bristling difficulties attending this phase of sex play called "reciprocal fondness". Every man and woman who are enmeshed in its thrills and vicissitudes ought to be fair enough with themselves to look the facts squarely in the face. They ought to be willing to study the history of their particular case, make a proper diagnosis of it, prescribe the medicine, and then take their own medicine.

A history of the case ought to show that it is largely instinctive and entirely sentimental,-the reason why every fellow does not fall in love with the same girl. A proper diagnosis ought to reveal to the parties involved that, although it is absolutely essential to a successful marriage, its fierceness may not permit either one to recognize glaring shortcomings in the other party until it is too late. The prescribing and

taking of the medicine may disclose to both of them that it had no chance of being anything more than "puppy love."

Association and companionship for a considerable length of time may point out to them in their lucid moments of reasoning whether there is a likelihood of permanent fondness resulting, or merely a familiar kissing affair. Permanent fondness may be depended upon as a basis for a love that will ripen after marriage. Evanescent partiality will automatically explode unless the infatuated parties impulsively procure the marriage license, approach the altar, and are tied hand and foot before the fuse of due consideration has had time to touch off the "puppy love" bomb-in which case, of course, they must be good sports and live it out in misery or resort to the divorce courts for relief. If unfortunately children have come to them before the explosion, husband and wife will come to the full realization that their weak natures have conspired not only to render their own lives miserable, but to put a blight on their own flesh and blood.

When man and woman become blindly infatuated, each, through inability to see clearly or to make correct analysis, is pitifully helpless to pierce the armor of the other. Insincerity and "lie-ability" have been developed and practiced by man as a race for so many generations that they have become instinctive reflex actions with him. Duplicity is as intricately and inevitably a part of man as his own conceit. Woman is not far behind him in the possession and exercise of these well-known traits, but they are not so noticeable in her because of the time-honored-historical privilege of man to "talk" and "pay compliments to the opposite sex". As a consequence of this restraint that has been placed on woman by custom and man in the matter of offense in romance, she has been put at a serious disadvantage in displaying her wares, and has been compelled, purely as a matter of self defense, to develop keen aptitudes for meeting lies with lies-not malicious ones, but the lies a man tells his sweetheart-and quits telling them to her when she is his wife. In most cases she is overpowered

by his superior weapons and armor and they go to the altar, both firmly believing that they are "in love". At the end of the honeymoon, which comes when they kiss only in the presence of their friends and relatives, they can begin to see and understand that "love" comes only after marriage. If it has not begun to sprout, at least, at the end of the honeymoon, they will have to generate immediately a wonderful store of reason, will and judgment to take its place. Even then it will be an exchange of pure country butter for oleomargarine.

In an alarming number of cases, the desire of the couple to keep hidden from their friends and relatives the miseries they are daily experiencing will be overwhelmed by infelicities of caldron proportions. A theory will be exploded by a fact, and the rumblings of a divorce tragedy will be heard in the offing. Nothing strange about it at all. Of course, they are willing to pay. They are no more unwilling to part with their money than they were when they bought their marriage license. Their viewpoint is as different as if they were physically another couple standing before him. They are, however, not buying the lawyer's time, theories, advice or knowledge of the law,they are buying their own freedom-their happiness, and it is just as big a "buy" as their married happiness appeared to be.

CHAPTER II SEXUAL CONGENIALITY 

This consideration in contemplation of marriage is undoubtedly the most perplexing problem that confronts the person who is thinking seriously of entering the most important contract in life, and probably takes up more time of the normal human being, male or female, from the age of sexual consciousness to old age than any other emotion permeating the mind, body and soul of man. 

In modern times the problem seems to be presenting itself with greater difficulties with each succeeding generation, and it is extremely doubtful whether the sum total of human knowledge and experience can offer anything close to a complete solution of it. The nearest solution will undoubtedly come through men and women, preferably fathers and mothers, who have gained vast stores of accurate information from reliable scientific sources, from their own experiences as normal men and women, and from what they have learned from other men and women with different varieties of experience under intense emotional conditions.

A purely scientific treatment of the subject of sexual relationship is so important that no appreciable advance can be made without using it as a basis for the more understandable social treatment of it. The scientific treatment is necessary for the education of men and women who can and will think sanely on the subject, and the social treatment is the means by which the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, can be disseminated effectively among the great mass of young men and women, who, on account of their youth, inexperience and inability to think sanely on the subject because of warped

judgment in sentiment, are woefully ignorant about practically all sexual matters.

Any sore in the human family produced by artificial means can be stamped out by an organized effort of the majority, but sexual instinct and sentimental fondness are the very roots of life itself, and the entire future of humanity depends not on the suppression, but on the nourishing and sanely controlling of them.

It must not be taken for granted that an effervescent personality attraction is anything like a sure index of sexual congeniality, and even though every other consideration in contemplation of marriage has been frankly discussed to the entire satisfaction of both parties, they are still face to face with the question of whether or not it is possible for either one to positively recognize the sexual capacity and intensity of the other. If we can not through scientific investigation and social education enable men and women to recognize the existence or non-existence of sexual distinctness, we shall have to continue to permit them to marry and blunder just as a large and alarming number of them are doing. 

To permit prospective bride and groom to test the reality of sexual compatibility by actual experience would, at first blush, seem to be a possible and logical solution of the problem, but it is positively not to be thought of for moral and aesthetic reasons. Such indulgence before marriage would be certain to result in bitter resentment after marriage and would inevitably destroy the entire effect of the original fondness. The tasting of the tree of knowledge before marriage has probably served as the actual basis of more divorce suits than any other element in married life. Such a procedure would undoubtedly destroy most of the ideals of a lasting love, for the two-fold reason that it would permit the man to solve the holy mystery of a pure woman prematurely, and plant the germ of a lurking suspicion in him after marriage that some other man could have solved the same mystery under similar circumstances. The man's true nature would

For these reasons, this consideration in contemplation of marriage is discussed frankly in the firm belief that the lure of sexual compatibility has brought about the marriage of more couples than any other consideration. Men especially are so blinded by it that they can not see that anything else is necessary to complete happiness in married life. The sexual instinct is the dynamic force that has been behind all human activities and emotions since the beginning of time. It is sexual desire that builds churches, inspires musing for the composition of poetry, music and art; without it all religious, economic and political forces would fall into hopelesss disorganization and inevitable decay. THE JEALOUS WOMAN 

In order to get at the very source of jealousy in woman, it is necessary to distinguish sharply between coquetry and flirtation.

Coquetry is confined solely to women, almost wholly unconnected with sexual appetite, and is downright vanity based largely on an inherited ambition to outshine other women in

the presence of men. It is the instrument which makes one woman envious of another and is rarely, if ever, employed to make another woman jealous of her husband.

Flirtation is characteristic of both men and women, and is a communication by actions and veiled words of the arousal of sexual appetite. If the man or woman on whom the flirtation is exerted sends back an affirmative message, the flirtation is understood to be mutual.

The power of making analysis of the cause of her jealousy is very weak in the woman, and although coquetry is found in woman only, and, although the jealous woman can usually recognize flirtation that is exerted directly on her, yet she, if jealously inclined by instinct, seems to be utterly unable to distinguish between coquetry and flirtation when it comes to analyzing a situation between her husband and another woman. The result is she almost invariably interprets it as a flirting of sexual appetites and jealousy of her husband results. 

So jealousy in a woman consists entirely of "exacting exclusive devotion" from her husband. She is not as persecuting as a jealous husband, but harbors it longer.

A rather mysterious form of jealousy that borders dangerously near the line of some sort of insanity is found in the woman who has married a man in complete ignorance of sexual sensation. The thoughts of any sexual relations with her husband or any man is as repulsive to her as a major operation under the knife of a surgeon without anæsthetics. 

She enjoys kissing and fondling from her husband merely as platonic friendship. She is, of course, true and devoted to him and may even have children for him. And yet she will fall into fits of jealousy without the slightest reason. 

The unfortunate man must watch his every move. She will accuse him of intimacy with other women when she knows he has not had a chance to even become acquainted with such women. She quarrels with him in the presence of the children, heaps gross insults on him, especially in the presence of other

people. She even sometimes pays money to a woman of low character to entrap him, in order that she may get "the goods on him". The most mysterious phase in the whole of this womans make-up lies in the fact that her jealousy will be frequently cured by actual knowledge that he has been intimate with another woman.

In exceptional cases her jealousy takes on the form of momentary maniacal insanity, which impels her to the throwing of acid to disfigure the beauty of her rival. This type of jealous woman is considerably more numerous than is generally realized. Womanly intuition has conferred on her a cleverness which shields her jealous disposition from all the world except her husband, of course, and a small circle of intimate associates. Superficial education has developed her into a very clever artist in demanding love and flattery from her husband, but her selfishness never permits her to reciprocate in any way.

Among women she is a great coquette, employing every device known to arouse envy. Among men she simulates flirtation and frequently gives them the impression that she is a modern Venus of voluptuous sensations. In order to extract sympathy from her husband in their home life she frequently feigns physical ailments of all sorts, demanding consultation with physicians, surgeons and specialists. The thinking man, who in a moment of weakness, marries this woman will employ his well-balanced sanity before she has completely dominated him by calling in an expert on psychosis (a disease of the mind), who will have intestinal stamina enough to tell her frankly and forcibly, in the presence of her husband, that sexual hypocrisy and selfishness are her only ailments. In this way she can be helped, but never entirely cured.

PHYSIOLOGICAL SEXUALITY 

The physiological sexualist is guided almost solely by his sexual appetite, and his instinct for detecting the willingness of the female of his species to accept his embraces. The male in the lower animal is excited by and led to the female by odors. He rushes madly down the street, up the alley or through the woods. The female is waiting, and anxiously engages in the sexual congress, with the first male of her species that arrives. The male's sexual appetite is satisfied and the female's maternal instinct is gratified. She will have nothing to do with another male until she has reproduced her species.

The male is satisfied for a short time only, and takes the trail for another anxiously waiting female. 

Physiological sexuality among men and women is similar to that of the lower animals except that human beings are somewhat limited in their activities by conventional restrictions. 

The woman particularly is restrained by public morals and custom from gratifying her instinct for motherhood, except in a state of marriage. These restrictions have compelled her to give careful attention to preventive methods. Even when there is a mutual gratification of sexual appetites between these two physiological sexualists, the elements of genuine sentimental fondness, sympathy and love are entirely absent and wholly unrelated, and nothing but extreme disgust follows. 

Since woman is considerably more pasive in sexual matters, the man frequently experiences considerable difficulties in finding the woman and is driven by the intensity of his appetite to the house of prostitution, where he pays for the service the same as he does for his hair cut and shave. It is pretty well agreed that practically all normal men, at least sometime in their lives, pass through short periods of physiological sexuality.

CHAPTER III CHILDREN AND THEIR RIGHTS 

An absolute agreement that any children that may come to the union shall receive serious consideration before they make their appearance here. 

From the standpoint of humanity, the welfare of the nation, and the happiness of the immediate parties concerned, this consideration can not be too heavily stressed. All nations that have crumbled in the past have done so because too little attention was given the building of human beings. 

The two most important elements in human life are nutrition and reproduction. The race would die out in one generation if either element failed to function effectively. Human beings are kept alive and are enabled to engage in the activities of life by the constant rebuilding of the tissues of the body through the process of eating and digestion, and the gaps in the ranks of the race caused by death must be constantly replenished through the process of reproduction.

Evidently at the first appearance of man on the earth, nature was afraid that he would forget to eat for nutrition's sake, and would not take the trouble to engage in the sexual function for reproduction purposes, because she has provided him with an almost insatiable hunger for both. 

Undoubtedly sexual union is the most thrilling single experience a human being ever injoys in sensual pleasures, and yet eating is almost as pleasurable and can be indulged in sanely much more frequently. So the conclusion must be that more genuine sensual pleasure is to be derived from eating than from any other activity in life. 

This brief mention of the two most important elements in

human life is made to serve as a basis for a discussion of the question of whether or not there is an overwhelming desire in normal men and women to become the parents of children. The inordinate interest in the health, education and general welfare of children shown by many men and women in recent years must not be confused with the desire to be the actual parents of children. Although too much praise and credit can not be given these wonderful men and women who are applying their philanthropic instincts, education and scientific attainments to welfare work, and who are exhausting their energies to the point of physical collapse in very successful efforts to rescue unfortunate children from ill-health, ignorance and immorality, it must be said in all truthfulness that these almost superhuman activities have very little, if any, influence on the children who are yet to make their appearance here. 

So far as guiding any circumstances that are in control in the birth of better children, their efforts are practically analagous to the locking of the barn after the horse has been stolen. In the words of Professor George E. Dawson:* "Here are two children, brother and sister. The public school is trying to educate them. The money of the community is being expended freely in their behalf. Well-trained and devoted teachers are giving their time, energy and patience to the task of making them fit to live out their lives as individuals and as members of society. But all these efforts of education are being defeated by the poor health, bad eyesight and irregular attendance of the children. Then medical and other expert help is called in, to assist the school in getting at the causes of the children's troubles and removing them. The boy is found to be color-blind and otherwise defective in vision. The girl has a chronic sore on one of her lower limbs. 

Both children have enlarged glands, defective skeletal development, and weak lungs. On the surface, some of those defects seem susceptible of cure; others are doubtful or impossible. 

Pushing the problem further back, it is found that the father died of syphilis and the mother is at present suffering from *"The Right of the Child to be Well Born”, p. 37.

the same disease in a chronic and incurable form. The children are thus the products of diseased parents, and their defects are constitutional, and, to a great extent, beyond relief. What, then, can all the educational and social activities in behalf of these children avail? And such children do but illustrate the problem that every one who works at the foundations of education and social regeneration sooner or later faces."

The theory in good government must always be that its children are liabilities until maturity, and the only justification for the expenditure of vast sums of money in maintaining efficient educational institutions for the training of their minds and bodies, is the expectation that they will become assets as men and women. This governmental responsibility is burdensome enough when the children are overwhelmingly normal. 

What, then, will be the result when the majority of the children come into the world as subnormals?

Past ex- perience in other countries would seem to indicate the slow but sure decay of the nation.

If civilization is to be handed down to our posterity through the process of refinement and education, we must nourish in this generation the primal instincts of sentimental fondness, marriage and sexual union, coupled with an intellectual desire for a better offspring.

Although nature has provided the instinct of hunger for sexual union between husband and wife, with its immediate pleasures and personal contentment, she has also exacted a sort of compensation for these pleasures and contentments by imposing on them the instinct of fatherhood and motherhood. 

The parental instinct is the one great instrument for the perpetuation of the race, and probably the most gnawing misery in the afternoon of life in any normal individual is a constantly recurring thought that this generation will pass on to the next unaccompanied by his own particular species. In other words, life without issue is a defeat not only for the individual but also of nature's favorite instinct.

Men and women are at least periodically beset by the yearning for offspring and nothing else can be substituted to appease that longing. A man who has been rough reared and has been indifferent all his life to the more refining processes of education and culture, may be miraculously changed in every part of his disposition and attitude toward all mankind on becoming the father of a youngster, and, in the words of Dr. C. W. Malchow: "Many women who have been habitually absorbed in their dress, society or profession have become judiciously neglectful of these frivolities when brought to realize the joy and to experience the satisfaction of being a mother. A willfully barren woman is usually the personification of selfishness and egotism, and neither merits nor receives the highest regard, but any woman, however foolish, has set her foot on the ascending ladder when she has ceased to be to herself the most important thing in her world. This step is most effectively taken when she occupies her natural sphere and becomes a real motherthan which there can be nothing more ennobling and without which a contented or desirable mental state in her declining years is inconsistent. A woman cannot be said to be really and truly a woman in all that the name implies until she has become a mother, for it is the state of motherhood that brings out the qualities that are so essentially womanly." 

That the imparted individuality that comes to any father or mother is the most exacting responsibility that comes to either during life is frequently shown by the lavish affections bestowed by men and women who are childless upon the children of other parents, and even upon the animals of the lower order.

The exacting demands of modern social conditions frequently influence wives, who could become mothers of excellent children, to defeat the primal instinct of motherhood by instrumentalities that would be considered nothing short of a heinous crime in the law of criminality. After frequent and

successful attempts to thwart motherhood, they eventually render themselves incapable of it, and thus willfully bring upon themselves the greatest loneliness and misery that women ever experience in voluntary punishment.

Assuming, then, that in all normal men and women there is the instinct and desire for parenthood, the problem resolves itself into the question of how the children shall be well-born. 

It is impossible for children to select their parents, but parents may materially improve the human race by exercising care in the process of reproduction. Health, character and well-balanced sanity must marry health, character and wellbalanced sanity. According to law of heredity, children can not rise above the level or quality of the mixture that brought them into the world.

Assuming that prospective husband and wife are in a state of permanent sentimental fondness and that there is, through examination by experts and by frank discussion between themselves, a likelihood of sexual congeniality, they have still to consider, before they are linked together by the bonds of matrimony, the question of whether or not their children will have a chance to be at least their equals. Surely a thinking man would not deliberately participate in the process of procreation with his wife, or any other woman, if he honestly and firmly believed the result would be a boy who would be inferior to him in body, mentality, or efficiency. Neither would a thinking woman deliberately conceive if she actually thought the result would be a girl inferior to her in any respect. And so the whole problem of the improvement of the human race must be solved, if at all, by a process of education and serious thinking before man and women are intimately associated as husband and wife.

Reproduction among lower animals is brought about by instincts, but men who are experts in breeding guide those instincts. The female of the lower animals participates in the sexual union solely by instinct, and entirely for reproduction

purposes, while the male is guided solely by his instinct to engage in sexual activities with any female of his species when he recognizes that that female is in heat for motherhood. The human female alone, of all the animal kingdom, submits to sexual union solely for sensual pleasure of herself and the male, and, while super-morality may dictate that sexual activities ought not to be indulged in except to replenish the race, the actual practice always has been, and probably always will be, to engage in sexual union more frequently for sensual pleasure than for reproduction purposes, and is all the more reason why husband and wife should premeditate under the most favorable circumstances the act of sexual union which they ardently want and expect to result in the creation of a new human being.

During the courtship days, assuming that there is sentimental fondness and sexual congenialtiy, the prospective husband must ask himself, and the prospective wife must ask herself, the question: "Do I think enough of myself to reproduce?" If both parties honestly answer in the affirmative, this pre-marital consideration is satisfied. If either must honestly answer in the negative, in the interest of the birth of better children, the engagement should be broken.

Even though both have answered in the affirmative, the children must be planned for and not accidental. How would one feel if his parents told him he was an accident? Not even a hint of birth control is intended here, but if the wealth and prosperity of the nation demand that great sums of money be expended in improving the stock in chickens, hogs and cattle, guided by the cold calculation and premeditation of men who are experts in breeding, why should it be illogical for children to demand that they receive serious consideration before they come into this world?

If the progress of the race is to be projected into posterity with sufficient physical fitness to meet the problems of the future, which will inevitably be quite different and equally as tedious as the problems of the present generation, it will have

to come through the health and physical stamnia of the boys and girls who are to be the fathers and mothers of tomorrow. 

The law of self-defense is the "first law of nature," and is probably the greatest and the oldest of human instincts. It is an instinct that has been so universally recognized for so long a time that the criminal laws of all nations provide in substance that an individual who is attacked by a member of his race may repel blow by blow, and if the one attacked has reasonable grounds to believe, and does actually believe, that he is in grave danger of losing his life or suffering great bodily harm, he will be justified in taking the life of his adversary. 

Without this instinct for preservation the human race would never have survived its first generation on the earth. 

Closely linked with this instinct for protection, amounting almost to reflex action, is the desire of the individual to project into the next generation the nearest likeness possible to himself, which can be accomplished only through a fusion of the two instincts of sexual desire and parenthood. 

Superficial examination of this phase of procreation would seem to argue that natural selection in conjunction with these two well known instincts would solve all the problems of eugenics and would regularly bring into the world healthy, intelligent and efficient children. The weakness of such a view lies, of course, in the failure to take into consideration that intellectual efficiency must guide the instincts, because nearly all instincts will run amuck when not controlled by brain cells.

A great deal of time, money and education are applied to the improvement of the stock in hogs, chickens, cattle and other animals for economic advantages. A race horse that wins the derby is probably given more serious consideration previous to his appearance than any other animal on the earth-the human race included. Men have learned from experience that they must control the instincts of the lower animals in order to insure better ones. If the instincts are not controlled, the issue will depend wholly on natural selection

in a very narrowly bounded territory. Disease will creep in and the quality of the animal will decline. In this respect, the life of man is very little different, which leads to the conclusion that physical efficiency is very important in improving the race.

But there is still another heavy responsibility resting on the prospective parents of children. What would it profit the race to improve it immeasurably in physical fitness and yet have the fine physical specimens becoming the criminals of the land? What character, honesty and dependability can be expected of children whose parents are not good citizens? All mental and character traits are undoubtedly highly heritable -not only from immediate parents but also from ancestors a generation or two back. A boy, for example, may be like neither father nor mother in character traits, but may jump back to a grandfather or great grandmother for a weak will power, an impulsive craftiness, or even a criminal disposition. 

If this were not true, how can we account for the following case? : 

A boy twenty years of age, a high school graduate with commendable scholastic attainments, healthy body and excellent personal appearance, previous good character, respected and seemingly respectable among friends and business associates, is dragged from the jail in shackles to the criminal court room, charged with robbery with a gun and an assault with a deadly weapon, with the intent to commit murder. The indictment alleges that he entered a place of business in broad daylight with a mask over his face; that he held up the proprietor of the place at the point of the gun and forced him to give up all the money in the cash register; that an innocent man and regular customer of the place of business came in to make a purchase, and was shot down by this masked twenty year old criminal (fortunately the wounded man recovered after three months in a hospital).

The evidence is so overwhelmingly against him that his attorney employed by his parents advises him to plead guilty

and throw himself on the mercy of the court. The judge imposes the sentence set out by the criminal statute of ten years to life in the penitentiary. The boy's attorney, the prosecuting attorney and the adult probation officer join in a statement of his previous good character, the fine qualities of citizenship of his parents, of their lasting love for each other and for the boy. The judge asks the boy why he did such a terrible thing, and the answer comes through tears and mingled with the heartbroken sobs of his father and mother: "I don't know! There has been sticking in me from my early boyhood days an impulse to impose on somebody for material advantage. I needed a little more money than I was earning or my parents could give, and my weakness urged me on.

I ex- pected to get $400.00 or $500.00 and meant to pay it back sometime. I deserve the punishment you have meted out to me, but I feel sorry for dad and mother, who have been good to me and given me every advantage they possibly could. I don't know what made me shoot at Mr. Johnson in the store; it was the first time I had ever fired a pistol."

And so we have many cases in which parents are physically and morally fit and yet the direct issue of their bodies may, by traits of character inherited from ancestors back a generation or two, become a heavy social burden. 

Well, what is the remedy? It is this: The prospective bride and groom must know something definite about the antecedents of each other, otherwise pre-existing but unknown hereditary traits and taints, such as social diseases, the weakness of tuberculosis or even criminal tendencies, may spring from innocent parents and poison a new generation. 

The difficult phase of the problem of better children comes to the forefront in the marriage in which the husband and wife are deeply in love and yet, due to taints and disorders in themselves, can not procreate children of good quality. If iaws can not be enacted to prohibit their marriage, or, if after marriage they can not be influenced to avoid reproduction by preventive means, their children, society and posterity will

have to continue to suffer. The possibility of such marriages and consequently children of such bad quality is all the more reason why men and women should seriously consider the children before the marriage actually takes place. 

Of course, the man and woman who are in a state of reciprocal sentimental fondness, are agreed that there is a strong probability of sexual compatibility, and that they will seriously consider the children before they make their appearance, will never earnestly contemplate any such thing as divorce or trial marriage, because they will be too sure of themselves to devote any attention to fallacies.

Dr. Harry Emerson Fosdick touches heavily on the foolishness of trial marriage in the following fashion: "Personally, I do not for a moment believe that the American people in the long run will consent to that exchange. If they do consent to it, the degeneration of American civilization will come on apace. For the attitudes and actions involved in trial marriage are, first, psychologically disruptive to the individual, and, second, socially ruinous to the nation. "The psychological aspect of the matter is primary. Nature has been at work a long time on the sex-problem, and we can not by any swift, slick changes outwit what nature has had in mind. Today an immense amount of cheap thought and talk is going the rounds, of novels, dramas, movies, magazines, and conversations, to the general effect that sex is an imperious urge toward personal pleasure in general and bodily gratification in particular. "The plain fact, however, is that from nature's standpoint sex is only a lure to get two people to love each other deeply enough and long enough to bring up children. What nature wants is children, and because nature always makes attractive the road to the goal she seeks she has allured men and women into family life by pleasant paths. What she was getting at, however, was not the pleasure of the path, but the goal of the children, and anybody who makes it his principle of action to steal the gratification of nature's lure without fulfilling nature's purpose is committing a psychological theft on which nature wreaks inevitable vengeance.

"The whipper-snapper in phychology may speak to contrary; the seers tell the truth. "Trial marriage, therefore, in its ordinarily accepted meaning, is psychologically a truncated, arrested, balked experience. It means emotional repression and disruption. Some people assume that because the idea is new it is an advance. 

As well assume that being killed by an airplane or going to war with poison gas is an advance. "Nature is too old at this sex-game to be so easily circumvented. And what she signifies by the game and intends to get out of it is clear. The complete sex-experience means falling in love, learning the secret of staying in love, mastering the art of growing up in love, enlarging the love-life into a family of children, until within the green cusp of a physical relation grow the flower and fruit of a spiritual union." 

NORMAL CHILDREN 

When children come to the lasting love marriage, the wife's sexual appetite is turned almost completely into maternal love, but without the loss of any love for the husband. Both husband and wife will be just as devoted to the children as they are to each other, and neither will be jealous of the other's devotion to the children. The birth of the child will be the greatest joy either has ever experienced, and will at the same time be the acid test of the permanency of their love. The mother who does not have an abiding love for her child is a most unnatural being, and while the fathers' love is probably never as great as that of the mother, he will always be overjoyed with the pride of his fatherhood, and with the thought of being the husband of the woman whom he loves, and who is the mother of his child.

If a man from one of these groups, and woman from another group become entangled in the meshes of romance, they are bound to experience considerable difficulty, because their religious experiences come from diametrically opposed sources. 

This is in the very nature of the case. There is bound to be conflict between any two of these groups, which does not prove that one is right, or the other wrong. It does, however, show rather conclusively that there are two distinct types of religious approach. SECONDARY OBSTACLE 

Of course, the secondary obstacle in religious affiliation

arises when children are born to mixed marriages. The Traditionalist group will insist that the children be trained in its religious philosophy. The Protestant or Prophetic group will insist that the child be given a more scientifically accredited view of Christian principles and precepts. The result is almost invariably tragic, for the child is torn between loyalty to one theory of religion, and loyalty to another theory absolutely opposed.

A ROMAN CATHOLIC VIEW 

"There is no subject that is more continually coming to the surface in these days than that of mixed marriages. The difficulties they involve may be treated lightly, in the manner we have described above; or they may be disposed of with considerabie qualms, and only when the reasons for such marriages appear to be unsurmountable, that is when mutual affection has gained the mastery to such an extent as to make it out of the question for either side to draw back. 

That there is something to be said for mixed marriages would probably be generally admitted, but that the Church reprobates them is shown by the difficulty with which she grants dispensations for them. We can never forget-for the Scripture never ceases to remind us of it-the high destiny to which we are called as children of God's Church. 'You are the salt of the earth', were our Lord's words to His disciples, and again we read: 'Because thou art a holy people to the Lord thy God; the Lord thy God hath chosen thee to be His peculiar people of all the peoples that are upon earth. and hath loved you '(Deut. vii. 6, 8). This is a great destiny-a noble vocation. Should we not hesitate before we risk anything who have so much to lose? And risk it we must if we marry out of the Catholic

Church. Moreover-as the Bishop of Salford has recently pointed out in a powerful letter to his diocese on the evils of mixed marriages-it is a most prolific source of leakage in the Church, and not only an evil in itself but leads to others: to loss of faith, unhappiness in the home, and tepidity and indifference in the discharge of religious duties.

It would be only wise for one about to marry a non-Catholic to face certain facts which sooner or later will in all probability force themselves upon him. One is that this is a drawback in life which tends to get more serious as the years advance. Another, that the punishment-or, if this be too severe a word, the disadvantages are apt to fall more heavily on the children of the marriage than on the parent. Further, can we doubt that life will present itself under entirely new conditions to a man or woman who has taken this step? Another may take life easily. How can he, if he has any faith, with so much at stake?

Controversy, at best but a broken reed, too often piercing the hand that leans on it, would indeed be fatal between husband and wife. Common sense shows that by one means only conversions can be effected: that is, by earnest, humble, unremitting prayer, accompanied if possible by practices of penance, for we know by the words of our Lord that certain crowning graces are obtained only 'by prayer and fasting'. It is not too much to say that there have been wives and husbands who have devoted their lives to this object, and counted themselves blessed when, at the eleventh hour, the grace has been granted to their prayer. But can a life so spent, in continual stress of mind, in constant watchfulness over self -for does not example rank next in importance to prayer?can such a life be reckoned a happy one? In addition to the positive drawback, there are negative ones, which are of no less salient a character. The beautiful picture drawn for us by Tertullian shows what married life is between two of the same faith. It is, in love, peace, and perfect sympathy, a foretaste of heaven. How much of this would be wanting when husband and wife dare not speak, except in general terms which mean so little, on the thoughts which must or should, lie nearest to both their hearts!

Besides the question of happiness there is the still more important one of duty, especially as it affects the lives for which parents are responsible to God, namely the children born of such marriages. It is difficult to exaggerate the importance of oneness of faith in the parents as a factor in the bringing up and development of the spiritual life of the children. It is a delusion, though not an uncommon one, that

children are born good. On the contrary, they have the germs of much evil in them. Their natural inclination is to resist good impressions; to assimilate the evil they come across rather than the good. The 'broad way' our Lord speaks of is no less attractive to the child, as far as he knows it, than to the man. But it is here that the influence of parents comes in. They have the first chance with their children, if they only know how to make use of it.

A Christian mother seizes her infant's first thoughts and actions, and dedicates them to God. She does not leave this to servants, though if she has a pious substitute in the nursery -a matter, indeed, of vital importance the work of training a child is carried on there also. She takes her child to church, into the presence of the Blessed Sacrament, so that he may not only derive benfit from that Divine Presence from his earliest youth but his first conscious impressions may go back to it, and be associated with it. She is careful not to overdo prayer and spiritual exercises, so as to connect religion in a child's mind (which otherwise would inevitably be the case) with tedium; on the contrary, following the Church's example, she seeks to invest it with what appeals to his heart and imagination. There are a hundred ways in which a mother can interest her children in subjects which have to do with God and His service; for the Catholic Church puts such means without number into her hands the crib at Christmas, the crucifix, Bible-history, the stories of the Saints and Martyrs, all of which are infinitely attractive to children if properly treated.

CHAPTER V MINOR CONSIDERATIONS 

Compatibility in temperaments:

It is the commonest kind of human nature for both parties to studiously avoid exhibiting any weaknesses in disposition during courtship days. After marriage she may make the startling discovery that he is easily angered on occasion, and his disillusionment will come when he learns that she is actually irascible in the presence of company, particularly at bridge parties. This matter of dispositions should be tested out in every possible way, without, of course, incurring the risk of inflicting irreparable wounds. However, it is much better in love affairs to be slightly wounded before battle as a conscientious objector than to be mortally wounded in the conflict of domestic infelicities. 

A certainty that neither her people nor his people will interfere with them to the extent of making them unhappy: 

He must bear in mind that she is her mother's daughter, but that he is not his mother-in-law's son. Blood is thicker than water. "A son is a son until he gets a wife, but a daughter is a daughter throughout life." Serious consideration of the probability of smooth sailing with "in-laws" should be had before the engagement is announced. One does not marry his wife's relatives, but he is a fool if he thinks he is not going to be associated with them more or less throughout life even after divorce, if there are children. This applies also to the wife in her association with her husband's relatives either by consanguinity or affinity. It is pretty well agreed that relatives by affinity are the more deadly.

A fondness for the same social activities-culture, dancing, cards, theatre, tennis, swimming, etc.:

Many otherwise happy couples have spent many miserable hours, before and after marriage, either because neither was interested in any social activity, or because they were not mutually interested in the same activity. Social activities should not only not abate after marriage, but should intensify. 

Misery may love company, but good fellowship demands variety.

He must be engaged in a legitimate business, and one which she can and does respect:

If he is a motion picture actor, lawyer, doctor or dentist, she must be reconciled to the fact that he will be rather intimately associated with other girls and women, and that she will have to guard constantly against an attack of the green monster of jealousy. If he is wage earner, she must be prepared to live in moderate circumstances for at least a considerable length of time, and must never injure his feelings by reflecting on his earning capacity. In all cases, she will be presumed to have entered the relationship with her eyes wide open as to his occupation, its dignity, and his financial ability to provide for them in their station in life.

CHAPTER VII DIVORCE IN ILLINOIS Points of Law and All the Necessary Steps in a Confessed Case Grounds for divorce: 

"In every case in which a marriage has been, or hereafter may be contracted and solemnized between any two persons, and it shall be adjudged, in the manner hereinafter provided, that either party at the time of such marriage was, and continues to be naturally impotent; or that he or she had a wife or husband living at the time of such marriage; or that either party has committed adultery subsequently to the marriage; or has wilfully deserted or absented himself or herself from the husband or wife, without any reasonable cause, for the space of two years; or has been guilty of habitual drunkenness for the space of two years; or has attempted the life of the other by poison or other means showing malice, or has been guilty of extreme and repeated cruelty; or has been convicted of felony or other infamous crime or has infected the other with a communicable venereal disease, it shall be lawful for the injured party to obtain a divorce and dissolution of such marriage contract."

Residence: 

"No person shall be entitled to a divorce in pursuance of the provisions of this act, who has not resided in the state one whole year next before filing his or her bill or petition, unless the offense or injury complained of was committed within this state, or whilst one or both of the parties resided in this state." 

Jurisdiction:

"The circuit courts of the respective counties and the Superior Court of Cook County shall have jurisdiction in all cases of divorce and alimony allowed by this act." 

Venue: 

"The proceedings shall be had in the county where the complainant resides, but process may be directed to any county in the state."

Hearing on bill confessed: 

"If the bill is taken as confessed the court shall proceed to hear the cause by examination of witnesses in open court, and in no case of default shall the court grant a divorce, unless the judge is satisfied that all proper means have been taken to notify the defendant of the pendency of the suit, and that the cause of divorce has been fully proven by reliable witnesses. 

Whenever the judge is satisfied that the interests of the defendant require it, the court may order such additional notice as equity may seem to require.” 

Collusion-both parties guilty, etc.: "No confession of the defendant shall be taken as evidence unless the court or jury shall be satisfied that such confession was made in sincerity and without fraud or collusion to enable the complainant to obtain a divorce."

Q. Do you know the nature of an oath? 

A. I think so. 

Q. Do you understand that it is very wicked and against the law to hold up your right hand in court and swear to tell the truth, and then tell a falsehood on the witness stand? 

A. Yes, sir. 

Q. Is the lady who was just now on the witness stand your mother? 

A. Yes, sir. 

Q. Is John Doe your father? 

A. Yes, sir. 

Q. Do you understand that your mother is suing your father for divorce on the grounds of drunkenness? 

A. Yes, sir. 

Q. Have you seen your father intoxicated in the last two years? 

A. Yes, many times. 

Q. How has he treated you, your mother and your brother when he was intoxicated? 

A. He has kicked and slapped me and my brother, Henry, many times, and threatened to drive all of us away from home. 

Q. If the Judge grants your mother a divorce, do you want to go and stay with her? 

A. Yes.

Judge: Take your certificate of evidence (which means that he grants the divorce).

Mary Doe's solicitor now pays the court reporter a fee for taking down the evidence, and lays before the judge a copy of the decree, which the solicitor has previously prepared in his office (in fact, he has prepared four copies of the decree,one for the signature of the Judge, one for the complainant, one for the defendant, and one for the files in the solicitor's office). This cause having come on to be heard upon the bill of complaint herein; and the defendant having been duly served with process of summons in this cause, more than ten days prior to the first day of the present term of this court, and having been three times called in open court, to appear, except, demur plead or answer the complainant's bill, came not, but herein made default; and the court having ordered that the said bill of complaint and the matters therein contained be taken as confessed; and the court having heard the oral testimony of witnesses, sworn and examined in open court; and having heard the arguments of counsel, and being fully advised in the premises, on consideration thereof, doth find that the defendant has been guilty of habitual drunkenness for more than two years last past, as charged in the complainant's bill of complaint.

It is therefore ordered, adjudged and decreed, by the court, that the marriage between the complainant and the defendant be dissolved, and the same is hereby dissolved accordingly;

and the parties are, and each of them is, freed from the obligations thereof.

It is further ordered, adjudged and decreed, by the court, that the complainant have the care, custody, control and education of said children, to wit, Louise Doe and Henry Doe, but that the defendant be allowed to see and visit said children at any and all reasonable times and places. 

The court further finds from the evidence in said cause that the complainant herein and the said John Doe, defendant, have made a property settlement between them, which is fair and just to both parties to said cause, and is satisfactory in every particular.

It is further ordered, adjudged and decreed, by the court, that the property settlement made between the complainant and defendant be, and the same is hereby, ratified and affirmed by the court as a complete and permanent settlement of all property rights between the parties, complainant and defendant herein, and that in consideration thereof, said defendant is forever barred from any claim of dower, homestead or other property rights in and to any property now or hereafter owned or possessed by the complainant, and that said complainant, in consideration of the provisions of said property settlement, accepts the same in full settlement and satisfaction of, and is forever barred from, any and all claims of every kind and character, including dower, homestead or rights of inheritance, in and to any property now owned or hereafter acquired by said defendant, John Doe.
