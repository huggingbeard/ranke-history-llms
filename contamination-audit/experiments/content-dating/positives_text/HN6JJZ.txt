}. Foreword

OUR ENGLISH POWER GROWS UP is a companion book to WORKING WITH ENGLISH. The latter, as the introductory book, gave the student an opportunity to develop the power of working with English in the more basic and fundamental forms of sentences, singly or in small related groups as paragraphs. The understanding of basic usages and the development of the skills involved in the interchange of ideas, which might be covered by the term communication, was the objective of the text material and exercises in the first book. The second book aims at a development of the power to use in larger units of communication expression the knowledge and skills so far attained.

Therefore the book begins with a consideration of the four types of purposive communication. 

The exercises and review of essential materials are planned to maintain an habitual employment of correct usages and forms. In brief, this book is definitely functional in its character. It is hoped that the use of it will develop in the pupil a consciousness of the fact that all good expression or composition means clear thinking, proper planning, effective clothing of the ideas, and technical skill. There is material here for such work in formalized grammar as a teacher may feel is necessary for a special group.

However, the authors wish to emphasize that the plan of the work is still based upon the following fundamental principles: 

1. Growth. Power in the use of English must come only as a gradual growth through continuous, understanding practice in such use. 

2. Thinking. Always the drill or exercise is one that involves the communication of thought on the part of the speaker or writer, or the comprehension of thought expressed by the speaker or writer, and of thinking in the process. 

3. Pupil discovery. The pupil is not asked to accept facts, rules, or principles on somebody else's say-so. He is led to develop and discover them for himself. He is eager to get to the facts in the matter, and what he discovers in this manner he does not easily forget. It becomes a part of him. 

4. Pupil interests. Illustrative material and all suggested topics of expression and communication are from current, familiar interests of the pupil-his school life, his home, his friends, his hobbies, his present needs. In fact, much of the work he is asked to do he can actually use and should use for real needs. The pupils are led to feel they are turning out not make-believe work but material that has validity and usefulness. 

5. Individuality. Provision is made throughout for such a variety of work that each pupil can do just the kind and the amount that he needs and wants to do. There is no lock step in the exercises. The work can be done on the individual pace-and-need system or on the class-pace basis, to suit the nature of the group and the method preferred by the teacher. 

6. Self-checking and criticism. The work is designed to lead the pupil into understanding how to check his own work and how to judge intelligently the work of others, basing his judgment on his own knowledge of the function of the expression involved rather than on rules found in a book or promulgated by the teacher. 

The authors wish to express to Miss Marie Howard and Miss Katharine Howard, teachers in the Chicago schools, their appreciation and their great obligation for much of the illustrative material and for their painstaking work in preparing the manuscript.

In Westminster Abbey, burial place of England's literary and historical great, is the tomb of one of the most vivid and probably most unhappy of the figures who have come down to us through history Queen Elizabeth. Upon the tomb in a small glass case is a ring which was presented to the Abbey by an American family not many years ago, as time goes in the Abbey. And this ring has a story which is as interesting as the 

Queen's, though less full.

Everyone knows the story of Elizabeth's unhappy childhood, born the second daughter of Henry VIII, whose political vacillations and rages made the position and even the life of the princess insecure. One day, so the story goes, Henry, in a mood of affection for his tempestuous daughter, gave her a ring. If ever he became angry with her, he promised, if she sent him the ring, it would remind him of his affection for her and turn the royal wrath aside. Whether Elizabeth ever elected to use the ring to save herself is not known, though it seems highly probable that she did. 

At any rate, in the course of time Elizabeth ascended the throne of England, as the second queen ruler, to become in her long reign "the best king the country ever had." 

Late in her life the Queen fell in love with the handsome, ambitious and youthful Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex. In many ways Elizabeth was her father's daughter, and she realized her faults, her violent rages, and hasty decisions. She knew the Earl was ambitious, feared even that he might aspire to the throne. She gave him the ring her father had given her, with the same instructions, hoping it would save them both from some rash and cruel action. 

Elizabeth judged the Earl well, for once he did organize a rebellion and try to seize the crown. The Queen, loving him and fearing him at once, had him sent to the Tower and placed under sentence of death. Up to the very moment of his execution Elizabeth hoped and waited for the ring to be sent, but it never came. Her pride and her sure knowledge of his guilt would not allow her to pardon him and so the Earl's handsome head rolled on the stones of the Tower courtyard, leaving Elizabeth a broken woman with only a few years of life remaining to her. 

It was not until her own death was near that she heard the full story. Lady Nottingham, dying herself, wished to unburden herself of a terrible secret. Carried on a litter to 

Elizabeth's chamber, she told her story. Essex had entrusted her with the ring, asking that it be brought to the Queen, but Lady Nottingham told no one of the fact and hid the ring, leaving Essex to die in the belief that the Queen whom he loved had deserted him. 

It was this bitter knowledge that swept over the Queen as Lady Nottingham whispered the story to her. "Madame, may God forgive you, for I never can," said Elizabeth, and

Lady Nottingham was carried from her presence. 

For many years the ring was lost, but it finally reappeared and it rests now on the tomb of the Queen whom it could not save.

NARRATION 

Here are some samples of narration, or storytelling. Obviously a storyteller wants to catch the interest of his readers or listeners at the beginning of the story and to hold that interest until the 2

end. Here are some questions to ask about these stories or any others you may read or write yourself. If the answer to all of them is "Yes," the story is probably a good one. 

1. Does the story catch the reader's attention and arouse his interest in the first few sentences? 

2. Are the background, the setting, and the situation made clear early in the narrative? 

3. Is there suspense; that is, is there at least one question aroused in your mind that you wish to have answered before the end of the story? 

4. Has the writer omitted all material that has nothing to do with the story? 

5. Has he presented the happenings in an orderly, logical fashion? 

6. Did he stop when he came to the end of his story?

How to Build a Fire* 

In the mountains of Pennsylvania the old backwoodsmen, of which there are very few left, invariably build their fires with dry pine or pitch-pine sticks. 

With their ax they split a pine log, then cut it into sticks about a foot long and about the thickness of their own knotted thumbs, or maybe a trifle thicker; after that they proceed to whittle these sticks, cutting deep shavings, but using care to leave one end of the shavings adhering to the wood; they go round and round the stick with their knife blade making curled shavings until the piece of kindling looks like one of those toy wooden trees one used to find in his Noah's Ark on Christmas morning.

When a backwoodsman finishes three or more sticks he sets them up wigwam form. 

The three sticks, having been cut from the center of a pine log, are dry and maybe resinous, so all that is necessary to start the flame is to touch a match to the bottom of the curled shavings.

Before they do this, however, they are careful to have a supply of small slivers of pitch pine, white pine, or split pine knots handy. These they set up around the shaved sticks, maybe adding some hemlock bark, and by the time it is all ablaze they are already putting on larger sticks of ash, black birch, yellow birch, sugar maple or oak.

For be it known that however handy pitch pine is for starting a fire, it is not the material used as fuel in the fire itself, because the heavy smoke from the pitch blackens up the cooking utensils, gives a disagreeable taste to the food, spoils the coffee and is not a pleasant accompaniment even for a bonfire. 

In the North woods, in the land of the birch trees, green birch bark is universally used as kindling with which to start a fire; green birch bark burns like tar paper. But whether one starts the fire with birch bark, shaved pine sticks or miscellaneous dry wood, one must remember that split wood burns much better than wood in its natural form, and that logs from twelve to fourteen inches are best for splitting for fuel; also one must not forget that in starting a fire the smaller the slivers of kindling wood are made, the easier it is to obtain a flame by the use of a single match, after which the adding of fuel is a simple matter. A fire must have air to breathe in order to live, that is a draught, consequently kindling piled in the little wigwam shape is frequently used.

Sharing Your Opinions 

How many times a day do you yourself use and hear others use expressions like these: "I didn't like that movie at all." "Isn't Jimmie fun to talk to!" "Be sure to listen to this radio program tonight." "I thought Tom was wonderful in the class play." "Here's a book you don't want to miss."

In more than half of what you say or write you express opinions. You may merely state what you think about the movie you saw last night. You may try to convince someone that he, too, ought to see that movie, or you may try to show him that his opinion of the movie is wrong and that it is really a very good movie.

In your reading, too, you constantly meet the opinions of others on the editorial page, in the radio and movie columns, on the book page, in the advertisements of the daily newspapers, in radio talks, in your schoolbooks. 

You have probably learned from experience that to convince another person you must first be convinced yourself and have reasons for your conviction. If you say to a friend, “Let's go to the game Saturday," and he says, "No, I don't think I want to go," the conversation will end there unless you can give some reasons why you both should go. Let us try to think of a few. 

1. It will be the last game of the season. 

2. You owe it to the team to cheer them on. 

3. The standings of both teams indicate that it will be a close game. 

4. You have a new jacket you'd like to wear. 

5. You have nothing else to do on Saturday. 

6. Loyalty to your school demands your co-operation in school activities.

Some of these reasons are strong, others rather weak. If you are wise, you will begin and end with strong reasons, leaving the weaker ones for less prominent middle positions.

Finding Material in the Library 

Do you ever wonder when you look at the vast numbers of books on the shelves of a library just how anyone ever finds anything? Of course you realize, if you have thought about it, that there must be a very efficient system of cataloging. If you know how the books are arranged, you can find what you want without asking any questions.

Let us suppose that you are reading A Tale of Two Cities and you want to learn something about the Reign of Terror. Go to the card catalog. Under the subject "Reign of Terror" you will find the names of all the books in that library dealing with the subject. In the upper lefthand corner of the card you will find a number which will tell you, or the librarian, where to find the book. This is the call number. It is repeated on the back of the book. 

In the catalog are three kinds of cards: subject card, which will tell you where to find information on any subject you are interested in; author card, which will give you the title of a book if you know the author's name; title card, which will give you the name of the author of any book whose title you know. All these cards give you the call number to help you locate the book. 

The cards are, of course, arranged alphabetically. The books are arranged on the shelves according to number. All the books under one number are arranged alphabetically according to author. 

Some libraries arrange biography alphabetically according to subject.

What you have been doing is known as scanning and is sometimes an interesting help in the study of poetry. There are different arrangements of the syllables in a line of poetry and a special name is given to each kind. A few of them are: 

1. One unaccented syllable followed by one accented marked thus: is known as an iamb: 

My heart leaps up when I behold 

A rainbow in the sky. 

2. Two unaccented followed by one accented syllable, marked thus: is called an anapaest:

For the moon never beams, without bringing me dreams 

Of the beautiful Annabel Lee. 

3. One accented followed by one unaccented syllable, marked thus: is called a trochee:

The first two, the iambic and the anapaestic, feet are the most frequently used. Often they appear in the same poem and even in the same line. 

Each unit in this measurement is called a foot or a meter. A line of poetry is named according to the kind of meter and the number of such meters to a line. This line: 

I stand has one iambic foot, or meter, and is therefore called iambic monometer. 

I stand and wait has two iambic feet and is called iambic dimeter. A three-foot line is called trimeter, a four-foot line tetrameter, a five-foot line pentameter, a six-foot line hexameter. Longer lines are named as follows: seven-foot line heptameter, eight-foot line octameter, nine-foot line nonameter. Lines having nine feet are unusual. In technical language, the poet uses figures of speech to say what he wishes to say and to create in the reader the emotion he wishes. There are no new subjects to write about, so any writer must treat old subjects in a new way. It is by the use of figures of speech that a writer gets this fresh approach.

In your chemistry class a pupil analyzes a liquid to find out the elements of which it is made. 

Such knowledge is useful later in making up other substances. In your English class you may do the same thing, for many words of our language are made up of several parts, and it is interesting as well as useful to discover the elements of which such words are composed. Knowledge of the nature of the different parts may be utilized later in understanding and using some of those parts.

For instance, let us analyze the word antebellum. We find: bellum, from the Latin, meaning "war," and ante, from the Latin, meaning "before." Putting the parts together, we get "before war." Now if you look at the sentence He wrote of the South in the antebellum days, you know exactly what is meant. Similarly, antecedent means "that which stands before." 

An element that is fixed before, or in front of, another part might be called a prefix.

Words, like nations and countries, have their history. For example, let us examine the word tantalize. Your dictionary will tell you that it means "to tease by keeping something desirable in view but out of reach." The dictionary also tells you that the verb comes from the name 

Tantalus. If you investigate further, you will learn that Tantalus was a king, in Greek mythology, who for his sins was punished in the Afterworld by being kept in a stream of water up to his neck. Fruit-laden branches hung over him. Eternally hungry and thirsty, he was punished by seeing the water recede when he bent to drink of it and the fruit move up out of reach when he raised his hand to grasp it. 

Perhaps you have heard your mother complain: "You meander along as if you had all day.

Hurry up, son!" The dictionary tells you meander means "to wander listlessly or aimlessly." 

There is a river in Asia Minor, formerly called the Meander, which pursued a slow, winding course. Hence we have the verb meander.

What All Good Expression or Composition Means 

If you have gone to a museum of art or any exhibit of pictures and listened to the comments about them, you have perhaps heard statements like these: 

A. (1) "That one certainly expresses a great idea." (2) "It seems so real." (3) "Notice how balanced the parts are. The composition is fine." (4) "The drawing is very craftsmanlike." (5) "The colors are put on so delicately."

And then when you happened to be at a concert you might have heard similar remarks about some of the selections: 

B. (1) "Wasn't that inspiring! It made you want to offer yourself for the service of your country." (2) "It gave you the story of the growth and struggle of a nation." (3) "Did you notice the arrangement of the three divisions and the harmony? The parts seem to fit together perfectly." 

(4) "The melodic parts just carry you away." (5) "How effectively the orchestra interpreted the music!"

Also perhaps in your literary club you and your fellow members were discussing a new book that some of you had read. Again you might have heard some remarks like the following: 

C. (1) "The book deals with a great human theme." (2) "The story is developed so logically you feel it couldn't have ended any other way." (3) "The author certainly uses wonderful English. (4) "He has a style all his own." 

Now, as a matter of fact, the points brought up by all the quoted remarks in connection with each of the three subjects really are only four in number and apply to all composition or expression. The three situations described above are all examples of communication or expression, each one of the three using a different language, or medium. The first represents communication by means of the graphic arts, the second by means of musical art, and the third by means of the art of the English language, spoken or written. The four essentials that enter into the making of any kind of composition are: 

1. Clear thinking 

2. Organization-arranging, outlining 

3. The style 

4. The technical skill, or effective mechanics

All of which is prefatory to a question: Have you ever known anything to change as much as the distance from the end of a diving board to the water, between the time that you look at it from the side lines with all your clothes on and the time that you stand clad in an unattractive tank suit, shivering and unhappy, ready to dive?

The first dive I ever made was years ago, but memory occasionally brings it back to me, sharp and clear, and I live through the whole horrible experience so vividly that I can almost feel the burlap under my toes again and hear the voice of the coach. That coach has become famous in the years that have passed since I was in his class-in fact, he had a not insignificant measure of fame then-and he has since coached Olympic swimmers and divers and seen the names and photographs of his students in newspapers all over the world. Not mine, though.

Somehow or other I had got myself into an advanced diving class. The hour was perfect; I wanted to stay in that class if it was humanly possible. I appeared the first day looking, I thought, not too unlike other members of the class. A big difference began to manifest itself early in the period, however; everyone else could dive, while I belonged 181

to the school of jumpers-in-while-holding-the-nose. The coach lined us up, told us to do the best dive we could, blew his whistle, and we were off. Jackknives, cannon balls, racing dives followed each other in rapid succession-too rapid, because the line got closer and closer to me. I wavered out onto the board when my turn came, hoping I would have the nerve to go through with it, but fearing I might either make a fool of myself or get hurt. The distance from water to board extended rapidly to at least fifteen feet. A sharp admonitory blast of the whistle moved me forward and closer to the abyss. 

I curled my toes around the burlap, bent my body as I had seen the others do, swerving forward and into the water in a nice, clean, straight-kneed dive-my first and, incidentally, my last. That entire semester I spent the class periods bobbing on that board and into the water at five-minute intervals. The distance no longer terrified me, but not once in the year did I again deliver a decent dive. The best I could achieve was one which was close enough to the real thing so that the coach could watch it without clutching his hair.
