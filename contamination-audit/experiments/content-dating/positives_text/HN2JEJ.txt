PREFACE. 

Under the provisions of AR 605-5, the Congress has opened to the American youth a number of routes for the attainment of a commission as second lieutenant in the Regular Army. With a few notable exceptions the most considerable group commissioned in the combatant branches and certain staff services each year since 1812 has been the class graduated from the United States Military Academy. The exceptions referred to occurred as the result of certain substantial increases in the Regular Army, particularly those which followed the Spanish-American War and the World War. Wartrained emergency officers were accepted into the Regular establishment in great numbers on both those occasions. In addition to these peace-time exceptions, the nation has turned in time of war to its citizen-soldiers for the emergency increments of officers needed to bring the Army strength to the desired totals. Aside from these two types of exceptions, the country has placed its main dependence on West Point to furnish the nucleus of its defense system. From 

1802 to June, 1939, inclusive, the Military Academy has graduated 11,790 men. On the latter date, 6,042 of the graduates were carried on the active list of the Army. The percentage of graduates to total commissioned personnel of the Army was slightly more than 48%.

The West Point system of today, the training and education of its cadets, are well understood by Army personnel. The civilian likewise has in recent years acquired a better knowledge of the facts, as a result of the increased attention given the Military Academy in the columns of the press and the magazines, and notably in Kendall 

Banning's informative "West Point Today", (Funk & Wagnalls).

The route over which West Point has marched to reach its today is less well known. Accordingly, this survey aims to give in brief retrospect the various steps covering developments from earliest days to the present in all the important features of cadet life, education, and training.

The reader will note at once that the survey is intended for the West Pointers, and primarily, for the cadet. Assumption as to the reader's knowledge of the terrain, of the outstanding facts of history and of current conditions are made throughout. The writer has borrowed from an earlier brief sketch, published in 1931, entitled:

In line with the treatment of the preceding pamphlet, the closing section of the present edition is devoted to a series of thumbnail sketches of the foreign counterparts of West Point. It will be noted that in some countries no true equivalent of West Point exists. The officer-candidate in such instances usually undergoes the apprenticeship for his commission while serving in the ranks. His mental and professional equipment is in the nature of training rather than a combination of education and training. Necessarily, any previous educational development which he has acquired through his own efforts is an asset in his efforts to secure his commission. And in some armies at least, advanced education is a prerequisite to his candidacy for a commission.

It is to be regretted that the pamphlet's treatment of the foreign systems of commissioning of officers is so fragmentary in form. 

Two factors are responsible: the difficulty of securing full and dependable information, and the fact that these systems are, in several instances, in a state of flux. Present day war and the feverish preparations for war have placed emphasis on the hasty training of considerable increments of commissioned personnel. Under those circumstances it was deemed best to omit from consideration certain institutions, such as for example the Military Academy of the Provisional Government of the Republic of China, established under Japanese auspices in Peking. In other instances it was found advisable to limit the discussion to general facts, omitting the features

which were apparently transitory in nature. For obvious reasons, the description of Poland's system of commissioning, which appeared in the 1939 edition, is now omitted.

THE UNITED STATES MILITARY ACADEMY AND ITS FOREIGN CONTEMPORARIES I.-West Point: The Influence of Natural History 

Strategic Factors Affecting the Hudson Valley. Before the outbreak of our War of Independence West Point was known to scarcely a handful of Colonials. Even these few knew of it only as an unusual land feature in the wooded wilderness covering the Hudson Highlands. To the crews of the sailing vessels which handled the ever-growing trade between New York and the Albany region West Point was always a nuisance, in high winds a hazard, as the ships maneuvered around the difficult double bend of the channel. Looking up from their work these mariners beheld a scene of wild natural beauty, scarcely touched by man's intrusion. Flanking both sides of the Hudson, the Highlands formed a succession of rugged and irregular mountain masses, running from northeast to southwest with deep valleys intervening. Only the woodsman, the trapper and the occasional farmer who had cleared an isolated plot in some valley or other found anything of interest in the Highlands region. 

The events which followed the Battle of Lexington led swiftly to the day when the Highlands were to play the role predetermined by Nature. The mere mention of West Point brings to the student of history the picture of Washington as a great military leader, of Kosciuszko as a military engineer, of Benedict Arnold as a traitor who all but succeeded in selling West Point and the nation's new-found liberty to the British. The military student, however, has only to look at the West Point landscape to find himself wondering as to the circumstances responsible for its becoming, in Washington's words, the "Gibraltar of America." Its natural strength, its points of weakness as well, are readily apparent in its topography. 

The strategic factors of the geography of the West Point area were two in number. The first laid it wide open to British attack, the second balanced that vulnerability with the means of impregnable defense. From Manhattan to Albany Nature had provided an open watercourse, a "sunken" river, in effect an estuary. British seapower, which had closed the sea-lanes to American sailors, except for a few intrepid spirits like John Paul Jones, had only to establish its dominance over the line of the Hudson to bring the Colonials' resistance to an early end. With the British in control of the river, communications between New England and the central colonies would have been cut. Piecemeal destruction of the Colonial forces must

inevitably have followed. Fortunately, something more than a mere parade of British sea-power up and down the Hudson was necessary to establish control. The banks of the stream too, particularly at certain critical points, had to be in British hands to afford the necessary security for the fleet. And here Nature had balanced the account. Having spawned young America with an Achilles' heel, she graciously favored her offspring with an iron shoe to protect that heel. It remained only for time to show whether the Colonials would use the iron shoe. For more than two years after the Battle of Lexington their ineptitude with this bit of armor left them wide open to disaster, as will be shown; again, in the dark night of Arnold's treason they all but lost it; thereafter, no mistakes were made. 

Dismissing the figure, the iron shoe consisted of hills and mountains lining the banks of the Hudson at intervals. More specifically it was to be found in the Highlands-of-the-Hudson, a northeastwardpitching mountain mass¹ which strikes across the river over the twelvemile stretch from Storm King and Breakneck on the north to Peekskill on the south. Properly defended, the Highlands could be held against anything short of overwhelming force. And so long as they remained in Colonial hands, Colonial artillery and river obstructions could turn back the British fleet. The key to such a defense system lay at West Point. There, for a few hundred yards, the north-south line of the normal stream axis is broken by a double right-angled bend before resuming its normal course. The channel in the bend narrows to half its normal width. As a result, tidal flow and river current (during the ebb) reach a correspondingly higher rate. Moreover, any sailing vessel, after rounding the turn, could anticipate treacherous air currents, with consequent loss of headway and steering difficulties. Dominating the waters of the bend and their approaches is a granite promontory, the West Point of geography. Its existence is in fact responsible for the double diversion of the Hudson gorge. It was in brief the iron shoe of the earlier figure.

Igenious activity at various times, as well as the submergence for millions of years at a stretch in later geological eras, with consequent superimposition of overlaying strata, have elsewhere erased the evidence of Grenville. One such period of plutonic activity toward the close of the Grenville period resulted in the metamorphosis of the sedimentary strata into the granite and gneiss characteristic of the Highlands of today. Under the terrific heat and pressure the earth's crust buckled and reared aloft into the great folds of mountain peaks and ranges. Then, after the subsidence of heat and pressure, the processes of erosion operated for some millions of years, gradually whittling down the mountain heights. 

A detailed account of the subsequent geological history of the Highlands lies beyond the scope of this survey. During the forty million years intervening between the pre-Cambrian era and the Pleistocene period, (the last great Ice age) it appears that the region surrounding West Point slipped below the rising seas on several occasions, there to collect the sedimentary strata which today cover the Grenville gneiss. It is also of interest to note two periods of great geological activity. The first is known to geologists as the Taconic 

Revolution. It produced a mighty range, the remnants of which, according to Miller, exist today in the Green Mountains, the Berkshires, the Hudson Highlands and the Piedmont plateau. It must be realized that no Hudson gorge existed in those days; the Highlands region was a barely distingishable elevation above the vast peneplain, its eastern and southern faces drained by a small, sluggish stream, the early Hudson. All the waters from the Adirondacks and from the area of the present Mohawk Valley poured into the Susquehanna, whose course had not yet cut a gap through the barrier separating it from the Atlantic coastal plain, but instead followed the line of the Great Valley of the Appalachians to empty at last into the Gulf of Mexico. According to this theory, a progressive downward tilting of the Atlantic coastal plain, from west to east, eventually dropped below the seas a vast land area, including most of prehistoric Appalachia. The inundation gradually retracted the continental coast-line until it approximated that of today. The tilting of the surface steepened in corresponding measure the course of the early Hudson, which now, with its tributaries, began to cut a definite channel through the hard rock. Headward erosion, the never-ending process by means of which the headwaters of a stream cut their way backwards and upwards through a drainage shed, finally enabled the Hudson to break through the northernmost barrier of rock, the ridge which at one time united Storm King and Breakneck.

The Age of Ice and Its Effects.-The advent of the Pleistocene epoch (Ice age) interrupted the channel-cutting of the Hudson. It is the last of the great geological events which have left evidence of profound effects on the physiography of the Highlands. The Labrador (or Laurentide) ice sheet, eastern-most and greatest of the three which formed across the width of what is now northern Canada, spread steadily to the south until its toe reached a line which has since been traced through northern Pennsylvania and New Jersey. 

The Champlain-Hudson lobe of the Labrador sheet followed a line of relatively low resistance, the Hudson Valley. Origin of the "Point" and Constitution Island. Two significant land features of West Point, the "Plains" and Constitution Island, merit special consideration. It is highly probable that the axis of the early Hudson of the Cretaceous periods lay slightly east of a line which may now be drawn from the east face of Crow Nest to the cliff face west of the Cadet Hospital. In other words it intersected the area now occupied by the Plain. At some stage the down-cutting process uncovered a rock level so resistant that the channel was deflected to the northeast. Neither the ice sheet which followed nor the ensuing post-glacial river could erase that promontory. The uniform strength of its structure, a close-grained granitized gneiss, carried down through the millions of years from the day when heat and pressure had given it birth, made possible its emergence at last as the key of America's defenses.

The circumstances surrounding the origin of Constitution Island are less clear. Like the West Point promontory, it is composed of northeastward-pitching granitized gneiss. It is probable that at some stage in the development of the Hudson Valley, the main river channel lay east and north of Constitution Island. The geological events responsible for the abandonment of that channel remain a subject of controversy among geologists. The single fact of strategic importance is the survival of Constitution Island to become a vital feature of the West Point defense system.

Present-day Evidences of Geological Action.-Evidences of these geological disturbances of past eras are present in the West Point landscape for those who will read them. In greatest abundance we find the record left by the Pleistocene epoch. Boulders strew the hill-sides where the ice sheet dropped them, glacial scratches on rock faces trace the passage of harder rock under ice pressure. Target 

Hill, northeast of the new North Area is a mass of glacial drift,--sand, gravel, clay and boulders. The rounded, blunted summits of the rocky hills, their "plucked" south slopes, contrasting with their scored and polished north faces, tell a vivid story of the tremendous and inexorable force exerted by the moving river of ice. And in the folds of the bare cliffs exposed today by the contractor's blasting one sees the record of the even greater forces which antedated the Pleistocene, when subterranean heat and pressure moulded the rocks as easily as a sculptor shapes his clay. Since the advent of humankind, the landscape has enjoyed geological quiescence. Except for the never-ending processes of erosion and a barely perceptible tilting and re-elevation in the upper Hudson Valley, little, if any, record is being made in this region today to stimulate the speculations of the geologist who will live a million years hence.

II.-Historical Background-1775-1790 The Highlands Defenses. First Steps.-Long before the outbreak of hostilities between the Colonials and the mother country, the vulnerability of the American cause on the line of the Hudson had been sensed by New York's patriots. However, it was not until after 

Lexington's disaster that the Provincial Congress of New York ordered a survey of the banks of the river with a view to setting up defenses. That body, supported by the Continental Congress, in 

1775 ordered the construction of various forts and redoubts. The principal effort at the outset was made near the river-mouth where the ill-fated Forts Washington and Lee were built. 

Soon after, steps were taken to create the defenses in the High-

lands area, though divided counsels produced more squabbles than forts in the period prior to 1777. Two strong redoubts, Forts Montgomery and Clinton, were constructed at the opposite sides of the mouth of Popolopen Creek (Pooploop's Kill), some four miles south of West Point. A heavy chain, guarded by a log boom, was stretched across the Hudson channel. These defenses were supplemented by the batteries, redoubts, and a cross-channel log boom installed at Martelaer's Rock (now Constitution Island). This island lies on the east (here, north) bank of the Hudson at the West Point river bend. 

Constitution Fort-so-called after the British Constitution-and its water batteries were not well sited. Worse still, all the Highlands forts were too weakly garrisoned. Major dependence for defense of the region was placed on the field army of General Israel Putnam. 

Highlands Campaign of 1777.-The British blow fell in October 

1777, at the very moment when General Burgoyne's northern army was moving from Canada to join hands with the British forces in lower New York. With some 3500 men Sir Henry Clinton eluded Putnam's army, and on October 6th stormed and captured Forts Montgomery and Clinton after suffering heavy losses. The chain at the point (not to be confused with the later West Point Chain) was cut into sections, carried to England, later to render notable service in the protection of the harbor of Gibraltar during the Franco-Spanish siege of "The Rock" (1779-1782). It was probably the first instance of European use of American-made "munitions". The fall of the Popolopen forts was followed the next day by the abandonment of Constitution Fort and the burning and sinking of the two Continental frigates, the Congress and the Montgomery; the former in the mudflats off the southeast end of the island, the latter in mid-channel opposite the present site of Ladycliff Academy. Clinton's victorious sweep carried as far north as Kingston, destroying stores and defensive works en route. 

However, the surrender of Burgoyne temporarily warded off the great danger to the Colonials' cause. Clinton withdrew to New York. 

West Point, Key to the Highlands.-The Colonials were at last fully awake to their danger. Baron von Steuben in a letter to Washington, touching the prospective British plans of campaign, declared: "Whatever means they employ, I am positive their operations are directed exclusively to getting charge of this rock (West Point) and of the river as far as Albany * * * * * on their success depends the fate of America." A resurvey of the Highlands region settled on West Point as the center of the new defense project. The narrowness of the channel within the bend, further restricted by mudflats and rocks at low tide, the difficulty of maneuvering sailing ves-

sels in the vicinity and the defensibility of both shores combined to make the location ideal for the Americans. Nowhere else between 

Albany and Manhattan could an effort by water be stopped so easily. 

However, Clinton's great effort, which in a fortnight had swept over the hundred odd miles between Manhattan and Kingston, showed the need of strong defenses against an attack by land.

The original plans for West Point were largely the work of a French engineer officer, Lieut. Colonel de la Radière. In their execution, a brigade under General Samuel H. Parsons began breaking ground January 20, 1778. Weather conditions could not have been worse (it was the "Valley Forge" winter). In spite of extreme suffering among the men, the construction of gun emplacements for the water batteries was pressed vigorously. Surmounting them, at the summit of the promontory, the stone walls of Fort Arnold (now Clinton) rose gradually. It commanded the river to the north and south; moreover, its guns controlled every immediate approach to the chain defense area. On April 20, 1778 the great chain, some sixty tons in weight and floating on a log boom, was stretched across the channel. It was manufactured at the Sterling Iron Works in the hills to the west of West Point and conveyed in sections to New Windsor (Cornwall). There, under the direction of Captain Machin, the links were forged together, and the whole mass was stretched on the log floats which were to furnish the needed support. A downstream log boom was also placed in position as additional protection against British attack. The chain was finally anchored without mishap. 

Thereafter, with the coming of each winter, it was released from one of its anchorages and beached until the spring break-up of the ice. 

A section of this historic chain is preserved today among the relics at Trophy Point.

Kosciuszko Appointed Engineer-in-Charge.-General Washington had for some time been dissatisfied with the rate of progress on the 

West Point fortifications. On March 16, 1778 he directed Major 

General McDougall to relieve General Israel Putnam in command of the Highlands. Ten days later Tadeusz Kosciuszko arrived at West 

Point, as engineer-in-charge, relieving Colonel de la Radière. 

No better selection could have been made by the Commander-in- Chief. Kosciuszko, a Polish patriot, product of the military schools of Poland, Germany, Italy, and France, had specialized in the art of fortification. Entering the American Army in 1776 as a volunteer, he soon demonstrated his capacities. The simple but wholly sufficient fortifications at Bemis Heights were his work, and the stepping stone to his subsequent assignment to West Point.

The West Point Fortifications.-The student of history and mili tary art can best picture the results of Kosciuszko's work by examining in detail what remains of it today. Time has leveled many of the old ramparts, built of logs, loose stone, rubble and earth; expansion of the Post facilities has sent many more of them down the jaws of the power shovel; the intrusion of the forest has likewise done its share to hide these relics. Enough remains for close study of the details. Then, if the observer places himself at some central point such as the parapet of the reconstructed Fort Putnam and notes the remembered site of each redoubt and battery, the strategy of the defenders becomes clear. 

Beginning at the point below Flirtation Walk where the west (or south) end of the great chain was anchored, successive water batteries and separate platoons controlled the water approaches to the chain. The northernmost, except for a water-level battery at the foot of the east face of Gravel (Target) Hill, was located near the north end of the present railway tunnel. Another was at Gee's Point, a third below the present site of Memorial Hall. The remains of all these works are clearly visible today, save for the Gravel Hill battery, a victim of the W. P. A. in 1938. Surmounting the water batteries was the strong work at the forward edge of the plateau, Fort Arnold (later, Clinton), mentioned above. Fort Sherburne, on the present 

Trophy Point, supported Fort Arnold. On the ridge immediately west and southwest of the plateau (present-day Plain) Fort Webb occupied the present site of the observatory. The 1931 housing project in that area obliterated most of its traces. However at the southern extremity of that ridge the ramparts of part of Fort Wyllys can still be found in the tangled woods. South of Fort Wyllys, on the slope overlooking the present Cavalry Drill Ground, Fort Meigs controlled the southern land approach. Landscape gardening in 1933 wiped out the last vestiges of its earthworks. 

The most imposing work in the system, key fortification of them all, was Fort Putnam, on a high projecting spur west of the present Chapel. Named after Colonel Rufus Putnam, who, with his Fifth 

Massachusetts Infantry began the work in 1778, it was reconstructed as a war monument in 1909, an outstanding example of the military engineer's art of the eighteenth century. 

To make the whole area even more secure, a line of outworks was established on the dominating ridge which lies a mile west and southwest of Fort Putnam. From north to south, Redoubts Four, Three, Two and One stood sentry against any surprise attack. Lastly,

Battery Number One, at the southern end of the ridge was available for long-range downstream fire against naval vessels. The outlines

As already mentioned, Kosciuszko, more than any other man, was responsible for the impregnability of the fortress, as finally completed. It is not difficult to picture the West Point of his day. At the height of the work in the late summer and fall of 1779, when General Washington for four months made his headquarters at West Point, the activity was intense. All the works were manned by detachments from the central garrison encamped on the present Plain. 

Reliefs moved to and from the redoubts, often using the excavated, parapeted routes (still visible on the hillslopes); drills and target practice on the Plain occupied the daylight hours; more than 2600 artisans and laborers worked day and night on the fortifications, supply trains rumbled over the roads, mounted couriers came and went with dispatches. By the spring of 1780 the great task was complete. 

West Point stood invulnerable against any effort which the British forces available in America might bring against it, save one,-treason. 

Appropriately, the spirit of the great designer and director of the work, Kosciuszko, remains here, symbolized by his monument on the parapet of Fort Clinton. Calmly and sternly he looks across the river. There, along a stretch of water scarcely a mile long, and on the rugged slopes which dominated it, had lain the hazard to America's liberty; there his work had served to nullify the danger. 

And as he gazes from his pedestal, his glance passes the spot where he used to find daily recreation from his labor, Kosciuszko's Garden as it is now called, a wooded retreat and spring in a rocky nook below the present Memorial Hall. The steps and walls and other stonework are the product of his leisure. 

The Critical Year-1780.-The summer of 1780 was a critical time in American history. The British, having transferred most of their activities to the Southern Colonies, were almost uniformly successful. The hopeless ineptitude, or worse, of Gates made their task easy. But for the the resistance of Marion, Pickens, and other partisans, that whole region might have fallen under British control. 

Elsewhere in the Colonies the spirit of defeatism was rampant. The public treasury was empty, the individual Colonies were coming for-

ward with neither money nor men. The Continental currency, all but worthless, was depreciating rapidly. A few well-to-do leaders like Washington and Robert Morris had bankrupted themselves by giving their wealth to the American cause. A handful of far-sighted patriots remained in the ranks, ready to give their lives for their country. These men were the core of such resistance to British arms as still remained. It was such conditions that led General Washington to remark: "There has never been, throughout the war, a stage when public dissatisfaction has been so general and so alarming." 

It was, in brief, the ideal time for a second and final British effort to cut off New England, thus ending the war. 

Benedict Arnold Appointed to West Point Command.-General 

Washington, aware of the British intentions, concentrated large numbers of troops in the vicinity of West Point, where Major General Benedict Arnold was in command. Unhappily, Washington had failed to appreciate the effects on Arnold's mind and soul of the many and serious grievances the man had suffered. The Commander-in- 

Chief saw only a brilliant and heroic soldier, who more than any other man was responsible for the defeat and surrender of Burgoyne. With the greatest skill and personal valor he had so handled his pitifully inadequate forces in the wilderness of southern Canada and northern New York as to delay for at least one season the progress of Burgoyne from Montreal to the upper Hudson. The little known naval battle of Valcour Island on Lake Champlain, hopeless in its bid for victory, but all-important in its delay of the British advance, was a typical and significant incident of Arnold's strategy. From time to time the political intrigues of lesser men removed him temporarily from the picture. However three military actions established his reputation as a soldier, whatever his subsequent conduct. In the Mohawk Valley, in August, 1777, he drove St. Leger's army back to Montreal, ending a serious threat to the right flank of Gates' army. He followed that victory with an attack on Burgoyne's army at Freeman's Farm, (Stillwater) September 19. It was a drawn battle, even though 

Gates' refusal to send reinforcements left Arnold outnumbered nearly three to one. At Bemis Heights, on October 7, he won his greatest victory. With Gates sulking in his tent, Arnold, in defiance of orders, took command of the American center, while Morgan struck Burgoyne's right. Once the British attack was broken, Arnold personally led the pursuit which captured a vital section of the British entrenched camp. Burgoyne's surrender at Saratoga, ten days later, was inevitable.

The unbiased historian can draw but one conclusion. The laurels

for one of the decisive victories of all history rest on Arnold's brow. 

But for Saratoga the Colonies would have been cut in two. There would then have been no occasion to fortify West Point. American history records no tragedy more stark than this,-that the man who gave his country the second and final chance to create its "Gibraltar of America" was the man who later chose to betray it. 

General Washington had supported Arnold at every crisis which arose between this undiplomatic, irascible soldier and the politicians of Congress. However, the influence of the Commander-in-Chief was not sufficient to prevent Arnold's being passed over again and again by the promotion of inferior men who, in some instances, were wholly unfit for command. General Washington, noting how Arnold, on leave in Connecticut after the last of such humiliations, had impulsively led a band of hastily assembled militia to drive back to the shore a British raiding force, never doubted the general's loyalty. 

On that day Arnold had two horses shot under him. Congress was forced to recognize the exploit by promoting him to the rank of major general. It failed however to restore his relative rank. 

Treason Enters.-Under such circumstance, Washington offered

Arnold command of the left wing of the Continental Army. Pleading physical incapacity, the result of wounds received at Saratoga, Arnold asked for the West Point command and was granted it. That assignment gave Arnold the opportunity to accomplish his own ruin, -almost the ruin of the American cause. Some months before his arrival at West Point, he had placed himself in secret communication with Sir Henry Clinton, the British commander at New York. Arnold, forwarding vital information through Major John André, Clinton's adjutant, signed himself "Gustavus". Five days after assuming his new duties, "Gustavus" suggested that André meet him to arrange terms for the surrender of West Point. By that time Clinton had no doubts as to the identity of his informer. 

The meeting was soon arranged. During the night of September 

21, 1780, in a sheltered cove on the west bank of the Hudson near Haverstraw, André and Arnold met, agreed upon terms, and completed the plans. Fortunately for the American cause, some 

American sympathizers accidentally captured André at Tarrytown, September 23, and from papers in his possession, discovered the plot. André had by Arnold's orders removed his British uniform while he was within the American lines. Under the rules of warfare his status as "Mr. John Anderson", bearing a pass with that name and signed by Arnold, and wearing the garb of a civilian, was that of a spy. The military papers, plans and information of

West Point, found on his person, confirmed the fact. André was tried by military court-martial, convicted largely on his own evidence, and on October 2, 1780, was hanged at Tappan.

Arnold's Escape.-Arnold meanwhile had escaped as a result of the stupidity of the American outpost commander at North Castle. 

That officer, failing to recognize the identity of Arnold as the traitor in the plot, forwarded to him as commander the information of André's capture. A few hours before Washington's arrival from Hartford to inspect the West Point defenses, Arnold was rowed downstream to safety aboard the British ship Vulture. Money and the rank of brigadier-general in the British Army were his rewards for the fruitless effort to betray America. For the remainder of the war Arnold fought against his former brothers-in-arms; then retired to Great Britain where he lived out his years, a shunned, ostracized and unhappy man. For Arnold there remains at West Point a wooden shield in the Old Chapel, where the Revolutionary generals are so commemorated. His shield, however, bears no name.

Modern research has gone to much effort to plumb the motives underlying Benedict Arnold's treason. Sympathetic commentators have dwelt at length on the theory of his conviction that the Americans' cause was hopeless, and that in the long run they must choose between living under a British or French flag. Hating the latter with all his passionate nature, he decided, according to these biographers, to return the Colonies to their former allegiance. What these writers choose to ignore or to gloss over is the fact that this brilliant and erratic soldier was as reckless in society with his financial means as he was on the battlefield with his personal safety. Debts and their consequences had brought him court-martial and reprimand, even though General Washington couched that reprimand in terms reflecting his unbroken faith in a great soldier. Was it then 

Arnold's fear of ultimate French domination over the inchoate elements represented by the American Colonies which swayed the balance for treason? Was it rather his need for money, at whatever costs? Was it, perhaps, his bitterness over his shabby treatment at the hands of the Continental Congress? Or was it some combination of all these elements that swayed the balance to treason? 

The record is too hazy to justify any concrete conclusion, due no doubt to the fact that the ostracized Arnold chose to bury the truth with himself.

Point garrison was reduced to the minimum limit of safety, while Washington hurried south with every man who could be spared for the investment of Cornwallis at Yorktown.

The following spring, 1782, West Point was the scene of an imposing ceremony for the celebration of the birthday of the Dauphin of France. A great colonnade had been built on the parade ground for the festivities. All surrounding garrisons sent detachments to participate in the review. Musketry and cannonading continued long, by way of salutes, and it appears that each salute was accompanied by the appropriate drinking of toasts. Whatever else it may have been, the occasion was a happy break in the bitterly hard life of the soldiers of that day. 

The "Newburgh Addresses."-With the coming of the winter of 

1782-'83 it was all but clear that the war for Independence had been won. British pressure, as much affected by conditions abroad as by her disappointments and defeats in America, had virtually ceased. 

But a new danger, disaffection in the Army, confronted General Washington and the Continental Congress. At that time most of the army's northern elements were either in garrison at West Point and surrounding posts or in camp at Newburgh. There Washington had his headquarters, and there he observed the steady decline of the troops' morale. Half-starved, half-clothed, long unpaid, they still had sufficient discipline to submit in an orderly manner their lists of grievances to the Massachusetts and other Colonial legislative bodies. When their claims were met with evasion, or at best the scantest consideration, they prepared their more drastic "Newburgh Addresses." A movement was even launched to set up their commander-in-chief as king. 

Washington was well aware of what was happening. Wholly sympathetic to the justice of the soldiers' pleas for redress of grievances, he knew that the mutinous tide had to be stopped if the new-born United States of America was, after all, to be launched. At the critical moment he forwarded the Newburgh Addresses to Congress, appraising the legislators of the hazard. Then he appeared before his officers at the meeting in the "Chapel" or "Temple", where a line of action was to be determined. When he had ceased speaking, the movement was dead. It was the greatest victory of Washington's career, this moral victory over "jealousy, just discontent and great opportunities."

In the fall of that year, 1783, Washington ordered part of the West Point garrison to carry out the occupation of New York, simultaneously with the withdrawal of the last British contingent. It was the final act of a long and bloody drama in which the most important of the Colonials' strongholds had never participated actively. West 

Point, in fact, had never been challenged. It was to become, in time, the oldest American military post over which the flag, once raised, had never been lowered.

The Early Post-War Years.-Hostilities having ceased, the final disposition of the West Point land was in doubt for some years. 

From a military viewpoint, its importance as a defensive position rapidly faded. The necessity for maintaining a strong garrison at 

West Point ended, and, in fact, soon became impossible, for after the war the American forces quickly disbanded. In 1780, General Washington had established a small engineering school at West Point, where organization plans and gunnery experiments were tried out on the plain. Except for the small detachment stationed here in connection with this school, the place was deserted. By January, 1784, the entire army numbered less than 700 men; and in June of that same year Congress passed this illuminating bit of legislation: 

Standing armies in time of peace are inconsistent with the principles of Republican Governments, dangerous to the liberties of a free people, and generally converted into destructive engines for establishing despotism......the commanding officer is hereby directed to discharge the troops now in the service of United States except twentyfive privates to guard stores at Fort Pitt and fifty-five to guard stores at West Point..... 99 66 Federal Government Acquires West Point Lands.-The occupation of West Point during the Revolutionary War, and its continued occupation by the military forces thereafter, brought action by one Stephen Moore, of Caswell County, North Carolina, who was the owner of the land. After several years of claims for damages caused by the occupation of the site by the military, Moore petitioned the government, asking that the United States purchase West Point. 

Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury, reported favorably on this petition June 10, 1790. Congress acted promptly on Hamilton's report, and by an Act approved July 5, 1790, authorized “to be purchased for the use of the United States the whole or such part of that tract of land situated in the State of New York, commonly called West Point, as shall be by him (the President) judged requisite for the purpose of such fortifications and garrisons as may be necessary for the defense of the same.' 22 In this manner the place, West Point, came permanently into the posession of the government.

III.—Legal Establishment of the United States Military Academy 

Initial Efforts to Establish a National Military School. As early as May, 1776, General Henry Knox proposed the establishment of a national military school. The plan received the indorsement of 

Colonel Alexander Hamilton and the approval of General Washington. 

On October 1, 1776, John Adams submitted to the Board of War a motion proposing, "that a Committee of five be appointed to prepare and bring in a plan of a military academy of the army." The project took form June 20, 1777 in a resolution passed by Congress, declaring: "Resolved, that a Corps of Invalids be formed, . to be employed in garrison and for guards in cities . . ; as also to serve as a military school for young gentlemen previous to their being appointed to marching regiments; for which purpose all the subaltern officers, when off duty, shall be obliged to attend a mathematical school, appointed for the purpose, to learn geometry, arithmetic, vulgar and decimal fractions and the extraction of roots. 

The "Corps of Invalids" identifies the pattern's origin as French. "Invalids", in fact, signifies "Veterans." In July, 1777, the Corps was organized at Philadelphia under a restriction imposed by Congress, which declared: “As this Corps is intended, not only as a provision for disabled officers and soldiers, but as a school for propagating military knowledge and discipline, no officers need apply but such as produce ample certificates." Meanwhile General Knox had independently established during the winter of 1778, in connection with his artillery command at Pluckemin, N. J., "an academy where lectures are read in tactics and gunnery." Beginning in the spring of 

1778, detachments of the "Corps of Invalids" were transferred to

West Point until, in August 1782, the entire Corps was stationed there. 

Little information exists as to the scope or character of instruction given these predecessors of the United States Corps of Cadets. Most of the records vanished in two destructive fires, one in 1796, the second in 1838. However, a map drawn by a French officer discloses the presence of three buildings, definitely of an academic character, an engineering building, an ordnance labratory, and a library. This promising beginning came to an end with the cessation of military hostilities in 1783. The idea, however, was kept alive by George Washington and a handful of distinguished Revolutionary War officers, notably General Henry Knox, Colonel Alexander Hamilton, and

Baron Steuben. It was not until 1793, ten years after the Peace of Paris had been signed, that Congress gave serious consideration to the plea in President Washington's message for the establishment on a sound basis of the training of army officers. The troubles

of these ten years gave weight to the President's arguments. Domestic revolt, Indian Wars, British sabotage of the terms of the treaty of Paris, boundary disputes with Spain, the humiliating necessity of paying tribute to the Algerian pirates, and the ever-growing danger of involvement in the European difficulties arising from the French Revolution of 1789,-these were a few of the bombs which exploded the bland pacifism of our first decade of national freedom. 

The Act of 1794.-Congress met the situation with the law of May 

9, 1794, establishing at West Point a "School for Artillerists and Engineers." It provided for the attachment of two cadets, thirty-two all told, to each of the companies stationed there, marking the first instance of that grade in our Army. The cadets enjoyed the “pay, clothing, and rations of a sergeant,"—and, like a sergeant, they carried muskets. The law further stipulated that the government should provide for all books and other equipment required for instruction of the cadets. It is hardly necessary to say that these first cadets received little more than a military apprenticeship. In fact, their educational diet was seriously below that of the "Invalids" of the Revolutionary War days. Disappointed, but still hopeful, Washington, on December 12, 1799, in a letter written to Alexander Hamilton two days before Washington's death, expressed for the last time his views as to the establishment of an Academy commensurate with the country's needs. The letter closes with the remark: "I sincerely hope that the subject will meet with due attention, and that the reasons for its establishment which you have so clearly pointed out to the Secretary (of War) will prevail upon the Legislature to place it upon a permanent and respectable footing." 

The Acts of 1798 and 1799. The law passed by Congress, July 

16, 1798 increased the number of cadetships to fifty-six, and fixed the pay at ten dollars per month and two rations per day. It authorized the President "to appoint a number, not exceeding four, of teachers for the arts and sciences necessary for the instruction of the artillerists and engineers, who shall be entitled to the monthly pay of $50 and two rations per day." International complications, notably our undeclared naval war with France which broke out that year, led to a further increase in cadetships, provided for in the Act of March 3, 1799. Under its terms ten cadets were assigned to each regiment of infantry and cavalry, thirty-two to each artillery regiment. None of these enactments had served to provide the "permanent and repectable footing" for the training and education of officers, urged by Washington. A school without buildings was at best a poor makeshift. True, the cadets stationed at West Point had one advantage

over their fellows serving with troops in the field-they could be assembled for occasional lectures and demonstrations in such buildings as the Post afforded. For many years the "old provost prison" served that purpose. Aware of these facts, the Secretary of War in July, 1801, directed all cadets of the Corps of Artillerists to report to West Point for instruction. In September a school was opened with four army officers and one civilian as instructors. 

A word at this point as to the responsibility or credit for the establishment of the United States Military Academy is not amiss. The question has raised controversy among historians. From the foregoing discussion it should be clear that the final achievement is due to the work of a number of far-sighted soldier-statesmen. Nevertheless, in that group one man, George Washington, stands out as the individual who worked unceasingly as the leader of those who saw in such an institution the one possible basis of a sound national defense. True, Washington was never to see the full fruition of his hopes. That end was not reached in fact until eighteen years after his death. However, his was the high purpose and the tenacity which, more than any other factor, made possible the attainment of the final goal.

The Act of 1802.-An Act of Congress signed by the President on 

March 16, 1802, established the Military Academy as a permanent national institution. The pertinent sections of this Act read as follows: 

Sec. 26. And be it further enacted, That the President of the United

States is hereby authorized and empowered, when he shall deem it expedient, to organize and establish a corps of engineers, to consist of one engineer, with the pay, rank and emoluments of a major; two assistant engineers, with the pay, rank and emoluments of captains; two other assistant engineers, with the pay, rank and emoluments of first lieutenants; two other assistant engineers, with the pay, rank and emoluments of second lieutenants; and ten cadets with the pay of sixteen dollars per month, and two rations per day; and the President of the United States is, in like manner, authorized, when he shall deem it proper, to make such promotions in the said corps, with a view to particular merit, and without regard to rank, so as not to exceed one colonel, one lieutenant-colonel, two majors, four captains, four first lieutenants, four second lieutenants, and so that the number of the whole corps shall, at no time, exceed twenty officers and cadets. 

Sec. 27. And be it further enacted, That the said corps when so organized, shall be stationed at West Point, in the State of New York, and shall constitute a military academy; and the engineers, assistant engineers, and cadets of the said corps, shall be subject, at all times, to do duty in such places, and on such service, as the President of the United States shall direct. 

Sec. 28. And be it further enacted, That the principal engineer,

and in his absence the next in rank, shall have the superintendence of the said military academy, under the direction of the President of the United States; and the Secretary of War is hereby authorized, at the public expense, under such regulations as shall be directed by the President of the United States, to procure the necessary books, implements and apparatus for the use and benefit of the said institution. 

The Act of 1802 finally provided the facilities authorized by the Acts of 1794 and 1798. Brief examination of its provisions will indicate that Congress had established a small engineering school, the first in the country, comprising ten "cadets of engineers" and their officer-instructors. They were a group apart from the "Cadets of the Service," serving their military apprenticeship with the infantry, light artillery, riflemen and light dragoons. A further distinction appears in the monthly pay,-$16 for the cadets at West Point, $10 for the others. The latter moreover were not eligible for the admission to West Point. In sum, West Point had not yet begun to take its final form. Another point of interest was the Academy's dependence on foreign sources of instruction in these early days. An order issued in 1796, reveals the presence of Lieutenant Colonel Commandant Rochefontaine, and Majors Rivardi and Tousard as faculty. 

IV.-Early Years of the United States Military Academy, 1802-1817. 

Half-Measures and Their Results.-The "gentlemen cadets” who, under the legislation previous to that of 1802, had been studying at West Point were in that year absorbed into the newly authorized Military Academy, and continued their studies as before. Instruction was elementary and suffered from frequent interruptions, lack of regulation and lack of discipline. The handful of cadets could with difficulty be forced to apply themselves to their studies, which at that time included mathematics, fortifications, artillery, surveying, and French. The Academy was regarded as a scientific school for the education of future officers in the Corps of Engineers. Few of the authorized number of cadets had been appointed and many of those who were appointed failed to graduate. There were no entrance examinations, no age limit, no law that required cadets to attend the Academy, and no system in the government of the student body. 

And the hapless cadets were left to shift for their own food and maintenance on their monthly salary of sixteen dollars.

The Act of 1812.-The war crisis propelled Congress into hasty action. Its Act of April 29, 1812 provided for the reorganization of the Military Academy. Pertinent provisions of the act are as follows:

Under Major Joseph G. Swift, the Academy reopened in 1813. 

Three years later two hundred cadets were enrolled. In the interim disaster had once more faced the Academy's existence when Congress failed to appropriate funds for its operation. The Superintendent met the crisis (1815) by borrowing $65,000 at seven per cent from 

Jacob Barker, a New York merchant. A commentary on the low state of our national fortunes and credit at that time appears in the fact that the New York bankers had refused to grant the needed loan.

V. The Superintendency of Colonel Sylvanus Thayer, 1817-1833. 

Summary of Conditions to 1817.-Perusal of the various enabling acts in the period prior to 1817 leads to a false picture of conditions in these early days. A careful scanning of the diaries and other records compels the conclusion that West Point could hardly have been considered an educational institution, even when judged by the standards of those days.¹ Cadets, varying in age from twelve to thirtyfour, married in two instances, reported for duty whenever they were appointed, and without undergoing entrance examinations. In fact they straggled in at all times through the year. They were graduated whenever they had established their proficiency in the eyes of the Academic Board. Sylvanus Thayer, later Superintendent of the 

Academy, entered West Point when he had all but completed his senior year at Dartmouth, with the result that he was graduated from 

West Point after less than a years' service as a cadet.

The deterrents to the establishment of a proper educational system were many. Most of the time Congress and the War Department showed no more than casual interest in the Academy and its work. 

The outright hostility of one Secretary of War, William Eustis, has already been discussed. Moreover, the fact that the Superintendents of those days exercised their office in addition to their other duties

and were in fact engaged most of the time on those "other duties," away from West Point, could not fail to frustrate the efforts of those officers of the Academy who were bent on developing an institution in line with George Washington's ideal. The physical plant was responsible for further difficulties. It was in fact so inadequate that vacations were granted to all cadets from December 1st to April 1st.

That device at least avoided the probability of pneumonia and frostbite as a result of conditions in class-rooms and barracks. In spite of such adversities the Academy managed to graduate 180 men in the first fifteen years of its existence. The average of their cadet service was two years, including the two four-month winter vacations. 

The fact that many of that number made brilliant records in after life, both in the army and out of it, must be attributed in most instances to their classical education prior to entering the Military Academy.

The Offer to Count Rumford. For several years President Mad ison sought to find the man who measured up to his conception of the head of the Military Academy. Soldierly abilities were in themselves an insufficient qualification to meet his requirements; they must be matched by educational attainments. At one time he thought he he had found him in Benjamin Thompson, better known to history as Count Rumford. Suspected in 1775 of Tory sympathies, the 23year old Thompson had left his native New England to seek his fortune abroad. Minister of War to the Elector of Bavaria for eleven years, an outstanding scientist and scholar of all time, he would no doubt have made an ideal head of West Point. He chose to decline the offer. It is interesting to note that in his will he left his library and his sword to the Academy.

Appointment of Brevet-Major Thayer.-The search for a Superintendent settled ultimately on a man who fully met the needs of the situation. Brevet-Major Sylvanus Thayer, a graduate of Dartmouth College and of the Military Academy, veteran of the War of 1812, later a student in Europe's leading military schools, was an ideal choice. He assumed the Superintendency July 28, 1817 at the age of 32. His breadth of vision, human sympathy, scholarly attainments and knowledge of educational purposes and methods, his vigorous enthusiasm and lastly his soldierly bearing and sense of discipline fitted him to cope with the problems facing the Academy. 

Two major factors favored Major Thayer's efforts from the outset. 

His office was designated in orders as a full-time job. And, after the appointment of John C. Calhoun as Secretary of War in October, 1818, he was assured of a period of full support and cooperation.

Reorganization of Corps Administration.-Immediately after assumption of his new duties, Major (later Colonel) Thayer organized the Corps of Cadets into a two-company battalion, officered by selected cadets. That organization furnished the means of improvement in drill and military exercises; more important, it gave greater opportunities for the exercise of command functions by senior cadets (first-classmen); lastly, it injected some sadly needed discipline into the Corps. Prior to 1817 the cadets had lived in much the same fashion as their contemporaries in civilian institutions. Their essential soldier status was assumed to begin only after their graduation and commissioning as officers. Colonel Thayer recognized the fact that only the man who has himself undergone discipline is capable of instilling its principles into his future subordinates. 

Accordingly he drew up a strict set of regulations governing not only the exercises on the drill-ground, but classroom procedure and the routine of daily life in the Corps as well. In addition he created the office of Commandant of Cadets and appointed an officer from the Department of Tactics to that position. 

The innovation brought prompt resistance from the Corps. A committee was organized to defend what the cadets considered to be their rights. Unhappily, its membership included several of the most brilliant and soldierly men in the Corps. All were tried by court-martial for insubordination. The conviction of the first group was appealed on the grounds that a court-martial had no jurisdiction over cadets. The court, after considerable deliberation, decided likewise. After still longer consideration, the Attorney General of the 

United States ruled that the Corps of Cadets formed a part of the land forces of the United States, and that therefore the cadets were subject to the Articles of War and to trial by court-martial. President Monroe confirmed the ruling. To the surprise of some, the 

Corps accepted the decision and final ruling wholeheartedly. Discipline had been born at West Point.

Curricular Reform.-Of at least equal significance was Colonel 

Thayer's dynamic reform of the educational system at West Point. 

The four-month winter vacations were abolished. To ensure thoroughness of mental application, Colonel Thayer established a rule of recitation,-"every cadet, every day, in every subject." Close supervision was provided by dividing cadets into small sections, ten or a dozen men at most, with an instructor for each section. Moreover, proven capacity or its relative lack moved the cadets at regular periods to higher or lower sections. As a result, a premium was placed on the final diploma and commission. The Army and the country as a whole were the chief beneficiaries of the change. 

Colonel Thayer found at West Point a curriculum which included mathematics, engineering, natural philosophy, drawing, and French. 

To these he added on his own responsibility, chemistry, general history, moral philosophy, law, geography and ethics. In time Congress gave legal sanction to his action. The scope of the curriculum after his modifications appears limited when compared with the program of a modern university. But if one goes back to 1817 he will discover that the civilian educational institutions of that day confined practically all their efforts to turning out men qualified for the ministry, law, or the teaching profession. In the light of that fact it is understandable that America in the early decades of its industrial development turned almost exclusively to West Point for its engineers. Incontestably, Colonel Thayer's work as an educator places him among the nation's pioneers in that field. 

The Honor System is Born.-His greatest achievement lies, however, neither in education nor military discipline, but rather in the field of moral training. His experience prior to 1817 had taught him that an army, to perform its mission, must have at its core,the officer personnel-a rock-hard integrity in thought and word and action. And whatever the background of the incoming cadet, he was still at an age permitting the inculcation of the principles of honor. Hence, the West Point honor code, established by Colonel 

Thayer. Wisely, he understood the fact that honor recognizes no distinction of rank or position. The cadets themselves were made guardians of the inviolability of their code. How well Colonel Thayer builded is evident from the fact that the Corps Honor Code remains today in the minds of not only the Army officer but of the informed civilian as well, the distinctive feature of the "West Point system." 

After sixteen years of continuous service as Superintendent, Colonel Thayer had finished his work. At his own request, he was relieved from duty July 1, 1833. Where he had blazed the trail, it would not be hard for his successors to follow. The curriculum might and did broaden in the years after, the Corps expanded vastly in numbers, the approach to problems of education and training changed in some degrees, but every vital principle envisaged and established by that great leader remains effective today. It is with this thought that West Pointers,-cadet and graduate-assemble

on Alumni Day each year to place a wreath commemorating those who have died in the past twelve-month. As the bugler blows "Taps", the wreath is laid at the foot of the monument of Colonel 

Thayer, "Father of the Military Academy."

Official Hostility to the Military Academy. The road marked out and followed by Colonel Thayer had not been an easy one. The thorniest stretches lay not at West Point, but in the almost continuous baiting inflicted on the Academy by elements in Congress and in various state legislatures. In March 1820, a resolution was offered in Congress to abolish the Academy and sell the property at auction in order to end "this retreat for the pampered sons of the rich." In 1833 a Congressional Committee voted 10 to 8 to carry out a similar proposal. At various times from 1833 to 1844, the legislatures of Tennessee, Ohio, Maine, Connecticut and New Hampshire memorialized Congress to the same end. One of the most serious difficulties arose in the period 1829-'33, when President Andrew Jackson repeatedly interfered with Academy discipline to the extent that it became virtually impossible for the time being to eliminate cadets who had been convicted of violations of the honor system. That situation was in fact primarily responsible for Colonel 

Thayer's resignation. Examination of the records leads to the conclusion that most of this hostility in official civilian circles was the direct result of Colonel Thayer's raising of the Academy's standards, particularly those which governed admission. Even though they fell far short of those in force today, they were high enough to bar a large percentage of youths who had not had the benefit of special schooling. In short, admission had become limited substantially to the sons of the well-to-do. 

VI. The Military Academy, 1833-1860. 

The Act of 1843.-Few noteworthy changes in the organization and regulations of the Military Academy occurred in the years im mediately following Colonel Thayer's relief. By an Act of Con gress approved July 5, 1838, provision was made for a Professor of Chemistry, Mineralogy, and Geology, and also for cadet engagement to serve eight years instead of five. And by an act of Congress approved March 1, 1843, new regulations were provided for cadetships, as follows: 

Provided, That hereafter in all cases of appointment of cadets to the West Point Academy, the individual selection shall be an actual resident of the congressional district of the State or Territory, or District of Columbia, from which the appointment purports to be made:

And provided further, That the number of cadets by appointment hereafter to be made, shall be limited to the number of representatives and delegates in Congress, and one for the District of Columbia, and that each congressional and territorial district and District of Columbia, shall be entitled to have one cadet at said academy: Provided, That nothing in this section shall prevent the appointment of an additional number of cadets not exceeding ten to be appointed at Large, without being confined to a selection by congressional districts. 

Necessarily the above Act of Congress produced a steady increase in the strength of the Corps, parallel to the admission of territories to statehood, and the resultant expansion of both houses of Congress. 

The same year, (February 19, 1838) brought tragedy to the Academy when fire destroyed the old two-story Academy building. 

Irreplaceable loss was suffered in the destruction of the Adjutant's office, containing the history of the Academy for the post-Revolutionary period. The cadets, at great hazard, managed to save some of the contents of the Library, Engineering Department, and Chemical Laboratory.

The War with Mexico.-The first full test of the product of the 

Academy and evidence of its value to the United States came with the outbreak of the Mexican War. At this time about 1300 cadets had graduated from the Military Academy. Over 500 graduates were on active duty in the Regular Army, and many more who had gone back to civilian life promptly joined the volunteers. Cadets who were about to graduate had a new rumor each day on the possibility of early graduation and the prospects for immediate active service abroad. Though many graduates had risen from the junior grades, there were, in 1845, no graduates among the general officers. Long years of intermittent warfare with the Indians on our western frontier, however, had given our officers practical training which well fitted them to handle larger forces, and to assume heavier responsibilities in the Mexican War. They led their men through thirty successive victories which resulted in the capture of 40,000 prisoners, 1,000 cannon, and immense quantities of war material. These victories extended from California through the northern states of Mexico to the mouth of the Rio Grande, and from Vera Cruz to Mexico City. In a brief but brilliant campaign, they overcame tremendous obstacles. Neither deserts, nor mountains, nor rivers could stop them. Lack of men, food, clothing, cannon, or ammunition seemed only to spur them on to greater efforts. They won against seemingly hopeless odds and against greatly superior numbers of the enemy. Indomitable spirit and a

thorough knowledge of their profession were responsible for their success. Their years of training at the Military Academy and in the Indian Wars had not been wasted. The list of graduates who were brevetted for gallant services runs into the hundreds; many were brevetted for gallant services two or even three times. Among those who rendered conspicuously distinguished services were Robert E. Lee, Bragg, Sherman, Sumner, Hooker, Beauregard, and

McClellan, all of whom later rose to high commands in the Civil War.

The able leader of the expedition that captured Mexico City, Major General Winfield Scott, though not himself a Military Academy graduate, paid a fine tribute to the West Pointers of his command, in a letter written in 1860, in which he said: “I give it as my fixed opinion, that but for our graduated cadets, the war between Mexico and the United States might, and probably would, have lasted some four or five years, with, in its first half, more defeats than victories falling to our share; whereas, in less than two compaigns, we conquered a great country and a peace, without the loss of a single battle or skirmish.”

The notable services rendered by the Military Academy graduates in the Mexican War demonstrated to the nation the wisdom of increasing the output of the Academy. Congress appropriated generous sums for new buildings, increased the staff of professors by the addition of Professors in Drawing and French (1846) and in Spanish (1857), and displayed its interest in the welfare of West Point by sending a Board of Visitors to inspect it each year. With improved facilities, the Academy resumed its task of preparing leaders for the next war.

VII. The Period, 1860-1898. 

The Corps in the Civil War.-The period immediately preceding the Civil War was a time of bitter stress in the Corps of Cadets. 

The outbursts on the floor of Congress were paralleled in the barracks and on the drillground of West Point. With the strong emotions of youth, the cadets showed even less restraint than did their elders in Washington. Personal combat was frequent. There was occasional sword-play. Fortunately no one suffered any physical hurt of consequence. Most sorely tried were some of the cadets from the border and southern states, torn between their loyalty to their nation and the home ties which bound them to their boyhood communities. Through the war years the Military Academy continued its regular functions. The vacancies created by the departure of the cadets from the South were immediately filled from the Northern States. In 1861, two classes were graduated, the result of the restoration of the four-year course, following a period during which the course had been maintained at five years. 

In the Union Army, at the beginning of the war, many of the regimental commanders were West Point graduates, but there were no graduates among the general officers. Within six months, the 

Federal government realized the seriousness of the conflict, and adopted the policy of appointing as generals those officers who demonstrated their ability to command troops in battle. As a result, within a year two-thirds of the higher commanders were West Pointers, and this proportion was fairly well maintained throughout the war. After nearly four years of war, in January, 1865, all the general officers of the line were graduates. The total number of graduates who rose to the rank of general in the Union Army was nearly 300. The fact that victory finally rested on Union arms is incontestably due in large part to the leadership furnished by the West Point product, from General Ulysses S. Grant down to the platoon commanders, just graduated from the Academy. 

The Confederate States had to create an army. They turned unhesitatingly to West Point men. Jefferson Davis, President of the Confederacy, was a graduate who had served with distinction in the Regular Army. He knew personally many of the 296 West 

Point graduates who served actively in the Confederate Armies. 

Over half of these officers (151) became generals in the Southern forces. The magnificent struggle waged by the South, with success in the early stages, and against overwhelming odds towards the end, is largely attributable to the skillful military leadership displayed by these officers. Military historians and students of mili-

tary art in all countries have placed the names of Robert E. Lee and Stonewall Jackson high among those of the great soldiers of all time.

At the end of the Civil War, West Point graduates were at the heads of the opposing armies. In every major engagement throughout the war, the forces on at least one side, and often on both sides, had been commanded by West Point graduates. Each of those men took something of the West Point spirit into battle with him; each one contributed something to the traditions that West Point stands for today.

The Act of 1866.—After the war was over, Congress recognized the fact that the Military Academy had long since ceased to be merely an engineering college and had been training officers for all branches of the service by passing an Act, approved July 13, 1866, providing (Sec. 6):

That the Superintendent of the United States Military Academy may hereafter be selected, and the officers on duty at that institution detailed, from any arm of the service; and the supervision and charge of the Academy shall be in the War Department under such officer, or officers, as the Secretary of War may assign to that duty. 

In the same year, legislation fixed the age for admission of cadets at between seventeen and twenty-two years, provided for designation of alternate candidates by the appointing powers, and added English grammar, descriptive geography, and United States history to the entrance requirements.

VIII. The Period, 1898-1916. 

War and Pacification Duties.-For over thirty years after the close of the Civil War, no great war involved the United States. During this period, however, practically all of the Regular Army units took part in one or more of the numerous Indian Wars. Few civilians felt the immediate effects of these wars. As a result they knew little of the arduous service rendered in isolated army posts on the western frontiers. The next great test, which again turned public attention to West Point, came in the Spanish-American War. 

Here at the Military Academy, this war changed early graduation from rumor to fact. Because of the shortage of trained officers, many instructors were relieved and sent to duty with troops; the class of 1898 graduated about two months ahead of schedule, and the class of 1899, nearly four months early. Despite these changes,

West Point maintained its customary standard of training and discipline.

In April, 1898, all the Regular regiments that could be spared from their stations concentrated at the general mobilization camps in the South, in preparation for operations in Cuba. Within a few months, by the enlistment of volunteers our small Regular Army of 26,000 men expanded to 223,000. Most of the officers selected to command these raw recruits came from the 1800 Military Academy graduates who saw active service. Actual hostilities lasted only a short time. Then the volunteer forces clamored for release from active service, and the bulk of the volunteer forces soon melted away. The tedious work of establishing stable government in territory freed by the War from Spanish control, fell mostly upon the Regulars. In Cuba and in the Philippines, officers became the actual governors of towns, districts, provinces, and larger territories. In Cuba, Generals Bliss, Ludlow, and Fitzhugh Lee, and in the Philippines, Generals Francis Vinton Greene, Wesley Merritt, and James F. Bell distinguished themselves not only as soldiers, but also as able executives and administrators. All of them were 

West Point graduates. The thoroughness and zeal with which these officers and their subordinates performed their civil administrative duties helped materially to establish promptly a stable form of government. By their constructive work in an unfamiliar field, they added fresh laurels to West Point traditions. In 1899, Elihu Root, then Secretary of War, reported upon the Military Academy as follows:-"I believe that the great service which it has rendered the country was never more conspicuous than it has been during the past two years. The faithful and efficient services of its graduates since the declaration of the war with Spain have more than repaid the cost of the institution since its foundation."

IX. The World War and Its Effects. 

Early Graduation and Its Effects.-The World War caused an up heaval at West Point. All the instructors who could possibly be spared were relieved and sent to duty with troops. The remaining instructors did double duty here during the winter, and then spent the summers in the training camps for officers. The Class of 1917 graduated about two months ahead of time; many of its members at once joined organizations that were under orders to embark for France. Other classes graduated in quick succession; 1918, nine months early; 1919, twelve months; 1920, twenty-four months; and

1921 about thirty-two months ahead of schedule.¹ On November 2, 1918, the Corps of Cadets consisted of the members of the Fourth Class. They had been at the Academy but four and a half months when they had to shoulder the responsibility of maintaining the traditions that had been handed down for over a hundred years. 

West Point, for the time being, had ceased to be an educational institution; it had become a training camp. 

When the United States declared war on Germany, on April 6, 1917, our Regular Army was being enlarged and reorganized. As a result of its expansion, even the Regular Army units were underofficered. The immediate demand for trained officers was overwhelming. Every National Guard Regiment needed at least one 

Regular Army officer to help train the troops. All the training camps for candidates who sought commission had to be built, the training had to be improvised, the courses to be conducted before this country could begin to mobilize the bulk of its man power. 

Then the National Army divisions had to be drafted, organized, equipped, trained, and carried over to France, before our efforts began to count on the firing line. We had millions of men, but they were almost entirely untrained. We soon had thousands of officers who had received little more training than the troops they commanded. The tremendous burden of directing our belated preparations for war fell most heavily upon the Regular Army officers—the men who had spent their lives in the service and knew something about the business of war.

3,445 graduates of the Military Academy saw active duty. From the most recent graduate to those who had long experience in the army, all had to assume much heavier responsibilities than ever before. Reorganization; First Steps. It was not only the "S. O." Class which suffered a loss of morale after the Armistice; in fact, the entire 

Corps was in a state of mental depression. These youths had come to West Point under the same impulse which sent thousands to the officers' training camps. In other words their motives and objectives were in many respects different from those of the candidates who enter the Military Academy in years of peace. Within a few months 24 

members of Fourth Class "A" and 85 of Fourth Class "B" resigned. 

The remainder, who presumably desired a lifetime career in the Army, were entitled to the maximum of education and training which could be given them in the absence of a final War Department directive as to the Academy curriculum. A stop-gap arrangement, providing for graduation of Fourth Class "A" in June, 1920, and of Fourth Class 

"B" a year later received War Department approval. 

Colonel Tillman's second and final retirement occurred, March 4, 1919. As Superintendent, he had seen the Academy through the worst stages of the most chaotic period of its history since 1817. He had witnessed the Corps of Cadets whittled down by successive early graduation orders to a point where on November 1, 1918, it consisted of a Fourth Class with less than five months' training to its credit. One month later the Corps included two added organizations quite unlike anything previous in the Academy's existence, one in soldier olive drab hastily assembled without a test of their mental qualifications; the other, already graduated, a body of officers who for seven months were not quite officers and certainly not cadets. The resultant damage to morale and standards of performance, traditional in the Corps since 1817, has already been noted. The signing of the Armistice finally gave the Superintendent and the Academic Board their first opportunity to salvage and rebuild. Lacking a final directive from 

Washington as to future intentions for West Point, they nevertheless drew tentative plans to give the classes in the Academy a course embodying the full scope of the pre-war curriculum. The final decision had not been reached when Colonel Tillman was ordered to his second and final retirement. XI-Final Reorganization and Later Developments; 1919-1940. 

The Three-Year Course Experiment. Brigadier General Douglas 

MacArthur took over the reins as Superintendent, June 12, 1919. 

He brought with him a directive from the War Department to initiate a three-year course, and after due experience with it to make a comprehensive report as to the advisability of its permanent adoption. 

The experiment, as it proved to be, was the result of recommendations by the Chief of Staff, General Peyton C. March, to the Secretary of

War, Newton D. Baker. The directive left the Academic Board with a choice between three alternatives, to lop a year from either the beginning or the end of the normal four-year course, or to telescope the curriculum, cutting all subjects to the bone. The last-named method was adopted. Its inadequacy promptly demonstrated itself to the extent where, in General MacArthur's words "the opinion of West Point itself from the Superintendent down to the lowest ranking cadet was in favor of the four-year term.'

Restoration of Traditions. More pressing even than the question of reorganization of the curriculum was the problem which followed on the heels of the rapid departure of prematurely graduated classes. 

As noted above, the morning of November 2, 1918, saw but one class at the Academy, a Fourth Class with four and a half months of military experience to its credit. The arrival within a month of two other classes, one newly entered and dressed in enlisted olive drab, the other a group of officers returned for further instruction and living apart from the Corps, produced a motley assemblage bearing little if any resemblance to the former Corps of Cadets. Shortly it became evident to the Superintendent and his officer subordinates that something very precious was vanishing, or perhaps had already disappeared. 

Corps traditions, the heritage of more than a century, had in time past become an accepted fact, resting on the expressed or tacit approval of superior authority. Their preservation, development and sanction had been the concern of all cadets, and above all of the First Class. 

Three years of service as an underclassman has always served to instill those traditions into the very fiber of the cadet, preparing him for the obligation of first sponsoring them, then transmitting them to his successors.

Most important in this body of traditions was the Corps honor system. Its significance to the army and to the country as a whole. appears in a letter of May 17, 1920, from the Secretary of War to the Chairman of the Committee on Military Affairs in the House of Representatives. It declares: "Men may be inexact, or even untruthful, in ordinary matters, and suffer as a consequence only the disesteem of their associates, or the inconveniences of unfavorable litigation, but the inexact or untruthful soldier trifles with the lives of his fellowmen, and the honor of his government; and it is, therefore, no matter of idle pride, but rather of stern disciplinary necessity that makes West Point require of her students a character for trustworthiness which knows no evasions." 

A moment's thought leads to the conclusion that standards of individual and group integrity are dependent in the final test on their wholehearted and willing acceptance by those who must live under them. Recognizing no distinctions of rank or position, they can not be imposed from above. Happily, the experience of Sylvanus Thayer

in 1817 was repeated at West Point in the post-Armistice period. One change in the administrative system materially hastened the reestablishment of traditional standards officers of the Department of Tactics moved their desks into cadet barracks. Meeting each problem and troublesome question at first hand, they served as mentors and and guides, rather than as mere dispensers of military discipline. An illustration of the methods employed appears in a meeting of the First Class (Class of 1920) held August 3, 1919 to reexamine the entire body of cadet traditions. Various deleterious practices which had crept in from time to time, importations usually from private military schools or civilian colleges, were discarded. The resultant body of customs and traditions, as submitted to the Commandant, was prefaced with the following comment: "These customs entail obligations upon both upper and lower classes. 

They are prescribed for the ultimate benefit of Lower Classmen and the Corps of Cadets. Relations between the classes will at all times comply with the practices in vogue among gentlemen. The spirit of democracy must prevail at West Point, and only those conditions which are believed to improve the value of the individual to the Government can be preserved. No practices and customs will be permitted contrary to the spirit of this document." 

Approval of the resolutions by the Commandant and the Superintendent gave the Corps a needed yardstick. 

Planning the Four-Year Course; the Problem. It would have been a simple matter to reinstate the entire curriculum of the pre-war days.

With few exceptions American educators held to their belief that a highly civilized people was capable of ruling itself.

In place of the standard general education, with its heavy emphasis on the classics, fields of concentration were organized, bearing directly on the requirements of the future career for which the student was preparing. 

The problem at West Point was at one and the same time more simple and more complex than that of the civilian institutions. Its simplicity lay in the fact that the career of the West Pointer is selected the moment he enters the Academy. Its complexity lay in the farreaching changes in the character of warfare and the consequent increases in the responsibility of the army officer. He could no longer be content with an intimate knowledge of military tactics and strategy. 

Totalitarian war, involving the employment of a nation's entire resources, human and material, demands of the soldier the capacity for their proper utilization. In the World War the country had turned to the "dollar-a-year men" to take over national production and transport. The resultant division of responsibility had entailed much delay and waste.¹ The obvious corrective lay in training selected army officers for preparation of war plans and production schedules in time of peace, and for their execution after the advent of the next emergency. West Point necessarily was the first rung in any such ladder of education.

The indicated changes did not stop with the class-room. The developments in weapons and tactics both during and after the World War called for corresponding changes in the military phases of the cadets' instruction. Lastly, battle experience had disclosed shortcomings in the physical development of a notable percentage of army officers, not excluding the graduates of West Point.2

Planning The Four-Year Course; Decisions. The Academic Board began its attack on the three phases of its problem-mental, military, and physical with a restatement of the Military Academy's mission, 

"The function of the Military Academy is to give, in addition to that character-building for which it has long been famous, and in addition to the necessary military and physical training, such a combination of basic general and technical education as will provide an adequate foundation for a cadet's subsequent professional career. 99 Turning to consideration of the academic schedule, the Board examined the possibility of introducing courses in economics, government, psychology, sociology, logic and moral philosophy. However, since it was clear that every subject introduced would entail a corresponding curtailment in one or more other subjects, only economics and government were accepted for adoption. Next, the Board determined to double the time allotment for English, principally to fill the gap left by the progressive decline in secondary school instruction in that subject. To make room for these changes, the pre-war courses in mathematics, Spanish, and drawing were reduced. All other subjects retained their former time allotments, at the same time increasing their content and scope to bring them abreast of the latest developments in their respective fields. 

Revision of Program of Military Training. It was recognized that the pre-war schedule had devoted an excessive amount of time to infantry close order drill, largely on the ground of its disciplinary value. Instruction covering the other arms of the Service had suffered in due proportion. The new program corrected this condition. 

Meanwhile, infantry and artillery instruction was modified to include work with the weapons developed during the war. In an effort to overcome the handicaps to training imposed by the narrow confines of the West Point Reservation, the first and third-classmen were moved to Camp Dix for their summer instruction in 1920 and 1921.¹ 

The general effect of the changes was a material broadening of the training program and a correspondingly increased advancement of the cadet in his practical knowledge of the arms and technique of the various branches of the service.

A two-way solution was indicated-material enlargement of the sports schedule and a system of compulsory training in a range of intra-mural sports sufficiently large to bring all cadets "down from the bleachers and into the mud of the playing field.”

It is of interest to note that by 1939-'40 this program had developed to the inclusion of twenty-four sports, eighteen of which were of the competitive type.

The mission of the United States Military Academy is to produce officers of the Army having the qualities and attributes essential to their progressive and continuing development, throughout their careers as officers and leaders.

The mission is accomplished by education and training directed toward the inculcation and developinent of the requisite moral, mental, physical and professional qualifications and attributes upon which graduates may build as commissioned officers and readily acquire the technical and professional proficiency commensurate with their duties and responsibilities.

Revision of Course in Modern Languages.—The Courses in Modern 

Languages underwent a number of changes. During the War, Spanish was discontinued and instruction was confined to French. The

Establishment of Department of Physics.-Another major change occurred in the establishment of a Department of Physics. Thereafter, until 1930 its program embraced a definite course in College Physics. The new Department of Physics received provisional sanction in a War Department directive dated May 11, 1931. In August of the same year an Acting Professor was appointed. New Contacts with Civilian Educational Institutions.-Early in the post-war period steps were taken to establish closer contact with leading civilian educational institutions. The curriculum more than met the requirements for such a degree and that fact had long been recognized by graduate schools cognizant of the facts. However, instances had arisen where Academy graduates who wished to pursue their studies abroad, and in some instances in this country, met with some inconvenience in explaining the lack of that "Open Sesame," the degree. For succeeding classes the degree is conferred upon graduation. The change was of particular benefit to West Pointers submitting their names as candidates for a Rhodes Scholarship at

Standish Weston, both of the Class of 1925, and Francis R. Johnson of the Class of 1923, a commendable number of the Academy's graduates have been granted that highly coveted distinction.

Tuition Funds for Graduate Study.-A further opportunity to strengthen and broaden the Academy's educational function had been opened by the provision in the Army Reorganization Act of June 4, 1920, authorizing graduate courses for Army officers at civilian educational institutions at government expense. Such tuition funds were first made available to the Military Academy in the fiscal year 1927. For many years they were sufficient only to assure one summer of graduate study to incoming instructors who had not previously enjoyed such advantages. A considerable and steadily growing number took additional courses at their own expense. Recent increases in tuition allotments, supplemented as before by officers' personal expenditures, have enabled many to secure their Master's and higher degrees. The cadets are the immediate beneficiaries of the richer background of knowledge brought to the section room by their instructors.

Fuller Employment of Lectures.-There remained one more opportunity to enrich the cultural side of the Military Academy's workthe fuller use of the lecture platform. The first large-scale effort in that direction was made in 1920. However, the paucity of appropriated funds, coupled with other difficulties went far to nullify the plan of bringing distinguished educators and other men of affairs before the cadets. Still, some progress was made, and in the fiscal year 

1939 the doubling of the Academy's previous allotment for lecture purposes gave promise of final attainment of the objective. 

Cadet Extra-Curricular Activities.-As already stated, the decision reached in 1920 aimed at improvement and development, not only through action taken by the Academic Board, but also through encouragement of worth-while activities initiated by the cadets themselves. The Corps' response to the effort at widening its range of interests was both prompt and effective, particularly in the field of extra-curricular activities. By 1938 practically every type of special interest was represented by some group, club, or similar organization. Two Corps publications, the "Howitzer" (annual) and the "Pointer" (bi-weekly), absorbed the surplus energies of the literaryminded. Cadets with a flair for musical comedy found their outlet in the Dialectic Society. The West Point Cadet Players served a similar purpose for those who were interested in the drama. A Glee Club and the Cadet Orchestra were organized to provide a medium of expression for cadets with a musical bent, supplementing in that respect the long-established choirs. In time, steps were taken sim-

ilarly to indulge those who were interested in a wide range of hobbies, among them, photography, oratory, debating, radio, chess, hunting, fishing, and skeet. One of the most notable developments was the launching of the Cadet Lecture Committee's work in the spring of 1931. Raising its funds through the Corps' contributions, it has brought to West Point a wide variety of lecturers and entertainers. 

The cultural value of these lecture and entertainment courses has been notably high. All extra-curricular activities were and are conducted at the expense of the cadets' free time. The gain in all respects more than compensates for the loss of idle leisure. 

All these activities were strictly intramural except for the few trips each year allowed the Cadet Choir and the Debating Club. 

However, a much enhanced athletic program, as shown below, brought for members of the Corps squads, and occasionally for the entire Corps, frequent breaks from the routine of Academy life. 

Optional visits by groups of the Second Class to nearby art centers and other places of cultural interest helped toward the same end. 

Lastly, each cadet of the First Class was permitted to spend six week-ends away from West Point. In sum, the long-mooted "monastic life" at West Point was effectively modified. 

Effects of the Changes.—One result of the enhanced program in physical training should not be overlooked. It necessitated termination of the academic day at 3:00 P.M., one hour earlier than formerly. To compensate for the difference in total time, a reduction was made in class-room hours and there was some reduction likewise in the intervening study periods of the cadets. Those changes in turn compelled other adjustments: the instructor had to place increased emphasis on pure instruction; the cadet had to make better use of his study hours. In the light of experience to date the change has been beneficial.

The final effect of the many changes, large and small, from 1920 to 1939 was the attainment of the major objectives outlined in 1920. 

In the academic field the scientific subjects were finally coordinated to ensure an unbroken progression of the cadet from first recitation to graduation without undue overlaps; the non-scientific subjects were given the additional time and emphasis needed to give the entire curriculum the rounded balance previously lacking. No less important were the improvements in the program of physical training, the modifications in military instruction, and the progress in extra-curricular activities. The resultant product was a man with a more balanced training, better fitted to meet the increasingly complex problems which faced him after graduation-as an army officer,

as a graduate student in a civilian school, or as a specialist detailed to handle any one of the wide range of non-military federal activities which have fallen to the Army's lot since 1932.

Entrance Requirements.—The ratio of graduates to the strength of the class at entrance, currently about 65%, has varied rather widely at times. A principal factor affecting the final ratio has been the entrance requirements, which have changed considerably since 1900. Speaking broadly, the effort has been made at all times to keep such requirements in line with the requirements for graduation from the better secondary schools of the country, with the added proviso that the field covered by any individual candidate must have covered the subjects necessary as a basis for pursuit of the studies embraced by the Academy's curriculum. Unfortunately, the very considerable variations between educational conditions in different sections of the country have always served to complicate the problem for both the candidate and the Academy. The efforts to make adjustments to meet the facts in the case, without lowering the Military Academy's standards, is apparent from the changes in entrance requirements since 1900.

Two years later the certificate privilege was extended to include public high school students who were due to graduate in the year of admission to the Military Academy, and subject to such graduation. By 1907 it had become clear that this certificate privilege had resulted in the admission of a considerable number of candidates who could never hope to complete successfully the course at the Academy. Accordingly the Academic Board reverted to the old practice of requiring all candidates to pass a rigid mental examination before admission. At the same time, the four-year Academy course was increased by three and a half months, the incoming class being required to report March 1, instead of June 15, as hitherto. That practice remained in force until 1911, when the old four-year course was reestablished. 

Renewal of College Certificate Privilege, 1914.-In 1914, Major 

General Leonard Wood, then Chief of Staff, took steps to restore the privilege of admission by certificate. Guided by its previous experience with the problem, the Academic Board secured approval of certain provisions which tightened materially, as compared with the former practice, the requirements for such certificates. The privi-. lege was limited to: (1) Regularly enrolled students in good standing without condition in any university, college or technological school accredited by the United States Military Academy, provided that the entrance requirements for the course they were pursuing in such institution required proficiency in 14 units of the College Entrance Examination Board. The further provisos limited these units to specific subjects basic to the Academy's curriculum. (2) Graduates of a preparatory school or high school on the Academy's accredited list, provided that they had established their proficiency in subjects amounting to 

14 units of College Entrance Examination Board re quirements. Again there was a proviso that the 14 units must be basic to the Academy's curriculum. (3) Candidates presenting a properly attested certificate from the College Entrance Examination Board show ing they had passed 14 units of its examination in the specified fields.

In the following year, 1915, these requirements were tightened to some extent. It had been found necessary in the first year of the new plan to reject a number of certificates because of low grades.

That provision was now officially introduced into the regulations. 

Rejected candidates, however, retained the privilege of presenting themselves for the regular mental examination. In addition, college students presenting a certificate showing but one semester of work were required to submit also the full record of their preparatory school studies.

Effect of the Act of 1915.-The Act of May, 1915 threw open to enlisted men of the Regular Army and National Guard the opportunity to enter West Point. The vacancies opened to them numbered not to exceed 180, to be divided equally, as far as possible, between these two elements of the nation's armed forces. To be eligible, these candidates must have completed a year's service prior to entering the Academy. The same year recognition was given to the military training conducted in the private military schools and colleges having Regular Army officers detailed as professors of military science and tactics, by allotting to their graduates a total of twenty vacancies to the United States Military Academy. To make its students eligible, such private military institutions had to receive first from the War Department, for the year in question, the designation of "honor military school." All these three new groups were placed on a competitive basis (within the group itself). Hitherto only the candidates at large, of which sixty were designated by the 

President (exclusive of the "honor military school" quota), and two by the Vice-President, had been on such competitive basis. 

For several years after passage of the Act of 1915, no important changes occurred in the matter of entrance requirements. One minor change in 1921 should be noted, permitting the candidate designated by the head of any "honor military school" as its "honor graduate" to enter the Academy without mental examination. However, there was no abatement in the requirement on such candidate to show by certificate his satisfactory completion of the fourteen units of preparatory work demanded by regulations, U.S.M.A. Subsequently, in 1930, two further provisions required such candidate to show by certificate, first, that he was a member of the current graduating class; secondly, that he had been graduated in the upper ten per cent of his class.

The Act of Congress approved June 8, 1926 authorized the appointment "of 40 cadets from among the sons of officers, soldiers, sailors, and marines of the Army, Navy, and Marine Corps of the United States who were killed in action or had died prior to July 2, 1921, of wounds or injuries received, or disease contracted in line of duty during the World War." Half of these were to be appointed from

among the sons of warrant officers, soldiers, sailors, and marines.

Such candidates were required to undergo competitively the regular entrance examination.

Introduction of the Validating Examination.-In 1930 the units of preparatory work required on candidates' certificates were raised by one, to a total of fifteen. Of greater importance was the introduction in that year of a validating examination for candidates whose preparation had not gone beyond secondary school, thus terminating for that group the former privilege of entering without mental examination. A similar requirement was placed on “honor military school" graduates who had stood below the upper ten per cent of their graduating class. As before, the rejection of any certificate because of low grades limited the candidate to the opportunity of entering by means of passing the regular entrance examination. The introduction of the validating examination was made necessary by the fact that a large percentage of the secondary school graduates who had previously been able to qualify by certificate proved to be wholly unable to cope with the Military Academy courses. 

The elimination at the outset of such candidates was a matter of simple justice to all concerned. It should be noted that this validating examination was devised to meet the needs of secondary school seniors or graduates, who, if properly prepared, should be able to take the test in their stride. In other words it was meant to obviate any necessity of their devoting an additional year to preparation for taking the regular entrance examination, and to accomplish that end by "validating" the evidence of satisfactory work in secondary school, as shown by the candidate's certificate. Accordingly, it was limited to testing the candidate's general knowledge of Algebra, Geometry, English grammar and composition, and English and American literature.

Most Recent Changes, 1939.-The most recent modification of requirements governing admission for candidates other than those taking the regular entrance examination became effective with the March, 1939, entrance examinations. It offered to secondary school graduates who had had no college work, the opportunity to submit with their secondary school certificate a supplemental report from the College Entrance Examination Board showing that the candidate had taken its scholastic aptitude test and mathematics attainment test (beta section), with the added proviso that the grades earned on these tests must be satisfactory. Candidates who were still in their senior year of secondary school and had not previously taken these tests were given the option of declining to take the Mili-

tary Academy's validating examination in March, electing instead to undergo in April the scholastic aptitude test and mathematics attainment test (beta section). Where the scholastic aptitude test included a mathematical section, the mathematics attainment test (beta section), was not required. At the same time the option of taking the validating examination, somewhat modified in form, remained open to this group of candidates. The 1939 regulations also brought a further modification of conditions governing the qualifications of candidates designated as "honor graduates" of "honor military schools", requiring them to qualify mentally for admission under the same rules as those governing all other candidates. 

General Effect of the Certificate Privilege.-—The employment of the college certificate as evidence of the candidate's qualifications has had a marked effect on the composition of the Corps, in raising very appreciably the percentage of the total enrollment with prior college work. In the current year 1938-'39, more than 52% of the 

Corps have had at least one year college; an additional five per cent have had at least one term. Meanwhile, in the case of a bare ten per cent, the preparation for West Point has been limited to a high school education. From these facts it might be concluded that the last group would be at a distinct disadvantage as compared with the relatively older, and further advanced, cadets. Before arriving at such conclusion, one should consider the facts developed in a survey of five classes which entered and graduated under substantially the present admission regulation. Dividing the Corps into five age groups, by years, it was discovered that two such groups, aged 18-19 and 17-18 at time of entrance to the Academy, led the older three, when judged by such criteria as graduation standing, discharge, turn-back, or conditions for academic deficiency, and military rank attained in the Corps organization. It is true that the two sets of facts do not necessarily dovetail, in that some of these younger cadets may have attended college, and vice versa. At the same time it is evident that such younger cadets, when soundly prepared, suffer no particular handicap in comparison with their older class-mates. 

Cadet Pay. Like any other individual in the fixed salary groups, the cadet is seriously affected by any marked fluctuation in the costof-living index. At various times, notably in the World War, he has found himself unable to meet his obligations at the Cadet Store. On 

March 31, 1917 the three classes present at the Academy were in debt for a total of more than $75,000. While prices were doubling, pay had remained static. For many years before the World War the cadet's annual pay was $600, plus a ration allowance of 30 cents per

diem, making a total of $709.50. A rise in the ration allowance to 

40 cents in 1917 was of little help in correcting the debt situation mentioned. The following year it was raised to 68 cents, and in 1919 the pay itself was raised to $780.00. A peak level of $1.08 was reached by the ration allowance in 1921 and 1922, raising the total of pay and rations to $1,174.20. Subsequent declines in the cost of living lowered the ration allowance to 75 cents, the present level, bringing the total annual income of the cadet to $1,053.75.

Pre-War Construction and Additional Needs.-The construction of facilities made necessary by the successive increases in the strength of the Corps has always lagged several years behind the increments in personnel. Three important periods of construction should be noted. The first, from 1909 to 1911 was principally the fruit of the efforts launched by the late Elihu Root, as Secretary of War.

XIV. Summary; The Role of the United States Military Academy. 

It is safe to say that the Spring of 1940 found the Military Academy in a better position to perform its mission than at any previous time in its history. Certainly never before had its plant and facilities for education, training and administration been so complete and modern. To appreciate the fact fully, one must note by contrast the conditions in the darker days which had so often fallen on West Point in the 137 years of its past. The halfstarved, poorly clad cadet of the earlier years, getting along somehow on his salary of ten dollars per month, presents one extreme of the broad picture. Lacking school-rooms or study halls, often without books, he had to depend on his instructors' capacity to give him a meager education; on his own faith and courage to make his effort worth while. Even that bare beginning vanished, as we have seen, in the days just preceding the War of 1812, when the Academy ceased to function. Reorganized under the press of emergency, it had to experience in later years more than one crisis before the American public and the Congress accepted it as a continuing and indispensable feature of the nation's defense system. There was at least one occasion when Congress failed to appropriate funds for salaries of officers and cadets. In another instance, only a private loan negotiated by the Superintendent (later ratified by Congress), kept the Academy's doors open. And on several occasions the cadets had seen the cost of living rise to a point where their expenses outran their pay and allowances by a wide margin. 

Lastly, the cadets of past years had only too often been hampered in their progress by inadequacy of plant and equipment. 

None of these conditions exist today. It remains only to secure the additional land now in sight to round out training facilities. 

West Point will then have for the first time in its history, every necessary instrument for the performance of its mission. 

To determine the precise time of the Academy's darkest hours is difficult if not impossible. Any decision on that point lies in the realm of opinion. However, thorough research would probably lead to the conclusion that the greatest hazards to West Point's existence and continuance occurred before the country had had an opportunity to measure the worth of its product under conditions of war. Such ex- perience at least led those in authority in 1861 to lay down a policy ensuring the preservation of everything vital in the West Point system. In sharp contrast stands the series of decisions which came near to wrecking that system in the final year of the World War. Predicated on the existence of an emergency, they definitely threw into the

discard the conclusions as to the Military Academy's role in the national defense system, conclusions which had been reached and held throughout the previous century. The Armistice, as shown, came just at the moment when West Point had become a military training camp. Moreover, the cessation of hostilities was not followed immediately by a restoration of the pre-war system. Instead, the Academy was launched on a three-year experimental course. Το those whose judgment, courage and persistence combined to convince Congress of the necessity of restoring the curriculum to a four-year basis, the country owes its gratitude, all the more so because that action was taken in the face of a rising national wave of pacifism. 

The lesson of the war years and their immediate aftermath remains. 

Future wars may be expected to bring again these demands for premature graduation of the Academy's classes. The decisions of that day will determine whether the lesson was learned, whether in brief, the dominant elements in the electorate have discovered that West Point's preparation for any war must end with the outbreak of that war. On that day the Academy must begin its preparation for the next emergency.

FOREIGN MILITARY ACADEMIES AND OTHER SOURCES OF COMMISSIONED OFFICERS.¹ I. GREAT BRITAIN. 

Great Britain maintains two schools of application: the Royal 

Military College (Sandhurst), and the Royal Military Academy (Woolwich). Sandhurst prepares candidates for commissions in the Infantry, Cavalry, Royal Tank Corps, or the Indian Army. The graduates of Woolwich, when commissioned, are appointed to the 

Royal Artillery, the Royal Engineers, or the Royal Corps of Signals. 

On July 28, 1938, the British Secretary of War, Mr. Leslie Hore 

Belisha, announced a series of sweeping reforms touching all grades of officers in the British Army, including also the candidates for commission at Sandhurst and Woolwich. In substance these changes make it possible for an English youth with the necessary educational qualifications and soldierly instincts to get his military training at these academies at government expense. Moreover, upon graduation the efficient officer is guaranteed a secure career up to a definite age and rank. Automatic promotion to his captaincy after eight years' service and to his majority nine years later, along with general increases in pay for all grades, have at last placed the British Army officer in a position more nearly comparable to that of the American 

Army officer than hitherto.

The reforms eradicate the final vestiges of a system dating back to feudal days, when officers served their King only at intervals and depended almost wholly on the income from their estates. That system, evolving gradually into a practice of obtaining commissions by purchase, effectively limited the officer class to those who could qualify by wealth and social position. This latter reform improves the prospects for the man in ranks. 

The principal source of supply of commissioned officers to the present has been:

R.M.C. and R.M.A.,-about three-fourths of the total. 

The Universities,-slightly less than one-fourth. 

Public Schools, Territorial Army, and the Dominions,-a small percentage.

The Army (enlisted personnel),-a small percentage. 

For all these groups, abilities and social standing of the candidate have been the prime criteria in their selection. It is not believed that the reforms mentioned will result in any appreciable change in those respects. Candidates for R.M.C. and R.M.A. will continue to be drawn largely from the Public Schools,-Eton, Harrow, Wellington, Rugby, etc. A considerably smaller group will enter from the 

Secondary Schools, comparable to the American public high schools. 

In addition provision is made for certain special groups, among them: 

King's Cadetships-Awarded to sons of officers killed in action or who died of wounds or disease contracted on service abroad. 2

King's India Cadetships-awarded to sons of persons who have served in the Indian Army or Indian Civil Service. 

N.C.O. Cadets-Outstanding non-commissioned officers from the Army.

Apprentice-Tradesmen Cadets—a small number of vacancies reserved annually for this group at both R.M.C. and R.M.A.

The fees for the full course at both Woolwich and Sandhurst (18 months) amount to approximately $1500 for civilians, $900 for sons of army officers. Scholarships and remissions of fees in varying degrees have for some time in the past scaled down these costs for various groups and individuals. The Hore-Belisha reforms have now established the rule for all cadets that payment of fees will depend on the parents' capacity to pay. In short, no boy otherwise qualified will be denied admission because of his parents' lack of funds to finance him.

As already stated the length of the course is eighteen months. 

The net period of education and training at the academies is 54 weeks. 

Aside from two brief vacation periods, the remainder of the time is occupied with training in the field. Necessarily most of the cadets' time is taken up with strictly military and professional education. 

And it should be noted that most of the cadets undergo considerable pre-Academy development. It begins for the boy of eight or nine entering the Public Schools, where regimentation and stern lessons in self-control are the keynote. For the boy who set the Army as his objective, the O.T.C., (Junior and Senior Officers' Training 

Corps), helps to develop the fit and to weed out the unfit. These "Gentlemen Cadets" who make up the bulk of the enrollment at R.M.A. and R.M.C. have gained their general education prior to their cadetship. The education of this group upon graduation, however, falls short of that of a graduate of the United States Military Academy, particularly as to scope and content of the courses covered, and as to thoroughness in their pursuit. For the fiscal year 

1939 (ending March, 1940) the program called for graduation of 

250 cadets from Woolwich, 540 from Sandhurst.

They average about eighteen years of age at entrance. The "A" 

Cadets entering from the Army and other sources average about 20 years. The University product, entering the Army directly, has a wider general education than the graduate of Woolwich or Sandhurst. 

His military training prior to commissioning is secured mostly through the O.T.C.

Summing up, the young British Army officer class of tomorrow,

will be more representative of the nation as a whole than has been the case in the past. Merit will in time become the outstanding determinant in his selection, first, as a cadet, and later as an Army officer. To what extent the social standing of the individual will influence decision as to his selection remains to be seen. In other words, even though it is clear that the candidate whose qualifications of mind, physique and character are below standard will be denied the opportunity to secure a commission in the Army, it is believed that social consideration will still play an important in the selection of candidates from the group posesssing the basic qualifications. 

Only time can tell how far the social factor will enter into the process. 

II.-FRANCE. 

Two schools are maintained to prepare candidates for commission in the French army; L'École Speciale Militaire, at St. Cyr; and L'École 

Polytechnique, in Paris. The course is two years in each case. In 

1938 there were 780 cadet at St. Cyr and 468 at Polytechnique. The great majority of these were of civilian origin and had gained their admission through competitive examinations. However both schools maintain platoons of non-commissioned officers selected from the regular army as candidates for commission. 

Candidates for both schools must be between the ages of eighteen and twenty-two years. French citizenship, by birth or naturalization, is required. The educational qualifications are high, particularly so in the case of candidates for L'École Polytechnique. In fact only the intellectual elite of the nation can qualify for admission to that institution.

The courses of instruction at both schools cover two years. However, upon graduation, the newly commissioned second lieutenants are given immediately a further two years' training in the Schools of Application of their respective branches. Incidentally their initial acceptance of the cadet training includes a contract to remain in the service for six years after graduation. An exception to this rule is that a few categories elect to serve for three years only, including the two years' instruction. However, a considerable percentage of graduates are assigned to government services of a semi-military or non-military character. Specifically, graduates of L'École Polytechnique are assigned to the Engineers and Artillery, and in addition to following special services:

Service of Powder and Explosives 

Navy

Naval Construction Service

Naval Artillery Service

Naval Engineering Service Air Force 

Corps of River and Harbor Engineers

Corps of Aeronautical Engineers Service of Roads and Bridges (Home and Colonial) Service of Mines (Home and Colonial) 

Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs

State Water and Forestry Service State Manufacturing State Railways 

The St. Cyr graduates are assigned to the Infantry, (including 

Tanks) the Cavalry, Train, and Gendarmerie.

The difference in objectives of the two schools is reflected in their respective curricula. The course at L'École Polytechnique is essentially scientific. It includes descriptive geometry, analytical geometry, calculus, mechanics, astronomy, physics, chemistry, architecture, and modern languages.

The St. Cyr curriculum covers general military instruction, tactics, history, geography, topography, applied science, target practice, engineering, legislation, administration, modern languages, physical training, riding and fencing. All first year cadets at St. Cyr are given the same course. At the end of the year the class is divided into two categories: those to be assigned to the Infantry and Tanks, and the remainder, who go to the Cavalry, Train, or Gendarmerie. The number in each category is fixed annually by the Minister of War. 

Board and tuition at both schools is gratuitous. Foreigners may be admitted, either as auditors living outside the school and paying no fees, or as students in dormitories. 

III.-GERMANY. 

Germany has five Basic Officer Training Schools called "Kriegschule"-War Schools-which correspond in a measure to West Point. They are located in Potsdam, Dresden, Hanover, Munich, and Vienna. The total attendance of the four schools is estimated at about 2300 annually.

The officer candidate applies to the Colonel of the Regiment he desires to join and submits the following evidence: 

1. Education at least equivalent to two years of college. 

2. Proof of Aryan descent.

3. Biography in own writing, photographs, birth certificate, proof of good character, German citizenship, marital status (unmarried), etc. 

If documents are satisfactory, the candidate reports for personal interview and preliminary examination, including physical, conducted by three officers of the regiment. If accepted as a provisional candidate and acceptable to the War Department he next undergoes a searching psychological examination, designed to bring out his fundamental characteristics.

Upon acceptance by the Colonel and approval of the War Department the candidate enters the army as "Fahnenjunker" on October 

1st to serve one year in the ranks, with periods divided as follows: 

1. October 1 to March: General recruit training; upon completion made lance corporal. 

2. March to July: Special training to fit him for command of combat group. About July 1st promoted to sergeant. 

3. July to October 1st: Intensified duties as noncommissioned officer. Colonel now decides which of his "Fahnenjunker" should go to "Kriegschule."

The course at the "Kriegschule" is designed to give all cadets a basic theoretical military training, identical for cadets of all arms. 

The following subjects are taught: 

Theoretical: Tactics and appreciation of terrain; military history; customs of the service; weapons; military engineering; signal communications; motors; air force; surveying; medical service. 

Practical: Drill, physical training, riding, motorcycling.

Courses in seven foreign languages are voluntary. 

Half way through the course all cadets take an intermediate examination.

The following excerpts from the regulations governing the training and instructions of these officer-candidates are of interest: (1.) Training "The students are to be trained to become personalities of strong character, who, as leaders and instructors, will be worthy men under all circumstances. "The Schools must lay the foundation for a unity of spirit and a high conception of their calling within the corps of officers. All teaching staffs must take every opportunity both on and off duty to develop, by personal example and by instruction, the cadet's devotion to duty, sense of responsi-

bility, unselfishness in the treatment and protection of subordinates, understanding, judgment; and a full development of the personality must be emphasized. The student must realize that he is judged upon his innate worth. Unhealthy ambition and seeking after personal advantage are flaws of character which render a man unsuitable as an officer. "The instructors must not consider themselves only as superiors, rather they must be the counselors to the students in all matters, not only those pertaining to the Service. The interchange of ideas in a spirit of comradeship is the best means to this end. "The governing spirit of goodwill in the treatment of students must rest upon a strict discipline. "Greatest stress must be placed upon simple living, regularity in financial matters, precision, and meticulous military procedure on and off duty. (2.) Instruction "The military academies prepare time schedules based upon the educational directive. Instruction in the classroom and practical training are to alternate appropriately in each day. "There is to be no scheduled instruction in the forenoon of one day each week. The senior instructor may utilize it as he deems best. In general, he should utilize it for terrain exercises, visits to troop units stationed in the vicinity, and for participation in exercises of the combined arms. 

The students are to have a certain amount of time at their own disposal, so that they may learn to apportion it between work, recreation, and self-improvement. For this reason 

Saturday afternoons are customarily set aside for the students' use. Scheduled instruction is to be amplified by stimulating and instructive lectures by experts in scientific knowledge and practical affairs so that the general development of the students may be enhanced thereby. Special funds will be made available for these lectures. "The applicatory method of teaching is the most valuable one for all branches of instruction. Theoretical principles must be developed from practical cases. Precepts do not become a part of one's life when they are merely memorized. The instructor must cite an instance from practical experience, develop it, and deduce from it the pertinent applications of the precept. The student must under-

stand how to explain clearly, expertly, and logically what he has learned and how to associate the applicable principles with the precept he has been taught. "All instruction must be as nearly visual as possible. 

The use of films, slides, epidiascopes, charts, and models must be required to the greatest extent possible. “In practical training the prime objective is the development of the student into a vigorous soldier and a military personality. Very special value must be placed upon the development of the personal bearing and the commandvoice of the students."

Upon successful completion of the course, the "Fahnenjunker" is promoted to "Fähnrich" (ensign). The final examination of the school, the "officer examination" determines the relative rank of the cadets for their career. It comprises written tests in five subjects: tactics; appreciation of terrain; customs of the service; weapons; military engineering. The subjects have unequal value in computing class standing. Most important is judgment of character made by each officer instructor. Upon passing this examination, the “Fähnrich" is promoted to "Oberfähnrich" (literally "upper ensign”).

The candidate then goes to one of the following branch schools for three months training: Infantry, Cavalry, Artillery, Motor, Combat Corps, Pioneers, Signal Corps. Here he gains experience in training and commanding the school units, and in the practical use of all weapons of the branch.

Upon completion of this three months' course the candidate goes to his regiment for two or three months' service duty as a lieutenant. 

During this time he is carefully observed by all the regimental officers. At the conclusion of this period he is voted upon to determine his desirability as a permanent officer of the regiment. If the vote is unanimous he is certified to the Commander-in-Chief for commission. If not unanimous, the Colonel makes a full report to the same authority, who in turn makes the final decision.

IV. ITALY. 

The system under which the Italian youth is trained for the duties of an army officer can best be visualized by comparing it, first, to the British and French systems, under which the candidates for commission in the infantry and cavalry undergo one basic course; and those for the artillery and engineers, a different and more scientific course. Secondly, the graduates of these two schools are immediately sent to Schools of Application, which are somewhat com-

parable to the American Service Schools at Fort Benning, Fort Sill, etc. Except for a short leave, there is no break in these primary and advanced phases of instruction. The graduate of the School of Application is presumed to be qualified for all duties of a company commander.

It is possible for outstanding enlisted men to get the benefit of advanced schooling, preliminary to a commission in the Army. With that exception the Italian officers are the product of a strenuous system of education and training beginning with one of the three military colleges at Rome, Naples, and Milan. Graduates who are bent on infantry or cavalry duty then enter the Infantry and Cavalry Academy at Modena. On completion of the two-year course they receive a final year in one of the Schools of Application: Infantry at 

Modena, Cavalry at Pinerolo and Tor di Quinto. Candidates for commission in the artillery or engineers are schooled for two years at the Royal Artillery and Engineer Academy at Turin, and for a further two years at the Artillery and Engineer School of Application, also at Turin. Thus, the specialized academic training of the officers in the scientific branches covers a total of four years, as compared with but three for the infantry and cavalry officers. 

The pattern of instruction for these various schools of the arm or branch is almost identical in some respects. The differences, however, are striking, in that the artillerymen and engineers undergo a stern schooling in the basic and advanced sciences with which their branches or arms are concerned.

The Infantry and Cavalry Academy at Modena and the Infantry Schoo' of Application at Parma are directed and administered by a single headquarters, located at Modena. Immediate responsibility for instruction, however, is vested in the colonel in command of each school The Academy students' two-year course comprises military and physical training, instruction in professional military subjects, cultural and spiritual education, and moral training. The major emphasis is placed on professional training; in other words, the cacet at this Academy receives very little pure education comparable to that available in a civilian university. 

The nilitary subjects of the curriculum are as follows: Military 

Art, Tactical training, Military History,¹ Topography,¹ Technical 

Means,¹ Military Geography,¹ Explosives and Chemical Agents,

Weapons, Elements of Ballistics and Fire, Foreign Languages¹ 

(French, German, Serbian), Science of State (emphasizing the virtues of Fascism), Automobile and Motorcycle Driving, Military Subsistence.1 

1. These subjects cover a two-year course; the others, a one-year course.

The West Pointer will be interested in the following excerpts from a recent circular of the General Commandant of both schools, as giving some insight into the methods of instruction and the curricular objective: 66* The course must be practical; all useless instruction must be eliminated * *

To the man of action a good teacher is one who knows how to interest students in the subject matter and develop their military culture which is the culture of the man of action.

A good teacher is one who develops the mind and character to absorb merely the outline of a subject matter. 

Accustom the students to constructive thought, not to destructive criticism, that they may consider analysis as the indispensable condition for synthesis, which is the ultimate goal.

Students must learn without undue effort. 

Measure character by hard work, spur it on by praise, awaken it by reproof. Character is will.

Char- acter is based on steadfastness, sentiment, enthusiasm without limit.

In movements within the Institute the walk is abolished; movements are always carried out at the run. * The students will be given ratings in the following activities whenever practicable: daring, enthusiasm, appearance, comradeship, will power, manner of expression, deportment, dress, energy, orderliness, salute, demeanor off duty, comportment at mess, personal care, care of armament, care of clothing. 

Ratings must not be assigned at fixed dates but whenever the instructor is in a position to evaluate the favorable or unfavorable qualities of the student. It is necessary that the student is aware of being continually supervised, observed and appraised."

Military training at the Modena Infantry and Cavalry Academy aims at qualifying the student for all duties of the platoon commander by the end of the two-year course. In scope, such training is considerably narrower than that given the West Pointer, but within its limits it is definitely more far-reaching in perfecting the student's technique. The full course covers basic and advanced work in military drill, maneuvers, and weapons; command school (teaching the student to teach), gymnastics (with heavy emphasis on daring and

1222 risk), equitation, fencing, marching (with emphasis on fatigue, endurance and continual hard work). 

For administrative purposes, and as a basis for military training, the students at the Academy are organized into three battalions. 

Commands of units, from platoon to battalion, are held by commissioned officers of appropriate rank. The students after April 1st of each year, are given frequent short opportunities to exercise command, usually for a day, and in turn. They are rated closely at such times by all officers in observation.

Successful graduates of the Modena Academy continue their training for one year at the Infantry School of Application at Parma. The technique of instruction is identical with that of the Academy. The field of studies likewise represents only an extension of the primary work into more advanced phases. The study of fortifications, liaison and map making is added to the theoretical-practical-applied work. Military training is advanced to include flying, skiing, and the pack train. In the cultural field, conferences are held on military aerial and maritime art, military didactics, military administration and accountability. At the close of the course searching examinations are given, including oral queries; written, graphic or applied tests; physical and practical exercises. Of particular importance in determining the student's final standing is his rating in military aptitude, awarded by a commission of all the officers who have supervised his training.

The Royal Artillery and Engineer Academy and the School of Application at Turin are organized administratively on much the same basis as their Infantry-Cavalry counterparts. Historically, the Turin institutions are much the older. They are in fact the ultimate descendants of the Royal Academy of Savoy, projected by Duke Carl 

Emmanuel II in 1675 and founded on September 1, 1677 by the Duchess Mary John Baptist of Savoy Nemours. After a checkered career and many suspensions, the Academy was reestablished in 1815 by 

King Victor Emmanuel I, as the Royal Military Academy. From 1860 

forward it has trained officers only for the Artillery and Engineers. 

The Academy differs sharply from the Modena and Parma schools in its emphasis on scientific instruction. Civilian instructors, ranking with university professors, conduct most of the scientific work. 

The list of "general studies" comprises algebraic and infinitesimal analysis, analytic geometry, physics, organic and inorganic chemistry, rational mechanics, drawing, topography, military geography, portable arms, artillery (materiel and fire), military art, science of state, fortifications, foreign languages.

Physical training covers gymnastics, fencing, equitation, motorcycle driving, and a broad range of sports. Practical military training aims at turning out a fully qualified squad leader at the end of the first year; platoon leader at the end of the second. For such purposes the engineers are necessarily separated from the artillerymen during much of the instruction. 

Among the many ceremonial occasions is one of long tradition, and of particular interest to the West Pointer,-the "Mak P 100" holiday. 

In the Turinese dialect the quoted expression is pronounced "Mach pi sent," meaning "but a hundred more," the Italian "Hundredth

Night." The occasion is celebrated with festivities of various kinds, including a special number of the school journal in which officers, instructors, and students are commented upon (in what manner, is not clear).

The Academy graduates continue their work for two years at the Artillery and Engineer School of Application, also at Turin. As stated above, the total course covers four years, whereas the infantrymen and cavalrymen have but three.

The School of Application dates back to the Royal Theoretical— Practical Schools of Artillery and Fortifications founded in Turin in 1739 by Carl Emmanuel III, King of Sardinia. It is thus the oldest theoretic school of these two arms in existence, antedating the establishment of the British Royal Military Academy by two years. 

For many decades it was the acknowledged leader in its field. Other countries adopted its methods of instruction and engaged its graduates as instructors.

The course of general studies, following that of the Academy in logical sequence, includes: architecture, military aeronautics, military naval art, exterior ballistics, applied chemistry, road construction, drawing, electrotechnics, explosives and chemical agents, permanent fortifications, hydraulics, artillery (employment and materiel), engineer employment and transport, applied mechanics, kinematics, dynamics, technical means, bridges, stresses and strains in materiel, machinery, science of construction, and history of military art.

Physical instruction continues the development from the first two years at the Academy. The military instruction does likewise, placing great emphasis on practical terrain exercises. Much of this work is carried on in the frontier zones. In addition, the effort is made to enure the student to hardship by the conduct of practical exercises in all extremes of climate, season, and altitude. 

Graduation leave is a delight unknown. The graduate, directly after commencement exercises are over, reports to his regiment for duty.

In summary, certain characteristics of the Italian system of officer production should be noted. It is essentially a system of professional training. True, the artillerymen and the engineers receive an extensive scientific education. At the same time only such courses are included, and within the courses such material, as bear directly on the future professional duties of the student. In other words the candidate for commission in the Italian Army is given an intensive but narrow professional training. Throughout these years of preparation the student is placed under constant high pressure, physical, mental, emotional. To find an equivalent in this respect in American experience one must go back to our officer training camps of the World War period. In other words, a war tempo prevails in the training of Italy's future army officers. The schools are in step with 

Italy's history under Fascism; they find their perfect parallel in the corresponding schools of Germany and Japan. 

V.-RUSSIA. 

Soviet Russia has no school for training officers of the Red Army comparable to West Point. Candidates for commissions are trained in separate schools for the various arms or services. Each of the 

15 Military Districts has an Infantry School, many of the districts have Artillery Schools. The schools for the other branches such as cavalry, engineers, ordnance, signal corps, motorization and mechanization, and chemical warfare are mostly located in European Russia.

The course of study in these basic schools is three years, the candidate entering between the ages of 17 to 21, after having served from one to three years in the ranks of the Red Army or having graduated with distinction from a high school. Usually there are about 10 applicants for each vacancy, ensuring selection of the best prepared candidates. The average cadet strength at each school is about 600.

In general all of the basic schools have the same curriculum. That of the Infantry school includes: 

1st and 2nd years: mathematics, physics, chemistry, German, Russian language and literature, politics, military tactics and history. 

3rd year: Infantry tactics and technique are emphasized and cadets spend much time at the sand tables and miniature ranges and on the maneuver grounds. 

The course also includes a wide range of lectures on current events.

Physical education is given daily throughout the course. It includes calisthenics, gymnastics, riding, shooting, running, swimming and all forms of track and field sports. Endurance races and other concentrated forms of military sports and training are required, including bivouacking in the snow during the severe Russian winter. Strenuous practical work is required in the summer camps of about two months.

For the other branches the tactics and technique of the branch is substituted for that of Infantry. Upon graduation the cadet is commissioned as second lieutenant.

VI.-SWITZERLAND. 

L'École Militaire, conducted as a department in L'École Polytechnique Federale, a civilian institution at Zurich, is in reality a part of the Army school system, and is the only school of its kind in Switzerland. The department is supported from Federal military appropriations, and the instructors are army officers. 

The school "is designed to give an opportunity to reserve army officers to enlarge and deepen their theoretical and practical military knowledge. It serves also to prepare for their duties the career officers of the combatant arms."

The full course comprises two winter semesters (about three months each), in consecutive years. Abbreviated courses are given in summer semesters. The general scope of the course includes: 

History of the World War; Swiss campaigns; Tactical leadership of infantry; Infantry and Artillery armament; Exterior ballistics; Practical infantry fire; Military gymnastics; Artillery fire; Demonstrative ballistics.

Career officers, prior to their permanent appointment are required to pass two winter semester courses in consecutive years. 

VII.-JAPAN. 

Under the provisions of a series of Imperial Ordinances, the junior and senior courses of the Military Academy have been reorganized such that two completely new schools now exists, the Junior Military Academy (Rikugun Yoka Shikan Gakko) and the Military Academy (Rikugun Shikan Gakko). In 1938 a separate Air Academy was established. 

Junior Military Academy

The Junior Military Academy has taken over the plant in Tokyo of the former Japanese Military Academy. Its principal mission is the training of junior students who are to become cadets for various

branches of the service. In addition it trains selected warrant and non-commissioned officers from the active army, known as senior students. These are given a year's training and are then commissioned. However they can not advance beyond the grade of captain and they must retire at the age of 49. 

Junior students are drawn from four sources: 

1—Graduates of military preparatory schools (three year course), of ages 16 to 18 years. 

2-N.C.O.'s on active list under 25 years of age, selected by their regimental commanders, having the equivalent of a high school education. 

3-Privates on active list, under 22 years of age; other requirements same as for N.C.O.'s. 

4-Applicants at large, 16 to 18 years of age, having the equivalent of a high school education. 

The Academy curriculum covers two years, a new class starting each year. The normal total enrollment is 750. About twenty per cent of this total are graduates of the Military Preparatory School.¹ 

Senior students are selected from warrant and advanced N.C.O. grades after competitive mental examinations. The maximum age is 38. They undergo a one-year course; then, upon graduation, serve in regiments for two months before being commissioned. 

The Junior Students, however, serve as first-class privates, corporals and sergeants for eight months after graduation. At the conclusion of that period they enter the Military Academy. 

Brief examination of the governing regulations makes it clear that the Japanese cadet lives a life of Spartan simplicity. He is clothed and rationed at government expense, his ration allowance being three sen (about one cent) higher per day than that of the enlisted men. Discipline is rigid; supervision, constant. There are no amusements, sports are discouraged, contact with civilians is frowned upon. 

School hours run from 9:00 A.M. until noon, again from 1:00 to 

3:00 P.M. Evening study, in halls adjacent to squad-rooms, is supervised by officers. Taps is sounded at 10:00 P.M. The intervals between daily school hours are filled with drills, fencing, gymnastics, or some other form of practical training. Only on Sundays or holidays are the cadets permitted to be out of barracks. However, there is a one-month vacation in each year, and a short period of leave over the New Year holidays. 

1. As a result of the emergency in China the length of the course was reduced by a period varying from four months to a year. At the same time the enrollment was increased to about 2700.

The Military Academy. 

As part of the reorganization mentioned above the Military Acaddemy was moved from Tokyo to Zama-mura, Koza-gun, Kanagawa Prefecture. It trains students for commissions in the various branches of the army, except the Military Police. Like the Junior 

Military Academy it is divided for instructional purposes into three main departments,-academic, military, and equitation. Its enrollment is limited to graduates of the Junior Military Academy who have successfully completed their eight months' tour of duty with troops.

Under normal conditions the total enrollment is about 750, and the duration of the course is 1 year and eight months. As in the case of the Junior Military Academy the length of the course has been reduced and the enrollment increased as a result of the "Chinese Incident." The changes are regarded as temporary. 

Upon successful completion of the course the student must undergo a final four-months' tour of troop duty as sergeant-major or probational officer before being commissioned as second lieutenant. 

The Air Academy. 

An ordinance of December 9, 1938, transformed the Aviation Branch of the Military Academy at Tokorozawa into the Army Air Academy. 

Regulations implementing that ordinance declared that the purpose of the Academy was to train cadets and enlisted students for commissions in the Air Corps, terminating such activities at the Military Academy. The cadets, graduates of the Junior Military Academy, are admitted annually for a course lasting two years and four months. 

Enlisted students are given a one year course. Regulations provide for a tri-partite division of the school curriculum: (1) A training department for theoretical instruction and related spiritual instruction; (2) A school unit, conducting practical and spiritual training; (3) A Service Park responsible for practical training and for repair of equipment.

VIII. CANADA. 

The Royal Military College of Canada opened its doors to its first class of eighteen gentlemen cadets in June, 1876. The preceding year an Act of the Canadian Parliament provided for the establishment of an institution to impart "a complete education in all branches of military tactics, fortifications, engineering and a general scientific knowledge in subjects necessary for a thorough knowledge of the

military profession, and for qualifying officers for command and for staff appointments."

The site chosen for R. M. C. was a small peninsula formed by the confluence of the Cataraqui River and Lake Ontario, immediately east of Kingston. That spot and the surrounding region had played a significant part in Canadian military history from 1673 forward, the year when Count Frontenac established a military post on the peninsula. Two structures in the Academy grounds remain today as picturesque evidence of the area's military traditions of past centuries: Fort Frederick, built soon after the War of 1812; and "The Stone Frigate," now a dormitory, but in the late eighteenth century a naval barracks serving Canadian activities on Lake Ontario. 

Eligibility for admission is, with a few exceptions, limited to British subjects whose parents have resided in Canada for the two years immediately preceding the candidate's date of entrance. A candidate must have attained the age of sixteen, and must not have reached the age of nineteen on the 1st of January in the year of entrance to the College. Admission is on a competitive basis. Allotment of vacancies to the various provinces are made in proportion to the population strength. Educational requirements for admission are high, calling for a "pass matriculation” certificate in English Literature and Composition, French Literature and Composition, Canadian History, Algebra, Geometry, Chemistry and Physics, plus one elective (History, language, or mathematics). Candidates who present senior matriculation certificates or any other form of qualification higher than the "pass matriculation" receive a proportionate bonus toward their totals for competition. As a result of entrance examinations, enrollment is brought up to a total of 200 at the beginning of each term. Normally, withdrawals reduce that figure to 

190 by the end of the term.

The military organization of R. M. C. resembles that of West Point. 

The gentlemen cadets are organized into a single Cadet Battalion, with the Commandant in command. Strict discipline is maintained in all phases of training.

A four-year course is given, broken into four terms of ten months each, with the new term opening September 1st. The total cost of the course to the cadet or his parents is $1500. It covers such major items as uniform, books, and other educational material, and laundry. 

Mess charges are apparently borne by the Government. 

The curriculum resembles that of the United States Military Academy in many important respects, notably in its emphasis on scientific subjects. The list of subjects is, in full: Mathematics and Mechanics, Physics, Chemistry, Geometrical Drawing and Descriptive

Geometry, English, History, French, Military History, Imperial Or ganization, Military Organization, Administration and Law, Tactics and Topography, Artillery, Military Engineering, Equitation, Physical Training.

The general objective of the education and training of the gentlemen cadets is covered by the following excerpts from the the Royal 

Military College's Calendar for 1939-1940: 

"(a) The College is a military institution and is maintained primarily for the purpose of training cadets in the moral, mental and physical requirements for entry into the commissioned ranks of the permanent and non-permanent sea, land and air forces. "(b) Moral training shall include the inculcation and development of the essential moral characteristics of discipline and leadership. "(c) Mental training training shall include the fundamental military knowledge required in all branches of the Services; the fundamental scientific knowledge required in all branches of the Services and, particularly, in such technical corps as the artillery, engineers, signals, ordnance, army service corps and air force; and such cultural knowledge as will insure an understanding of the general political, economic and social environment in which, subsequent to graduation, the military and scientific knowledge of the graduate will be applied. Education at the College, therefore, shall include the study of cultural, military, and engineering subjects. "(d) Physical training shall include such instruction and exercises as will result in the balanced development of a healthy body and the fostering of an alert mind." 

The standards of graduation designed to aceord with the above interpretation of the objective are as follows: "(a) The production of young officers capable of entering any of the following branches of the permanent or nonpermanent land forces;-cavalry, artillery, engineers, signals, infantry, army service corps and army ordnance corps. "(b) The production of young officers capable of entering the permanent or non-permanent air forces. "(c) The production of special entry cadets for the Royal 

Canadian Navy and of young officers for the Royal Canadian 

Naval colleges. "(d) A standard of military and cultural knowledge on

graduation generally equivalent to that of graduates of 

British cadet colleges. "(e) A standard of engineering knowledge on graduation which will permit of specially recommended graduates entering fourth year and of recommended graduates entering third year in certain universities in civil, mechanical and electrical engineering. As high a standard will be attained in other branches of engineering or applied science as time, instructors and equipment will permit." 

In recognition of the wide range of post-graduate activities listed above, the educational policy of R. M. C. has been modified to confine the basic course to the first three years and a part of the fourth year. 

Thereafter the gentleman cadet has the privilege of choosing from among five engineering courses and one general course for the continuation of his studies to graduation. 

Graduates are eligible for commission in the Royal Canadian Navy, the Permanent Active Militia of Canada, the Royal Canadian Air Force, the British Army, the Indian Army, and the Royal Air Force. 

Candidates for commission in the Royal Canadian Navy leave the college at the end of their second year for probationary service with the Navy. In parallel with the practice at West Point, seniority in assignment is based on academic standing. The majority of the graduates are not given permanent commissions. In 1937, for example, twenty-two of the thirty-eight graduates turned to civilian pursuits.

The Royal Military College is justly proud of its record in the World War. More than 95 per cent of the graduates and ex-cadets served in the armed forces of the Empire, fifteen per cent made the supreme sacrifice; and more than six hundred military decorations or mentions in dispatches were awarded, including one Victoria 

Cross and twelve knighthoods.

IX.-MEXICO. 

The military school system of the Mexican Army and Navy was reorganized in March, 1932, under the Direction General of Military Education, which, after several changes, now operates under the direct control of the Office of the Secretary of War. 

The only schools in the Mexican Army which function in any capacity similar to that of West Point are as follows: (a) Preparatory School (experimental). Open to corporals and sergeants of the infantry, cavalry and artillery

on duty at the Military College; course 1 year; graduates eligible to enter the Military College, and thus to become officers on graduation. 

(b) Training School. The Military College at San Jacinto, 

Federal District, Mexico City, is the only purely training school and the only school which admits civilian aspirants for commissions in the army; two courses are offered, each of 4 years. Students of the first course are qualified civilians (about 78 students) and enlisted men; graduates enter the Army as sub-lieutenants of infantry, cavalry and artillery. This course is also open to officers who have not previously taken up military studies. Usually about one hundred and seventy cadets are graduated annually and commissioned in the infantry, cavalry, and artillery. The second course is a special one to produce construction and industrial engineers; open to qualified officers of at least two years' service with troops. A special course for field officers has been established recently X.-CUBA. 

The Military Academy, with the maximum authorized enrollment of 75 cadets, is available to enlisted men of the army who have at least two years service. Students are selected as a result of a competitive examination held in August of each year. Candidates with the highest satisfactory grades are appointed to fill vacancies caused by graduation or other separation from the academy. The course is of two years' duration, during which time all cadets must pursue the basic studies necessary for qualification as commissioned officers. 

The faculty is composed entirely of commissioned officers, who are selected for their efficiency and ability as instructors, though, according to law, civilians may be appointed as instructors, if necessary. 

Upon satisfactory completion of the two years' course, the cadets are commissioned 2d Lieutenants. In case there are not enough vacancies to include all graduates, they become additional 2d lieutenants until such time as vacancies occur.
