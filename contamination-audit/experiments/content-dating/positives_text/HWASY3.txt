INTRODUCTION 

HIS little brochure has been written solely for the 

THIS members of the Anglers' Club of New York. 

When the reader considers the long list of exhaustive and excellent English and American treatises on the subject of Fly Tying he will, no doubt, marvel at my temerity in adding this unpretentious effort to the imposing list of those that have gone before. No urge toward authorship inspired this modest opus! I received my orders from our justly esteemed President! "Mine not to reason why,-mine but to do-!" The fact that some of my fellow members, among them no less a light than Mr. E. R. Hewitt, have been good enough to admire flies tied by my hand has lent me the requisite courage to proceed. 

This little essay does not purport to be in any sense exhaustive. It is intended to serve as a guide to those amateur tyers who fish in Eastern waters for Brown Trout. It confines itself to a half-dozen of the most universally used American Brown Trout flies. Every man has his own list of "favorite flies" but it is believed that those here listed may fairly be called the "indispensables." The flies selected represent a number of different types as well as different patterns and the directions given for the various types will enable the

reader to tie an almost endless number of other patterns.

I have given only my own methods, without consideration for those of others. This will not, I trust, be taken as indicating a fixed belief in their superiority. 

There are many ways of killing a cat! I do know, however, that the technique described has served me well. 

It is my hope that it will do as much for those of my club-mates who may desire to try their skill at fly tying. 

EDGAR BURKE, M.D.

EARLY DAYS OF AMERICAN DRY FLY FISHING 

HEN we say that Theodore Gordon was the "father of dry fly fishing in America” the statement must not be taken to mean that he invented fishing with floating flies. Such a claim, if made, is easily disproved. English anglers used dry flies before his birth (September 18th, 1854, at Pittsburgh, Pa.) but, so far as is known, he was the first American dry fly fisher. 

He was also a skillful fly tyer, who taught his art to a whole school of successors, some of whom are still practicing his teachings in the Catskills. 

Animated by Gordon's success, these men began upstream fishing with floating flies and their example gradually spread until, from this small nucleus, there developed the present day cult of devotees of the dry fly in America.

Undoubtedly Gordon was influenced by the writings of British authors. During the 80's and 90's he shot and fished in England and cannot have failed to observe the tactics of Halford's disciples there, but he was a highly original and ingenious angler who left his mark indelibly on the sport in the land of his birth. American trout fishermen of today owe him a posthumous debt of gratitude.

In the 90's and early 1900's a small group of Catskill anglers, Christian, Jenkins, the Snedecors, Dr. Halsey [1]

and others, had already adopted Gordon's teachings and were using his flies tied to snells and without oil. 

Gordon preferred eyed flies used singly, as is the custom today, and tied directly to the end of his leader. 

Mr. George LaBranche was an early convert of Gordon's as was Mr. Hewitt. Gordon, himself, considered the dry fly ill suited to other than smooth water. Mr. 

LaBranche, in practice and by his writings, has done more than any other man to extend the field of usefulness of the floating fly. Probably his invention of the "Pink Lady" and the consequent increase in the popularity of wingless patterns, "tied buzz," such as the ever useful Hewitt's "Bi-Visible," have done much to lend effectiveness to his advocacy of dry fly fishing in broken and swift-flowing water, which constitutes so large a part of our Eastern trout streams, for this type of fly floats infinitely better and is far less easily "drowned" than the daintier creations which Gordon employed in his fishing. 

Theodore Gordon died at Bradley, on the Neversink, Sullivan County, N. Y., on May 1st, 1915. His name is perpetuated by the "Quill-Gordon," probably the most successful brown trout fly ever invented, as well as by the less known and less used "Gordon" which he, himself, always referred to as the "Golden 

Spinner." There is also every reason for giving him credit for being the first to tie the "Royal Coachman" with fan wings, a favorite style with many anglers. [2]

AMERICAN DRY FLIES OF TODAY 

Every angler admires a well tied dry fly and every angler has his own idea of what the perfectly constructed dry fly should be like. Contrary to the belief of most fishermen, the making of a first class fly is not difficult. Any one can learn the basic principles of its manufacture by watching a proficient fly maker attentively while he constructs a single specimen. The rest is simply a question of adequate materials and practice. It is for the benefit of those who may not have the opportunity of watching experts at work, and who would acquire a knowledge of this fascinating and useful pastime, that these directions have been written. 

While beautiful examples of the fly maker's art can be found in the tackle shops, the angler who is without knowledge of fly tying must take what is offered by the dealer, regardless of whether or not it meets his individual ideas and wishes. Obviously, the only remedy is to learn to tie one's own flies. 

Three qualities the perfect dry fly must have. It must be constructed of the correct materials, it must be durably made and, lastly, it must be as light as is compatible with a reasonable period of usefulness.

There is nothing more vexatious than a fly which unravels or otherwise goes to pieces after a few casts have been made with it or after it has taken a fish or two. 

Dry flies are intended to float and the longer a fly [3]

retains its buoyancy the better it is. Lightness and delicacy of construction and choice raw materials alone can impart these qualities to the finished article. 

The great truth that the beginner in fly tying must learn is that only material of one hundred per cent. quality can make a one hundred per cent. fly.

HOW TO OBTAIN MATERIALS FOR FLY TYING 

The primary requisite for dry fly making is good hackles. Hackles are the neck feathers of domestic cocks. They can be bought by the dozen of dealers in fly tyer's supplies, but much the more satisfactory way is to purchase whole necks. Not only are the feathers much cheaper when bought in this way but one has the additional advantage of obtaining a large assortment of varying sizes. A whole neck is much more easily cared for than an assortment of loose feathers. 

Each individual hackle is detached as needed. Strangely enough, most amateurs experience the greatest difficulty in procuring an adequate supply of really good hackles. Any hackle will not do by any means. Aside from its having the desired color and size, the feather used to hackle a dry fly must be resilient and stiff, the more so the better. Soft hackles are suitable only for wet flies. Three or more year old Pit Game Bantam cocks furnish the very best obtainable, but individual birds of almost any of the common domestic breeds [4]

of poultry may yield excellent material. One of the surest and cheapest sources of supply are the poultry stalls in Washington Market, in New York City. The employees of several of the firms who do business there are well aware of the value of properly preserved cocks' necks and regularly save and prepare them for fly tyers, both amateur and professional.

Various species of wild fowl, particularly ducks, contribute their quota of materials to the fly maker; chief among these is the Wood Duck, loveliest of all 

North American birds, recently threatened with extinction and now, very properly, strictly preserved by Federal law. Its flank feathers are essential for winging the following flies: "Cahill," light and dark, “Quill 

Gordon" and "Hendrickson." The writer can offer no hint on how to obtain the highly prized feathers of this bird. He, himself, has resorted to plucking old mounted specimens, long the prey of moths and gladly yielded to him by their owners. 

Flank feathers of the Mallard Drake are often dyed into a semblance of the Wood Duck's coveted plumage, but they are never as satisfactory as the genuine article. 

Just how essential the wings of any dry fly really are is a subject that has been a matter of debate for years. The only purpose that they serve is the parachute action which they give to the fly in its descent to the surface of the water, by virtue of which a gentler alighting and better "cocking" is believed to take place. [5]

Palmer flies, like the Hewitt "Bi-Visible" or the

LaBranche "Pink Lady," however, alight just as gently as the best made wing patterns, so that it seems reasonable to assume that this is just another of the many unsubstantiated theories piously adhered to by certain anglers. It seems probable that the trout cannot possibly see the wings of the artificial flies unless it be that the fly in question has been tied with extended wings as a so-called "Spent Gnat," like the familiar "Woodruff."

The floss silk and the crewel wools used for the making of fly bodies are obtainable at the notion counters of any department store.

Second in importance only to the right quality and size of hackles is the tying silk. Attempting to use even the finest size of sewing silk obtainable in the shops is a good deal like trying to sew on a shirt button with grocer's twine! Silk thread specially manufactured for fly tyers is generally imported from England, where 

Piersoll's "Gossamer” has long been the favorite, but a very superior article is now obtainable in this country. 

It is, if anything, a trifle finer in texture than the imported, while quite as strong as the latter.* The American silk comes only in white, whereas that manufactured by Messrs. Piersoll may be had in various colors and shades. *To be had of Reuben Cross, Neversink, N. Y., at a price of $1.00 per spool, the spool containing 2,000 yards. [6]

Tinsel and fine gold wire are obtainable from a number of wholesale milliners' supply houses, most of whom have their places of business between 30th and 

42nd Streets, just west of Fifth Avenue, in New York.

While the body of the dark "Cahill," when tied true to the original pattern, requires mole's fur, a very acceptable substitute for this last is dark bluish-grey crewel wool. For those sticklers for accuracy who wish to tie their "Cahills" in strict accordance with time honored custom I may say that the common house mouse or the grey rat of stables and out-houses will be found to furnish fur of a shade virtually identical with that of the much more difficult to obtain mole. 

Fly tyers' vises of various types may be had of 

Messrs. William Mills & Son, Park Place, New York.

Tastes differ as to this very necessary part of the amateur fly tyer's equipment and the more exacting will do well to place their orders for this article with one of the larger British tackle houses in London. The vises sold by Messrs. Mills & Son will, however, be found to answer every purpose, as well as having the additional merit of being much cheaper in price than the imported. Hackle pliers, of which at least two should be provided, may be had of Messrs. Mills & 

Son or any other large tackle house. They should be as light as is compatible with firmness of grip. Avoid large, heavy pliers. An ordinary pair of nail scissors completes the outfit essential for fly tying. [7]

Very much less "messy" than the various types of fly tyer's wax recommended by the authors of the many well known text books on the subject of fly tying, is plain spar varnish. The writer uses a brand known as "Sapolin Speed Varnish" made by the Sapolin Co., Inc., of New York City, and obtainable at any hardware dealer or paint shop. An ounce or two of this may be conveniently kept in a small medicine bottle, in the cork of which a toothpick or other wooden sliver of suitable size has been stuck as an applicator. Rub a drop of the varnish between the left thumb and index finger and then draw the tying thread between the two fingers once or twice. This will impregnate the silk sufficiently without making it sticky and unpleasant to handle. The fingers may be easily wiped on a small rag kept at hand for the purpose. Varnish dries more quickly than the wax preparations and, wanting a certain degree of deftness, the amateur may find that his thread has dried before he has completed his fly. This situation is easily remedied by a fresh application to the thread as it hangs down from the half completed fly. The silk used for dry fly tying is so fine in texture and so weak in tensile strength that it will be found difficult to draw it through the ordinary wax preparations without breaking it. One is spared this vexation by using the liquid varnish, which offers no resistance to the thread as it passes between the lightly approximated finger tips. [8]

REMARKS ON THE CONSTRUCTION OF DRY FLIES 

Proper "cocking" of a dry fly on the water is largely a matter of proper balance. Most commercial flies are defective in this respect. Figure 8 shows a typical "Store" specimen. Figure 7 depicts a fly tied in accordance with the writer's idea of correct construction. 

Mr. Hewitt, keenly alive to the question of balance in dry fly construction, sought to improve on the conventional models by having special hooks made by Farlow of London. These hooks have peculiarly heavy shanks and eyes, tapering off toward the bend. This construction, of necessity, adds materially to the gross weight of the finished fly, which is a serious detriment. 

It is this writer's contention, in his belief amply borne out by experience, that precisely the same improvement in balance can be obtained by varying the construction of the fly in the sense indicated by Figure 7, and this without adding a milligram to the feather weight of the finished article. It will be noted that the bodies of these flies are shorter than is usual and that the wings "set" much further back from the eye of the hook than is conventional. A very few casts made with flies so constructed will convince most men of their advantages. Reuben Cross, one of the outstanding exponents of the Catskill "school," and one of the most expert fly tyers living, incorporates this principle in his flies to a large extent. [9]

Of vital importance is the fact that the hackles used should be of a size exactly suited to the size of the hook on which they are wound. Too small hackles will not properly float the fly. Too large ones destroy its balance and make an unsightly object out of what should be a miniature work of art. A rough way of measuring this relationship is to select a hackle feather whose longest barbules are of the same length as the shank of the hook. Practice soon develops a surprising degree of accuracy in simple eye measure but the method mentioned is recommended to the beginner. 

In winding the tying silk about the hook shank be careful to avoid too many turns. Every turn adds to the bulk and weight of the fly. Never use two turns where one will do the work. 

Flies other than "Palmer" patterns depend largely upon their tail stylets, or whisps, for proper floating and "cocking." Nothing equals the barbules of a cock's neck hackle for stiffness and resiliency. Barbules cut from a Wood Duck's flank feathers are much softer and more yielding and do not answer the purpose so well as do hackle whisps. It is, therefore, recommended that in tying patterns like the "Cahills,” the “Quill 

Gordon" and the "Hendrickson" the former be substituted for the latter in making the tails. Use whisps from a coarse hackle feather taken from the same neck from which the smaller feathers for the legs of the fly are taken. [ 10 ]

HOW TO TIE A HENDRICKSON Material required: 

I flank feather of a male Wood Duck 

2 smoke-grey hackle feathers 

I small strand of light cream-grey crewel wool. 

Most amateur fly tyers will contend that the ideal hackles for this pattern and for the "Quill Gordon" are those taken from the necks of Andalusian Cocks.

Where this error originated is a mystery. It has been repeated by one man after another. Standard bred 

Andalusian Cocks have deep black neck feathers, obviously entirely unsuited for the "Hendrickson" fly.

The proper color is a translucent, bluish, smoke-grey, generally to be found only in game fowl. No feathers are harder to obtain than these and a neck of good quality having this color is a treasure of no mean value. 

In giving the directions which follow it is assumed that the tyer is right handed. 

Clamp the hook securely in the vise, the eye pointing to the right. In the exact middle of the shank (see 

Figure 1) fasten the end of an eighteen inch piece of varnished tying silk by making several overlapping turns away from yourself. Fasten a clip to the long end of the thread and let it hang down over the edge of your work table. Cut off the short end where it projects from under the turns of silk around the hook shank.

From one side of a Wood Duck's flank feather strip or, better, cut the barbules and bunch them between the [II]

thumb and index finger of the left hand. Hold them along the shank of the hook, the points directed toward the eye of the same, i. e., to the right of the tyer. (The length of the barbules projecting from the shank, when they have been tied to the same, should be approximately the length of the shank itself.) Now wind your tying silk (always winding away from you) as tightly as the tensile strength of the thread will stand about the bunch of barbules and the hook shank immediately in front of the finger tips holding the former. When a number of turns, sufficient to firmly bind the wings to the shank, have been taken, make a half hitch knot and let the thread hang down, a hackle plier attached to the distal end. Now seize the wings between the tips of the left thumb and index finger and turn them as far as possible back toward the bend of the hook, i. e., to the left of the tyer. Make a number of turns with the tying silk in front of the wings and as close up against them as possible. Release the wings. 

It will now be seen that they stand erect, at right angles to the shank of the hook. With the fingers of the left hand separate them into two equal bunches and pass two turns of the tying silk criss-cross between them, making an "X" between the two bunches.

This maneuver will hold them permanently apart and tends to still further maintain their erect position. 

Make another half hitch knot and let the tying thread hang down, a hackle plier attached to the end of it. [12]

Cut off the end of the wing barbules where they project from under the tying silk. It is well to hold the scissors at an angle of about 45 degrees to the long axis of the shank of the hook in doing this. This will cut the ends on a "slant," and make a tapering foundation for the body of the fly.

Now select the tail stylets or whisps, three to six in number, and hold them along the top of the shank, the tips pointing to the left, and wind the tying silk about them, starting at a point exactly opposite the tip of the hook and winding back to the wings. Now wind your tying silk back to the point of the first turn, i. e., opposite the point of the hook. Take up a small whisp of crewel wool,-a very small amount will be found to be sufficient,-attach one end of it under a turn of the tying silk at the base of the tail stylets. Wind the tying silk back toward the wings and make one turn more in front of them, letting the plier-weighted thread hang down as before. Wind the wool around the hook shank toward the wings, thus finishing the body, and bring one turn more in front of the wings. At this point catch the wool whisps with another half hitch knot of the tying silk and cut off the redundant end. 

Take up one of the two hackle feathers between the thumb and forefinger of the left hand. Pluck off the downy fuzz at its base, letting the bare basal end of the midriff project out from between the finger tips. 

Hold this projecting end of the midriff against the [13]

shank of the hook in front of and against the base of the wings, the shiny surface of the hackle being turned toward the eye of the hook. This will place the feather edgewise against the shank. With the tying silk make a turn about the base of the midriff and the hook shank and fasten off with two half hitch knots. The tip of the hackle feather is now pointing directly away from yourself. Seize the tip with a hackle plier and begin winding the hackle about the hook shank. This winding should be done with considerable tension, in order to cause the hackle barbules to stand out as much as possible, "like quills on the fretful porcupine." Make your first two turns in front of the wings and as close up against them as is physically possible. This prevents a subsequent tipping forward of the latter, a frequent fault of too loosely wound flies. Having made two turns in front of the wings with the hackle make another immediately behind them, finishing off the winding in front of the wings. 

Catch the tip of the hackle under a turn of the tying thread and make a half hitch knot to secure it. Cut off the redundant tip and the projecting basal end of the midriff. Now proceed exactly as before with the second hackle feather, tying it in as closely as possible against the one already wound and making all your turns in front of and also through the previous windings, thus making the latter more bushy and thick. Finish off by [14]

catching the tip of the second hackle just as you did the first, under a turn of the tying silk. Cut off the two projecting ends of this second feather and make a few half hitch knots,-usually three or four. Touch these last with a drop of varnish on the end of the small wooden toothpick or other applicator in the cork of your little varnish vial and lay the fly aside to dry. It is now completed and will be ready for use in half an hour, if needs must be, as the small amount of varnish used dries rapidly.

Some anglers prefer a fly tied with the minimum of hackle that will float it. In that case use but a single feather in place of the two which the tying directions here given call for. Flies so tied are suitable for the quiet, low water of mid-summer, but for early season fishing, when higher and rougher water may be expected, it is the writer's belief that flies hackled with two feathers will be found more satisfactory.

The directions here given are typical, so far as the actual technique is concerned, for the procedure employed in the making of most of the common patterns of dry flies, the only variation, of course, being in the materials used for the different patterns. Having mastered this method for the "Hendrickson" the beginner will be in a position to undertake the tying of any pattern with which he is familiar. The steps as outlined above will be frequently referred to in the directions given for the tying of subsequent patterns. [15]

There are minute refinements of technique which every one must work out for himself. No two men proceed precisely alike. There are little touches, so subtle that it is difficult to so phrase their description that another will understand the directions. The foregoing will be found to contain every essential step in the process; the "hyper-technicalities" already referred to reveal themselves with practice. Facility develops quickly, and initial discouragements are soon overcome. Of course, there are many other ways of accomplishing the same end. The method described is that always followed by the writer and is the outcome of the experience gained by the tying of, literally, thousands of dry flies.

HOW TO TIE A QUILL GORDON Material required: 

I flank feather of a Mallard Duck 

2 smoke-grey hackle feathers 

1 strip of bi-colored Peacock quill 

I strand of finest gold wire.

The chief difficulty in the making of this fly will be found to lie in the procuring of the bi-colored Peacock quill. This is obtained by stripping off the iridescent bluish-green herl from a barbule cut from the “eye” portion of a Peacock's shoulder plume, commonly miscalled a tail feather.

It may be of passing interest to state here that the [16]

train of the Peacock is not made up of its tail feathers, but of its shoulder plumes. The actual tail consists of relatively short, hard and coarse feathers, rusty brown in color, which the bird erects at will and which in their turn raise up, spread and support the long shoulder plumes.

About one out of every twenty to twenty-five feathers selected will yield the bi-colored quill; the rest will prove to have solid brown quill, unsuited to our purpose. Having found a strip of the desired color, remove the fuzzy green herl from the barbule by means of 

Stripping with your thumb nail. Care must be exercised as the quill tears very easily. 

Proceed precisely as in tying a "Hendrickson" up to the point where directions are given for tying in the crewel wool strand. In place of this last tie in one end of the quill strip and the fine gold wire. Wind the quill carefully to the right up to the wings and tie off just in front of them. (This, of course, means making one turn of the quill beyond the point where the wings are tied to the hook shaft.) If properly wound, the body of the fly will now present alternate rings of lighter and darker quill. Next take up the free end of the gold wire and wind it in the opposite direction over the quill, finishing off, as before, in front of the wings. The hackles are put on exactly as described under the paragraph dealing with the tying of the "Hendrickson." The purpose of the gold wire is to pro- [17]

tect the easily damaged quill from the teeth of the trout. Were it not so protected, one strike from a fish would ruin the fly. The body of this fly will present a neater and better shaped appearance if the gold wire be tied in with the end reaching as far forward as the base of the wings, parallel with and along the top of the hook shank.

HOW TO TIE HEWITT'S BI-VISIBLE Material required: 

4 orange-brown hackle feathers in assorted, graded sizes 

I pure white hackle feather of the same size as the largest orange-brown hackle feather. 

Clamp the hook in the vise, precisely as in tying a "Hendrickson." Fasten the end of your tying thread around the hook shank, exactly opposite the point of the hook. Take from three to six barbules of equal length from one of the orange-brown hackle feathers and lay them along the top of the hook shank, the points being directed toward your left. The length of these barbules must depend upon your own idea of how long the tail should be. Bind them to the shank with a sufficient number of turns of tying silk to fasten them firmly to the shank. This completes the tail of the fly.

Having previously stripped the fuzzy down from the bases of your hackles, take the smallest of the four [18]

and, setting the feather edgewise, lay the base of the projecting midriff across the shank of the hook at the point where the tail stylets come out from under the silk-i. e., at the base of the tail, which will be the point exactly opposite the point of the hook. The shiny surface of the hackle should be directed toward the eye of the hook, which is to the tyer's right. Seize the tip of the hackle with a hackle plier and wind it about the hook shank, making each turn as close to the preceding one as possible. Catch the tip of the hackle under one turn of tying silk and make a half hitch knot to secure it. Now, take the hackle feather next in size and proceed exactly as before, tying in the end of its midriff as close to the preceding winding as you can. Do the same with the third and fourth hackle. 

The largest of these four feathers should come last.

This will give the bushy body of the finished fly a gradual tapering effect from eye to bend of hook. 

As the final step, tie in the white hackle feather in front of the others, in the same manner as has been described for these. All of the hackle windings should be crowded together as closely as possible in order to attain a maximum of "bushiness" in the finished fly. 

Make three or four half hitch knots about the tip of the white feather. When you have finished winding it, apply a drop of the spar varnish to the head of the fly after cutting away the silk and the projecting tip of the last hackle feather and the fly is done. [19]

HOW TO TIE A DARK CAHILL Material required: 

I flank feather of a Male Wood Duck 

2 dark mahogany colored hackle feathers 

I strand of very dark bluish-grey crewel wool or a pinch of mole fur, mouse or rat fur. (If either of the last named is used, have it approximate the mole color as closely as possible.) 

The standard "Dark Cahill" body is tied with mole's fur spun on silk. Mole's fur is not always readily procurable when wanted and the process of spinning it on the tying silk is more bother than necessary. 

Crewel wool, of the proper shade, makes a very efficient substitute and is infinitely easier to handle. The writer has never found that flies so tied were any less "taking" than the more orthodox pattern and none of his friends, for whom he has so tied them, some of whom belong to the most exacting of our cult,-have ever voiced any objection nor complained of the effectiveness of "Dark Cahills" so made.

HOW TO TIE A LIGHT CAHILL Material required: 

I male Wood Duck's flank feather 

2 light brownish-yellow or buff hackles 

I strand of light cream-grey crewel wool. [ 20 ]

The technique of tying this and the preceding fly is identical with that given for the "Hendrickson" and the reader is referred to the section dealing with the tying of that fly. Substitute the materials listed above for those of the "Hendrickson" and proceed exactly as in making the latter.

HOW TO TIE A FAN-WINGED ROYAL COACHMAN Material required: 

2 concave breast feathers of a white domestic duck 

2 orange-brown hackle feathers 

Several strands of green peacock herl 

4-6 barbules from a cock Golden Pheasant's ruff feather 

I strand of bright crimson floss silk. 

Clamp the hook in the vise as before, the eye of the hook pointing to the right. Fasten the tying silk in the exact middle of the shank, as described under the section dealing with the tying of the "Hendrickson."

Having stripped the down from the base of the midriff of the two white duck feathers, which are to serve as the wings of the fly, lay the bare strip of the midriff along the top of the hook shank in such a manner that the first basal barbules will be in contact with the shank at its exact middle. The convexity of the feather should, of course, be toward the eye of the hook, the [21]

tip of the feather pointing directly away from you.

Now wind the midriff to the shank by as many turns of the tying thread as are necessary to firmly secure it in position. Taking up the second white duck feather, destined to form the fly's right wing, lay it along the top of the shank exactly as you did the first but this time with its tip pointing directly toward you. Wind it securely to the shank as you did the first one. Cut off the superfluous ends of the two midriffs where they project to the left of the last turn of the tying thread. 

Wind the tying thread back to the right, making one turn in front of the two wings and let it hang down weighted by a hackle plier attached to its end. Now place the tip of your left index finger under the concavity of the left wing feather and the tip of your left thumb under the concavity of the right one. Bring the tips of the index finger and thumb firmly together, thus pinching the two wings back to back against each other. Holding them securely in this position bend them back toward the bend of the hook until they assume the position which they should retain in the finished fly and make a number of turns with the tying thread in front of and as close up against them as is possible. This will hold them in the erect position desired. It is well to soak the bare ends of the midriffs in water before tying in the duck feathers, as they are likely to break when bending them back into position if this precaution be not taken. The writer usually [ 22 ]

holds them between his lips, a less aesthetic but equally effective substitute.

Having gotten the wings into place wind the tying thread back to the bend of the hook and fasten in the Golden Pheasant ruff-feather barbules, exactly as described under the section describing this part of the procedure for the "Hendrickson." The technique differs in no way. Wind the tying thread toward the left when the tail stylets are in place until you reach the base of the tail. Here tie in three strands of peacock herl by a turn or two of the thread and a single half hitch knot. Wind the herl about the hook in such a manner that a tuft or ball is formed at the base of the tail. When this has reached satisfactory proportions catch the herl under a turn of the tying thread and, having made a half hitch knot, cut off the projecting ends. Now tie in the end of a small bit of red floss silk and wind this about the hook shank until a "belly band" of sufficient breadth and size has been attained. 

Catch the end of the floss with the tying thread just as you did the herl and cut off the superfluous remainder. Now tie in the second trio of herl whisps and do exactly as you did in the case of the first ones, ending your winding hard against the back of the wings. Bring one turn of the tying silk in front of the wings and tie in your first hackle feather in the manner described under "How to Tie a Hendrickson." Wind it in back of the wings once or twice and end up in [23]

front of them and as close up against them as possible. 

Finish off as before and tie in the second hackle, bringing it hard up against the finished turns of the first one. When this second hackle has been wound as far as its length permits finish off as described for the "Hendrickson" and the fly is done.

HOW TO TIE A WOODRUFF Material required: 

2 hackle feathers from a Plymouth Rock Cock 

2 orange-brown hackle feathers 

I barred black and white flank or back feather from a Green-winged Teal or a 

Widgeon (drakes)

I strand of bright, grass-green, crewel wool. 

Clamp the hook in the vise, the eye pointing to your right. Fasten the tying silk in the exact middle of the shank, as described in previous paragraphs. Strip the barbules from the two Plymouth Rock hackles until only about one-half an inch of untouched feather remains on each at the tip. These are the wings of the "Woodruff." Now take the first of the prepared hackles and lay its bare midriff along the top of the hook shank, its tip pointing toward the eye of the hook and the lowermost of the barbules just touching the middle of the shank, where the tying silk is fastened. The upper or shiny surface of the feather should be uppermost. Fasten the midriff to the shank by a sufficient [24]

number of turns of the silk to secure it firmly. Do exactly the same thing with the second wing feather and make a half hitch knot when you have secured it firmly. Cut off the projecting ends of the midriffs where they project toward the left of the last turn of your tying silk. Now take each wing separately between thumb and forefinger of your left hand and bend it back until it stands with its midriff at an exact right angle with the long axis of the hook shank.

Properly done the two wings will be in a straight line making a perfect cross with the hook shank. The wings should lie in the same plane as the latter. Now make a sufficient number of turns with the silk immediately in front of the wing feathers to hold them in the position in which you have bent them. To still further maintain them in this position it will be found well to make a number of "criss cross" turns between them, thus making an "X" with the thread. From here on the tying of this fly differs in no way from that of the "Hendrickson" and the reader is referred to the paragraph describing its making for further directions. The tail stylets are barbules cut from the Teal or Widgeon feather and one striped boldly in alternate lines of black and white should be selected. Either of these ducks yields feathers of the kind necessary.

This fly is always tied as a "spent gnat" and is a valuable pattern when no hatch is on.
