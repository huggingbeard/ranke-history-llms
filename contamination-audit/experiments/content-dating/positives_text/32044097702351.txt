FOREWORD 

For eleven years South Carolina has had a permanent Committee on 

Children and Youth. The Committee is an outgrowth of the State Youth Conservation Committee appointed by Governor Ransome J. Williams in 1945. Governor Strom Thurmond in 1948 requested the Committee to make a survey in preparation for the 1950 White House Conference on Children and Youth. The Committee has been endorsed by each 

Governor since that time.

Since there would not be time after the inauguration of Governor Hollings to make a state survey for the 1960 White House Conference on Children and Youth, Governor Timmerman appointed an interim committee which requested the South Carolina Committee on Children and Youth to inaugurate a study to present to whatever group Governor Hollings might appoint. This report, based on that study, is the official 

South Carolina Report for the 1960 White House Conference. 

In preparation for the report, committees in all 46 counties of the State conducted county surveys by means of a standard questionnaire. Fortythree counties made reports to the state committee; from these county reports the official report was made. The entire survey and the report were handled by volunteer workers, with professional consultation given on a voluntary basis.

So many hundreds of volunteers spent so many thousands of hours on gathering this information that the Editing Committee was anxious to make the complete data available to them and to the people of South Carolina. For that reason, this booklet has been published. 

The reports from the various counties contain thousands upon thousands of factual items. Composite reports made up from the county findings, together with their recommendations, will be found in Section 

B, pages 23 to 48 of this booklet.

WHAT IS THE WHITE HOUSE CONFERENCE ON CHILDREN AND YOUTH? 

In 1909 President Theodore Roosevelt called the first White House 

Conference on Children and Youth, inspired by a request for national action on the problems of helping to keep dependent children in their own homes and providing care for the homeless. 

In March 1960, thousands of United States citizens will participate in the golden anniversary of this great national tradition affecting the welfare of children and youth. For the sixth time in fifty years, citizen representatives from the States and Territories will gather at the call of the President of the United States for the White House Conference on 

Children and Youth. 

The White House Conferences have represented variations on a single theme: how the welfare of children, in general or in some particular category, can be advanced. In 1909, the emphasis was on the care of dependent children. In 1919, the focus was on child welfare standards. 

The 1930 Conference produced the most comprehensive statement ever written on the needs of children. In 1940, the special needs of children in a democracy were highlighted. By 1950, a decade and more of popular and professional interest in dynamic psychology made a careful appraisal of its role in improving the well-being of children a natural focus.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower, in his initial communication concerning the 1960 Conference, said: "The rapidly changing times in which we live, and the increasingly fast pace of change, make it incumbent on us to do everything we can to plan ahead and to see that we prepare today's children well for life in tomorrow's world."

The purpose of the White House Conference has always been to inspire citizens to action to meet the needs of children and youth. Preconference activities in South Carolina have already produced information on which action can be based: the findings of the state-wide survey as listed in this booklet.

The purpose of the Committee which prepared this booklet was to point up the progress that has been made in the ten years since the last 

White House Conference (see “General Summary," page 21) and to indicate the present needs of our boys and girls. In short, our purpose was and is to inspire us to greater action in our efforts "to plan ahead and to see that we prepare today's children well for life in tomorrow's world.”

SECTION A UNLIMITED OPPORTUNITIES BECKON SOUTH CAROLINA YOUTH Introduction The title of this booklet-UNLIMITED OPPORTUNITIES BECK- ON SOUTH CAROLINA YOUTH-is a true statement. The South Carolina White House Conference Report of 1950 stated the following: 

"Today we in South Carolina may look forward with greater confidence than ever before to a shining future.' Nine years later we see many evidences that the future was a shining one. The State has undergone many changes in those years: growth of industries, increase in incomes, expansion of educational facilities and programs, and many others. 

The changes are bringing great opportunities to our young people. 

For them to make the most of these opportunities, they will need preparation, guidance, and, perhaps more than anything else, understanding from us-the adult citizens of South Carolina. We must know what is happening to them in their homes and communities, what problems they are facing, what services are available to them for assistance in solving these problems.

To understand the problems of youth, we must understand ourselves, what we are like, why we act as we do. 

South Carolinians are peculiarly the product of traditions and beliefs. 

Our population is 99.6% native-born; many of us trace our ancestry to pre-Revolutionary days. Our ancestors were Englishmen, who settled the fertile coastal area and brought with them the ideas and ideals of aristocratic seventeenth century England; and Scots, Irish, French, German, and English who settled the up-country. Thus we had on the one hand the wealthy, educated, plantation-owning group in the low-country and on the other the self-reliant, self-sufficient, hard-working, independent group in the up-country. Today's South Carolina citizen inherits many of the characteristics of both groups. 

In addition to these two population groups, there was in early South Carolina a large number of Negroes brought in to work the coastal plantations. Although these Negroes contributed little to the thinking of our ancestors, they tremendously influenced the mores of both the plantation owners of the low country and the small farm owners of the upper part of the State.

The wealth of South Carolina agriculture was practically wiped out in the War Between the States. Reconstruction years found us trying to rebuild our agrarian way of life, while the rest of the nation was moving into great industrial developments. The swift pace of life in those 11

industrial centers was absent in South Carolina where the tempo was slow, geared to easy living rather than to the accumulation of large bank accounts. In spite of poverty, the emphasis here was on enjoying life rather than on the frantic struggle for the acquisition of things to make life enjoyable. A relaxed, leisurely way of living has been the pattern in South Carolina almost up to the present time. We are just beginning to experience the quickening pace, the faster tempo that has existed in other sections of the country for almost half a century, and which accompanies industrialization. Now that we in South Carolina are moving into industrialization, we shall undergo some of the changes that have occurred in other parts of the Nation in the past 50 years, and we shall have to adjust to these changes rapidly. 

Other factors have influenced us and made us what we are. The church has always played an important part in our lives. It was at one time the center of social life for most of our people. It still maintains a strong influence in our lives. Education, too, was important in the early life of the State. It was socially important for one to acquire a good education. This is still true today. 

These traditions and attitudes, handed down to us from generation to generation, strongly influence our lives today. We are self-reliant and independent, resenting the encroachments of government and organizations on cur affairs. We have a keen sense of personal responsibility for neighbors in need or difficulty and have thus been slow to see the advantage of group action, by professional organizations, for serving those who need help.

Our children have inherited these same traditions and attitudes. How will they serve our youth in the future?

Youth Faces a Different Future 

In the 1930's South Carolina was a significant part of the "Number One Economic Problem" of the Nation. Today the eyes of the Nation are on the South and its rapid economic growth.

The change in agricultural methods, along with the mechanization of farms, is revolutionizing agriculture. The number of large farms has increased and the incomes of these farm families have risen. There is an increase in the number of rural non-farm families who derive their incomes from near-by industries. Some farm labor, left with limited employment, is now migrating to the cities. This migration has intensified some problems of the cities: poor housing, over-crowded schools, inadequate recreational facilities, inadequate services from health and welfare agencies, and unemployment.

Industry, needing more and more labor to man its factories, has found in South Carolina a labor supply which meets many of its needs. South 

Carolina, which for many years thought of industry only in terms of the 12

textile mills, is aware of other possibilities. Industries that have moved in have brought prosperity. Between January 1, 1945, and July 1, 1959, new industrial plants valued at $923,230,000 have been established or announced. Plants already located in the State have made or announced expansions valued at $659,496,100. The new industries employ approximately 88,725 persons who earn an estimated $246,897,000 annually. It is estimated that the expansion of existing industries has added or will add 68,589 jobs which will pay wages of $190,860,000. In addition, the 

Atomic Energy Commission's Savannah River Plant, operated by the Du- Pont Company in Aiken County, South Carolina, was constructed in 1950-51 at a cost of $1,300,000,000. From 1951 to January 1959, a total of $648,000,000 in wages was paid by this plant. Today, the annual payroll is $50,000,000. An additional $36,000,000 is spent annually for equipment, materials, and services. There are now 7,000 DuPont employees and 230 AEC employees at the plant. 

During World War I, military installations brought to the State thousands of people from other sections of the country. For the first time many South Carolinians came into close contact with large numbers of people whose habits, attitudes, and interests were different from their own. During World War II, seven large military installations in the 

State brought even greater numbers of people from other states. This influx of people from various parts of the Nation, many of whom have remained in the State, is bringing about a change in many of our own habits. 

Although our per capita income is still below the national average, it has risen 319% since 1929. In 1956 the average income in South 

Carolina was $1,133, as compared with a national average of $1,940. 

Much of the increase in average income is due to industrial growth and to improvements in agricultural methods. Our introduction to industry has shown that we can adjust to its demands and produce as well as people in other parts of the country. The future is bright. 

As we envision the unlimited opportunities beckoning to our youth, do we see clearly the situations confronting them? Are we giving them adequate preparation to meet these new conditions? Have we fully grasped the tremendous responsibilities which are ours in preparing our young people for the future? Have we recognized the implications of the fact that South Carolina's population is the youngest in the Nation, with 43% of our total population being under 18 years of age?

Fortunately our youth are ahead of us in adjusting to changing situations. Like young people everywhere, they accept change more readily than their parents. They welcome the increased tempo in our way of life. Contact with people from other sections of the Nation has widened their horizons and given many of them new incentives and ambitions. 

More young people are seeking employment in out-of-school hours. The higher standards of living among rural farm families have increased the 13

possibilities of higher education for youth from these areas. Television and radio, now available to the majority of them through rural electrification, have widened their insight into other ways of living and working. 

Youth will make the transition more easily than older people, but the adjustments will be made to more than an industrial economy. They will be living in an entirely new age too. Along with the youth of other parts of the country, they will be adjusting to an age of electronics, automation, jets, space travel. We cannot imagine the problems they will face any more than our parents could have imagined television and earth satellites.

We can, however, be as enthusiastic and aggressive in trying to prepare for this known and unknown future as we are enthusiastic and aggressive in securing the benefits of this new way of life. We can make a serious study of those devices which have proven of service to youth in other areas.

Our Services for Children and Youth 

The agencies which have been found essential for the service of youth in modern society have been surveyed by the county committees. Many of these agencies are inadequate for our present rural and small town conditions and some are completely lacking. From county after county came the plea for better understanding on the part of the community of the problems, the needs of youth, and the urgency of providing adequate, professionally-directed service. 

Education (See page 23 for complete report)

During the past ten years South Carolina has practically rebuilt her school buildings. Since 1951, when the three percent sales tax for educational purposes was inaugurated, more than $225,000,000 has been provided from state and local sources for school construction. School districts have been consolidated, reducing their number from 1690 to 108. Consolidated schools have been built to replace the small inadequate ones. School bus transportation has been taken over by the State and transportation provided for all children who live further than a mile and a half from school. With the determination to give incontrovertible evidence that the State was complying with the repeated decisions of the courts that separate but equal school facilities were legal, the whole school system has been reorganized. 

Now more than half the children in the State attend school in new buildings. Enrollment has increased, and attendance has improved despite the repeal of the compulsory attendance law. 

Recently state aid has been provided for special classes for the mentally and physically handicapped and aid has been increased for mainte- 1 14

nance and supervision. Teachers' salaries have been significantly increased.

Much, however, remains to be done to improve the educational program in order to give our children adequate preparation for the highly competitive and demanding world in which they will live. Because of the high mobility of the American population, even those children who remain at home will be forced to compete with children educated in the best schools in the nation. As industrialization moves into full flood, well-trained people will be needed. They will be brought in to fill the positions if our youth are not adequately trained. 

South Carolina has the second highest percentage of school-aged children, per thousand population, in the Nation. At the same time we are a low per capita income state. Our effort to support public education places us near the top among the states of the Nation. However, the amount spent for each pupil each year in South Carolina is only about two-thirds of the national average. Extensive research in educational problems shows that this "per pupil cost" is the best single index of quality of instruction. There is as much variation in quality in education as there is in any other product which one buys. Our children get what quality we pay for.

The quality of the teachers is the most important factor. A poorly qualified teacher cannot give the kind of instruction that will prepare our children for life in a competitive future no matter how many "A’s she gives. Because of low salaries, schools in the State are now forced many times to employ people because they are willing to teach rather than because they are well-qualified. Our certification requirements for teachers grant an "A" certificate to a person who passes the National Teachers Examination with a score of 500, which nationally is the average score for college sophomores. Yet many of our teachers hold "B" and "C" certificates.

Good teaching requires good equipment-materials of instructionwith which to work. These are meager and of poor quality in many of our schools. We would not employ a carpenter who had inadequate tools. Yet we expect teachers to work with our most valuable assetour children-without the tools needed for good teaching. 

Too many classes are too large. In 1958-59, there were 267 classrooms which had 50 or more children in them; 1827 teachers had 40 or more students. Even the most capable teacher cannot help individual pupils with their individual problems when the classes are large. Learning is an individual process.

Not only our public schools but our colleges need more support. There has been a revolution in college attendance. Not so many years ago, most people aspired to a high school education. A college education was considered one of the nice things which the wealthy could afford. 15

Now, most of our high school students hope to go to college. The trend may have been hastened by the large number who attended because of the G. I. Bill. Those G. I.'s did well in college. They found that a college education "paid off" economically. Other young people should have an opportunity to attend college. With our national and state economy demanding more and more educated leadership, we cannot let some of the best minds in the State fail to achieve their maximum potential because their possessors happen to be financially unable to acquire the best possible educational advantages. 

Health (Complete report on page 28)

The detailed findings of the county committees on health are combined in the report on page 28 of this booklet. Since health is of primary importance to people and people are our most abundant and valuable natural resource, the report is well worth detailed study. 

The Editing Committee wishes to call special attention to a few very important facts. Much progress has been made in the past ten years; much more remains to be done if we are to have adequate protection in the future. There is an organized health department in each county. 

New and well-equipped public health facilities are now available in 37 counties, and 78 auxiliary health centers have been constructed. But facilities do not guarantee services. Only 12 counties are served by a full-time health officer; other counties either share a health officer, use a local physician part-time, or depend upon medical supervision from the State Board of Health. Also on the staff of the local health departments are 198 public health nurses and 87 sanitarians.

The South Carolina State Board of Health through the 46 county health departments render services in communicable and chronic disease control (venereal diseases, cancer, cardiac condition, and tuberculosis), environmental sanitation, maternal and child health, crippling conditions in children, dental health, laboratory tests, vital statistics, and health education.

The per capita expenditure for these services is $2.31 per year or about two-thirds of a penny a day. Local communities are paying only a quarter of this amount or one-sixth of a penny a day.

There are approximately 40 public and private organizations primarily concerned with providing various services for children with handicaps. 

There is a very great need for coordination of these services. 

In South Carolina there are 1541 practicing physicians, including 69 pediatricians, serving the health needs of children and youth. The 472 dentists are scattered unevenly throughout the State with some counties having only one or two.

Health is a highly complex and specialized business that requires efficient and trained personnel of which South Carolina is currently short. 1 16

Law Enforcement, Courts, and Legal Aid (See page 34 for complete report)

Changing patterns of living necessitate a different approach to law enforcement which involves children and youth. The basic philosophy of juvenile courts and of other specialized courts for youthful offenders should be "individualized justice." Medical, social, psychological, and other factors need to be considered in the disposition of each case. 

We need not expect youth to be perfect in the years ahead. We must help them learn . . . learn the infinite complexities of acceptable conduct necessitated by group living in a civilized society. 

In South Carolina only five counties have, in their law enforcement departments, special units for working with juvenile offenders. Twelve ccunties have no separate facilities for the detention of children pending the decisions of the courts. Only 14 counties are served by special courts for children. Thirty counties sentence juveniles to the chain gang or the South Carolina penitentiary.

Only 26 counties keep records as to juvenile arrests and therefore statistics for the State must be studied with this in mind. 

We must make special provisions for helping and caring for our youth who come in conflict with the law. The more congested our population, the more urgent the need and the more specialized must be the services and personnel which we provide. Juvenile courts, special facilities for detention when necessary, adequate services for counseling and guidance, and adequate institutions for rehabilitation must be provided. 

Money invested in prevention will pay much higher dividends than money spent for jails and penitentiaries later. 

Public Library Service (Complete report on page 36) 

At one time it was thought that schools could give children all the answers to all the questions. With the general increase in knowledge and the many fields of specialization, we know now that schools can give training only in the skills and methods of learning. No one can know everything. The truly educated person continues his learning as long as he lives.

To make it possible for adults to continue to learn and for children and youth to extend learning outside the classroom, libraries are as necessary as schools and colleges. In fact, they are an extension of our educational institutions. 

Forty of our 46 counties now have library service. Our per capita expenditure for public libraries has almost doubled during the past ten years, rising from 33 cents to 62 cents. However, to provide our citizens. 17

with the type of service available to most of the citizens in most of the states, we would have to double this amount again. 

The books which our libraries have are inadequate to meet the wide range of needs and interests of a modern society. Even more important is the type of service offered children and youth by a trained library staff. 

South Carolina is short of trained librarians and we are handicapped because this type of service is not available to our children and youth. 

Recreation (Complete report on page 38)

People are going to have some form of recreation. Most people desire the socially acceptable forms, if they are attractive and well-planned. 

If these conditions are not present, the socially unacceptable forms are apt to be sought. The re-creational value of recreation is greatest when the person is participating rather than when he is acting as a spectator. 

This applies both to our physical re-creation and our mental and emotional re-creation. 

South Carolina is fortunate in its wide variety of natural resources in recreation. Most citizens are close to beautiful forests and lakes and, within a few hours, can be enjoying deep sea fishing or hiking in the mountains. South Carolina has those features which make recreation easily available. But over the years we have failed to take full advantage of the natural resources with which nature has endowed us. 

As our population increases, as our way of life changes, the urgency of planning now for the future is tremendous. Industrialization is changing some of our patterns of family living. More women are going into employment. Cities are rapidly expanding without any planned recreational areas. Shorter working hours emphasize the need for recreational facilities for all ages. Family recreation is becoming more and more a necessity in maintaining family unity. 

At present there is no organized recreation program on the state level and in only a few counties is there planning for the recreational needs of the people. The state parks division of the State Forestry is understaffed and has inadequate funds for providing the needed leadership. 

The 22 state parks do not meet the needs of our total population. 

We may feel that we cannot afford the luxury of a recreational program at this time, but investment in facilities and programs now will pay high dividends in the not distant future.

39. Most of us are church members. Most of us know the acute needs of at least one church. Most of us have had ample opportunity to see how difficult it is to get members to give of their time, their thought, their effort to promote the programs of the church. The influence of the church will not remain strong just because it has been strong.

It has been said that each generation must fight to gain the ideals of democracy. We must be equally aggressive in our church and religious activities if our children are to receive from us the spiritual and moral support which has been so important in our own lives. There are the children who are neglected, mistreated, exploited, deserted; children that have physical handicaps, that are mentally retarded or emotionally disturbed; children that are delinquent or are on the verge of becoming delinquent. 

There are also the problems of unmarried parents with their unwanted babies. These intensify the problems of adoptions. At present some adoptions are actually sales to pay the medical and hospital expenses of the mother.

It is to take care of these needs that the social agencies have been organized and developed. 

In South Carolina the Department of Public Welfare is concerned primarily with these needs and with the responsibility of administering whatever public funds and services are available to people in need. Their work is supplemented and complemented by private agencies. In addition to the agencies, there are fraternal organizations, church groups and others which are able, as a rule, to give only emergency help.

Every county in the State now has a Department of Public Welfare which works with adults and children in these counties. However, they 19

lack the staff to do the type of work that they are expected to do. Many of the workers are inadequately trained, and the counties find it practically impossible to get trained social workers in large enough quantity because of the small salaries paid in South Carolina. 

We must realize that social work is a profession like medicine, or law, or teaching. A trained social worker renders a service which cannot be rendered by a person who is willing but does not know the proper methods, does not have the skills and understandings which are necessary to rehabilitate a family or a child in time of need or social stress. 

The social worker is not some one who simply hands cut a dole to a person who asks.

In considering the adequacy of our social services, we must always remember that this is an investment in the future of our children. It is another effort on our part to see that children will be prepared for a life of productive activity so that they can contribute to the general welfare.

Youth Employment (Complete report on page 47) 

South Carolina has a higher proportion of youth in its civilian population than any other state except New Mexico. Forty-three percent of our present population is under 18 years of age, as compared to 42% in 1950. This in itself would pose a tremendous employment problem, but at this time when the State is changing from an agricultural economy to an industrial way of life the urgency of the situation cannot be overemphasized.

When a child knows that he is going to follow in the footsteps of his father and is trained from early childhood to go into this same type of work, there is comparatively no employment problem. However, when the child can foresee that there will be no opportunity for him in his father's work and when he does not know what openings are available or may be available to him, when he is uncertain as to what he would like to do, the future citizen needs help and guidance from an expert. 

It is important for us as taxpayers to help other people have chances to earn a living and pay taxes. For this reason any help which we can give the job-seeker is really a help to ourselves. 

The schools and the South Carolina State Employment Service are the two agencies giving advice on this problem. Unfortunately, the schools have not been adequately supported and the guidance services in most of the high schools are inadequate in the field of job counseling. 

The South Carolina State Employment Service is handicapped in that it does not have adequate staff and that its services are not well enough known to be used to complete advantage. It is handicapped too by a lack of coordination of available information. By pooling the facts from 20

all sources at the state and local level, better service to the youth of the 

State could be provided.

The report on youth employment on page 47 points up the specific needs with recommendations for improving this service to youth. It is well worth serious study.

General Summary 

Much has been accomplished during the last ten years. In 1950, the following Legislative Goals for the ten years between 1950 and 1960 were set up in the report of the South Carolina Committee on Children and 

Youth: 

1. A state-wide system of juvenile courts, either in each county or one court to serve two or three counties. 

2. Amendment of child labor law to limit hours of work and establish certification system. 

3. Increased appropriation for aid to dependent children. 

4. Training school for mentally defective Negro children. 

5. Repeal of state law allowing deeding of children. 

6. Revision and strengthening of adoption laws. 

7. Licensing of institutions caring for children. 

8. Licensing of independent foster homes. 

9. Establishment of a state-wide recreation system. 

10. Enactment of legislation to provide for buildings, reorganization and extension of transportation, and better salaries for better-trained teachers.

Progress to date includes building a school for mentally defective Negro children; state laws for licensing foster homes, day care centers, and child care agencies and institutions; a state-wide school building and school transportation program and increased salaries for teachers; and increased appropriations for aid to dependent children. Although the ideal in each of these instances has not been reached, there has been progress.

Although no state-wide system of juvenile courts has been established, there has been an increase in the number of juvenile and domestic relations courts, established locally.

No progress has been made on Goals two, five, six, and nine above; but because of more community consciousness of what is happening to children, there is reason to believe that, before the ten years is up, progress will be made in these areas also. 21

Progress in areas not listed in the Goals of 1950-1960 includes improved services for physically and mentally handicapped children, particularly in the field of education, and broader services to unmarried mothers.

Much remains to be done. There is still great need for improvement in our provisions for juvenile courts, more adequate child labor laws and marriage laws, provision for the emotionally disturbed, more realistic provision for aid to dependent children, more understanding of the need for expanded services and adequate support of our public welfare program, and improvement in the quality of our educational program. 

The agencies serving children and youth in South Carolina agree that their greatest need is public understanding. They ask that we study the problems of youth and the methods by which they can best be met. If we fail to do this, we will have failed our children. 

We cannot know what their future will be, but we know it will be different from ours and it will be in some ways difficult. Inevitably, they will have to adjust, and adjust almost immediately to an industrial economy, to a space age, to a way of living and thinking quite different from ours. They will have new and greater opportunities, new and different problems.

Leaders in the State have as great opportunities and problems in 1959 as had our Revolutionary leaders in 1759 or as had our Confederate leaders in 1859. Because the mass of the citizens foresees the future no more clearly than did the citizens of those times, the leaders have the responsibility of making every effort to prepare the people for the change and to help them avoid problems of adjustment. In the turmoil of conflicting emotional reactions which will come, we must hold fast to all that is good in our way of life and realistically meet the demands of a new era. 22 22

SECTION B COMPOSITE REPORTS BASED ON, COUNTY FINDINGS 

The following are composite reports of the findings of the county committees, 43 of whom made reports to the state committee. The reports are arranged alphabetically by titles. 

EDUCATION 

Public school education in South Carolina involves one of the largest segments of the State's population: more than one-fourth of the entire population of the State is enrolled in the public schools. The total enrollment for 1958-1959 was 584,283 and is expected to go over the 600,000 mark during the 1959-1960 school year.

South Carolina has more youth per thousand population than any other state except New Mexico. With 43% of the total population (2,407,000) being under 18 years of age (1,045,000), we have the youngest population of any state in the Nation. 

Coupled with this is the fact that the per capita income in our State is among the lowest in the Nation. Our ability to pay for public education, on a per capita basis, is only about 58% of the national average. 

In spite of this heavy burden in numbers of children to be educated and our relatively low ability to pay for it, our State stands near the top in its effort to support public education. In 1957-58, the people of 

South Carolina paid 3.9% of total personal income for the support of public schools as compared to 3.3% for the Nation. 

It is this effort on the part of South Carolinians that is, to a great extent, responsible for the many improvements and developments in education that have occurred since 1950-1951. 

In the last decade, school districts have been reorganized so that their number has been reduced from 1690 to 108. Schools have been consolidated so that the total number has been reduced from 3507 to 1421, almost eliminating small inefficient schools. The state-supported school construction program and local effort have provided more than $225,000,000 in new facilities, so that more than half the school children are now in new buildings. State ownership and operation of the school transportation system provides transportation for all children who live more than a mile and a half from school. 

Enrollments have been increasing at the rate of about 12,000 per year for the past five years. White schools have maintained a fairly consistent average; the greatest percentage increase has been among the Negro high schools; the smallest percentage increase has been among Negro elementary schools. 23

In spite of the repeal of the compulsory attendance law, average daily attendance has increased from 83.7% to 85.3%.

Although teachers' salaries are still comparatively low-as compared with the national average and in terms of the cost of living and of salaries and wages in other occupations-they have almost doubled in the last ten years. The average salary in 1948-49 was $1795; in 1958-59 it was $3274. The number of teachers holding bachelor's degrees increased from 63% in 1948 to almost 93% in 1958. Much of this is due to teachers in service continuing their studies through summer schools and extension courses. 

There has been a general increase in the number of principals who have been relieved of teaching to give full time to administrative and supervisory duties. At present 38% of the elementary principals and 

68% of the high school principals are on a full-time basis. Approximately 60% of the elementary principals and 75% of the high school principals either hold or qualify for principals' certificates. 

Provisions have been made for the educational training of both the physically handicapped and the mentally retarded children in the State. 

Today there are 165 classes for this group: 150 are for educable mentally handicapped, 11 for physically handicapped, and four for trainables, involving a total of 2500 children.

The holding power of the school, which is one indication of whether the instructional program is meeting the needs of the boys and girls, has shown considerable increase in recent years. Of the first grade enrollment in 1944-45, 40% had either been retained or had dropped out of school by the time the class reached the eighth grade, and 65% of the first grade enrollment had been retained or dropped out by the time the group reached the twelfth grade. These drop-outs have been reduced so that of the 1947-48 first grade enrollment 29% had dropped out of the class or been retained before reaching the eighth grade and 58% before reaching the twelfth. Further reduction in drop-outs is noted for the class that entered school in 1951-52. Of this group only 

23% had been retained or dropped out before reaching the eighth grade in 1958-59. Further evidence of the increase in the school's holding power is noted when we realize that this reduction in drop-outs has occurred simultaneously with increases in enrollments.

The length of the school term has increased from 177 days to 180. 

The schools with lunch room service have risen from 50% to 87%. 

Among the accredited high schools of the State, a large percent have approved physical education programs, and in 1958 there were 54.4% of the total high school boys enrolled and 46.1% of the girls taking physical education.

Seventy-two percent of the school districts in the State have stated aims and objectives to guide them in curriculum planning. 24

Reports show 30% of the parents participate in parent-teacher associations or some other type of parent organization, with 85% of these being in the elementary schools. 

Most schools report some provisions for taking care of the gifted and talented pupils. These programs take the form of enrichment, special grouping, and acceleration.

The number of elementary schools with libraries has risen from 35% to 58%. Sixteen percent of the elementary schools and 45% of the high schools have full-time librarians, while 42% of the elementary schools and 55% of the high schools have part-time librarians. There is an average of two library volumes per pupil in the elementary schools, four and one-half volumes per pupil in high schools. 

Music instruction on an organized basis is offered in 82% of the schools and 61% provide band instruction. All high schools offer home economics programs, 82% have programs in agriculture, and 73% have shop programs.

From district funds, approximately $1.50 is spent per pupil annually for instructional materials. The average cost to the individual pupil of rental textbooks is $4.55 for elementary and $5.91 for high school. 

This does not include the expendable materials that are required of most pupils.

Eighty percent of the schools have systematic screening programs for visual and hearing defects. Screening services for hearing defects are available to all schools. Fifteen full-time speech therapists are employed by local schools. In addition, there are eight demonstrational clinics in various areas of the State staffed by State Department of Education personnel. There is at least one visiting teacher in each county whose primary duty is to work with schools, pupils, parents, and ccmmunity agencies in improving school attendance. Thirty-four health nurses are employed by local school systems. Homebound instruction during 1958-59 provided educational services for 500 children confined to their homes or in hospitals.

Despite this great effort, this commendable improvement in our educational program, there are still weaknesses and needs which must be met. 

Although there has been a great reduction in the number of school districts through consolidation, there are still a few districts too small and too weak financially to function properly. All schools receive state aid on the same basis; but in order to have a complete educational program, the districts find it necessary to raise an average of nearly one-third of their school funds from local taxes. There is a wide variation in the ability and effort at the local level to supplement state funds. 

District millage for school purposes ranges from 20 mills to 56 1/2 mills.

The annual income per pupil, from local sources, ranges from as low as 25

$to as high as $95. Supplements for the state-aid salaries of teachers range from zero in some districts to as high as $1500 in others, with isolated cases going even higher. 

South Carolina ranks thirteenth from the top among the states in the percent of total income devoted to education, yet the average expenditure per pupil, per year, is about $100 less than the national average. 

To reach the national average would require an additional $60,000,000 for schools annually. Considering the low average per capita income, this would be difficult to achieve.

Although the present status of school personnel in South Carolina, viewed in the light of progress toward desired standards, shows some elements of achievement, there are still many deficiencies which are a matter of grave concern.

Of the more than 20,000 teachers in the State, 3.9% taught on permits during the 1958-59 school year. However, most of these permits are issued to college graduates teaching out of their major fields of preparation. The average age of teachers which, a decade ago, was 37 years, is now about 45 years. Of the 5150 graduates from teachers' colleges during the past five years, only 2500 are reported teaching in the schools of the State. Schools are finding it increasingly difficult to obtain and hold better-qualified teachers, and especially to attract a sufficient number of young people.

No provision is made for the education of children under six in the public schools of the State. Approximately 300 play schools and kindergartens for this age group are in operation under the auspices of private and parochial agencies. 

Only fourteen high schools employ one or more full-time guidance persons; 29.6% of the high schools made provision for part-time guidance.

Eighty-six of the 108 school districts reported that some programs of testing and guidance services were carried on within the districts, but this does not necessarily mean the service was district-wide. Only 15% of all parents participating in parent-teacher organizations are in such organizations on the high school level. Since guidance of high school youth is of special concern, it seems imperative to have better communication between the home and school through some type of cooperative endeavor. It is also imperative that guidance and testing services be expanded, that better provision be made for guidance in the field of job opportunities, and that more counselors become available for working with youth on personal as well as educational problems. 

This area of the educational program is receiving vital attention at this time through the provisions of the National Defense Education Act. 

Most schools reported that they have a balanced curriculum, but some expressed a need for more offerings in mathematics, art, foreign languages, driver education, health, safety, and physical education. 26

Less than half the schools have regularly planned instruction in art. 

Only two elementary schools teach foreign languages. Only nine and one-half percent of the high schools offer distributive education to a total of 875 students. This program involves part-time employment. 

Only one county in the State employs a system-wide director of special services. Only three school systems in the State employ psychologists. 

Although there has been a gradual increase in the number of school systems and counties which have provided for supervisors of instruction, there are still less than one-third of the counties having such supervisors, either on a county or district level. 

Our teachers are still poorly paid, and although practically all hold state certificates, many are poorly qualified to teach. Instruction ranges from excellent in some places to poor in others. There is a wide variation in the quality of instruction in many places. School libraries also differ in quality from excellent to poor, particularly on the elementary level where in some cases there is no central library at all. Materials of instruction are in many places meager and lacking. Too many classrooms are overcrowded, making individual attention difficult. 

Needs of Education 

Probably the greatest need in education is the securing and holding of an adequate supply of excellent teachers. To meet this need, increases in teachers' salaries will be necessary as a first step. 

An adequate supply of instructional materials and provision for more supervisory services in the area of instruction are also necessary if wẹ are further to improve the quality of instruction. The extension and improvement of school libraries, with emphasis at the elementary level, would further help to strengthen the educational program. 

In the field of guidance, a realistic study of drop-outs needs to be made; there should be planned follow-up of students after they leave schools; and all guidance services, particularly on the high school level, should be expanded.

We should begin to make some provision for educational opportunities for children under six. The number of special classes for retarded children and children with physical handicaps should be increased. There should be provided services of school psychologists and an extension of health services for all school children.

We need to make a careful analysis of the tax structure with special attention to assessment practices in the various school districts. We also need to revise the organization for administering school affairs in some of the districts in the interest of more efficient operation and for clarifying duties and responsibilities of boards and administrative officers. 27

We recommend that: Recommendations 1. A realistic increase be made in teachers' salaries that will result in a schedule adequate to attract and retain the number of highly skilled teachers needed for today's schools. 

2. For at least the next four years schools spend not less than $7.00 per pupil per year for equipment and materials of instruction including library materials, but excluding athletic equipment. 

3. A state appropriation be made for non-teaching principals and/or supervisors, to be determined on the basis of one for each twenty teachers for which the district qualifies. 

4. A realistic study of drop-outs be made, including the socio-economic cause and the curriculum's contribution to drop-out. 

5. The State begin planning toward the inclusion of kindergarten in its public school program as early as is feasible. 

6. A more effective and more uniform administration of the public schools at the local level be developed. 

7. The State make a complete examination of the tax structure with a view to bringing about a more equitable assessment of property for tax purposes. 

8. Enrollment and average daily attendance requirements for state aid be placed on the same basis for both elementary and secondary schools, thus reducing class size in the elementary school. 

We recommend also that the State seriously consider the advisability of requiring local districts to meet certain educational standards and make a certain effort to support their schools in order to be eligible for full state aid.

HEALTH 

South Carolina has an organized health department in each of its fortysix counties.

New and well-equipped public health facilities are available in thirtyseven counties. These have been built with or without Federal aid during the past twelve years. In addition 78 auxiliary health centers have been constructed. New health centers not only serve as the offices for the county health department but have facilities such as an auditorium, kitchens, and other space for teaching and demonstrations by community groups.

Old and inadequate buildings, which should be replaced, house the remaining nine health departments. 28

Twelve counties are served by a full-time health officer; 21 counties are served by a full-time health officer working in more than one county; and one county is served by a part-time health officer. Four counties are served by part-time health officers who also engage in private practice. Eight counties are without health officers but have administrative assistants serving directly under the Director of the Division of Local Health Services of the South Carolina State Board of Health. The 46 counties employ 198 public health nurses, 87 sanitarians, and 125 clerks. Approximately one-third of the county health departments have governing boards composed of both medical and lay persons. 

The county health departments render services in communicable and chronic disease control (cancer, tuberculosis, cardiac and venereal diseases), environmental sanitation, maternal and child health, crippling conditions of children, dental health, laboratory tests, vital statistics, and health education. These important responsibilities are attempted on a per capita cost of only $2.31 per year. The local communities pay only onequarter of this, the state pays a half, and the Federal government pays the other one-fourth.

The professional and technical staff of the South Carolina State Board of Health, consisting of doctors, dentists, nurses, engineers, sanitarians, nutritionists, medical social workers, health educators, physical and occupational therapists, and various technicians, serve the county health departments and allied health agencies in an advisory and consultative capacity as well as assist in carrying out certain programs. 

There are 1541 physicians in South Carolina, including 69 pediatricians, serving the health needs of children and youth.

The 472 dentists are scattered unevenly throughout the State with some counties having only one or two.

There are 73 hospitals in the State with 7,476 beds of which 1,248 are classified as "not acceptable". According to the national formula of 4.5 general beds per 1,000 population, the State should have 10,621 beds. 

The State has, therefore, about one-half the number of acceptable general beds needed.

Ten of the 73 hospitals offer out-patient service to maternity and pediatric patients.

Communicable Disease 

Communicable diseases are investigated and such control measures as may be indicated are instituted by all county health departments. All of the acute bacterial diseases have been reduced, some of them, such as typhoid fever and diphtheria, almost to the vanishing point. The State 

Board of Health Laboratory is now equipped to perform viral studies. 

Since the introduction of poliomyelitis vaccine, this disease has been 29

reduced from a normal annual expectancy of 100-150 cases to 32 cases in 1958. 

The percentage of child population inoculated for communicable diseases can not be accurately determined because this information is not readily available from private physicians. The child population, however, is not adequately immunized since in 1958 there were reported preventable communicable diseases as follows: diphtheria 35, whooping cough 449, tetanus 2, poliomyelitis (all ages) 32, and typhoid 11. 

Services for Mothers and Children 

There were 63,375 babies born in South Carolina in 1958. Services for mothers and children are available on a limited basis through the health departments in all counties. The quality and quantity of these services vary according to the preparation and number of personnel. The services include clinics for prenatals, postnatals, and children; individual and group conferences and home visits by public health nurses, instruction in dietary needs; and, in several localities, classes for prenatal patients of private physicians.

The program planning for the teaching, training, and supervision of midwives is the responsibility of the Maternal and Child Health Division of the State Board of Health. The direct supervision of midwives is the responsibility of the county health departments. All patients delivered by midwives are required to have prenatal care either by private physicians or by the health department. The 1,029 certified midwives in the 

State delivered 10,018 of the 63,375 babies born in 1958. There has been a continuing reduction in the number of midwives and midwife deliveries during the past ten years, as in 1948, of the 58,131 total births, 16,869 were delivered by the 1507 midwives. Hospital deliveries have continued to increase as in 1948 there were 30,531 and in 1958 there were 48,148. 

The need for more services for mothers and children is evidenced by the 1957 statistics on deaths: stillbirths 1,308, premature 371, infants 

2,001, and maternal deaths 64.

Preschool clinics are conducted in 43 of the 46 counties. Services given at these clinics varies according to the interest of the local parents, doctors, staff of the health departments, and public schools. Parents are encouraged to take their children to their family physicians for examinations and appropriate inoculations prior to entering school. 

The school health program is a team activity that should involve the planning and cooperation of parents, school and health personnel, and private physicians. Approximately half of the counties report community-school health committees with varying degrees of activity, but the majority of the counties report that the local medical associations do not have school health committees and do not participate in the planning of school health programs. 30

Most counties report that the physical examination of students is not done by physicians except for students who participate in competitive athletics. Some type of hearing and vision tests are reported by most of the counties. Limited state and community funds are available for remedial measures for students unable to pay. It is estimated that 95% of all school-age children have dental caries. No provision is made for the majority of children to have dental care. Nursing services for schools are provided primarily by public health nurses.

Since 1949 an official committee composed of representatives from the State Department of Education and the State Board of Health has been working jointly to promote better health for the school-age children. 

School lunch programs are conducted in 87% of the schools. During 

1958, 51.3% of the children participated in the program. Milk at a reduced rate is available in many of the schools. The county health department has the responsibility for the inspection of school sanitation. 

While the State is considering the health of children, more emphasis should be put on accident prevention. Accidents kill and disable more children under fifteen years of age than the total of all diseases of childhood. In 1957, 458 children and youths under 21 died from accidents. 

The major causes were vehicles 158, suffocation 88, fires 72, drowning 68, firearms 22, poison 11, burns 10, and other 29.

The formula for estimating permanent and temporary injuries is as follows: 

Total deaths times 4 equals the number of permanent injuries. 

Total deaths times 150 equals the temporary disabilities.

According to this formula there were 1,832 permanent and 68,700 temporary accidental injuries to children in South Carolina during 1957. 

Two poison control centers have been established for both treatment and information concerning poisons.

The medically needy children and youth with handicaps are furnished medical and allied services through both public and private agencies. 

Please see the Proceedings of the First South Carolina Conference on

The planned and united efforts of many individuals, professions, and agencies are essential to provide services to children with handicaps. 

With this awareness, the South Carolina Coordinating Council for Services to Handicapped Children was organized in 1958 as the outcome of 31

In the last decade progress has been made in extending and establishing new services for various categories of handicapping conditions_such as congenital heart, mental retardation, speech and hearing abnormalities, and epilepsy. However, there is not a complete coverage in any single diagnostic category.

Mental Health 

One of the most urgent health needs of the State is in the field of mental health. Only five counties report mental health clinics. Practicing private psychiatrists are located in only 5 counties. No county reported a professionally recognized clinical psychologist or social worker in full time private practice.

Whitten Village, the State Training School for mentally retarded white children, currently cares for 1,031 children and youth under 20 years of age. Pineland, the State Training School for mentally retarded Negro children, is currently caring for 269 children and youth under 20 years of age. Both schools have waiting lists for admission. The Maternal and 

Child Health Division of the State Board of Health has a special project for the evaluation of children under seven years of age who are mentally retarded or suspected of being mentally retarded. The clinic is located in the department of pediatrics at the Medical College of South Carolina. Its limited staff can care for only about 100 children a year. 

There is no facility in the State for the treatment of the emotionally or mentally disturbed children (as distinguished from children who are mentally retarded). The five county clinics report 70 children who are in need of placement in a residential treatment center. Half of these are diagnosed as "schizophrenic reaction,” which attests to the serious nature of their illness. There is no estimate of the number of children in the other 41 counties who are in need of such treatment. 

State aid is now available for classes in the public schools for the mentally handicapped. Provision is made for both educable and trainable. Recreational, vocational, and day-care services for the mentally retarded are meager.

The local Mental Health Associations and the Associations for Retarded Children have done an excellent job in promoting healthier attitudes toward and more adequate services for the emotionally and mentally handicapped child but the State is still unaware of the tremendous need in this area.

LAW ENFORCEMENT, COURTS, AND LEGAL AID 

South Carolina has made progress during the past ten years in be coming aware of the special needs of children in conflict with the law. 

However, the progress is by no means uniform. Even the term "juvenile' has different meanings. In some counties a juvenile is a youth under 

16, in others he is one under 18. Seventeen counties had no record of juvenile arrests in 1957. Nearly half the counties had such meager records as to the disposition of juvenile cases that they were unable to answer many items on the questionnaire with confidence. The statistics which follow, therefore, do not give an adequate picture of current conditions in South Carolina.

Law Enforcement 

Only five counties of the 43 reporting have special units in their law enforcement departments designated as having major responsibilities for work with juveniles. Officers of these five units have had special training 34

for their work. Seventeen counties report that all officers in their basic training course receive instruction in the handling of juveniles. 

Only 26 counties had records to show the number of juveniles arrested in 1957. These counties reported 4,017. 

Regarding the disposition of juvenile cases, 36 counties reported 537 placed in state industrial schools, 22 counties reported 70 placed in schools for mental cases, 23 counties reported 78 placed in church orphanages, 18 counties reported 322 placed in foster homes, and 21 counties reported 94 placed in state-supported orphanages. 

Thirty-six counties reported that the background of all juvenile offenders is investigated, that the parents or family are contacted, and that special effort is made by law enforcement officials to see that the proper disposition is made of juvenile cases. Seven counties reported that investigations of juvenile cases are made by the Juvenile Court; in 17 they are made by the arresting officer; and in 19 investigations are made by the Department of Public Welfare. In the light of other items reported in the questionnaire, the adequacy of these investigations is open to serious doubt.

Twelve counties reported no separate facilities for the detention of juveniles pending hearings. No special provisions are made by law. enforcement officials in 14 counties in the disposition of mental cases. 

When juvenile cases are not committed to an institution such as the State Industrial School, 27 counties place the offenders on probation, seven return the offender to the parents, nine counties report no follow-up of the case is made. Courts

Fourteen counties have special courts to handle juvenile cases. The 

South Carolina Code of Laws provides that, where separate juvenile courts have not been established, the Probate Judge shall serve as the juvenile judge. Despite that fact, it was reported that in nine counties the Magistrate handled juvenile cases, and in seven counties they were handled in the General Sessions Court. 

A social study is available to the courts in all counties through the services of the local welfare departments. A lack of awareness of this service was revealed in the reports of 17 counties, which stated that no social studies were available to their courts prior to final action. Elever counties reported such studies are made by a court counselor. 

Twenty-nine counties report that after the child is adjudged delinquent, the court's decision as to the disposition of the case is based strictly on the need for treatment. This is not the case in the other 14 counties reporting.

Juveniles are sentenced to the county gang or the state penitentiary in 30 counties. Thirteen counties do not give these sentences. 35

Legal Aid 

Free legal services are available to juveniles in 19 counties. The services of local agencies that deal with children are used in connection with juvenile offenders in 35 counties, not used in five, and not fully used in three. Only 15 counties report that there is a club or organization in the county that has a committee on juvenile delinquency for rendering services and assistance to delinquent minors. Recommendations 

We recommend that: 

1. All counties have uniform laws for dealing with children. 

2. Juvenile courts be made available for the hearing of cases of all juvenile offenders and these courts be staffed with qualified personnel. 

3. A social study of each case be made by a qualified person prior to the hearing before the court. 

4. All agencies dealing with juvenile offenders keep records and each agency provide other agencies with periodic reports. 

5. The State Industrial Schools not be made the dumping ground of all juvenile offenders; placement be based on careful study of the needs of the child. 

6. The public be given an awareness of the special needs of juvenile offenders and an understanding of the importance of providing adequate services to help these children grow into law-abiding, self-respecting, successful citizens.

PUBLIC LIBRARY SERVICE 

South Carolina has a state-wide system of public libraries through which some form of service is given in all of the State's forty-six counties. 

Forty counties have county-wide service provided either through regional or county library systems. The six counties which have as yet been unable to achieve county-wide service have public libraries within their borders which are open to readers within the county. The quality of service varies from that which can be described only as "token" to that which merits the term "excellent."

Basic to the improvement of public library service is public understanding of what constitutes good service and how much it costs. That this public awareness is being achieved is evidenced by the opinion expressed by thirty-five counties that public library income was inadequate to provide the service to children and youth which they considered desirable.

Public library service in the State is supported almost exclusively 36

from public funds. All counties report public library service supported from local funds and thirty-five report additional funds from state aid to public libraries. Although the goal of $1.50 per capita has not yet been achieved, public library income has almost doubled in the past ten years, rising from 33 cents per capita in 1949 to 62 cents per capita in 

1959. 

Improvement in financial support is reflected in improvement of service. Forty-one counties report public library service easily accessible from headquarters, branches and bookmobiles; thirty-eight report adequate hours to meet the needs of children; twenty-five report adequate provision for discussion groups and story hours; thirty-two report special provision for children and youth in both library buildings and bookmobiles. 

There has been improvement both in the quantity and quality of books available to children and youth in the State. Every county reports the use of standard book selection guides in the selection of books for children and youth. Thirty-four counties report a total of 491,230 books for children and 82,811 books for youth; thirty-one counties report a total of 337 periodicals for children and youth.

This improvement in book collections is reflected in the increased use of libraries by children and young people. In 1959 nearly three million books were borrowed by children from the public libraries of the State. 

Thirty-three counties report that their libraries provide books on parent education; twenty-six report some guidance service to parents. counties report that their libraries make use of the State Library Board's Reference and Interlibrary Loan service in filling requests for material and information not available locally.

All Much has been achieved but much remains to be done before the public library realizes its full potential of service to the children and young people of the State. With public awareness of the need of good public library service for the full development of the child, with the understanding of what constitutes good service and how much this costs, and with willingness to provide more adequate support, public libraries should be able within the next ten years to reach a high level of service. 

South Carolina Public Libraries Need: 

Public support and understanding of their efforts to achieve a high level of service for the children and young people of the State. 

More librarians with professional training and personality to serve children and youth.

More books-at least 2 books per capita. 

More adequate provision for service to children and youth and additional services to this group.

Greater use of audio-visual materials in providing service to children and youth. 37

Recommendations: 

We recommend that:

RECREATION 

South Carolina has been fortunate with its natural resources, its climate, and its spaciousness for a population which was primarily rural. 

Changes have been made and are being made to alter these conditions: the attraction of more industry within its borders; people leaving the farms for the towns and cities; the growth of towns and cities; and the automobile and the super-highways. A decade or two ago South Carolina's recreation needs were not as great as today because of the working and cultural conditions. Today, it is a different situation. In the towns and cities recreation space is not being planned or provided by the developers; highways and streets are barriers and hazards; money is not available for the purchase of land, development of areas, or the hiring of adequately trained personnel to operate a recreation program. 

There are many South Carolinians who know of the needs and the values of recreation programs for both urban and rural people. Much work toward this end has been done by state and municipal officials, by industrial organizations, by civic, private and lay groups, and by individuals. Most of our larger towns and cities, to some degree, have made provisions for recreation for their citizens. Many of the textile industries have provided programs for their employees and their families; some have continued and some have been dropped. Many private, church, and civic groups sponsor recreation programs which in many counties is the only recreation offered, except for the commercial recreation. The twenty-two state parks provide excellent recreation areas and accommodations for those nearby.

The various county reports revealed too many counties having no trained leadership, no planned programs, except through private agencies, i. e., 4-H clubs, Scouts, churches, etc., and no playground facilities, except school grounds. All the reports showed a lack of play areas, ballfields, tennis courts, swimming pools, community centers, gymnasiums, and other facilities.

If a comparison of South Carolina facilities were made with the National Recreation Association's standard of areas and facilities, South 38

Carolina would rate very low, except perhaps in lakes and ponds, and they are not adequately distributed over the State. 

The private agencies are reaching more people, but they do not have programs that reach all ages, sexes, or races. The churches are beginning to offer more recreation, but they are dependent on volunteer help and lack space and facilities. The commercial agencies were listed as offering recreation, but some were questionable as being desirable for our youth. 

The municipalities offering recreation programs were listed as offering more diversified programs but lacking adequate trained personnel, facilities, areas, and finances for a more comprehensive program. 

In the reports from the counties was a constant plea for county funds for recreation, and for trained personnel to plan and to guide the volunteers. The reports also revealed the need for a state recreation commission with highly trained personnel to serve as advisors to the smaller towns and communities which are unable to finance a program with trained recreation personnel. 

There are a few places in the State where recreation is available and it is being utilized by the citizens close by, but there are more places where none is offered and none available from any agency or agencies. 

We recommend that: Recommendations 1. A State Recreation Commission be established and staffed with personnel to advise and to assist counties and municipalities, churches, private agencies, industries and other groups on their recreation needs and problems. 

2. Laws be enacted permitting counties to use funds for the establishment of county recreation departments. 

3. Citizens recognize that recreation is not only needed, but should be financed by them through adequate tax levies. 

4. One or more of the state colleges or university provide a curriculum for training personnel with a major in recreation. 

5. More area and facilities for recreation, for all ages, sexes, and races be provided. 

6. Adequate numbers of personnel to provide recreational activities for our youth be employed. 

7. All existing recreational areas within any community be fully utilized and developed to the fullest possible extent. 

RELIGIOUS AND MORAL INSTRUCTION 

A study of the religious and moral instruction of children and youth in 39

South Carolina becomes largely a study of the ministry of the church in South Carolina. A state-wide committee, representing the major denominations and divisions, made a preliminary study of the ministry of the Church to children and youth. This study was then set up in questionnaire form for study by similar committees in all the counties of the State. Possibly such a study by so many in our State will be one of the major benefits of this program. 

Statistical reports from denominations in the State, and from the committees in the counties, indicate that the churches in South Carolina are growing in number and members and improving in program. 

Many rural areas are decreasing in population and weakened churches are struggling to survive. However, many more rural churches have been renewed and enlarged, and have up-to-date fellowship halls and programs and improved educational facilities and work. 

The booming cities of our State and the bustling towns invaded by industry are the major problems of the churches. Existing churches are expanding their facilities and programs, and new churches in new communities are being established. A sharp criticism of this program of church extension is that these new churches almost exclusively follow the more substantial people, economically speaking, and have neglected the more needy communities where the problems of poverty aggravate the religious and moral problems of children and youth. 

Of the 4,500 churches reported in our State, one-third are Negro. Two great needs among the Negro churches are raising the standards of trained leadership and training, and financing the building of better facilities. About 4,000 ministers serve our 4,500 churches, one-half of them serving one-church pastorates. The rest serve two or more churches in one field or circuit. An increasing proportion of our churches have services every week (the Sabbath), and many have through-the-week programs. 

There are one-third more Sunday Schools in the State than there are churches. This indicates a growing zeal to reach and serve children and youth. Not only this, but the churches are having more and more training schools (estimated at 1,656) for leaders and teachers of youth. Of these, approximately 550 were held by Negro churches.

Furthermore, summer programs such as Daily Vacation Bible Schools and camps and conferences sponsored by churches are becoming increasingly wide-spread_and_effective. Twenty or more camp and conference areas are owned and operated by the churches in South Carolina and are used for the total program of education and fellowship, particularly for youth. A growing emphasis is being placed on the family as a unit in recreation, at home and at camp. This should mean much in the years ahead.

Religious instruction in the public schools of the State, which reach 40

practically all children and youth, is officially restricted, but the tremendous influence of teachers of high faith and character cannot be overestimated, whether they teach beginners or seniors. Of course, the reverse of this can be true in scattered cases. But the whole atmosphere of our public educational system is solidly behind high moral standards of living. A mutual respect and cooperation between church and school in the religious and moral instruction of children and youth, each supplementing the other, is invaluable to our purpose and program. 

A small section of the religious and moral instruction of youth in our State is handled by parochial or religiously motivated private schools. 

Most of these serve only the lower grades of school. High schools of this type are chiefly, if not entirely, schools of the Catholic Church. 

The Salvation Army, through its church and social service programs, reaches a large number of children and youth. 

In the light of the much publicized problem of juvenile delinquency, churches and allied agencies need to give more attention to programs for youth from the two extremes of the socio-economic scale. On the one hand, we need better facilities and programs and better trained leaders to appeal to and hold the seemingly sophisticated youth. On the other hand, we need the pioneer and missionary spirit that gives itself in service to the needy with no concern for financial reward but only for the good that can be done.

In this regard, the strong should help the weak, whether it be the churches from better areas of the city concerning themselves for the churches in the needier areas, or the helping hand extended to the struggling church or mission in the scattered rural community. In this same connection, churches and ministers are urged to extend the hand of fellowship and cooperation across the boundaries of congregations, denominations, and communities in order that they might unitedly face the spiritual needs of men, women and children, families and individuals, and cover completely the community and serve efficiently in the moral and religious instruction of children and youth. Recommendations 

We recommend that: 

1. Larger, wealthier churches give serious consideration to providing aid to the smaller churches, both white and Negro, which lack the money to pay preachers, buy literature, support an adequate program. 

2. Ministers of all faiths on the local, county, and state level, through better participation in ministerial organization, share their awareness of special present-day problems of broken homes, alcoholism, delinquency, and public entertainment, though these problems should be handled in their individual parochial way. 41

3. More leadership training courses, family life education, and premarital counseling by the church be provided. 

4. More educational and recreational programs; more church-sponsored day nurseries for working mothers; more kindergartens, camps, and 

Bible study courses be provided to widen the scope of the Church's influence. 

5. Lay members assume more responsibility for the Church program and more acceptance of opportunities for leadership of youth activities promoted by the Church. 

6. Churches become more aware of their opportunities for helping young people attain spiritual strength and develop a moral code on which to base their actions.

SOCIAL SERVICES FOR CHILDREN AND YOUTH 

During the past ten years real progress has been made in the services available to children in South Carolina. A State Training School, Pineland, has been established for mentally defective Negro children. A state law has been enacted for the licensing of foster homes, day care centers, child caring agencies and institutions. Seven new Juvenile and 

Domestic Relations Courts have been established. The Foster Home program has been expanded. The Department of Public Welfare has established an adoptive service as a part of its program. The Department also offers broader services to unmarried mothers. More adequate funds are available for Aid to Dependent Children grants. There are more casework services through qualified agencies. Old Age Survivors and Disability Insurance benefit allowances have been increased. More concentrated efforts are being made to keep children in their own homes. 

Community consciousness of what is happening to children is increasing. 

Every county in the state has a Department of Public Welfare and a Public Health Department to help provide services to children. Court services are available in each county, either through the Probate Court or a special court designated to handle cases involving children. Available also are other public agencies, some on a state-wide basis, others in some counties but not in all. Although the reports indicate that most counties have some private agencies and organizations (fraternal, religious and other) which offer limited services to children, there is a lack of such organizations on the local level. 

Thirty-three counties of the forty-three reporting feel the staff of local agencies is inadequate. There is also a dearth of trained workers. Only thirty-eight counties give figures as to numbers of workers on local agency staffs who have duties relating to the care and protection of children. These counties report a total of 805 workers. Of this number, 126 have had some training in social work and 51 have had special training for Child Welfare Services. 42

The major obstacles which stand in the way of obtaining staff, in order of frequency of listing, are: inadequate appropriations; staff not available; salary; lack of community interest.

The largest single financial program for children is Aid to Dependent Children administered through the Department of Public Welfare. As of 

January 1, 1959, a total of 9,665 families having 30,256 children under eighteen years of age were receiving this aid. This represents an increase since January 1950 of 1,421 families and 6,646 children. The average monthly award per family has risen from $30.73 to $55.98 during the same period and the average per child has increased from $10.73 to $17.88. All counties reported that the awards are still inadequate to provide proper housing, food, clothing and other necessities of life. State funds appropriated for Aid to Dependent Children for the fiscal year 1949-1950, amounted to $800,000. State funds available for this program for 1959-1960, amount to $1,255,000, representing an increase of $455,000.

Even with this increase twenty-nine counties reported there were children in the county who should receive aid but to whom it was not available due to inadequate funds and restrictive policies such as those regarding desertion, father's responsibility, able-bodied but unemployed fathers, residence requirements, and degree of relationship. County committees felt that if the public were informed of the unmet needs of dependent children, communities would insist upon more adequate funds and more liberal policies. 

Family assistance other than Aid to Dependent Children was reported as available in a limited way in thirty-one counties and provided primarily by civic and church groups. It is usually given only in a temporary or emergency situation, until eligibility for public assistance can be determined, or when a family has been determined not eligible for public assistance. Emergency help is available to migrant families in twentysix counties. Although records were not kept in all counties, records which were kept showed that a total of 2,013 migrant families representing 1,155 children under eighteen years of age were helped during 1958.

On January 1, 1959, non-financial aid to children in their own homes was being provided 6,065 children by the Department of Public Welfare as compared to an approximate 2,200 in 1950.

Very little information was available regarding services to children through private, fraternal, and other organizations, but the counties which did report such activities showed a total of 6,680 children receiving services as of January 1, 1959. These included casework, referrals, financial assistance, medical care, school lunches, clothing, school books, glasses, psychological testing, counseling and transportation to clinics.

Only four counties offered homemaker service to keep children together in their own home while the mother was ill, in the hospital, or absent from home for some other reason.

YOUTH EMPLOYMENT 

South Carolina has a higher proportion of youth in its civilian population than any state in the Nation except New Mexico. This poses a tremendous challenge for meeting the needs of these young people for adequate vocational guidance and job placement services. Providing the bulk of these services are the public schools and the South Carolina 

State Employment Service. 

Because of the changes in agricultural methods and the mechanization of farms, and because of the passage of the "Soil Bank" law with the resulting further decrease in the demand for agricultural workers, thousands of youth who were reared on farms are being forced to leave home to find employment. At the same time thousands of farm families are moving to the cities so that the youths are competing with adults for employment.

Compounding the problem is the fact that more and more youths are seeking part-time and summer work as they attend school or college. 

Employment is often hard to find for young adults. Limited industries in many labor market areas provide limited job opportunities. Too, many employers prefer experienced workers and do not provide sufficient opportunities for on-the-job or apprenticeship training. Others do not encourage part-time or summer work. 

However, as more and more industries move into South Carolina more and better job opportunities are opening up in all parts of the State. 

The significance of labor market trends needs to be better understood by youths, their families, the schools and the community so that the choice of a career will be more realistic. Too many youths lack adequate knowledge of job opportunities and job requirements at an age early 47

enough to take full advantage of training offered in secondary schools. 

Too many schools are not aware of opportunities and fail to offer courses which will prepare youths for available careers. 

In this period of revolutionary change in our economy, it is imperative that all efforts be coordinated so as to give youth the guidance necessary to have a realistic understanding of their talents and their weaknesses and to make a wise selection of available careers. Our high school courses should be re-examined in the light of new opportunities. Schools have an opportunity at the present time through the provisions of the National Defense Education Act, to improve their counseling services on the finding of temporary jobs and on planning careers. 

Despite the change in agricultural methods, there are periods when there is a shortage of youths over 16 who want agricultural work. Summer agricultural jobs are available in many areas. When there are no jobs available in one area there are sometimes acute shortages of labor in other areas. Local areas need to organize labor pools of students 16 years old and over who are interested in working in agriculture during the peak seasons.

The coordination of all information and effort through the South Carolina Employment Service, with adequate referral procedures, would greatly benefit our youth and adults alike. The testing and vocational guidance now available through the Employment Service are not fully understood or used. Employers too frequently depend on local applicants and few have training programs. Tragic loss and inefficiency result from our failure to develop an effective coordinated plan. 

South Carolina is one of only four states having no provisions for issuing certificates for employment to minors. Under the present state law, a minor under 16 years of age may work as much as 57 hours a week during school session, as much as 90 hours a week when school is not in session. Minors may operate power-driven machinery; may work in hazardous occupations, except in factory, mine, or textile establishments. Enforcement of even the present inadequate law is difficult because of the lack of employment certification. 

We recommend that: Recommendations 1. State labor legislation to coincide with Federal child labor laws be enacted. 

2. Employment or age certificates for young workers under 18 be required. 

3. Local and state agencies offering vocational guidance services coordinate their work and information. 

4. The public give more understanding and support to what is involved in effective vocational guidance services for youth. 48
