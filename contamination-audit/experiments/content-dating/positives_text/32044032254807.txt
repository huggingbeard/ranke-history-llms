BALLISTIC IDENTIFICATION 

Boulder, Colo., 1939

The old experts of bygone days are rapidly passing out of the picture and will soon become extinct. Among them were a few fakirs, although I believe most of them were honest and upright men.

I do not like to believe that many of them were dishonest, but the fact remains that we did have experts who could be depended on to render a favorable opinion for either side. 

The witnesses of today who are qualified to testify as to finger prints, ballistics and many other subjects do not come to the witness stand with an opinion. They come armed with photo micrographs made directly from the evidence submitted. to them.

And these photo micrographs are finished large enough so the jury may see the evidence with the naked eye. Th witness lays his cards on the table-“This is what I found." The jury is not asked to accept any opinion. They are able to see the evidence and judge for themselves.

Photo micrographs must not be confused with the ordinary enlargement finished from a small negative. A photo micrograph is made directly from the object itself and the negative will register detail which cannot be seen with the naked eye. 

Anything too small to be seen with the naked eye would not show in a negative made in the usual manner, and consequently would not show in an enlargement finished from that negative.

The witnesses who prepare photo micrographic evidence are for the most part professionals and specialists in the different arts and sciences.

The writer was for more than thirty years a commercial photographer specializing in machinery, record, progress, construction and other technical work which calls for detail and accuracy.

This training and practice I feel has been of untold value to me in identification work which now has increased to where I devote practically all my time to it. Since 1923 I have been 

Identification Officer for Boulder County and City of Boulder, Colorado.

The first scientific identification as to a certain bullet having been fired from one particular gun was made not much more than 15 years ago and from that day until the present time the science of ballistic identification has advanced to a stage which was not even dreamed of when finger prints were first used for identification purposes in the United States about the year 1900.

When a crime has been committed with a fire arm such as a rifle, pistol or revolver, the calibre and make of gun employed may in most cases be told by examination of the fatal bullet or bullets.

RIM FIRE ARMS 

In crimes where rim fire arms are concerned we have one advantage which can be counted on in only a few cases where centre fire arms are concerned and that is the firing pin mark on the fired shells.

The firing pins vary as to size and shape in the different makes. Many factories use more than one size or shape but as a rule all guns of the same model have the same shaped firing pin.

In a crime committed with a rim fire arm and no bullet is recovered, or if recovered is defaced too badly for identification, be sure to take good care of any empty shells found at the scene of the crime. These should always be searched for as they often prove of greater value than the bullets when it comes to identifying a suspected gun. 

A careful examination of rim fire shells will in the majority of cases determine the make and model of gun in which they were fired unless a gunsmith or other person has repaired the original pin or replaced it with another. You will then know the make of gun to search for and when the right

SHOT GUNS 

In the identification of shot guns we do not have any markings made by the grooves and lands of a barrel such as are found on bullets fired from rifles, revolvers or pistols. But nevertheless a shot gun may be identified as having been employed in committing a crime, providing shot, wads or shells are recovered.

The size of shot may be arrived at by weighing and measuring. Where wads are recovered, unless the pieces are too small, they help in identifying th gauge of gun employed. And by comparing the material of which they are composed with that of different brands the source of ammunition may in most cases be decided.

Should the suspected gun be of the same gauge and the same brand of ammunition be found in possession of the owner it should call for further and intensive investigation. But your best bet is the shell. 

The firing pin, firing pin hole and breech block of a shot gun will leave marks on the primer of the shell in the same manner as is done in the case of centre fire rifle, revolver or pistol.

GATHERING EVIDENCE 

Guns and Shells

When a gun is found close to the body of a murder of suicide it should always be examined for finger prints. Apparent suicides are not always suicides. Some of them are murders. Where fingerprints are found the corpse should be fingerprinted and checked against the gun.

But the officer should be careful as to how the gun is handled. Many persons believe that to wear gloves and wrap the gun in a handkerchief is the proper procedure, but such is not the case. The prints would become blurred and perhaps be rubbed off entirely. The officer should caution people and ask them not to touch the gun at all. - 19-

Plate XIII. The proper way to pick up and carry guns.

Should the gun be a revolver or pistol, secure a cigar box or anything else of a similar nature. Shove a clean stick down the muzzle of the gun, lift it up and lay it in the box. Or it may be picked up by the trigger guard. 

In case the gun is a rifle or shotgun, secure a stout cord. 

Tie a stick to one end and place it down the muzzle of the gun. Tie the other end of the cord to the trigger guard and carry or hang up the gun by this cord until it can be gone over for fingerprints. All cartridges and shells should be left in the gun until this is done. That fingerprint may be of great value. The gun should not be cleaned or fired. Leave it exactly as it was when found. 

If you will please examine the photo mirograph of the 

Marriott death bullet you will readily see the reason for this advice. The upper part is from the fatal bullet. The lower part is from the test bullet fired after the gun was turned over to me. You will notice that all the fine markings meet exactly, but in the fatal bullet they are plainer, sharper, and deeper. 

This is because the party who found the gun cleaned it, oiled it, and put it in as good condition as possible. 

A body, gun or empty fired shell, should not be moved or disturbed until photographs have been made at the scene showing the exact position of all objects concerned in the crime. Also the exact measuring of distances should not be forgotten. 

There is no excuse for anyone but the investigating officer touching the gun. And unless the investigating officer feels that there is absolutely no doubt as to whether death was by murder or suicide there is no excuse for him to tip out a cylinder, to extract a clip, to pull a trigger, or to move a safety catch from "off" to "on" or from "on" to "off." Should there be the least doubt the gun should not be manipulated in any way, shape, manner or form. 

Many little things not noticeable to the average person will often decide whether death was by murder or suicide. 

Among these are the position of the gun with reference to the body-the particular shell on which the hammer is resting whether an automatic is found with safety catch "on" or "off."

Bullets 

As to any bullet or bullets in a body where murder or suicide is concerned, the coroner or surgeon will understand how to recover them without mutilation, but there is no harm done in asking him to be careful.

In cases where bullets are found imbedded in wood or earth after passing through the body or missing it entirely, great care should be taken in their recovery. The holes should not be probed with knife, wire, nails, etc. When found in wood, cut the wood away from around the bullet until it is liberated. 

Before doing this a photograph should be taken of the fence, door, wall, window frame, piece of furniture, or wherever the bullet is found. This should show the bullet hole around which a chalk mark may be drawn. Taking this photograph is not absolutely necessary, but things like that help out wonderfully at the trial.

When a bullet has entered the ground, the earth should be taken up and screened. If a shovel is used, care must be taken that the bullet is not struck and mutilated. Where the ground is soft or sandy the best tool to use is a lawn rake with looped wire teeth. With this the earth may be loosened, after which it is gathered up in the hands and transferred to the screen. 

Here also a photograph should be taken to show the location of the bullet before the ground is disturbed. 

Take good care of bullets. They should be wrapped in cotton or tissue paper and locked up in the safe. Never rub or polish a bullet or carry it in the pocket along with other articles. Should that be done those fine markings made by the rifling tools will soon disappear, making it very difficult and at times impossible to identify the fatal gun.

The person who recovers the bullet should place a mark on it and all persons through whose hands it afterwards passes may identify it by that same mark. 

In the case of lead, soft pointed or electro plated bullets one should be very careful. Scratch a cross, letter or number on the base of the bullet. Such a bullet should not be marked anywhere else.

In making negatives from which photo micrographs are to be finished a stand is employed which is made of solid metal throughout and weighs more than a hundred pounds. 

This weight is needed to eliminate vibration. To this stand a camera and microscope are attached. 

However, in the finishing of photo micrographs from ballistic exhibits such as bullets, shells, etc., a microscope is not employed as it will not cover a large enough surface. 

In its place micro lenses are employed which are capable of making micro negatives large enough for all purposes. 

Accurate focusing of the camera is accomplished by the use of a screw thread having a very slow pitch.

The stage which carries the object to be photographed is also manipulated by screw threads. These permit the stage being slowly moved in any of four directions without disturbing the object until it is placed directly under the center of the lens. 

Unless identification is accompanied by photo micrographs it can only be considered as an opinion by the jury. But ordinary photographs or enlargements from such photographs are of little or no value. The exhibits must be made through a microscope or micro lens in order to convince the jury.

In the western part of Moffat county, Colo., near the Utah and Wyoming lines, a four-wire fence was found cut between every two posts for a mile and a quarter with a pair of pliers. 

This called for 1600 cuts.

After a thorough investigation Sheriff Tom Blevins of Moffat county arrested two brothers and charged them with the crime. Among other exhibits which he brought in was a pair of pliers which he had found in a chicken nest on the ranch. One of the brothers admitted ownership of the pliers. 

District Attorney Ferry R. Carpenter sent these pliers and a couple of ends from the cut wires to me. Sheriff Blevins had marked the ends of the wires where he had cut them off. 

A quantity of barbed wire of the same gauge as that received from Mr. Carpenter was secured. The wire was 12½ gauge and the mark made by the jaw of the pliers was only 68/1000 of an inch wide, and the exact spot on the particular jaw which made this mark had to be located. 

After this was done a number of cuts were made to show that striations were identical whenever the wire was cut by this particular section of the jaw. Photo micrographs were then made of the marks on the evidence wire and also of several test cuts made with the pliers.

These photo micrographs were finished 2500 times actual size. When the top was trimmed from the micrographs made of the test wire all those small striations which could not be seen with the naked eye joined exactly with those on the evidence wire when the test micrograph was superimposed over that of the evidence wire. This is shown in Plate XVIII and XIX. By examing Plate XIX you will notice how these striations flow together throughout the entire width of the mark. I have called attention to 22 of the most prominent ones. 

The case was tried in the district court at Craig, Colo., on 

Dec. 8 and 9, 1931. After deliberating only a short time the jury brought in a verdict of guilty and Judge C. E. Herrick pronounced sentence before adjourning court.

But he was real peeved when on visiting one of his pumps he found it entirely empty and the "kitty" filled with lead slugs the size of a silver dollar.

This tank had been emptied during the night, and unless the thief had a tank wagon he must have made a number of trips to haul away all the gasoline. Evidence led to a man who lived at a small mountain town about eight miles from Boulder.

In his garage and shop we found a large quantity of barrels and cans, every one filled with gasoline. On his work bench was a plank full of shallow round holes. That man had bored a shallow hole in the plank with the extension bit and poured melted lead into it. He then placed it in the vice and filed it off around the edges until it was exactly the size of a silver dollar. And those marks were placed on the slugs by the jaw of the vice. The reason there were so many holes in the plank was because he could only pour lead into the same one two or three times before it was burned out too much to serve his purpose.

Wire Mill Machinery

In the latter part of January, 1932, Benjamin Bower, a merchant in Denver, Colo., was kidnapped. That same day a car which belonged to a man at Erie, Colo., was stolen in Denver. The next day the owner's brother saw the car going through Erie and gave chase, but had to stop when occupants of the stolen car started shooting at him and throwing roofing nails into the road, and these nails punctured his tires.

A few days later the car was found abandoned in the mountains west of Loveland, Colo. It was taken to Loveland

and I was sent up there to check it for fingerprints. But I found not a single one. Every piece of glass in the car had been smashed. There wasn't as much as an unbroken headlight or reflector. And the car showed signs of having been gone over with a cloth to obliterate any prints which may have been placed on the car.

On the floor of the car we found a quantity of roofing nails and three fired cartridges. The shells proved that the occupants had fired two different guns. On returning to 

Boulder I checked the nails with those picked up in the road and they were the same brand. 

Chief of Police Clark of Denver asked us to check the hill country in all directions from where the car had been found. Six of us, including Sheriffs Robert Blum of Boulder and Orval Kelly of Fort Collins, spent an entire day up there, but could not uncover a single clew which might lead to the kidnappers' hide-out.

Examination of the roofing nails proved that both batches had come from the same lot, as I found seven different header marks in each batch which matched exactly. One from each lot having the same header marks was selected and from them a photo micrograph was finished which is illustrated in Plate XXI and you will notice that marks are identical on the two nails.

We were preparing to secure a lot of nails from all stores in Denver handling this particular brand of roofing nails when word reached us that Mr. Bower had been released in 

Denver at 2 a. m., Jan. 24. His eyes were taped and he did not believe that he had been taken very far.

In wire mills where nails are turned out, they have a large battery of nail-making machines and nails from one machine are marked differently from those of any other machine in the entire battery. Any nail may be identified by gripping, heading and pointing dies as having been turned out by any particular machine. Ultra Violet Rays 

Wave lengths of the different colors vary greatly. Among the visible rays those of red are the longest while those of violet are the shortest. Beyond the violet rays are those of the Ultra Violet. These Ultra Violet rays are invisible under ordinary light.

The lamp illustrated in Plate XXIV is of the mercury vapor type. Electric current is passed through mercury vapor. The mercury is contained in a quartz generator. The 

Ultra Violet rays are then passed through dark colored filters. 

These filters hold back all ordinary, or visible light, which is emitted by the mercury quartz arc.

But the Ultra Violet rays pass through these dark filters and under this light substances which are invisible under ordinary light are made visible. The results may be photographed for permanent record, but this is very seldom necessary as the lamp is portable and may be brought to the court room where the jury can see with their own eyes the fluorescense of the exhibit under examination. It is only necessary to partly darken the room by drawing the shades.

Secret Writing is detected instantly and without disturbing the paper in any way. Prisoners correspond back and forth with accomplices outside by spacing the lines far apart in a letter which appears absolutely innocent, and between the lines of this letter invisible writing is done with a tooth pick or match, which leave no scratches in the paper as a pen would do. 

I have upwards of thirty different formulas for detecting secret writing but there is no way of telling which formula to use. One after the other must be used until the proper one brings out the secret writing.

Some writing is brought out by heat, light or water. Others by chemical fumes or direct application of certain chemicals. 

Prisoners use the simpler forms of secret writing, but they may also use some of those which must be tested with chemicals. A prisoner has access to a dozen different substances or can ask for them without causing any suspicion on the part of the jailer.

Among these are milk, urine, cold tablets, aspirin, alkaseltzer, the juice of potatoes and many other vegetables.

When such writing is placed under the Ultra Violet ray the secret writing will immediately fluoresce and become readable.

Paper and Envelopes may be identified as being or not being of the same composition as to pulp, sizing, coloring matter, etc. Imitations and adulterations of all kinds are detectable by the difference in flourescence. 

Lip Sticks, creams, powders, lotions and practically all toilet articles may be examined under the Ultra Violet ray and unless two exhibits are of the same brand there will be a difference in the color or shade of fluorescence. Marks made by twelve different brands of lip sticks which are of the same color under ordinary light range all the way from yellow to very dark brown when examined by the Ultra Violet ray. 

The underworld of today is a far different proposition than the underworld of yesterday. Its members are better organized. They cover a larger territory by moving in highpowered cars. They are well supplied with funds to work with. 

When apprehended they have more funds available for the employment of the best attorneys obtainable. 

Peace officers are charged with the investigation of crime and the apprehension of criminals. The duty of presenting the evidence falls upon the shoulders of the prosecuting attorney. 

Many cases are lost to the prosecution due to the failure of the investigating officer to properly obtain evidence. Peace officers cannot be expected to match the legal knowledge of practicing attorneys. 

Catching the criminal does not put him "out of circulation." That's the prosecuting attorney's job, and we should all assist him to the best of our ability. The criminal is not obliged to prove anything. He is not even obliged to go on the witness stand. The law says he is innocent until proven guilty. You and those who work with you must do the proving. Many peace officers have found this out to their sorrow.
