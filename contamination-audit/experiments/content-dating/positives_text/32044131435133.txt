CHAPTER ONE 

Rising like a sheeted ghost from the ashes of its countless thousands of hapless victims, to point the chalky finger of accusation at the greatest curse ever to enmesh the heart of humanity in the slimy coils of its deadly tentacles, the gaunt spectre of narcotic drug addiction is, today, a force of evil, despair, debauchery and frightfulness that bids fair to undermine-yes, to even destroy, that which we so proudly and so smugly look upon as our advanced civilization. 

As recent as fifty years ago the Occidental world raised its collective eyebrows in synthetic surprise and evinced a mild curiosity at the queer custom among Orientals of smoking and chewing that exotic, sinister substance known as Opium, the coagulated juice of the "sleep" poppy. The Western

Hemisphere, however, had no fear of it and openly sneered at, or ignored altogether, the weakness of those who succumbed to its tragic power. 

But the trail of this demon flower across the face of the earth is and has been a

thrilling, albeit tragic, story of seared souls, wrecked families, blasted promises, hideous crimes, agonizing diseases, national greed, international dishonor and world disaster. 

For scores of generations-from the very gates of Eden itself the world has been encumbered with the dual evils of war and pestilence. One or the other has always been in the ascendency and, under the terrible burden of these blighting influences, the human race is gradually sinking. But this more recent horror-narcotic drug addiction-bids fair to far outstrip the other two in sheer malignant destructiveness of mind, body and soul.

The whole human race is in the relentless grip of an evil so monstrous that it staggers the imagination and baffles the efforts of the wisest statesmen to prevent. Almost overnight it has taken the nations of the earth captive—the United States far more so than all others, according to statistical records, holding millions in the cruelest slavery ever known.

Of all the plagues visited upon our land, narcotic drug addiction is by far the most horrible and the most deadly. Meanwhile science is stunned and almost helpless before the stealthy but swift inroads of an

all-but-incurable disease for that is what narcotic drug addiction really is, all other opinions to the contrary-that is sweeping over the whole world; a disease that, alone, costs the United States of America one billion dollars every year.

Nearly every boat that plies the seven seas carries, lurking somewhere in its hold, camouflaged under some common name and hidden from the prying eyes of officials, a packet of this diabolical abomination. Some of the most highly civilized and intellectually advanced nations of the world, not only connive at the spread of this plague, but publicly encourage it, for they derive vast sums in revenue from its use, mounting in some instances to scores of millions of dollars annually; and, as the profound Tennyson so eloquently put it: "The jingling of the guinea helps the hurt that honor feels."

A recent and careful survey in China brought out that that country, alone, is producing in excess of twelve thousand tons of Opium annually; more than ninety per cent of the world's output. This, for the most part, is controlled by Great Britain, just as this same nation controls the fabulous production of this commodity from the back country of India, that enriches the

Empire's treasury and brings wealth beyond belief to the Maharajahs of the Indian provinces. It brought out also, in the most vehement terms, the wanton activities of the Japanese and Koreans, who would stop at nothing and from whom anything might be expected, in the exploitation of Manchukuo and North China, Japan taking advantage of her position in these regions; her position of dominance in Manchukuo and her position of extra-territoriality in China.

On the whole, the legitimate manufacture and trade in narcotic drugs appears to be under effective control. But, in spite of this fact, the illicit traffic continues active and vast quantities of surplus narcotic drugs are still reaching the illegal market.

Traffickers, in order to avoid serious risks and losses, now that there exists the shadow of an abortive attempt to "do something about the situation," are dispatching the contraband in small lots instead of in the large quantities as was the case only a few years ago.

There seems to be an increased use of postal facilities, too, as well as ships' crews, for the illicit distribution. Likewise, seized drugs are now seldom found bearing manu-

facturers' labels or trade marks unless they are false. All this indicates that it is the clandestine factories which have sprung up with mushroom growth rapidity, rather than the legal sources of manufacture, that are now the sources of supply for the illicit traffic, even though some legitimately manufactured drugs have been diverted to this despicable trade by means of false import and export certificates. Appreciable quantities which reach the illicit channels of the United States have been traced to this source.

Since it is well known that narcotics of American manufacture seldom, if ever, find their way into unlawful marts, and since it is, therefore, the smuggled narcotics of foreign surplus manufacture to which the United States is a prey, one step in the remedy clearly lies in a common agreement between the eleven countries which manufacture high-powered drugs, to limit the output of them strictly to conform with the world's medical requirements.

In other words, if the world manufacture of these narcotics-which the League of Nations, as well as all independent authorities on the subject, agree is vastly in excess of legitimate requirements-was so lim-

ited, the illicit traffic, thus deprived of its sources of supply would, of necessity, disappear.

It is utterly impossible to overstress the stealthy but swift inroads narcotic drug addiction is making in the United States, resulting in an alarming annual increase in completely shattered lives. This is not the bugaboo of cracked minds nor faddists like those advancing such nit-witisms as, for instance, that all life can be guided by psychology, children raised wholly by the application of a theory of which the child knows nothing, its parents, possibly, even less (in that the child makes no pretense) and of which the so-called experts have, in all probability, but a vague notion, in that they cannot seem to reach an agreement as to just what is the true basis of their theory, if any. No, the horror and danger and stark tragedy of narcotic drug addiction is not theoretical in any degree but something very concrete and its seriousness is not to be minimized.

That this is emphatically the true state of affairs may more eloquently be emphasized by the statement recently of Frederick 

A. Wallis, former Deputy Police Commissioner of the City of New York, then United

States Commissioner of Immigration and, later, State Commissioner of Correction of

New York: 

"I can say without reservation that, in the light of my experience, the greatest menace confronting civilization today is narcotic drug addiction."

Narcotic drug addicts are divided, broadly, into two classes: first, legal medical addicts and, second, "dope" fiends, as they are most commonly referred to. 

The former are a negligible factor and are provided for by the state and federal laws. 

They are legal users of narcotic drugs either because of incurable diseases, accident or injury, or the infirmities of age, to whom narcotics, in one form or another, are legally furnished or prescribed in good faith by licensed physicians, with the full sanction. of authorities.

The latter are persons entirely apart from the above, who habitually take or otherwise use narcotic drugs, and who have become so thoroughly addicted to their use as to have lost the power of self-control insofar as it applies to their addiction. A large percentage of this second class consists of the following specific types: neurotics, psycho-

4 paths, inebriates, habitual criminals, prostitutes, men and women of unstable moods, social misfits and irresponsible individuals seeking new excitements and sensations. 

But the above is by no means thoroughly representative. Many there are who live in the belief that it is only people who come from the other side of the tracks" or, who are denizens of the so-called underworld, who use "dope." This is, of course, sheer stupidity. The disease-and this word is used advisedly-of narcotic drug addiction afflicts alike the educated American, the titled Englishman, the sophisticated Frenchman, the erudite German, the ignorant

Bushman of Australia, the Chinese coolie and the "Untouchable" of India. It seems to thrive on the frantic but futile efforts of some well meaning nations to check it. 

Fortunately, however, it is being realized more and more that no one nor several nations, standing alone, can suppress the illicit traffic. And out of the fact of that knowledge something may come. For that we can but offer a prayer.

Before entering upon a discussion of the narcotic drug problem, what constitutes it, and what the only sane solution to it can

be, it might be advisable to dwell, briefly, on the various and more commonly known habit forming drugs, their properties and reactions upon the human system.

Those, aside from the devastating Marijuana which is of sufficient importance that an entire chapter will be devoted to it later, with which the public, generally, is most familiar are four: Opium, Morphine, Heroin and Cocaine, the latter in a classification by itself, insofar as its relation with the others mentioned. There are also Codein, Laudanum, Pantopan, the salts of opium or its derivatives, all of which are likewise habit forming. But the four indicated are the most common.

Opium, of course, forms the principal basis for narcotic drug manufacture. It's derivatives in their respective forms, all of which are habit forming when used regularly or daily, long enough to cause addiction, are obtained from the plant known as the poppy.

The poppy is cultivated in China, British 

India, Persia, Egypt, Asia Minor, Turkey and several parts of Europe. Commerce is mainly supplied with Opium from these countries. 

Large quantities are also produced in the

Indian provinces of Benar Benares and in the more remote interior ones of Malawa. 

There are several species of the poppy plant yielding more or less Opium. Papa Oriental is its real source. The black and white poppy are cultivated for commerce.

Opium is obtained by incising the unripe calyses of the poppy and collecting the exuded sap or inspissated juice. The process requires four or five days, and is accomplished by scraping the calycem into containers. After treatment, it finds its way into commercial channels and is received in the United States as Gum Opium. It contains from five to twelve per cent Morphine. 

The legal standard is nine per cent. The 

Morphine content, however, seems to have no direct influence on the Opium used for smoking.

Gum Opium contains numerous ingredients from which the various derivatives are extracted. They are, primarily, for use in medicine. The most effective and widely known ingredient, termed an alkaloid, is Morphine. The name is derived from Morpheus (Mopers), the Greek god of dreams. 

Morphine relieves pain and quiets nerves.

The victim who has been enslaved to this most exacting, toll-taking master is more

to be pitied than censured. A Morphine fiend is very likely to turn criminal to obtain the required drug when his funds are exhausted. The pain and craving are so great as to blind him to all sense of proportion.

Heroin is a synthetic preparation made from Morphine. In proper doses it is an ideal sedative, especially in acute bronchial coughs, etcetra. But, like strychnine, it is "bad medicine" when taken in large doses.

Heroin is more deadly than Morphine, as it produces the effect of Cocaine and Morphine combined. It is at once a sedative and a stimulant. Due to its conflicting chemical properties, the effect is of much shorter duration than that produced by Morphine. The victim must, therefore, use more of it. And this, naturally, increases the dosage. Physical degeneration is more rapid and terrible.

Hailed originally as a possible cure-all for 

Morphine ills, if Opium may be said to walk and Morphine runs, then Heroin gallops. In 

1898, a German chemist by the name of

Dresser treated Morphine with acetic acid and produced Heroin. No remedy was ever heralded so enthusiastically. It was put forth as a substitute for Morphine, and was

even advocated as a cure for Morphinism. 

It was introduced into the United States about 1909, and first began to be dangerous three years later, in 1912. Then physicians were horrified to discover that so far from possessing all the virtues of Morphine with none of its habit forming qualities, Heroin was infinitely more dangerous.

Contrary to all opinion otherwise, Cocaine is not a narcotic drug, though habit forming to a terrible degree. It is a derivative of the Coca leaves. The slopes of the Andes are the main sources of cultivation and production. Various salts of this alkaloid are used in surgery for local anesthesia. 

Cocaine is habit forming only in the sense of indulgence. In other words, Cocaine does not cause addiction. Persons who have used it for many years, and in large quantities, can discontinue without suffering physical ill effect other than extreme nervousness over a prolonged period.

But Cocaine must be considered as the most deadly of all drugs. The victim is wholly irresponsible for his actions while under its influence, just the contrary to Opium addicts. He is at once robbed of all mental function, reason and common

sense, experiences hallucinations of a most terrifying kind and is rendered suspicious and in fear of everyone and, thereby, is extremely dangerous. Therefore, though very definitely not a habit forming narcotic in the sense that we understand addiction, more heinous crimes are committed under the influence of Cocaine than narcotics and alcohol put together.

Unlike the necessary treatment for Morphine and Heroin, it is purely a question of will power in breaking the Cocaine habit, for the withdrawal symptoms are not the same. And it is this fact that has probably given rise to the belief that abstinence from all drugs is but a matter of the user's ultimate will to abstain, once having become addicted.

Addicts of Opium and its derivatives suffer excruciating abdominal pains when their drugs are withdrawn. Those who indulge in Cocaine do not. Opium and its derivative, Morphine, are sedatives, whereas Cocaine, on the other hand, is a stimulant. 

Heroin is a combination of both. 

Opium smoking, at least in the United

States and among white people, is almost a thing of the past. The cumbersome "layout" consisting of the pipe, the bowl, yen

shee gow, yen hock, suey pow, gee rag, the ho-toy of Limka or Li-yum-brands of mud Opium-and the give-away aroma of the smoking, all make it easy for exposure.

The narcotic drug most frequently used in this country is Morphine, hypodermically administered. Heroin and Cocaine are taken mostly by sniffing. If, after prolonged addiction, injecting the hypodermic instrument in the usual manner does not give sufficient "kick," the user substitutes a large safety-pin and an eye-dropper, shooting the fluid directly into the veins and even the main artery of the arm. He or she is then what is known as a "main line user."

To become addicted to the use of narcotics such as Morphine and Heroin requires ninety to a hundred and ten days of daily use. With Opium it would require almost a year of daily smoking to acquire the habit.

Therefore, to elude the evil completely is not difficult, once one knows the facts. But so many don't.

CHAPTER II 

When people speak of the "narcotic drug problem" or the "dope menace," as it is generally called, what is meant? Just what is the narcotic problem?

Even those of high estate who, because of their very position in law enforcement, civic welfare work and activities for the betterment of humanity, should know, find themselves, when pressed, woefully at a loss to adequately set forth just what it is. 

Too much heresay and theorizing on a subject the basic principles of which are very simple if viewed honestly and unbiased -has left the so-called "experts" or "specialists" in much the same dilemma as are, once more, the child psychologists who cannot agree among themselves whether a deliberately spoiled, unlovable child should be punished for some act of juvenile vandalism by having ten cents of a weekly allowance, that may not at the time even have been inaugurated, deducted; or to detract its mind from the refractory act by completely ignoring it, instead of dealing out a good old fashioned spanking when the occasion demands. 23

That the above condition clearly exists is amply indicated by the wide divergence of opinions by those supposedly in the know. 

If the average government official or police is asked, he will declare that it is only a matter of law enforcement, of keeping narcotics out of the country and of putting peddlers and addicts in prison.

Some maintain that it is only a moral problem; that vicious persons of the underworld type are the only ones who become addicts, and as we take care of our criminals adequately (if it can be truthfully said that we do) the narcotics question will be automatically solved.

There are still others who claim it is mainly an international problem; that it can be dealt with only through the concerted action of the nations of the world. 

Many doctors and jurists tell us that it is only a psychological problem, that addicts only imagine the terrible pains that drive them back to taking narcotic drugs after desperately attempting to get along without them.

In some few isolated instances, educators advance the theory that the problem is one of instruction; that when the public knows

the awful facts, the problem will vanish as a serious menace.

While all these phases enter into the situation, the latter more so than any of the others, the whole problem has recently been further complicated by evidence that it is also a very decided pathologic one. Addicts are really very sick people; addiction is actually a terrible disease, just as cancer is. True, addiction may be considered a vice and a habit, but it is also a dread disease as well. 

Narcotic drug addiction is a real physical illness, and narcotic drugs are a deadly poison. The victims are just as much poisoned as though they had consumed other deleterious drugs. They are so filled with accumulated toxin that they are driven almost beyond human endurance with the pain. and suffering. The sudden withdrawal of their narcotic supply, therefore, is dangerous-very dangerous. And in some instances it will cause death. Such an addiction is very definitely not a neurosis, but is solely a toxemia of acute and intestinal origin. It belongs absolutely to the medical province and not to neurology.

Therefore, anyone using Opium or its derivatives, Morphine and Heroin, for a prolonged period, develops a pathologic condi-

tion; changes that are not yet fully understood take place in the body chemistry. They are sick people from that time on and require one of two things: intelligent treatment or the narcotic drug of their addiction. 

To deprive an addict of his or her drug does not result in a cure. Will power is not a cure for addiction any more than it is for cancer or any other terrible physical affliction. The narcotic problem, thus, resides to a large extent in the recently recognized fact that drug addiction is an extremely painful and almost incurable disease, unless properly handled, that requires the drug of addiction, no matter what the cost, to make the life of the victim at all endurable. 

Therefore, addicts will go to any lengths and pay any price for their drug.

Thus the demand is created and in satisfying this demand for a prohibited article huge profits are made and narcotic "rings" are formed, which is traffic in drugs on an international scale, it being recorded that single shipments worth from one to ten million dollars alone have been made.

The truth, in brief, about a condition of drug addiction is very ably advanced by Dr. 

Ernest Bishop of New York, well qualified

2.as an expert, for he has worked many years on scientific investigation of the narcotic question. Dr. Bishop says: "A narcotic drug addict is an individual in whose body the continued administration of opiate drugs has established a physical reaction or condition of mechanism, or process, which manifests itself in the production of definite and constant symptoms and signs, peculiar and characteristic phenomenae, appearing inevitably upon the deprival or material lessening in amount of narcotic drug and capable of immediate and complete control only by further administration of the drug of the patient's addiction. "The result of a sudden or too sudden withdrawal of the drug is that, in a general way, the patient begins with a vague uneasiness and restlessness and a sense of depression followed by yawning, sneezing and excessive mucous secretion, sweatings, nausea, uncontrolled vomiting and purging, twitching and jerking, intense cramps and pains, abdominal distress, marked circulation and cardiac insufficiency and irregularity, pulse advancing from a low ebb to the extreme of rapidity, with loss of vocal tone, face drawn and haggard, pallor deepening to grayness, exhaustion, collapse and,

in not infrequent cases, death." 

A decidedly pleasing picture, isn't it?

In plain English, a narcotic drug addict is one who experiences all the signs and symptoms as outlined above simply as a result of complete lack of or an insufficient supply of narcotic drug.

Further proof that drug addiction is much more and worse than just a vice or a habit is advanced by the fact that a baby born of an addict mother is an addict at birth. 

A few hours after delivery the baby will develop what is known as withdrawal symptoms, the same as the adult has, and which used to be supposed to be purely imaginary or simulated. The baby will begin to sneeze, stretch and yawn; pain will follow and it will draw its little legs up and cry in agony, vomit violently, develop uncontrollable spasms, and die in agony unless given some Morphine. When the drug is administered the baby becomes apparently normal. Certainly no one would accuse a day-old baby of having a habit or vice or of imagining all this. Then, again, perhaps some individuals would. 

Dogs, too, have been given drugs until, upon their withdrawal, they manifest the

same symptoms as do human beings. Surely the poor dog can hardly be accused of making believe, or can it? In fact, any warm blooded animal can be made an addict of narcotic drugs. 

There is no type or race or color or age or class that is especially susceptible to addiction, nor free from susceptibility. All classes, types and races become drug addicts with equal ease. The preacher and the prostitute, the king and the slave, succumb with equal celerity. And the result is the same in all cases: complete subjection to the drug, intense torture if it is withdrawn for twelve hours and a determination to obtain the necessary opiate no matter what the cost. 

The recent government report on "Traffic in Narcotic Drugs" gives the order of frequency of addiction according to nativity as follows: American, Italian, Irish, English, Russian, Jewish, French, Negroes, Chinese, Mexican, Austrian, German and Canadians.

The report covered the addicts in penal institutions. A report based on the state, county and municipal almshouses, state hospitals, insane asylums and other institutions gives a slightly different result, but varying only a little.

The occupation of addicts in the order of their frequency was found as follows: 

Housewives, laborers, clerks, physicians, salesmen, nurses, pharmacists, actors, prostitutes, waiters, cooks, sailors, horsemen, barbers, bartenders, draftsmen, teachers and unemployed.

The government findings, based on returns from the private hospitals and sanitariums of the nation revealed a slightly different frequency of addiction, as follows:

Housekeepers, laborers, physicians, salesmen, actors and actresses, unemployed, business men, nurses, farmers, office workers, professional men and women, prostitutes, pharmacists, dope peddlers, mechanics, merchants, gamblers, newspaper men and printers.

From these facts it is clear that it is not only the down and outers, criminals or morons alone that make up the great, gaunt army of addicts. If some certain nationality or particular class were especially susceptible, some encouragement might be offered, for then we might isolate our problem and study it with more hope of solution. But we are stunned to realize that no one is safe, that the lightning of addiction may strike the best and noblest of us and drag

the most favored to perdition while we stand by, helpless and despairing, utterly unable to save these innocent victims.

Today we have highly specialized institutions for the care of the insane, the feebleminded, the epileptic, the consumptive and the incurable. But the narcotic drug addict, who is the helpless victim of a more serious malady than any of these, is still merely put in prison. ap- 

Any reputable medical man, SO proached and it being within his abilities, should explain intelligently to the victims the ultimate and terrible reaction of narcotics. The patient should be re-educated in an intelligent way with kindness and truth. 

Is a jailer qualified to do this? Does he educate? Does he encourage? Does he have any human consideration? Is he really kind?

No permanent good is achieved by sending addicts to regular penitentiaries, for not only do they make addicts of other inmates (narcotic drugs have never successfully been excluded from penal institutions, for wherever there is money a way will be found to smuggle them) but they come out worse than they went in, wiser in crime,

and still addicts; more hardened and indifferent to their fate. We of today, seem to maintain the same unthinking attitude toward narcotic drug addicts as a century ago our forefathers maintained toward the insane, who were callously imprisoned.

Obviously, prison is no place for a sick man. He does not deserve punishment so much as medical attention. One who would suggest sending a sick man to the penitentiary because he has tuberculosis would be regarded with amazement. True, the consumptive is dangerous to society, but since we found a better way to treat the consumptive, so should we find a better way to treat the addict, who may actually be more ill than the tubercular.

Drug addiction is pestilential, and should be put in the same class with leprosy and smallpox. While the addict longs to be cured himself, paradoxical as it may seem, he is constantly seeking or endeavoring to make addicts of those around him, especially his own friends and family.

The warfare of dope against humanity is never in the open. It is anonymous and invisible-the butchery of the unknown by the unseen. Everywhere the whole subject

seems to be surrounded by secrecy and mystery, God, alone, may know why. Unfortunately these very elements of the strange and hidden serve to lure people into the power of the trafficker from whom they may never escape except through the door of death.

The Hellish peddler first teaches the use of the habit-forming drugs to his victims, and then lives off of them. Finally, as a reward, he may even betray them to officials. 

This leprosy of soul can be paralleled only by imagining an undertaker who might kill a friend in order to bury him.

When narcotic drugs have been taken to the point of addiction, it is slavery, bondage. 

One is no longer free to roam at will. One cannot leave their source of supply, whether it be two grains or forty grains a day they are taking. What difference does it make whether you are hanging by a rope or a thread if you are hanging and can't get down? In exchange for a few grains of drug, career, love, friends, health, everything held dear, is sacrificed. Business is neglected, for without the necessary opiate one is too ill to work. And yet one must work to secure means to buy the drug at exorbitant prices, for traffickers ask any-

where from $50.00 to $250.00 an ounce.

If one loses their business or means of livelihood because of their addiction-and they most surely always do—and have no means with which to buy their necessary relief, there is only one thing left: a life of crime, for the craving for narcotics must be satisfied, regardless of the cost, as the pain and suffering is beyond human endurance.

While narcotic addiction and crime run hand in hand, there are instances of addicts who have no criminal tendencies whatsoever until their source of supply is cut off. 

Then, when there is no one anywhere who will give them, or help them get the drugs they need, these same people will rob, lie, steal and cheat, in order to secure the drug to satisfy them. Once their nerves are calmed, the pain gone, they become meek as lambs. The crime they have just committed is forgotten and if conscience should remind them of their dereliction, they easily alibi it by saying to themselves: "What else could I do? I had to have my dope. I had to have it!" 

Crime comes easily to those so afflicted, for once blinded by the power of Dope, the helpless slaves of this insidious master must

do the master's bidding. As if it were not enough to have one's body racked with pain for the want of the deadly drug, the addict must ever supply the money that these traffickers ask. Narcotic drugs being sold illicitly, any sum may be demanded and the victim always pays. How this Demon God must laugh at foolish mortals, when he sees to what event his victims will debase themselves before his altar.

So much has been written recently regarding the forward march of juvenile crime. If we would contribute immeasurably to rid our nation of this evil, put a halt to the march of the narcotic drug menace, for narcotic drugs are the cause of more crime today than any other single factor. 

Whereas, every crime ever committed has not been done by drug addicts, every type of crime in the book may be traced to the very doorstep of drugs. One-third of all the prisoners in our Federal institutions have been placed there either by their addiction or because they were traffickers in this ghastly commodity.

Because opiate using of any nature has always been considered by the general public as only a habit, a sign of weak will and of dissipation, addicts have naturally en-

deavored to conceal their unfortunate condition. This is, of course, not as it should be, but society has made it that way. 

Statistics disclose that drug addicts, as a class, are idlers and not workers; not that they originally held such mean stations, but that they have been debased to them. The majority are dependent upon petty larceny, stealing, begging, drug peddling, prostitution and other illegal methods of obtaining money to satisfy their craving. Our narcotic officials estimate that approximately eighty per cent of our addicts become criminals, either because of their abnormal personality or to satisfy their craving for the drug.

In that it has been shown that when the seed of addiction has taken root, spiritual, moral and physical degeneration is not far behind and utter irresponsibility follows, it would be far cheaper in view of the present situation and method of procedure, to segregate and care for these people, save those who are curable, and protect society from the spread of this contagion. Destroy the market for the peddler and put him out of business by isolating his customers, rather than permit them to remain at liberty to prey upon society. But even that would

be but an attempted compromise with an enemy within our gates which gives no quarter and asks none.

Under all the existing circumstances, who is safe from this dread evil? What causes it? What can we do to stop it? What can we do? We can educate and do so with the enthusiasm of desperation and, thereby, possibly help to retrieve thousands of souls before they are hopelessly lost.

CHAPTER III 

Because of its many ramifications the problem of narcotic drug addiction is far more intricate and complex than might be realized from superficial scrutiny; one of many and conflicting angles.

It is a health problem because its consequences lead to pain, anguish, misery and physical wreckage.

A social problem because it means financial ruin to the victim and enormous economic loss to the government. 

A psychological problem because it leads to mental and moral deteroiration. 

A medical problem because it frequently results from self-medication or carelessness, ignorance or neglect on the part of those administering narcotic drugs and because it very definitely requires medical care for its treatment.

A legislative problem because effective laws and their enforcement are necessary for its suppression.

An educational problem because the 38

youth and the public at large, for their own self-protection must understand its evil consequences. Moreover, constructive narcotic legislation can be based upon but one thing-good, sound knowledge of the problem;

A police and law enforcement problem because it leads to crime and is so closely allied with wrong doing that if it is desirous of checking the crime wave, narcotic drug addiction must be likewise checked; 

An agricultural problem because countries producing the Opium poppy plant and the Coca shrub have such vast acreage and so many laborers engaged in this industry that suitable agricultural substitutes must be found to supplant the excessive cultivation of these products; 

A scientific problem because when science discovers suitable, efficient non-habit forming substitutes to supplant the use of narcotic drugs in medicine, then production and manufacture of narcotic products would cease;

A co-operative problem because no nation standing alone and no state acting alone can suppress the illicit drug traffic; the problem requires co-operative efforts on

the part of nations, states, municipalities and individual citizens in order to put into action the legislative, educational and rehabilitation processes required to meet the situation. 

Statistics in any form are likely to be prosaic and not of general interest to a layman except insofar as they may shed some light upon a problem at hand and are, even then, endurable only to the extent mentioned. 

According to federal government records there are some two millions of known narcotic addicts. If there are two milllions of such sufferers known, we may rest assured that there are six or eight millions that are unknown—and we cannot ignore the plight of millions of people. 

From the first indulgence, down the path that leads to eventual addiction, the steps are so gradual that the victim is unsuspectingly caught in an undertow that grips and binds.

When properly used and until some equally effective but harmless substitute can be had for them, narcotic drugs are one of God's noblest gifts to mankind. It is not their use, but abuse, that has wrought so much wholesale havoc.

The narcotic drug situation will not improve one iota until arrested by a safe and sane solution. This solution must be twofold in its purpose: prevention (which embraces the all-important factor of education) and cure. The root of the evil must be attacked and wiped out at its very source. 

Legislation cannot cure drug addiction.

Neither can laws prevent the use of drugs. 

This is not within the power of the judiciary. 

Do what we will, say what we will, there are certain classes who will use drugs just so long as money can buy them and until the traffic is permanently stamped out so that future generations will have no recourse to it, and through education, no de- ´sire for it. 

The problem waxes complex. And the tragedy is that it is not something applicable only to the future, with ample time to forestall, but is actively and insistently present today, now at this very moment.

Much has been said about limiting the source of supply of narcotic drugs. Unless any such move conforms strictly with the minimum in medicinal requirements, it is putting the cart before the horse. Just so long as there is a demand there will always

be a supply and, horrifying and astounding as this fact may be, there is not a city, nor a hamlet, nor a community in this vast country-no matter how small-but what an illicit demand does exist. Therefore, retard the demand. End it and there will be no need for supply. How can this be accomplished? The answer again is only through education!

The crime element did not enter into either narcotic addiction or the sale of narcotics until the passage of the Harrison 

Narcotic Act on December 17, 1914. Very few people are actually acquainted with this measure; a law which makes punishable as a felony a situation or condition, the exact nature of which, as a misdemeanor, has never been explained, especially to that young man or young woman who has become a drug addict, and through no fault of their own, perhaps, except ignorance.

With the passage of the Harrison Narcotic Act it became unlawful to possess, use or sell narcotic drugs unless prescribed by a reputable physician; a very reputable one. 

Over night, addicts who heretofore could purchase their necessary supplies at the corner pharmacist became felons in the eyes of the law.

But what provision did the Harrison act make for these unfortunates? None whatsoever. It deprived them completely of their source of supply, a source where, up to then, they had legitimately been able to make purchases, but it made not the slightest provision for their rehabilitation. Having started on a course, the Harrison Act did not go the whole route, but stopped halfway, thereby creating a Frankenstein monster. That halfway mark was to forbid the sale of narcotic drugs without taking the many other and equally as important phases of the matter into consideration. 

The addicts themselves, when finally driven to crimes of violence in their frenzy for relief to tortured bodies, usually ended up in the hands of the police who, in turn, did the only thing within their power. They locked them up and let them rant and knock their heads against the walls and bars of a cell.

The effect of the Harrison Act was, at first, to direct a growing number of addicts to physicians and druggists for treatment. 

This stimulated interest on the part of the physicians and, to some degree, led to the study of addiction, which should inevitably have led to many cures and methods of treatment.

Enforcement of the regulations of the act, however, called into question the judgment and good faith and integrity of a number of physicians and pharmacists. Indictments and convictions were brought against both classes. The press publicity following what were supposedly willful violations of a beneficient law was most disastrous to the physicians and druggists. These occurrences caused others, in both professions, to fear similar court actions and finally to refuse to have anything to do with treatment of addicts. Doctor after doctor and pharmacists, likewise, have declared emphatically that, not for a fortune, would they have any part of it.

In dwelling on this astounding phase of the matter, it should be remembered that the Harrison Narcotic Act was not drawn by physicians as a medical measure, but by lawyers as a revenue measure solely. And that is what it is. It was framed on the general theory that drug addiction is but a vice or habit.

If it was intended as a check to the illicit traffic in narcotics, its success now and ultimately can be determined by two pertinent facts. Drug peddling, as a well organized industry, thrives more now than ever before

in the history of this country, and the number of those addicted to the use of habitforming narcotics has increased to an alarming degree in the past twenty-three years, all other opinions to the contrary. 

Statistics do not lie.

That the best of motives may have impelled enactment of the Harrison Narcotic

Act is not disputed here. But whatever may have been the guiding impulse, if it was with the best of intentions toward society that it came into being, it still remains just a good intention, and no more, from the standpoint of any good that may have been accomplished. Hell is paved with good intentions, and the worth of a dormant one no matter upon what a foundation of high idealism it may have come into being-can easily be computed.

This act has, however, served a very definite purpose and only too well. It has enriched the vultures who traffic in human souls. It has created a demand for many more of their kind. It has increased the cost of the drug to the victims to such a staggering figure that, in many instances, the most dastardly of crimes have been committed in order to pay the exorbitant demand of the trafficker. But of the welfare

of the man or woman already in the clutches of addiction at the time it became a law; of what was to be done to bring about a lasting cure for them, the Harrison Narcotic Act provides exactly nothing. 

Perhaps this latter phase is, rightly, a social problem, but, at all events, the enormity of the ommission and the frightful. consequences to which it has subsequently led, is borne out by indisputable facts. 

Years ago, before enactment of the Harrison Narcotic Act, there was little or no general connection between crime and narcotic drugs. The reason is obvious. There being no need for an illicit traffic the crime syndicates, or underworld, as you please, were not interested.

Arbitrarily, however, that law gave birth to a whole new crop of criminals. This situation is very comparable to the era of Prohibition, that bloody, harrowing decade of the not too distant past. Where, previously, no transgression had existed, the Volstead Act fostered and brought into being the greatest army of vicious criminals that a horrified and disbelieving world had ever known. And all because of the tremendous fortunes that awaited those who trafficked in a commodity that, overnight, had be-

come unlawful-Liquor. 

Before passage of the Harrison Narcotic 

Act, it cost from five to six dollars an ounce to purchase narcotic drugs at a corner apothecary. But those who now illicitly traffic in narcotics ask anywhere from fifty to two hundred and fifty dollars for an ounce a two weeks' supply for the confirmed addict.

If, because of the Harrison Narcotic Act, it be against the law to be a narcotic drug addict, then the law must be enforced, but, in the name of an Almighty spirit, give these people humane treatment. Do not just lock them up in cells and forget them. If they have committed crimes, of course they must pay their debt to society, but first treat them for their narcotic addiction so that their eventual rehabilitation may be complete.

Narcotic drugs are, it goes without saying, essential to addicts. To estrange the majority of these - especially those who have contracted the habit because of no willful fault-from their crying need, without making provision for the proper humane treatment is, in effect, equivalent to murder. It is, indeed, far more cruel, for murder is usually swift. It is not, or at least should

not, be a violation of any natural law to alleviate pain. The Harrison Narcotic Act, however, is not a natural law. 

In this day and age when there are so many opportunities for better health and better hospitalization, plus pleas for laws providing for the protection of public health and safety, what a pity it is that there is not one really intelligent move afoot to assist the great army of drug slaves in this country. 

As has been recorded previously, a jail term or prison sentence is not a cure for narcotic addiction. This "lock-them-in-acell" procedure, where they cannot secure drugs, and treating them with a dose of salts or putting them on a skim milk diet, is cruel and inhuman. Such does not constitute a cure, no matter how long they may be incarcerated. Under such treatment if the bedeviled sufferer even survives he is lucky.

In most of our jails and prisons if any treatment at all is given it is usually the Hyocine treatment. The patients are kept completely under the influence of Hyocine for about sixty hours-practically knocked out cold-after which they become desperately sick and limp with weakness, suffer-

ing from a nervousness bordering upon complete collapse and their vision distorted by the treatment. In this pitiable state they are then pronounced cured. Indeed! As a matter of fact and truth, it takes many, many weeks of convalescing with expert medical care, after a Hyocine treatment, before the patient has become anywhere near a state bordering on normalcy, let alone being cured. And the pitiful part of the jail treatment is that many judges really believe that addicts are actually given beneficial care and are cured. Think it over. 

The educated men of this country, presumably, are our legislators. They are the most learned; peers of the erudite. They were elected to office in this belief, we hope. It was they who legislated the drug problem, but they did not solve it nor did they attempt to. Solution was left to the police of the nation. And the police did the only thing in their power to do. They locked the addict in a steel-ribbed cell. This process was supposed to cure the addicta sufferer of real physical illness-but it didn't.

In this action may be discerned the brilliant wisdom of our learned law-makers, so anxious each and every one of them to make

a showing; any kind of a showing. Their footsteps are illustrious on the gleaming sands of this, our modern age; our present civilization. But it isn't necessary nor would it be charitable to criticize them here. Their own deeds do that.

CHAPTER IV 

It is not sufficient that we should carry the triple burden of Morphine, Heroin and

Cocaine addiction, with their attendant evils, plus widespread ignorance of the subject and a general lack of interest other than in official circles, but now a new Gorgon rears its head above the horizon of our national life to add its sinister threat to a condition that is already far out of bounds. 

In recent years our Garden of Youth has become over-run with weeds, the most loathsome and deadly of all parasite growths -Marijuana. 

Here indeed, according to H. J. Anslinger,

United States Commissioner of Narcotics, is the unknown quantity among drugs. No one can predict its effect. No one knows, when he places a Marijuana cigarette to his lips, whether he will become a philosopher, a joyous reveler in a musical heaven, a mad insensate, or a murderer.

That youth has been selected by the peddlers of this most recent and fatal poison "delight," as an especially fertile field, makes it a problem of serious concern to 51

every man and woman in America, according to Mr. Anslinger.

Marijuana, frequently called Indian Hemp, but more specifically referred to as the Assassin of Youth, is a poisonous weed belonging to the hemp family. From the dried, unfertilized tops of the female plant is extracted a soft brown resin which contains a dangerous narcotic principla called 

Cannabinol. Marijuana is also known by many other names, such as Cannabis Indica, Cannibas Sativa, Cannabis Americanus, Hashish, Loco Weed and the Killer Drug, just to mention a few.

From time immemorial Marijuana has been extensively grown and used in many countries of Asia and Africa. It is the oldest known narcotic drug in Turkey. In India the use of this drug for its narcotic effect is based upon long tradition that has been traced back as far as Vedas. 

It is cultivated and grown wild on practically every continent of the globe. The strength of the narcotic content of this plant depends largely upon the climate and altitude of the region where it grows, as well as upon the extent of the cultivation. 

The plants grown at suitable altitudes in certain warm climates of India, Turkey,

Africa and Southern United States have a much more powerful narcotic content than those in more temperate regions.

Indian Hemp has long been used in the United States for many legitimate purposes. 

The fibre of the plant has been utilized to make rope, twine, cloth and hats. The seed is often part of the commercial seed sold for pet birds. From the seed of the plant, also, a drying oil has been obtained for use in the arts and as a commercial substitute for linseed oil. A resin contained in the dried flowering tops of the pistillate plant is widely used in pharmaceutical preparations.

Marijuana is not indigenous to the United States, but has been found growing in almost every state of the Union. In some states it has been cultivated for commercial purposes only. In several the seeds have been clandestinely planted by Mexican laborers as many Mexicans are Marijuana addicts. Its appearance in this country now represents a terrible invasion.

So rapidly has it taken roots in the United States that in nearly every state it can be found growing wild as a roadside weed. It is easily distinguishable by its seven-bladed, saw-tooth leaves. It grows in stalks from 1

three to seven feet high. The wild and cultivated plants appear to flourish most prolifically in those states bordering on the Gulf and Republic of Mexico, where for many years it has been illicitly grown and extensively used, and in Mississippi, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Kansas and Colorado.

The use of Marijuana is increasing among the youthful cigarette and pipe smokers of our large cities. The domestic supply has become so abundant and cheap because the plant grows wild and likewise because it is secretly cultivated in so many different parts of our nation, that it is now assumed that the total quantities of this drug clandestinely introduced into the United States from foreign countries is negligible. 

Vast areas of the plant were found growing in the vicinity of Denver and Colorado 

Springs, Colo.; Atlanta, Ga.; Jacksonville,

Fla., and Tulsa, Okla. In the state of New 

York alone, the State Supervisor of Narcotic 

Control estimates that almost two hundred tons of the plant were destroyed in a recent confiscation; most of this being found near 

New York City, Rochester, Buffalo and in

Erie county. In California the plant was actually found growing in the yard of San 

Quentin prison; six acres were discovered

as a cover crop in a citrus orchard in Orange county; enough Marijuana to make about twenty-five thousand dollars worth of cigarettes was seized by agents in a raid on a plantation near Azusa and, in San Joaquin Valley alone, about four thousand pounds of the growing plant were destroyed. 

Addicts to Marijuana use it in numerous different ways in addition to cigarette smoking, pipe smoking and chewing. Some mix it with beer and other alcoholic beverages for its stimulating properties. It is frequently mixed with honey and sugar and used as a confection. The most prevalent method in the United States and several other countries is in the form of cigarettes mixed with tobacco. These cigarettes are known by various names such as "Muggles," "Reefers," "Mary Warner," "Moota" and "Mooters." Smoked in pipes it is generally referred to as "Kiff," or by its more respectable name "Indian Hemp." Marijuana cigarettes are usually sold for between fifteen and twenty-five cents each. 

Marijuana is the most virile and powerful stimulant. The psychological effect of this drug produces a peculiar psychic exaltation and derangement of the central nervous system. This stage of exaltation and confu-

sion, more marked in some addicts than in others, is generally followed by one of depression.

Sometimes the subject passes into a semiconscious state, experiencing vivid and extravagant dreams which vary according to the individual's character and mentality. In some the stage is one of self-satisfaction and well-being. In others, it is alarming, presenting the fear of some imminent and indefinite danger of impending death. Later the dreams are sometimes followed by a state of complete unconsciousness. Sometimes convulsive attacks and acute mania are developed.

The narcotic content in Marijuana decreases the rate of heart beat and causes irregularity of the pulse. Death may even result from this effect upon the heart. 

Prolonged use of Marijuana frequently develops a delerious rage which sometimes gives man the lust to kill, unreasonably and without motive, and leads to high crimes such as assault, rape, robbery and murder.

Hence Marijuana has been called the "killer drug." The habitual use of this narcotic poison likewise always causes a very marked mental deterioration and sometimes produces insanity. On this account, Marijuana

is frequently referred to as the "loco weed" -loco being Spanish for crazy.

While the Marijuana habit leads to physical wreckage and mental decay, its effect upon character and morality are even more devastating. The victim will frequently undergo such moral degeneracy that he will lie and steal without scruple; he will commit acts that, in the light of returning reason, causes him to hide his face in horror and shame and even indulge in greater excesses in an effort to forget. He becomes utterly untrustworthy and often drifts into the underworld where, with his degenerate companions, commits high crimes and misdemeanors.

The Marijuana vice in the United States is no longer confined to our Mexican population and to the regions of the south and southwest. It has extended into the middle west, the southeast and the northeast. 

Luckily, though for no clear reason, the northwestern part of our country has so far remained singularly free from its contaminating influence.

It is especially serious in this country, too, because it is being carried as a new habit to circles which heretofore were not contaminated. It is making dangerous inroads

in our large cities among the youth, so many of whom are already cigarette smokers and, therefore, easy victims to a new "kick and thrill" habit.

Police officials in some of our large cities report that so many crimes are traced to the use of Marijuana, and its ensuing addiction, that this vice has become a major law enforcement problem.

A recent investigation covering widely separated communities revealed that, in 

New Orleans, La., out of a group of four hundred and fifty persons there were one hundred and twenty-five Marijuana addicts, all of whom were young people ranging in age from between 18 and 31; out of thirtyseven murderers, seventeen were Marijuana addicts, and out of one hundred and ninetythree persons convicted of important thefts, thirty-six were similarly addicted. Also, about one out of every four persons arrested on a miscellany of charges were Marijuana addicts. Other convictions were one hundred and thirty-five in California, seventyone in Pennsylvania, sixty in Ohio and fifty in Texas.

In the State of Florida, there have been many Marijuana victims. For a long time this state had no effective law against the

menace. But recently Florida enacted the

Uniform Narcotic Act, including the Marijuana control provision, as recommended by the Federal Bureau of Narcotics. Within a month after the passage of this act twentytwo arrests were made in the city of Tampa. 

And shortly thereafter the state, as a whole, reported forty-one convictions, the seizure of 1,770 Marijuana cigarettes and fifty pounds of bulk Marijuana.

Marijuana peddlers and addicts are not hampered by the necessity of smuggling the weed into this country because so much of it is clandestinely cultivated here and so much grows wild. Hence there are no evidences of very extensive smuggling, although small amounts are known to have been smuggled between Mexico and the

United States and from the United States into Canada.

Our federal government has no law against the production and use of this plant in our country. It is maintained that federal legislation on the subject is restricted by constitutional limitations. Therefore, the legal fight against Marijuana in the United

States is largely a problem of state and municipal legislation, and each state and large city should have strong, uniform statutes,

efficiently enforced, against this growing menace.

A few states still have no laws controlling the production, possession and use of Marijuana. Some states and the territories of 

Hawaii and Puerto Rico have enacted legislation to control the traffic of this drug. 

However, in many cases, these laws are far from being uniform and there is laxity and inefficiency in their enforcement. One state enacted laws against Marijuana, but made no provision or appropriation for enforcement.

Some states have adopted the Uniform 

State Narcotic Drug Act, like Florida, but failed to include the Marijuana provision.

This was evidently an oversight in some instances. In the future, those states adopting the Uniform Act, should make it a point to include the Marijuana provision as recom mended by the Federal Bureau of Narcotics. 

In other states their larger cities have passed ordinances against this drug. Among these may be mentioned Minneapolis and 

St. Paul, Minn.; Kansas City, Mo.; Lexington, Covington and Newport, Kentucky. 

Ignorance concerning the Marijuana evil is found among all classes of our population

and is surprisingly prevalent among our educated citizens, high school, college and university graduates and even among leaders in our social, business, political and professional world. This ignorance concerning 

Marijuana prevails not only among our youth and the public at large, but in some districts even local enforcement agents are almost as ignorant as the general public on the subject.

Many informants who report the sale and use of Marijuana to federal officers appear to be surprised and amazed when told that federal officials have no authority for prosecuting such cases. They apparently do not know that there is no federal law against the cultivation, sale and use of Marijuana.

Many citizens do not even know whether their own state or their own city has any law concerning the weed.

As the federal government has now listed Marijuana as a narcotic, cigarettes manufactured from the weed are, of course, sold illicitly. Hence an avenue is opened that will enable those so inclined, or so induced, to purchase other narcotics from the same source and Marijuana thereby becomes but a stepping stone that may lead down the path to eventual addiction in one form or

another. They are sold cheaply, thus placing them within the grasp of school children, who, comparatively speaking, are the principal users in this country. 

In commenting upon the Marijuana situation not long ago, Mr. Anslinger said: "A Chicago mother, watching her daughter die as an indirect result of Marijuana addiction, told officers that at least fifty of the girl's young friends were slaves of the narcotic. This means fifty unpredictables. They may cease its use, for it is not so difficult in the case of Marijuana as with some narcotics. They may continue addiction until they deteriorate mentally and become insane. Or they may turn to violent forms of crime, to suicide or to murder.

Marijuana gives few warnings of what it intends to do to the human brain. "Unhappily so far Marijuana has won by many lengths. The years of 1935 and 1936 saw its most rapid growth in traffic. But at least we know what we are facing. We know its history, its effects, and its potential victims. Perhaps with the spread of this knowledge the public may be aroused sufficiently to conquer the menace. "It began with the whispering vendors of

the southwest who said Marijuana would perform miracles for those who smoked it, giving them a feeling of physical strength and mental power, stimulation of the imagination, the ability to be 'the life of the party'. The peddlers preached also of the weed's capabilities as a 'love potion'. Youth, always adventurous, began to look into the claims and found some of them true, not knowing that this was only half of the story. 

They were not told that addicts may often develop a delirious rage during which they are temporarily insane; that this insanity may take the form of a desire for self-destruction or a persecution complex to be satisfied only by the commission of some heinous crime. "It is this useless destruction of youth which is so heart-breaking to all of us who labor in the field of narcotic suppression. 

No one can predict what may happen after the smoking of the weed."

In view of the afore-mentioned conditions we are forced to the conclusion that this menace in the United States has become serious, acute and urgent. What, then, are the best measures for fighting the evil? 

1. Constructive legislation and efficient law enforcement.

2. Education. 

What should be done in the field of legislation? (a) Those states having no law on the subject should enact legislation controlling the production, sale, possession and use of Marijuana, as recommended by our 

Federal Bureau of Narcotics; also provide for efficient enforcement. (b) Large cities in those states which have no laws on the subject should, without delay, enact effective city ordinances against this menace. It is very important for them to act at once because it generally takes a long time to get state legislatures to act and it will be a long time before many of them assemble. In the meantime this vice will continue to spread in the large cities unless appropriate city ordinances are enacted. (c) Those states that have already enacted laws on the subject should, where necessary, see that they conform to the uniform state measure suggested by the 

Federal Bureau of Narcotics. 

Our state laws controlling all dangerous, habit-forming narcotics should be uniform and adequate.

The intelligent enactment and enforcement of laws depends largely upon education. How can lawyers put through adequate laws on the subject unless they have a reasonable understanding of this evil and, without this knowledge, how can enforcement agents efficiently enforce the law?

What, then, should be the objective of education against the Marijuana evil? (a) To present sane and authentic information on the subject of Indian Hemp. (b) To impress our youth and the public at large with the reality and seriousness of this menace. (c) To fortify the youth and the public at large so that they will intelligently avoid contact with this dangerous drug. (d) To so educate our youth and every adult citizen that they will join the crusade to demand and get adequate laws and efficient law enforcement against the 

Marijuana menace.

Herein lies the responsibility of an entire nation as represented, not alone by law enforcement officials, civic bodies or any restricted group, but by every individual citizen in the land. 

The time to act is now!

CHAPTER V 

Education, and education alone, is the most fundamental, the most constructive and the most powerful force for the suppression of the narcotic drug menace. 

No law is perfect and no law enforcement is above reproach. When law enforcement does become lax, and that is frequent, the illicit drug traffic increases its vicious activities. However, our unaided youth and unaided citizens will refuse to have anything to do with or use narcotic or habit forming drugs of any kind, providing they are efficiently educated against the evil consequences that follows swiftly in the footsteps of such an addiction.

Some progress has been made in the field of anti-narcotic education as evidenced by the many requests for narcotic literature, especially from libraries of schools and colleges, received by Federal Bureau of Narcotics and other agencies interested in this most important activity. Teachers, legislatures, clubs (especially women's clubs) and philanthropic organizations are giving more attention to serious study of this problem. 66

The Federal Bureau of Narcotics contin ues to receive and to answer, during the year, numerous inquiries from various organizations and private citizens, relative to all phases of narcotic law enforcement. The 

Commissioner of Narcotics, personally, and by representatives detailed by him, continues to make addresses in increasing numbers before associations and organizations, upon the federal system of narcotic control and international action taken to render this system more effective. A number of addresses, likewise, have been and are continuing to be made, to graduating classes of pharmacy schools to outline the application of narcotic laws to the practice of pharmacy. 

Special representatives of the Commissioner are appearing before state officials, pharmacy and medical association officers, and civic and patriotic organizations to instruct in, and urge the enactment of, the proposed Uniform State Narcotic Law as an instrument to carry out the policy of complete compensation between state and federal enforcement agencies in attacking the narcotic problem.

Articles dealing with the subject are being prepared in the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, at the request of a number of organ-

izations for publication, by these agencies, in the magazines and newspapers. An intelligent and sympathetic public interest, helpful to the administration of the narcotic laws, has been aroused and maintained. 

In 1931 the Federal Bureau of Narcotics established a correspondence school for the special instruction of federal officers engaged in the enforcement of the narcotic laws, which achieved instant success. The course embraces instruction upon the provisions of the Constitution and federal laws relating to the control of the narcotic drug traffic. Remarkable interest has been displayed by the agents and inspectors who are studying and submitting quiz papers on these lessons.

In addition to all this, measures have been instituted by the Treasury department for the acquisition of sites for United States narcotic farms to augment those already in operation, the first two of these communities having been established at Lexington, Kentucky, and Fort Worth, Texas. 

That there is, throughout the nation, too, a general trend toward "looking into this drug matter" is evidenced by such headlines from the daily press as: "Governor Horner

Calls on Illinois to Fight Narcotic Evils,"

"Marijuana Test Kills Mouse As 100 

Watch," "Ask Long-Term 'Dope' Cure For 

Store Thieves," "Canada Acts On Marijuana," "Ministers' Association Attacks Drug

Menace," "Civic Leaders Fight to Clean Up 

Champaign" (seat of the University of Illinois where recent raids uncovered many Marijuana flats and unbelievable vice conditions), and many others of similar nature. 

However, in spite of these varied moves in the right direction, there still remains an appalling ignorance of and indifference toward the narcotic menace; some people not even knowing what action their own states have taken on the Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act, nor on legislation against the growing menace of Marijuana. There still is a great lack of adequate, accurate, scientific fact on dangerous habit-forming narcotic drugs in school text books. 

And it is in our educational institutions that eternal vigilance is necessary, if narcotic drugs are to be kept out of the lives of our growing generations. 

No youth would desire to play with a rattlesnake or cobra, nor would so much as dream of permitting these reptiles to make a "strike" at him after learning of their poisonous fangs. No one knowingly plays with

poison ivy or oak, the Gila monster or the terrible tarantula. Narcotic drugs, and Marijuana, especially, are so comparatively new to our civilization that the race has not yet learned to fear them as it does poisonous reptiles. The attitude seems to be, narcotic drugs look harmless, so why fear them?

But when youth has been taught that it is really much safer to play with rattlesnakes, if it must encourage hazardous playmates, than with narcotics, then youth will shun narcotic drugs with the wholesome enthusiasm of horror that is now manifested toward a rattler.

Important issues, concerning this subject, that must be given prompt and intelligent attention, according to a recent summary by the World Narcotic Defense Association, are: 

1. Thorough narcotic law enforcement, including urgent need for prompt, reasonable and efficient action on the part of state courts in the trials of narcotic law violators. 

2. The urgent necessity for the enactment of the Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act by those states which have not already adopted same.

3. The inclusion of the provision against 

Marijuana in the Uniform Act in order to make uniform and effective the legislation against the growing use of this drug; also, a concerted drive for its suppression. 

4. The need of an intense campaign against the crime wave and the illicit drug traffic by both federal and state governments, co-operating with each other. 

5. Schools, colleges and universities to provide more thorough instruction concerning dangerous habit-forming narcotic drugs, and the incorporating in the school text books of ample and accurate scientific facts in regard to these drugs. 

6. Necessity for the public at large to study the narcotic problem, especially the consequences of drug addiction, and to co-operate in the fight for the suppression of this menace. 

7. Each state to establish a narcotic institution and farm for segregation and rehabilitation of its drug addicts, with provisions made for women addicts, as well as for the separate housing of criminal and non-criminal addicts-all rehabilitation to have careful follow-up and after cure programs.

8. Necessity for nations already parties to the Geneva Convention of 1931 to honestly and efficiently enforce those obligations and for other nations to ratify same without delay.

In connection with this educational plan, a course of visual instruction should be given, at least in our junior high schools. 

Enlighten the students in regard to Opium and its many derivatives. Explain to them, intelligently, facts concerning the planting and cultivation of the Coca leaves, and the deadly Marijuana. Go even further and show them pictures of victims before and after the dreadful toll of addiction has been taken. A harrowing, unpleasant experience, perhaps, but worthy of the subject and their own safety.

The state of California, some years ago, made narcotic drug education compulsory in its public schools, and every other state in the Union would do well to follow this example.

Even in far away, strife-torn China, always synonomous with the demon poppy and Opium smoking, greater efforts on the part of the whole nation are being taken-and some frightfully drastic ones—to successfully combat this evil and stamp out drug

addiction within its borders, a well nigh impossible task. But the sincerity of the effort can never be doubted.

Narcotic conditions in China and the Far East, naturally, continue to be serious and acute. The springing up of so many clandestine factories for the manufacture of Opium and all its derivatives, in those regions, has become a menace not only to China, but to all the world. Nevertheless, the Chinese government reports that it has inaugurated drastic measures to meet this serious emergency. They are, as follows: (1) Rigid laws against the manufacture, sale and use of high-powered narcotic drugs, the penalty for violation of which may be death, and this penalty has, likewise, been extensively inflicted for the illicit manufacture, illicit sale, illict transport and for addicts who have relapsed back to the habit the third time after being cured of their vice. (2) Measures have been adopted for the suppression of Opium production, immediate prohibition of the cultivation of the poppy plant in the interior provinces and gradual reduction of this industry in the border provinces so that, within a decade, the suppression will be complete.

(3) Steps have been taken for an efficient, though humane, cure for those suffering from addiction; all drug addicts being required to register and take the cure; addicts relapsing to their vice to receive severe punishment; numerous hospitals for the treatment of drug addicts to be established throughout the empire. (It is reported that one thousand of these rehabilitation institutions are already in operation and that hundreds of thousands of Opium smokers and narcotic addicts. have been cured.) (4) Educational measures against drug addiction; use of schools, the screen, the lecture platform and distribution of antinarcotic literature.

A noble program this, but one that can be successful only if China is able to restore a stable government, which the world seems confident that it will under the guidance of its youthful Generalissimo Chiang Kai Shek and the cultural influence of his beautiful wife.

In addition to the intensive educational program that must be inaugurated, overstress cannot be placed on the great need for more vigorous action of the public generally, to combat this evil, as well as the

equally important fact that the state and federal narcotic laws should be harmonized in order to bring about closer co-operation between state and federal law enforcement officials. 

The elaborate government survey "Traffic in Narcotic Drugs" concludes with this wise counsel: "It is recommended that educational campaigns be instituted in all parts of the United States for the purpose of informing the peoples of this country, including the medical profession, of the seriousness of narcotic drug addiction and its extent in the United States, and thereby secure their aid and co-operation in its suppression."

For many years the United States Federal Bureau of Narcotics, under the very able directorship of Mr. Harry J. Anslinger, has waged a splendid uphill warfare against the narcotic drug menace. It has kept up a relentless fight against the illicit drug traffic. It has also displayed a marked efficiency in formulating a campaign for the enactment of a Uniform State Drug Act. 

Although not charged with the enforcement of Marijuana laws, it has carried on an active campaign to influence state legislatures to enact adequate laws affecting this evil.

It has furnished effective aid to the various states in educating enforcement agents and state and city chemists concerning Indian Hemp.

But our federal bureau cannot win the fight against narcotic drug addiction and the illicit traffic alone. It is incumbent upon all good citizens, educators, legislators, philanthropic associations, clubs, religious and civic organizations to join in this fight for suppression of a vice which is spreading with an incalculable swiftness and has become such a blight to the United States and the entire North American continent.

There is an old saying that "Speech is silver, but silence is gold."

Adages, undoubtedly, have their place in the scheme of things and serve their commendable purposes, but, according to the 

Funk & Wagnall's standard, unabridged dictionary, "silence" is defined: "To hide; secrecy; absence from or refraining from mention; to take away the authority to speak or the power to answer.'

No definition could more eloquently describe the true situation with regard ot the subject at hand, than does that of Silence. 

Narcotic addiction and the illicit traffic in habit forming drugs flourishes under the cloak of an appalling secrecy; a silence that is truly golden from the fact that untold millions annually find their way into the coffers of the "rings" or "syndicates" or "interests" that control the Hellish blight and a silence that, in this instance, amounts to little short of being a monstrous conspiracy. 

Just why have we remained silent so long? 77

Why has narcotic addiction, from the standpoint of widespread discussion, been placed in the Tabu category? Did we deliberately enter into this intrigue or are we, ourselves, afraid?

How and in what manner was this conspiracy started? And by whom? Just why has the matter of narcotic drugs-DOPEalways, for the most part, been a “Shhh, don't mention that in front of my children' subject? No! Of what are parents apprehensive? What do they fear? Is it a virtue to keep children in ignorance? Would parents rather have them find out these things for themselves, for find out they may, and in a manner disastrous beyond description. 

Have the children, too, entered into this conspiracy? Once initiated into the realm of addiction, it is turn about: "Shhh, don't say anything about this in front of mom and dad; they wouldn't understand."

Poor little ignorant fools, kept in bondage through fear. Fear of what? The wrath of the parents; of having their secret found out? Better that than a lifetime of misery. 

But parents must understand. 

Have you, reader, if you are a parent, completely won the confidence of your child?

Are you a parent to whom your child can confide? If not, you have failed in your mission. Parenthood should not be yours. 

If you do not forewarn your children of this evil, do not be surprised should they fall easy victims to the lure the prey of narcotic drug addiction. If you have locked the door of your heart against their confidence, to whom shall they appeal in their hour of need? They will, of necessity, become someone else's problem. Whose?

It seems that the Gods, too, have conspired to keep secret, to throw the veil of mystery over the Demon Dope, for now we come to another side of the conspiracy. 

To the poor unfortunate victims of this menace, so little hope has been given that they again may find their places in the sun. 

No one knows what to do for them, or how to go about it. Why?

Only our physicians and surgeons and registered pharmacists have have the the right to possess, use or sell narcotic drugs, according to the Harrison Narcotic Act. 

Only our medical men have the right to treat narcotic addiction, according to the law. Surely there are narcotic treatments available. But where? Do you know?

Just what would you do if a beloved son or daughter suddenly confessed to you that he or she was addicted to narcotic drugs?

The family physician would undoubtedly be summoned and, lacking a better solution, would probably advise the child be sent to one of the state institutions. Would you want to send a son, or daughter, to such a place? Hardly. But where would you send them? What would you do?

It is considered most unethical by the medical profession for any reputable doctor to "advertise" any system of treatment, irregardless of the malady it might apply to. 

How, then, will the world ever know if any physician has a successful treatment for narcotic addiction? Unless the truth can be made known through some channels of advertising, thoroughly investigated, of course, to determine their legitimacy, where victims can be treated, what will these poor souls do? Where can they hope to find help?

Are the medical men, too, a part of this conspiracy?

There are so many people who become quite offended when one dares to speak of the narcotic subject openly, just as a few years back, the matter of syphilis was never a subject of genteel conversation, much less

the word itself being mentioned except in hushed whispers. The possibility that it would ever be smeared across the front pages of the newspapers, as it has of late, would have been ridiculed. It is the same the world over; people are not interested in the thing that is not their immediate concern. But, terrible as it is and loathesome to contemplate, syphilis might be considered in the light of a mild affliction as compared to the ravages of narcotic drugs. Science knows, at least, that syphilis can definitely be cured.

Can it be possible that any parents believe that by not mentioning this subject, they can avoid it; that through their silence their children will be saved? If they do they are more to be pitied than censured. If you have ignored this narcotic problem so far; if you, too, are one of the many who never wanted the subject mentioned in your presence, heed a warning now before it is too late. Narcotic drugs are no respecter of persons. Someone dear to you may be the very next victim.

Our children can, in a measure, be just what we want them to be. It is within our power to prepare them for the kind of a life that we want them to live. There never

was a greater mistake than this idea of keeping children in darkness.

It is safe to assume that most parents are devoted to their offspring, that they live for them. Then tell them the truth, the whole truth, about narcotic addiction for, sometime, somewhere, your child may be tempted. If you are not familiar with the subject there are agencies only too willing to help and assist, if you will but call upon them.

If we would save our children from this evil, then throw off the cloak of ignorance. 

Let us make a vigorous war on Dope. Let us take this problem out of the Tabu category and bring it right out into the open, into the light of day, where all may more fully understand.

Too long have we been inert and listless. 

Too long have we been silent. Let us face this thing openly in our schools, in our pulpits, in our theatres. 

If, in far-away China, drastic steps have been taken to suppress that which bids fair to destroy a great nation, surely such measures can be taken here. Of course, our highly specialized brand of civilized life would forbade us going to the extreme of executing a narcotic addict for relapsing
