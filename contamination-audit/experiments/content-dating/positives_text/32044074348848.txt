Kamehameha Statue 

In 1877 the Legislature of Hawaii appropriated $10,000 for a statue of Kamehameha I. The contract was awarded an American sculptor, Gould of Boston, and it was fashioned in Italy. The vessel carrying the statue from Europe was wrecked off the Falkland Islands and a replica was ordered and safely delivered where it was placed opposite the Palace Grounds. The original was salvaged and shipped to Kohala, Hawaii, where it now stands.

The Hawaiian Islands are called the "Paradise of the Pacific." Mark Twain has aptly described them as, "the loveliest fleet of islands anchored in any ocean." While these islands are in the tropics, the soothing trade winds keep them refreshingly cool and comfortable. The temperature ranges between 56 and 88 degrees throughout the year as recorded by the U. S. Weather Bureau. seldom goes over 86 degrees with an average ideal mean temperature of about 74.5 degrees. Add to this wonderful climate, the bountiful array of beautiful flowers, the gorgeous sunsets, the many perfect rainbows, both solar and lunar, and the rare jade shores skirting the many well kept beaches, and it can be easily understood just why Hawaii has attracted tourists by the thousands. 

Anchored in mid-Pacific, the Hawaiian Islands are just about 2,100 miles southwest of San Francisco, between 

155 and 160 degrees west longitude and between 19 and 

22 degrees north latitude. Mail and passenger service is made by Clipper to and from the mainland in about 18 hours while the regular ships make the voyage in from four to six days. Passengers can leave Honolulu by Clipper at noon and arrive in San Francisco the following morning.

It can be readily seen that these beautiful islands are "not so far distant" from the mainland in this day of marvelous transportation.

Hawaii, the island of active volcanoes, is the largest of the group from which they are named. The main port of call is Honolulu, a city of about 153,073 and located on the island of Oahu. Here the naval and military personnel are garrisoned and the industries have their main offices. 

Here also is the famous Waikiki Beach which attracts tourists from all over the world and swimming can be enjoyed the year round. Molokai is known as the Friendly 

Island where the large leper colony is maintained. Kauai, known as the Garden Island boasts a miniature Grand Canyon. The other islands are rich in vegetation and produce an abundance of pineapples and sugar cane.

Discovery of the Islands 

Captain James Cook, a brilliant and bold English sea captain, sailing under his patron, the Fourth Earl of Sandwich, had mapped the mouth of the St. Lawrence River, when he turned southward, rounded Cape Horn, then touched at the Society Islands and Tahiti. He then sailed northward and on January 18, 1778, sighted Oahu of the 

Hawaiian group.

He called these the Sandwich Islands in honor of his patron.

Captain Cook landed at Waimea, Kauai, and after taking on provisions, he left a few seeds, a goat and ewe at Niihau, a small island nearby, then cruised northward. 

On this northward trip he probably sighted Molokai February 2, 1778. After spending a few months in the northern Pacific, perhaps looking for a water passage around Alaska to the Atlantic side, he sailed south again sighting 

Maui, November 26, 1778. A few days later, November 

30, he sighted Hawaii, landing at Kealakekua Bay December 1, 1778. The natives were enraptured with the large ship which they called a heiau (temple) for they thought the white men gods. They paid Captain Cook the respect and honors due their gods and supplied him with all the provisions he requested. Captain Cook then put to sea, but having trouble with one of his ships he

9 returned. His first stop had been quite a drain on the natives and his return was "not so welcome," however, the people and King did all they couid to supply the visitor's needs.

While taking on water it seems that one of the sailors hit a native over the head. In retaliation a boat was stolen from the ship. Captain Cook went ashore next day intending to decoy the Chief on board to hold as hostage. The 

King was suspicious and refused to go. At the water's edge a native wrenched one of Captain Cook's arms so as to make him cry out with pain. This caused the natives to exclaim that he is not a god, but "like one of us.". Fleeing from the trouble Captain Cook was stabbed in the back and fell face downward in the water and died February 14, 1779. His body was taken to a heiau where the flesh was stripped from the bones and he was given the same honors as a native chief. Later upon demand a part of the bones were returned to the ship and these were given a military funeral in the waters of Kealakekua Bay. 

Other Europeans had probably reached the Hawaiian Islands before Captain Cook. In the time of King Kealiiokaloa, about 1521-1530, a Spanish ship was wrecked on Hawaii. The Hawaiians called it Konalihoa and the only male survivor was Kukanaloa. It was probably one of the ships of Alvaro de Saavedra which was wrecked in that vicinity about that time. The man reached shore and knelt in prayer. The place where he knelt is called 'kulou' meaning 'to kneel,' and the cliff just above where he landed is called Mauna Kapu, or Sacred Mountain. This 

Spaniard married the natives and lived with them until his death. 

On June 20, 1743, Lord George Anson, of the British ship Centurion, captured the Spanish galleon Nuestra Signora de Covadonga, which was commanded by General Don Jeronimo de Montero, a Portuguese in the employ of the Spanish crown. Besides the one and a half million gold bullion, there was also taken a map. On this map, it is claimed by the Spaniards, was the Hawaiian Islands called

Le Mesa, which had been so named by Gaetano. It is said by some that Captain Cook had with him this map when he discovered the Hawaiian Islands in 1778.

Bishop Henry Bond Restarick, who spent many years. in Hawaii, at one time voiced the opinion that there might be some credence to the discovery of the islands by Gaetano. In 1927 he issued the following statement: "Two years ago I accepted the idea that the Spaniards discovered Hawaii, and I voiced that theory in a lecture which I delivered at the University of Hawaii. I with many others, had accepted the statements made in books, but when I began to search for evidence, I found that there were no historical facts to sustain the assertions made. 

All was based on theory, an anonymous chart, an anonymous note, an impossible date, and surmises based on these." Summing up he said, "It was Cook who discovered Hawaii and made these islands known to the world."

Origin of the Hawaiian People 

Rev. William Ellis, who had spent some time in Tahiti, arrived in Hawaii in 1822. He immediately began to trace the geneology of the peoples and came to the conclusion that there had been three distinct arrivals of Europeans before that of Captain Cook. The first was the arrival of the priest Paao who landed at Kohala. All priests in the islands traced their geneologies to Paao when Ellis arrived. The second arrival was that of Opiri, the son of 

Paao. He with his followers went to the mountains and there took up their abode. The natives did not know whether they were human or gods. The King had an interview with them and they departed. It seems that the principal party of this group was called Manahini, which accounts for all strangers in Hawaii being called Malihini. 

The third arrival was in the time of King Kahoukapu of Kaawaloa. At this time seven foreigners arrived, dressed in white, and sailing a painted boat. One wore a pahi (a knife or sword,) and had a feather headgear. They remained in the islands, married natives and became

It is a generally accepted fact that the Hawaiians are of the Polynesian race and migrated to the Hawaiian Islands by way of Samoa and the Society Islands or Tahiti sometime early in the Christian era. Some have tried to connect them with the Aryans, the Egyptians, the Persians or the Jews. Their religious practices relate them closely with the Jewish race. The Hawaiians established cities of refuge, practiced circumcision, had certain tabus respecting the burying of the dead, observed certain regulations respecting the periodical infirmities of females and their being placed apart for seven days after childbirth, purified their temples with salt while the priests observed certain rites similar to the practices of the Jews. Professor 

W. D. Alexander, for many years a notable historian of 

Hawaii, suggested the possibility of a Persian origin. Dr. 

J. McMillan Brown of Australia, scouts the idea of people sailing east from Asia when all the tides, currents and winds would be adverse to an eastward journey in the frail craft of the day. However, it is possible that they could have drifted southward, thence northward to the mid-Pacific.

There is another theory which has been seriously considered by many scholars. Dr. Brown is one who suggested that perhaps Easter Island was the acropolis for all Polynesia. He contends that it might be a part of a lost continent as shown in his 'Riddle of the Pacific.' (1924) As late as 1920, Dr. William Alanson Bryan, a member of the staff of the Bishop Museum in Honolulu, and on the faculty of the University of Hawaii, set forth upon an expedition to the south seas to prove his theory of a former continent, now mostly submerged, stretching down the Pacific. This theory supports the legend of the Hawaiians that they are a part of a lost continent. 

If you should ask the Hawaiian of old from whence they come, they will answer, "Mai ka po mai, mai lewa mai, makou," (We come from the night, from the moving space.) In substance this means that they are children

HAWAIIAN NUGGETS 

of God. One of their legends also indicates their close relationship to the children of the first creation. In the sonorous and grandly poetic geneological account called 

Kumulipo-'kumu' meaning foundation, root, and 'lipo,' meaning from the depth of the sea, blackness, cavern, it is related that the very first person was a female child called Lailai. It is said that she sprang from the night and from her came the Hawaiian race. The first male was Kealiiwahilani who came from Heaven. When he saw the beautiful Lailai who was living at Lalowaia, he took her to wife and their descendants were the progenitors of the Hawaiians. Their legends also tell of a great flood, all of which closely links them with the life and practices of the early Jewish race. 

Religion 

When Abraham was told to spare his son Isaac on the sacrificial altar, he substituted a ram instead. That incident may have been the end of human sacrifice among the Jews. The Hawaiians were not so rapid in their development and their last recorded human sacrifice was on Punch Bowl in 1810.

The people of Mexico selected their victim for human sacrifice at least a year in advance, from which time he was honored and feasted until the fatal day when he was sacrificed on the cruel stone altar to their gods. The practice of the Hawaiians was somewhat different. Slipping up from the rear, the victim was surprised, struck over the head and killed, then dragged off to the heiau for the sacrifice. During the time of such ceremonies many people fled to the mountains until the danger was over. While human sacrifice is looked upon by this enlightened age as a gruesome practice adverse to any religion, the Hawaiian should not be severely criticised for they were still living in the stone age, while only a short time ago Christian people burned witches in the name of religion.

Kawaiahao Church

Located on King Street, opposite the Municipal Building and near the Palace Grounds, is the "old stone church," or the "Westminster Abbey" of Hawaii. The first church was completed and dedicated September 15, 1821, just a little over a year after the arrival of the first missionaries. This building being built of grass and poles, caught fire and burned in 1824. By command of High Chief

Kalanimoku, a second building was immediately erected with materials contributed by various chiefs. While this building seated 

600, it proved too small and the next year, 1825, the third church was built through the contributions of Queen Kaahumanu and Kalanimoku. Four years later, in 1829, the fourth church was built having a seating capacity of 4000 people. 

The fifth and present church was completed and opened July 21, 1842. While the natives contributed in the building of this church by carrying the coral from the sea to the site of the new buliding on their backs, the cost was some $33,000. In this building services

In the year of 1809, The Rev. Edwin W. Dwight, resident graduate of Yale, was passing one of the main buildings when he heard a sob. Stopping, he learned that it came from Obookiah, a Hawaiian who had shipped to the states after his parents had been killed in one of the district wars of Hawaii. The lad was weeping because there was no one to carry light and religion to his people. Dr. Dwight took the lad to his home where he was taught and then calling in a group of young men told them the story of the young Hawaiian. As a result, five young men met in the shadow of a haystack and pledged themselves to carry religion to Hawaii. The Cornwall School, Connecticut, was established and of the first twelve pupils, seven were Hawaiians. This was the beginning of the Americans' interest in the Hawaiian people.

The first group of missionaries to arrive in Hawaii consisted of seven males, seven females, with four Hawaiians from Cornwall. They arrived April 4, 1820. The Rev. 

Hiram Bingham and others went to Honolulu while Samuel 

Ruggles and Samuel Whitney went to Kauai. Following the first arrival of missionaries, others followed in 1823, 1828, 1831, and 1832. As a result of the faithful work done by those sturdy pioneers, the people of Hawaii, according to a statement of the former delegate from Hawaii, Hon. Victor S. K. Houston, are one hundred percent Christian, despite the fact that like all peoples, they still cling to many of their primitive superstitions. 

The Roman Catholic Church has played an important part in the Christianization of Hawaii. When the 'L'Urana' 

arrived in Honolulu in 1819 under command of M. Freycinet he carried aboard a Chaplain, M. L'Abbe de Quelin. 

This Chaplain perhaps performed the first baptismal service for Hawaiians. The persons so honored were two high chiefs, Kalanimoku and Boki. However, many of the natives did not look upon this as a Christian ceremony, but more as an honor bestowed upon distinguished persons of one country by representatives of another.

It was not until 1831 that the first Roman Catholic missionaries arrived. These were John Alexius and Augustin Bachelot, Frenchmen, and Abraham Armand and

When Kaahumanu, regent with Liholiho or Kamehameha II, learned the nature of the Catholic services, she likened it to idol worship and ordered it discontinued and the priests were banished to California. The next year, 1832, some forty native Catholic converts-men, women, and children-were employed on a coral wall outside Honolulu, and were not allowed to visit the city because they would not renounce the Catholic faith. Other Catholic priests tried to land in Hawaii but were not received until Captain La Place arrived in 1832. He demanded that the prisoners be released and that Catholics be given the same privileges as Protestants. He furthermore demanded a deposit of $20,000 as security which was furnished. As a result Catholic miss.onaries began to arrive in 1840 when on July 9 some 280 candidates were baptized. Two years later the cathedral was dedicated and some eight hundred persons were baptized. Government The feudal system prevailed in Hawaii up to the middle of the nineteenth century. The islands were divided into

HAWAIIAN NUGGETS 

19 several kingdoms each ruled by a king and a retinue of chiefs and court attendants. All land belonged to the king up to the grand Mahele in 1848. Gradually the government was converted into a limited monarchy, then into a republic and finally ceded and annexed to the United 

States as a territory.

In 1782 Kamehameha I fell heir to about one half the island of Hawaii. By clever maneuvering he was able to gain the whole island by 1792. Having thus strengthened his possessions he pushed across the channel in 1794 and in 1795 took Maui, Lanai, and Molokai. In spite of the fact that his very efficient general, Kaina, had transferred his allegiance to the king of Oahu, Kamehameha pushed onward to the western island landing at Waikiki early in 1795. He then pursued the Oahuan army up the Nuuanu valley and by April captured and killed both Kaina, his traitor general, and Kalanikupule, King of Oahu. The

Oahuan army was pursued on up the valley to the Pali and those that were not killed were pushed over the thousand foot precipice to their death below. Having added 

Oahu to his kingdom, Kamehameha waited until 1810 when Kaumualii, King of Kauai, ceded his possessions to 

Kamehameha, thus completing the Hawaiian kingdom under one sovereign, who then became known as the Napoleon of the Pacific. 

Kamehameha was a good and mighty king. He not only made friends of all transients, but was especially anxious to learn the ways of other peoples. He requested Vancouver to send him missionaries. He tried to improve his laws.

One incident of his life shows the character of the mighty king. When Kamehameha was young he went fishing. Leaving the boats he went ashore to capture some nets which he intended to take from some fishermen. 

He caught his foot in a crevice of lava and one of the men whom he had assailed broke a paddle over his head.

Later when Kamehameha became king and the men were apprehended and brought before the King expecting

death, he dismissed them and issued the Mamala Hoe, or law of the splintered paddle which included, "Let the aged men and women and little children lie down in security on the highways." 

When Kamehameha died in 1819 at Kailua, Hawaii,

Liholiho, his son, assumed the throne as Kamehameha II.

While Kaahumanu did not accept Christianity for several years, she assisted the missionaries in many ways.

The President of the United States appoints the Governor and Secretary of the Territory, who must be residents of Hawaii, and they serve for periods of four years.

Industries 

Hawaii first became commercially attractive when European vessels learned that the islands were rich in sandal wood. Immediately large shipments were made to the 

Orient and within a few years the islands were virtually stripped of the precious growth. The Hawaiians received but very little from these shipments, and while they were realizing some $400,000 annually when shipping was at its peak, by 1836 they were getting only about $36,000 a year from their shipments.

Whaling in and around Hawaii became attractive in the early part of the last century. The "Mary," commanded by a Captain Allen, arrived in 1820 and within a short time a large fleet of whalers were common around Honolulu. 

This industry was discontinued within a few years due to the depletion or migration of whales, so the whalers left for other waters.

Hawaii turned her attention to other industries and the records show that they were able to export 197 pounds of silk in 1844, 284 pounds of coffee in 1848, 158 pounds of beef in 1849, 43 barrels of flour in 1855, and then by 1895 shipped 486 cases of canned pineapples. Fishing around

Hawaii has always been good and during recent years they have exported annually some half a million dollars worth of canned tuna. The climatic conditions and soil in the islands, together with the highly specialized scientific process, has made various crops especially profitable.

Sugar holds first place of all Hawaiian industries. With an investment of nearly two hundred million dollars in the industry, and with an annual pay roll of a quarter of a million dollars, Hawaii contributes nearly five percent of the total world's sugar output. The climate and soil, together with scientific farming of the land, enables Hawaii to excel any other country in sugar production per acre. 

There are about forty-one sugar plantations in the Hawaiian Islands employing nearly 55,000 people. The island of 

Kauai produces about 21 percent of the sugar crop, Oahu twenty-five percent, Maui twenty-one percent, and Hawaii thirty-three percent.

The 'lowdown' on the sugar production can best be obtained by a visit to the mill. The mill of the Honolulu 

Plantation Company at Aiea is typical and is generally chosen for three reasons. First, this is the only mill on the islands which refines sugar. Second, it is near Honolulu. 

Third, any stranger will find a warm welcome and interesting information about sugar production by two most patient and congenial gentlemen, Mr. L. E. Phillips, Chief

Chemist, and his assistant, Mr. F. W. Nott. These gentlemen have been gracious enough to supply the information on sugar contained herein.

The Honolulu Plantation Company cultivates about 5,000 acres for sugar production for which they use about 100 million gallons of water daily. The crop is planted about once every nine years, the intervening crops shooting up from the roots after each harvest, and so are called. ratoon crops.

It takes from 13 to 20 months to mature a plant or ratoon crop and these provide a harvest about nine months of the year. The mill operates 24 hours a day with three shifts. The land produces from 7 to 12 tons of sugar per acre and the mill turns out about 200 tons of sugar daily. The record for this mill is 548,000 pounds of sugar in 24 hours.

The Honolulu Plantation Company employs about 1500 men. The common laborer draws an average of slightly over two dollars per day together with house, light and

Two club houses are on this plantation for the benefit of the employees, one for skilled and one for unskilled labor. An excellent welfare director plans for movies, athletics, games, dances, reading material and such things as contribute to the welfare of the families. 

When the cane is ripe, the fields are set afire to burn all dry leaves. It is a common sight to see the light of these fires at night. Workmen begin cutting at daylight. It is loaded on small railroad cars by mammoth grappling cranes and hauled to the mill. It is first run through cut-

HAWAIIAN NUGGETS 

29 ters, then through a series of huge rollers with about 500 tons pressure. There is a crusher composed of two rollers, and four mills of three rollers each making a total of 14 rollers or five separate crushings that the cane goes through. From 96 to 98 percent of the sugar is extracted in this process. The refuse or bagasse is used as fuel and there is sufficient to operate the entire factory. 

The juice is mixed with lime and heated to boiling which clarifies and aids in neutralizing the juice and it is then run into a large clarifier to settle. The acid in the juice will rust out iron pipes in about 18 months so copper pipes are used throughout the mill. After settling, the clear juice is drawn off the top. The sed.ment which remains is run through a special vacuum filter which removes the mud, leaving the clear juice to be put through the same process as the juice originally drawn from the settler. All the juice then goes through six large evaporators which concentrates it down to heavy syrup. 

The syrup is then crystalized into raw or brown sugar which is the first step in making refined sugar. The raw sugar is now melted back to a syrup again in hot water. 

This syrup is then run through large filters of animal bone charcoal which completely decolorizes it. The resulting colorless syrup is then recrystalized into refined sugar. 

The sugar is separated from the remaining syrup by centrifugal machines, which revolve at about 1000 revolutions per minute. The damp sugar when it leaves the centrifugal machines is put through a rotating dryer and is then ready for bagging. The Honolulu Plantation Company manufactures about 30,000 tons of refined sugar from their own cane and about 10,000 tons from raw sugar bought from other factories, making a total yearly output of about 40,000 tons. This then, is disposed of by shipping onethird to the mainland, one third is used for local consumption in the islands, and the other third is used by the pincapple canneries.

The Hawaiian people have many interesting legends connected with their gods. One of these well illustrates

33 the vivid imagination of the natives. 

About three miles from Hauula, up the shadowy glen of Kaliuwaa is Sacred Falls, the best known to malihinis for the legend of Kamapuaa, the great pig god. Here dwelt Kamapuaa, who frequently ventured forth to ravage the countryside in a spleen of anger against his father, Olopana, ruler of the Koolau coast of Oahu. Most of the time Kamapuaa went about as a human, cultivating stiff hair which stood out on his body like a pig's bristles. He was tattooed, and instead of a feather cloak, generally carried a hide of pig's skin as a coat. Gifted with marvelous powers, he could turn himself into the body of a pig with human head and limbs, sometimes the head of a hog with a human form and at times would turn himself into a hog to escape capture.

Kamapuaa's mother was Hina, the beautiful wife of Olopana who disclaimed the fatherhood and who vented his anger on the child, finally casting him from the bosom of the family. Kamapuaa then went to live in the valley of Kaliuwaa from which retreat he carried on his depredations. Olopana sent a large group of warriors to capture him, following him into the gorge where the unscalable walls prevented his escape. Finding a pig they put it on a litter and started down the ravine. The pig grew larger, broke from the litter, gored several warriors to death and escaped.

More outlaws joined Kamapuaa and they terrorized the neighborhood, carrying their depredations to the doors of the enemy. Again the army was sent against the outlaws who followed them to the edge of the precipice when Kamapuaa stretched himself out, throwing himself across the precipice over which his followers escaped to safety. 

Kamapuaa could not contract quickly enough and Olopana's warriors found a large pig which they carried triumphantly home for a feast. During the night Kamapuaa again escaped, rejoining his men in the hills. 

Once more Kamapuaa extended his raids and Olopana swore to make him a sacrifice to the gods. This time Kama-

Kamapuaa had taken with him this time his grandmother who had taught him the wonderful magic. He placed her on his neck, threw himself against the water flow stopping the falls while his warriors climbed his long back on the stiffened bristles to safety on the plateau above. When

Hina, the mother, pleaded for the life of her son in vain. 

Her wiles were more effective with the high priest, who promised to save Kamapuaa's life.

Thinking him dead he turned to leave, whereupon Kamapuaa jumped up, grabbed the sacrificial knife, stabbed the chief to death and escaped. Many thrilling adventures were made in distant parts, one of which was his effort to win the hand of Madam Pele, the volcano goddess. Rain generally falls in spots except at certain periods when rainfall is general. At Waikiki the average rainfall is about 20 inches a year while it mounts to 27 inches in downtown Honolulu. The Hula-The Hawaiian Hula is the world's most expressive dance and is of religious origin. Every movement of the body carries a special meaning as the dancer tries to express his feelings whether of love, joy or sadness, telling of a fishing trip or the death of a great chief. 

The Hawaiians derive great pleasure in telling or watching this expressive dance and this has made them very graceful in execution of the intricate and difficult movements.

Aloha Tower 

Aloha Tower stands at the water front in Honolulu. This adds the finishing touch to the ultra modern concrete piers which replaced the ramshackly wharves of olden days. In the tower is a clock and at the top a beacon light. The tower serves as a pilo house and extends a typical Aloha to all incoming vessels. 

It was occupied in 1926 and is a thing of architectural beauty.

The Hawaiian Language 

When the first American missionaries arrived, they found a people with no written language. They immediately set about the task of putting their language into

HAWAIIAN NUGGETS 

43 writing. Vowels were used extensively and every letter is sounded. Each syllable ends with a vowel. There are only twelve letters used, five vowels and seven consonants. 

The exception to this is the occasional interchange of a t for k, r for 1, v for w, etc. These words with the t, r, v, etc, were probably introduced by the Polynesian group from Tahiti. The vowels have the following values-a as in father, e as in they, i as in machine, o as in blow and u as in blue.
