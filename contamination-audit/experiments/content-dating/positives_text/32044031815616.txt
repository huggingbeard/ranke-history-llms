INTRODUCTION WHAT THIS GUIDE IS 

This is a working draft of a guide to the development of legislation for programs of public child welfare and youth services developed and operated by State and local administrative agencies. Its preparation was undertaken in response to many requests received by the Children's Bureau. Coming from State agencies and others concerned about such programs and about services for delinquent children and youth in particular, these requests voiced the pressing need for help in drafting legislative proposals. 

The guide is the result of research and study, including review of State laws. It was prepared by staff of the Division of Social Services of the Children's Bureau, with assistance from staff of the Division of Juvenile Delinquency Service of the Bureau and of the Office of General 

Counsel of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. 

An Advisory Group which the Children's Bureau called together in October 1953 reviewed the first draft. The latest draft was sent for review to about 130 agencies and individuals. These included members of the Advisory Group; State departments of public welfare; State youth authorities; Federal agencies and national voluntary agencies having programs for or affecting the social well-being of children and families; a number of juvenile court judges, probation officers and superintendents of training schools for delinquent youth; and others. It was also discussed at a regional meeting of representatives of State departments of public welfare in the southeastern States. 

On the basis of the comments received, it seems advisable to issue the document, with only minor revision at this time, as a working draft subject to testing through further group discussion and actual use by individuals and groups engaged in drafting legislation. 

Comments on this document will be welcomed by the Children's Bureau and will be given careful consideration in the preparation for its issuance in final form. 

1/ As distinguished from social services furnished by judicial or law enforcement agencies, or by State and local administrative agencies in connection with programs whose primary purpose is other than the provision of such services.

This guide contains suggestions for content and language that are offered as possible help to those who are planning for the improvement, coordination or unification of existing laws, or for the drafting of new laws. It is hoped that they will be of help especially to State and local agencies, legislative committees, State and community planning groups, and all others interested in improving legislation for State and local programs of public child welfare and youth services. They are presented with the assumption that all provisions with respect to public responsibility for the welfare and protection of children and youth are interrelated; and with the recognition that the administrative structure and program planning through which this responsibility is fulfilled may follow various designs, as appropriate to the different States. They are made available for such use as any State may wish to make of them whether in a gradual developmental process which preserves gains already made, or in more extensive or radical reorganization of State law required to achieve specific objectives or to lay the groundwork for a more effective program. 

Each State can start where it is in relation to present services. 

Although achievement of improved, extended or new services comes step by step, the goals for a broad program of services can be seen clearly. 

While the present is being faced realistically sights can be set for the future. Achievement of these calls for increased cooperation and coordination among all groups providing or concerned about services for children and youth.

PRINCIPLES UNDERLYING THE GUIDE 

As background for consideration and use of the suggestions for legislation there follows a statement of principles on which they are based with a brief discussion under each principle. 1. 

Parents have the primary responsibility for meeting the many needs of children and youth, and society an obligation to help them discharge this responsibility or to assume this responsibility when parents are unable to do So. 

The young because of their youth and immaturity need care, nurture, love and attention. They need also protection, and opportunity for the fullest development of their capacities. Parents have the primary responsibility for meeting these needs but obviously, they cannot, alone, meet all of them. Society, therefore, has an obligation to help parents by furnishing: certain resources and services for all children, such as schools, and health services for promoting good health and fostering normal physical and emotional development; as required, specialized resources and services for children with particular needs, such as diagnostic and remedial services for the sick and for those with physical, mental, or emotional handicaps; and social services, care and financial assistance for those whose parents are unable to meet or carry their responsibilities. Society also has a duty to promote conditions that are conducive to the wholesome development of family and child life and that will enable parents to discharge their responsibilities toward their children in a manner which will benefit both their children and their communities. 

2. Creation of a comprehensive program of child welfare and youth services is a joint responsibility of public and voluntary social agencies. These services should be universally available and adequate to meet the social welfare needs of children and youth and strengthen their family life. 2 2/ For a comprehensive description of the nature and content of these services see Child Welfare Services: How They Help Children and Their Parents. The children and youth whose social well-being such a program seeks to promote and protect include those: who are dependent, neglected, or delinquent, or in danger of becoming so; who have social, economic or behavior problems arising out of disturbance, incapacities or conflicts in family and social relationships, birth out of wedlock, or need for custody, guardianship or adoption; and who have similar problems resulting from community conditions, such as agricultural or industrial migration, prejudice and discrimination with respect to minority groups, socially and economically depressed neighborhoods, child labor and inadequate educational or employment facilities. In many instances the child welfare and youth services program provides social services for children and youth who are ill, physically handicapped, or suffering from emotional or mental disabilities. Often, however, social services to such children and youth are provided through other community agencies whose function is primarily the maintenance or restoration of health, physical or mental. 

The meeting of the social welfare needs of these children and youth and of their parents requires a variety of skills, services and facilities. A united approach of public and voluntary agencies is imperative. As they recognize their interrelationship, join their efforts, and reenforce each other, they can, together, achieve effective coordination of services and assure the use of all available services and facilities in the interest of children and youth and of their families. 3. 

State government has responsibility for planning and providing a program of public child welfare and youth services and facilities, or for seeing that such services are provided by local governments throughout the State.

The States have responsibility for promoting and safeguarding the welfare of their people. From this derives the authority of the State government to plan, establish and maintain necessary services that promote the social well-being of children and youth and to erect safeguards for their protection. Responsibility may be placed with the local governments for providing some of the services in accordance with policies, rules and regulations of the State government; but the State government has an obligation for the maintenance of a program, whether that government, itself, provides all of the services, or whether they are provided in part by local government or through the appropriate utilization of facilities and services of voluntary agencies. 

4. Enlistment of the participation of representatives of other public programs, voluntary agencies, and citizen groups in the planning and development of the program of public child welfare and youth services is essential.

The participation in the planning and development of the program of those who represent citizen groups, voluntary programs, and other public programs, affords opportunity for bringing in expression of the hopes and interests of the people of the State, for interpreting the program to the people and to other agencies, and for giving due regard to other available services and resources. 

5. The program of public child welfare and youth services should be comprehensive, stemming from three types of responsibility: Establishment and maintenance of ade quate standards for social services and facilities for children and youth; provision of social services to individual children and youth and their parents, of a variety and quality necessary to meet their needs effectively, and promotion of coordination and cooperation among organizations, agencies and groups in community planning, organization and development of services. 

The services and activities that stem from these three types of responsibilities include in their objectives both prevention and treatment of social problems. Probably no State program, however administered, at present encompasses all of the services and activities provided for in the suggested legislative language. Separately, if not collectively, however, most of their counterparts will be found in present programs of various States. They are set forth as representing the elements of what, today, is regarded as a comprehensive and wellrounded program of public child welfare and youth services. 

6. Public child welfare and youth services may be entirely 

State administered or may be partly State and partly locally administered. In the latter type of program, the local units should be vested with as much responsibility as is possible and appropriate, the State government making this possible by providing consultation and adequate financial assistance. In addition, to promote quality of service, uniformity and efficiency, local administration should be subject to State rules and regulations. Subject to differences that exist between 

State and local governments with respect to jurisdiction, organization and administration, the principles applicable to the State agency are applicable also to local agencies. 

In a State administered program the law gives responsibility for all duties and functions to a State agency financed by State appropriations. In its administration of the program the agency makes provision both for state-wide services and facilities and for local services. For the latter, the State agency may establish its own local or district offices, through which it provides these services.

In a partly State and partly locally administered program the law gives certain direct operating duties and functions to the State agency and others to agencies of local government. The law should provide for the State agency to issue policies, rules, regulations and standards, binding upon the local agencies, in accordance with which the local agencies administer the aspects of the program for which they are responsible. Generally, the unit of local government is at least as large as a county. In some instances it may be a large urban area governmentally independent of the county. Or it may be two or more counties, small in area or population, which combine their resources in a single agency. Both State and local funds are appropriated for the support of the program. States with State and locally administered programs vary as to which services are provided by the State agency and which by the local. This depends upon the pattern and the situation in a particular State. It also depends upon what is appropriate to the function of each agency. In a partly State and partly locally administered program, setting standards for, and licensing of, child placing and child caring agencies and institutions, and establishing and maintaining treatment facilities for delinquent youth, have been developed as appropriate State functions; while providing casework and group work services to children and youth and community organization of services for them have seemed to be appropriate local functions. 

7. With each State rests responsibility for determining through what form of administrative organization it can best achieve the goals of a strong and efficient program of public child welfare and youth services, make possible the most progressive development of that program, and give the program a clear cut identity. 

No single pattern for the administrative organization of child welfare and youth services - whether in a State administered or a State and locally administered program is applicable to all States. Most 

States administer all or most of such services in a department of public welfare. Some States administer certain types of institutions for children and youth, such as those for dependent and neglected, emotionally disturbed, or delinquent children and youth, through another agency. 

Some States have set up a separate agency to provide State services and care for delinquent children and youth.

Also, in the organization of services within a State agency, a number of variations are possible. Provision of services for delinquent children and youth may be taken as an example. A State department of public welfare administering all child welfare and youth services for which the State is responsible might have a separate division of services for delinquent children and youth, parallel with a division for

other children and youth; or, it might have a single division of services for children and youth with two or more branches, one of which provides for the delinquent. A State agency dealing only with delinquent children and youth might have several divisions, organized according to types of services. Still other patterns are possible. 

Decision by a State as to the type of organization it regards as best for the administration of its child welfare and youth services involves consideration of a variety of factors, including: the administrative pattern of the State and how these services have previously been organized and administered; the resources available and the stage of development of the program; and, most importantly, how the most effective work in behalf of children and youth can be organized and carried out so as to reach all children in need with the best quality of social services that can be made available. 

To achieve the best results possible, the organization and administrative pattern must allow for the identity of child welfare. 

As a specialized service, it calls for staff qualified by training and experience, who understand the nature of the child and the formative stages of development he goes through, and who are able to relate to him. Recognition of this identity, whether the program is administered through one or more agencies, is essential if it is to receive the attention it merits and if public attention and support is to be gained for it. 

8. The imposition, by statute, of duties in the program of public child welfare and youth services carries with it responsibility for the provision of funds to discharge these duties.

Each State should consider carefully the quality and extent of the services that the State and local governments are prepared to support, and decide which services and facilities will be "required" and which will be "authorized," and how the program will be financed. 

There are great advantages in statutorily imposed duties to provide certain services in that, in this manner, all persons similarly in need of the services will have an equal opportunity to have them made available. Coupled with this responsibility must be sufficient 

State and local funds to carry it out. The imposition upon an agency of a duty to make certain services available without the concomitant dedication of sufficient resources to fulfill that statutory duty can wreak harm upon the program. Decision as to how and at what levels the various parts of the program are to be financed depends, not only upon existing patterns of financing other State functions, but also upon the relative requirements of other State programs - including other public welfare as well

as education and health programs, the financial resources of the State, the stage of program development reached, etc. Involved also is the extent to which the cost of services will be borne directly by the State, by local governments, or by the State through allocation of State funds to local agencies. Consideration should be given to State financial assistance to or sharing with local units on a flexible or variable grant basis as a means of raising standards, maintaining the quality and adequacy of care and equalizing opportunity for services throughout the State.

Whatever method of financing the State decides upon, the funds provided should be sufficient to discharge adequately the duties and functions outlined by law for the agency or agencies administering the program. This is particularly true of services and care for children and youth whose legal custody has been removed from their parents by a court on the ground that they need treatment which the parents cannot give. 

9. All public child welfare and youth services should be coordinated, regardless of the organizational structure through which they are provided. 

Whether the services are administered by a single agency or by more than one agency, there should be coordination and cooperation to keep the focus on individual children and youth; to provide for continuity of service as an individual's needs change, without frequent shifts from one agency to another; to give recognition to social needs common to children and youth; and to avoid duplication or overlapping and assure maximum use of services and facilities. 

When one agency is responsible for all or most of the program, this coordination may be achieved through administration; when several agencies are responsible for the different parts of the program it must be achieved through clearly defined cooperative relationships and coordinating mechanisms. 10. 

Public child welfare and youth services should be closely associated with other public services designed to help individuals and families with their economic, personal, family or community problems.

These other public pro- Maintenance of such relationship and cooperation promotes the most effective functioning of each program. grams include many services related to child welfare and youth services, such as family welfare and public assistance especially aid to dependent children. They also include programs providing other services for the well-being of individuals and families, such as health service, including mental health, vocational rehabilitation, education, social insurances, and employment services.

11. Public child welfare and youth services should be available throughout the State to all who require such services and for whom provision is not otherwise made.

The services should be made available to children and youth and to their parents without regard to social, ethnic, racial or religious status or affiliation and without regard to duration of residence in the State or local unit, or other criteria such as financial status. 12. 

Public child welfare and youth services should always have regard for the relationship between parents and child and for safeguarding the rights of the child or youth and those of his parents. In the rendering of these services there should be recognition of the parent-child relationship and of the importance of preserving its positive values whenever possible, whether or not the child or youth is living with his parents. 

Although a public agency may not provide services and care for a child or youth without the consent of his parent or legal guardian except upon court order or upon a request authorized by law, the agency should make known its services and, when appropriate, afford the parent or guardian opportunity to avail himself of them voluntarily. 

13. When the legal custody of a child or youth is vested in a public agency, the agency should have the statutory authority, consistent with the rights of the child or youth and of his parents, and with the authority of the court, necessary to determine and carry out the treatment that will best meet his needs. Successful treatment necessitates that the agency having custody of a child have authority to make a variety of major and minor treatment decisions. It cannot, however, make decisions or take actions that are the right of the parents or legal guardian, or abrogate, remove, change or limit in any way the legal status of the child or youth with respect to his parents or guardian without resorting to legal process. If it finds that the child or youth has neither a natural nor adoptive parent in a position to exercise effective guardianship nor a legal guardian, it should petition the court for the appointment of an individual as a personal guardian. The authority given the agency by law with respect to a child or youth should be designed, therefore, to avoid either disregarding the rights of the child or youth or of his parents or so circumscribing the actions of the department that its treatment program might be seriously disrupted.

The agency should make periodic reexamination and reevaluation in the case of each child or youth, for neither the right of a parent to the legal custody of his child nor the right of a child to live in his 431032 O-57-2

· own home should be taken away for longer than is reasonably necessary. 

Major treatment decisions should be subject to court review upon the petition of the child or youth, or of his parents or legal guardian; other decisions designed to implement a major treatment decision, but involving no treatment inconsistent with it, should not be subject to such review. 

14. Parents and others legally responsible for children and youth should be required, insofar as they are able, to discharge their legal and financial obligations for them.

It is in the best interest of both the individuals and the community that parents and others legally responsible for children and youth should be required, insofar as they are able, to discharge their legal and financial obligations. Payment by them for foster care or for homemaker service may be an important factor in the maintenance of family ties or of a sense of self-dependence. 

15. Payment by a public child welfare agency of costs for maintenance in foster family or group care should be limited to children and youth whose legal custody is vested in the agency or for whom it is providing care under agreement with parents or guardian or upon request of a court or law enforcement agency for emergency care.

Payment for foster care of children and youth by a public agency should go hand in hand with acceptance of responsibility for particular children and youth and for meeting their needs, either directly through the agency's own resources or through purchase of such care from other agencies, public or voluntary. 

16. When a public agency purchases service from a voluntary agency, payment should be computed on a per capita basis. 

Payment should bear a direct relationship to the services rendered, or to be rendered, individual children and youth, as distinguished from a subsidy unrelated to the number of children to be served. 

17. Research is essential to development of a forward moving program of public child welfare and youth services, and to periodic evaluation of its success in achieving its objectives for children and youth.

11. Research, well planned and conducted, has a significant role in the development of public, as well as voluntary, child welfare and youth services. Through it the social needs of children and youth and of communities can be identified, and factual bases established and methodology defined for constructive State and local planning and action. The extent to which public, as well as voluntary, child welfare agencies provide opportunities to qualified staff to undertake original research and studies in the field of child welfare and related social and biological sciences and to report their findings in its literature will be an important factor in determining future progress in improving present practice and in developing new ways of meeting the needs of children. 

18. Personnel for public child welfare and youth services should be qualified by personality, training and experience, be selected on a merit basis, and have the conditions of their employment safeguarded. The agency or agencies responsible for the program should make provision for the training and development of staff. The quality of the services that reach the children and youth depends upon the individuals who carry out the program. The comprehensive nature of the services requires staff with many kinds of specialization, including child welfare workers and specialists in other fields such as psychiatry, psychology, and public information and interpretation. All of these should have the appropriate professional preparation. For the child welfare worker this means acquiring in a school of social work through academic work and field placement basic knowledge, skill and experience in casework, group work, community organization and research. 

The great shortage of child welfare personnel thus qualified, as well as the need for all staff continually to improve and add to their knowledge and skill and to develop their potentialities, makes it imperative that an agency administering the services carry on a program for the careful selection of staff and for their professional development, including full academic education in social work and in-service training. Such a program assures staff opportunities for continuing acquisition of new technical knowledge and for increasing their ability to use and develop new methods of work and new skills in areas such as casework, group work or community organization.

HOW TO USE THE GUIDE 

In the guide material which follows, an attempt has been made to translate the principles outlined above into suggested legislative language for a program of public child welfare and youth services. 

The provisions suggested in the guide are designed to reflect concepts which, to date, have found acceptance as desirable for inclusion in State legislation. It is recognized, however, that each 

State using the guide will need to determine how best to use the suggestions in meeting its own requirements.

Suggested Legislative Language Related to Other Laws 

Each State will need, also, to adapt the language to the provisions of its State constitution, of its general laws and of other State legislation. In some instances, a State may have to go further and consider change in its basic law, or even in its constitution, in order to make possible adequate protection of children and youth. 

No law relating to the welfare of children and youth stands alone. The principles and standards essential for their full protection are the foundation of a whole network of statutory and administrative provisions benefiting children and youth.

Closely related to the law establishing and setting forth the duties of the agency or agencies that administer the program of public child welfare and youth services, are other statutes that require or authorize the agency or agencies to perform certain duties or functions. 

Among such laws are those dealing with regulation of child placing activities, licensing of foster homes and child caring agencies, guardianship, adoption and termination of parental rights and responsibilities. The guide recommends that the act for which this document suggests language set forth the scope of the duties and functions of the agency or agencies with respect to such subjects, but that it do so in general language and not attempt to deal with the procedural or substantive details of the particular services to be provided or the particular persons to be served. 3/ 

3 For example, in the subject of adoption, these details are more appropriately part of the statute relating to adoption, since they should be considered in connection with other provisions that would best serve to protect the interests of the child, his natural parents, and the adoptive parents. This is not to imply that a general codification of all laws related to children and separate grouping are not appropriate. Some 

States already have established such codes, others are in the process of study and drafting preliminary to ultimate codification.

-- · - A State using the guide will need to decide as to the general mandates to be included on the basis of existing laws, the stage of development of the program, and the readiness of the State, financially and otherwise, to undertake certain functions or to provide certain services. 

In some instances only the general mandate for which provision is made here will be needed, the related statutes already being on the statute books; in others it will be necessary to draft the related statutes needed.

A number of the legislative provisions recommended in this document are predicated upon the existence of certain complementary provisions in the "Juvenile Court" statute. This is particularly true of the "Definitions" in Part I and of the procedural provisions in Part 2. 

A State using this guide, therefore, should review its "Juvenile Court" law for revisions or additions that may be needed. 

What the Guide Does Not Contain 

Not all of the provisions needed in a bill or bills for a program of public child welfare and youth services are set forth in this guide. 

The sections of the suggested language, therefore, are not drafted so as to be lifted bodily and formed into one bill. 

Because of the different agency structures through which such services may be organized and administered, suggested language is not included for the actual establishment and the overall organization and functions of the agency or agencies administering the program. Appropriate provisions will be required, or may already exist. Cognizance is, however, taken of the possibility of providing services in different ways.

Nor does this guide contain suggested language relating to the financing of the program. Further study and analysis is indicated prior to the drafting of suggested provisions with respect to the relative roles of the State and local units in the financing of locally administered programs.

Certain additional provisions will be required under the general laws of the State, e.g. power to do certain things with respect to real property, appropriations, etc.

Many of the sections are interdependent. Change in wording of one may require change in another. Although some sections or subsections might lend themselves to use out of context, others if used without the inclusion of certain interrelated sections might lead to serious confusion.

Suggested Legislative Language Presented by Type of Administrative Organization 

The guide recognizes that a State can organize and administer its public child welfare and youth services in at least three different ways, which it presents as alternative patterns: 5/ 

ALTERNATIVE I - A STATE ADMINISTERED PROGRAM FOR ALL CHILDREN AND YOUTH. 

The whole program of public child welfare and youth services, including those for delinquent children and youth, would be administered by the State government and through a single State agency. 

This agency could be either a multi-function department of public welfare, with a division, or divisions, of services for children and youth, or a separate State agency serving only children and youth.

ALTERNATIVE II -A PARTLY STATE AND PARTLY LOCALLY ADMINISTERED PROGRAM FOR ALL CHILDREN AND YOUTH. 

The program of public child welfare and youth services, including those for delinquent children and youth, would by administered partly by the State government through a single State agency, and partly by the local governments through an agency in each local governmental unit, in accordance with policies, rules, regulations and standards of the State agency. Here also, the State agency could be either a multifunction department of public welfare or a separate 

State agency serving only children and youth.

ALTERNATIVE III-A STATE ADMINISTERED PROGRAM FOR DELINQUENT CHILDREN AND YOUTH. 

Responsibility would be divided for the program of public child welfare and youth services administered by State government. State services for delinquent children and youth would be administered through a separate State agency serving only such children and

60/ It is often advisable to include a "separability" clause. 

This is especially important where a wide variety of individuals in a wide variety of different circumstances would be affected by the provisions enacted. It cannot, of course, serve as an absolute guarantee. 

Even when included, if the application of the act to a particular set of circumstances or to a particular group of individuals is declared unconstitutional, the courts may still find that the legislature did not mean that if the provisions of the act could not apply to all circumstances and individuals it should apply to none and hence that all the provisions are void.

This (Act) shall be liberally construed to accomplish the purposes herein sought. 61/ It is advisable to include a "construction clause" where there is also a purpose clause. If the "purpose clause" is clearly, accurately and concisely expressed, a "construction clause" can often be of assistance in securing an interpretation of other provisions of the act which will aid the attainment of the legislative purpose. A "construction clause" cannot, however, be used as a substitute for accuracy in legislative drafting. Thus, it cannot be argued that a legislative injunction for liberal construction taken in conjunction with a "broad" purpose clause would permit a court to interpret a specific provision contrary to its clearly expressed meaning.

Effective (90) days after the effective date of this (Act), Chapter of the laws of shall be considered repealed. 

62/ It is important to specify the laws or parts of laws which are to be repealed and when the repeal is to be effective. As a general drafting device, it is not recommended that the "repeal clause" consist merely of the statement that "all laws or parts of laws in conflict herewith are hereby repealed." This recommendation is made for two reasons. 

First, unless the specific laws or parts of laws to be repealed are specified, the inclusion merely of such a flat statement is tantamount to leaving the determination of what existing laws are in conflict with the newly enacted provisions to be determined by the courts. Even disregarding the fact that such a determination should be made by the legislative rather than the judicial branch of the government, such a bald statement leads to confusion in administration. Until each possible interpretation has been finally resolved by the courts, there will remain doubt and confusion as to whether certain provisions of existing laws have been repealed or whether they should be construed in conjunction with the newly enacted statute. 

Second, where the clause requires specificity, it will also require an examination of existing statutes to determine which should be repealed and more important - which should be amended in the light of the newly enacted provisions. The legislature will therefore have before it the full picture not only of what it is enacting but also specifically what it is repealing.

The purpose of this (Act) is to promote, safeguard and protect the social well-being and general welfare of children and youth of the State, through a comprehensive and coordinated program of public child welfare and youth services providing for: social services and facilities for children and youth who require guidance, care, control, protection, treatment or rehabilitation, and for their parents; setting of standards for social services and facilities for children and youth; cooperation with public and voluntary agencies, organizations, and citizen groups in the development and coordination of programs and activities in behalf of children and youth; and promotion of community conditions and resources that help parents to discharge their responsibilities for the care, development and well-being of their children. A "purpose clause" is useful in that it gives a bird's eye view of the general objectives of the proposed legislation. After the legislation is enacted, it can be of assistance in the interpretation of various sections. Reliance for authority to perform a certain act should not, however, be placed solely on the "purpose clause." Specific provisions authorizing the performance of particular act should be included in the substantive portions of the legislation.

The granting of legal custody to an agency limits the exercise of parental or guardianship rights but does not terminate them. The latter should be done only through court action for that specific purpose.

Even though legal custody is transferred, the natural or adoptive parent of the child or youth, or an individual appointed as the guardian of his person by a court, still retains the power to make major decisions affecting his life and development, such as consent to marriage, to enlistment in the armed forces, and to major medical, psychiatric, and surgical treatment; to represent him in legal actions; and also has the right and duty of reasonable visitation of the child or youth, except as this is temporarily limited by the court. 

A parent retains certain additional rights and responsibilities, including the right to consent to adoption, to determine the child's religious affiliation, and the responsibility for support.
