FOREWORD 

The purpose in presenting this little story pertaining to Pawcatuck River and Little Narragansett Bay is to bring together in chronological order the names and characteristics of the various steamboats that served the excursion-loving people of Westerly, Stonington, Mystic, New London and Norwich together with the steam craft that were employed commercially for so long a period. 

Likewise, to perpetuate the names of the men who possessed the public spirit that prompted them to invest their funds in such enterprises which were not over lucrative, so far as operation was concerned. 

Again, to make a permanent record of the names of many of the men who were instrumental in maintaining a schedule day by day. 

Finally, to illustrate many of the subjects relative to their appearance and character. 

Fortunately, beginning with the advent of the "Belle" photographs of these steam craft have been preserved and it is hoped that their reproduction here will afford an added interest to the narrative. 

During the days of the earlier boats snapshots were unthought of; photographs were rare and even so late as the days of the "Florence" no one seems to have made her the subject of a picture, for after diligent search extending over a period of years, none can be found. However, a fair likness of this steamboat, drawn from memory, as she appeared in the changed character of a tow boat during the '70's is presented herein. 

The history of the early steam craft was learned many years ago from the late William D. Wells of Westerly and the late William M. 

Williams of Norwich, Conn., both of whom were actively interested in them. Since then years of contact with the service and personal observation afford the opportunity to record facts and figures that we hope may prove to be of historical interest to "our children, our children's children, and so on-and so on.”

HILE there are no records regarding the first boat to be propelled by steam on the Pawcatuck River, it has been handed down to us that a Mr. Sprague

Barber in the year 1848 built a boat near the head of navigation in Westerly. It was a small craft fitted with side wheels and although its machinery was crude, it managed to whip its way down the river and back. They used to say that when 

Pawcatuck Rock was reached, they were obliged to stop, tie her up to a stake and blow off all the steam pressure in order to feed up the boiler with water. The Lighters

Those scows that did such vallant service on the river bore no emblazoned boards on their bows, nor on their quarters, yet they were all known by their own dignified names. The largest was the "Great Eastern," then the "California," "Oregon," "General Grant," "White Rock I" "White Rock II," and "Jason."

One of the good old souls that was employed with the stevedore gang along the docks during that period remarked one day that he was pleased that one of the fleet was named after an Irishman- "O'Regan." 

During busy seasons the number of lighters was inadequate on many occasions to handle the traffic, especially so when a large quantity of lumber was being shipped in. 

All of the timber, lumber, shingles and laths bound to Westerly were freighted to Stonington harbor in down-east schooners of deep draught. Consequently their entire cargo was unloaded on to lighters as a rule. Sometimes the captain of a to schooner in order keep things moving during a shortage of lighters found it necessary to build a several raft containing thousand feet of lumber out of his cargo and alongside the schooner.

When completed it was towed up the Pawcatuck by the "Florence."

We boys were always looking for the job of caring for the raft and spearing boards that now and then started adrift. Although it service for which we were well naid, it also savored of fun. It was was a tow boat rule in those days that [ 13 ]

the consignee of a lighter load of any material must pay one dollar in addition to the towing charges if someone was not provided to steer and care for the craft during transit. In view of this fact we were on hand early Saturday mornings during the school period for the purpose of securing some of those jobs.

On one of those days it so happened that all seven of the lighters were at the end of a tow line and it chanced that one boy was given charge of all of them while they were being towed. To illustrate the appreciation and generosity of "Captain Frank" as he was affectionately called, he mustered the boy to the pilot house after the long day's work was done and handed him a five-dollar bill for his part in the day's doings. In relating this reminiscence to a company a short time since, it was remarked that all the boy needed in addition to the five spot in his pocket while on his way home was a plug hat and a cane to complete the picture. 

Before the advent of the tow boat and lighters the local schooners and sloops of light draught were warped slowly up the river by running a long line ahead made fast to a kedge anchor or to some stable object on the river bank that chanced to be in the channel On one occasion a vessel range. was being warped between Gran'ther Sam's Point and Pawcatuck 

Rock. The warp line was made fast to one of the three ancient cedar trees that surmounted the rock. As she proceeded the vessel sagged on the mud bottom to the west'ard of the channel which brought a smart strain on the line as the crew hove it in with the windlass. Suddenly the tree snapped and the old Yankee skipper sang out, "There, By Gosh! We never done that before." The other two trees are still standing and are reputed to be over two hundred years old.

During this period Captain Lodwich Hazard manoeuvred an old sailscow up the Pawcatuck. It is said that while he was beating to the windward down the river on one of his commercial trips, a friend of his-a stranger on the riverwas on board. Said he, "Lodwick, I suppose you know where every rock in the river is located." About that moment the scow pounded her way over a big pebble and the captain quickly replied, "Yes, suh, and that's one of 'um!"

The "Belle" ran continually on this route for thirteen seasons. Due to her great measure of open deck room and cabin space, she was always able to handle any crowd that chanced to appear, excepting on two occasions. One of these made mention of above; the other was on the day the steamer "Metis" was sunk after which her upper structure came ashore on the East we Beach at Watch Hill. It was on the morning of August 30, 1872, that the "Belle" left her wharf on time 9 o'clock. It was raining a bit which accounted for only a small number of passengers being aboard. Upon arrival at the Hill all hands learned of the terrible crash that had happened on the beach. In those days there was no telephone service to apprise Westerly people of the disaster before the "Belle's" morning trip. However, the overland messengers to the town doctors and druggists occasioned the general distribution of the news. Soon after this every means of transportation was pressed into service and when the "Belle" started down the river on the afternoon trip, she was well loaded with people also. The return trip in the afternoon was delayed for a while for the benefit of the passengers but when the start was made the "Belle" was loaded so heavily that her starboard guard was flush with the water while her port paddle wheel fanned the air instead of digging into the water.

In this fashion and under these circumstances, she paddled her way slowly up the river with one wheel doing service. Those people who were left on the dock were fortunate. Those who made the trip were most uncomfortable, for they were unable to move about. No fares could be collected and it is doubtful if anyone ever knew just how many persons there were aboard. They were landed at the first available dock upon arrival at Westerly, as she would not have been able to continue on to her regular landing owing to the lesser depth of the channel above.

During 1871 and 1872 her terminal was at the point between the stone engine house and the brick mill on Main Street where there was an ample passageway to the street. Throughout the period of the "Belle's" service on the river, moonlight excursions were exceedingly popular. Osbrook and Watch Hill were the objective points. At the former landing Joseph Crandall, better known as "Osbrook Joe," maintained a fairly large pavilion which contained a spacious dance hall, bowling alleys, dining room and bar. The dance orchestra of 

William Kenneth, the veteran master and prompter, always accompanied these excursions and being the first passengers to go ashore they were soon tuned up, and before the "Belle" was ready to continue on to Watch Hill the dance hall would be alive with repeated calls for "two more couples this way." The formation was quickly completed for the stereotyped quadrille while the orchestra piped up "Turkey in the Straw" and "Mollie 

Darling" as Mr. Kenneth called "Salute your partner; first two couples forward and back; side two couples forward and back; swing your partner there;" and they certainly did swing in those days. 

However, the sound of this merriment faded as the "Belle" began to paddle her way to Watch Hill with the greater portion of her company. These excursions were invariably managed by some of the local organizations whose members canvassed for the sale of the tickets. Two-fifths of the gross receipts were retained by the society. 

On one of these early excursions there were eight hundred fares collected. More often three to four hundred would be the limit. Either the Westerly Brass Band or St. Michael's Brass Band enlivened the trip and many times the excursion was made under the auspices of one or the other of these bands. 

On the return home trip which was usually completed just before midnight, the band always did their prettiest in playing "Home Sweet 

Home" as the "Belle" skimmed through the glassy channel and past James Gavitt's ice house, Greenman's shipyard and the lower wharves. There was another feature of these excursions that sometimes occasioned the blowing of four blasts on the "Belle's" whistle just before the finale of the band. 

This was done to inform the police in town that there were some frisky ones aboard that needed their fatherly care, for on some of these trips a few troublesome characters that had loaded up with too much tomato juice or something would interfere with the operation of the steamer or stage a fight. These "birds" were controlled in a measure by a constable until the wharf was reached. One of these offenders who became wise to the four blasts of the whistle bided his time during one trip, and as the "Belle" slowly passed the Cottrell wharf he sprang over the rail into the river and swam to the Connecticut shore, thus avoiding possible arrest by the Rhode Island officers.

Labor troubles were not usual among the boat hands in those days, but one instance comes to mind, however. The crews received no extra pay for running moonlight excursions, for it was generally understood at the outset that there would be a few night excursions to run in addition to the regular day schedule. One week there were three of these-Thursday, Friday and Saturday nights. On the last mentioned night a large crowd was on board, and it was about starting time when the engine room crew announced that they would not make the run without extra compensation. No amount of persuasion on the part of the management would satisfy them. Hence they were promised extra pay "and the band played on."

Besides lengths and breadths, depths and draughts, side wheels and propellers, beam engines and otherwise, the reader might be interested in some of the happenings on the boats. We will not call these "yarns" for they are all true stories, and while many more incidents could be related, the few herein narrated may serve to add a trifle of spice to the prosy character of the little history. 

Just before starting time one afternoon a middle-aged man sauntered up to the engine room door and while looking at the machinery chanced to take notice of the steam gauge. "Gosh!" said he, "I didn't know it was as late as that," and then ran up the gang plank-up the dock and out of sight. Just how he read the time on that gauge, no one was able to explain. 

However, there was one fellow who was something of a ventriloquist and who enjoyed himself during some of the trips by stopping at the engine room door, while the engine was in motion, and by exercising his powers produced a certain squeak that the engineer was unable to locate. In his endeavor to eliminate the squeak the engineer, of course, was obliged to take a good many steps and yet he would be baffled. Now, this afforded amusement to the smarty passenger. But his day of reckoning arrived when the engineer who had determined the sourc of the noise casually instructed his fireman to squirt a little engine oil down the back of the fellow's neck during one of his performances. This ended the squeak.

In making mention of oil one is reminded of the freight that was carried to the "Hill." On some days there would be a quantity of ice: on other days it might be a lot of cord wood or a lot of bags conin taining, various kinds of grain and feed, but during every day there was more or less foodstuff-meats, groceries and fruit. Kerosene oil supplied house illumination those days, therefore many barrels and cans of kerosene were also included in the freight. However, the amusing part of this story is that on some occasions, a few gallons of liquor contained in what appeared to be a kerosene can all smeared over with oil and a potato driven on to the nozzle for a plug, appeared as part of the cargo. Hence, as it rested out on the deck with the rest of the supplies, no one would ever surmise its contents and consequently it made the trip unmolested.

An elaborate celebration had been mapped out by the people of 

Stonington for the 10th of August, 1875. The Antiques and Horribles were to be the great feature of the parade. Scores of Westerly people planned to witness the affair, but the younger fellows in the steamboat crew bemoaned the fact that they were compelled to work all day-both trips.

On that morning the fifteen minute whistle had just been blown; a lot of passengers were already aboard and all the preparatory work under way when suddenly a chunk of iron the size of your hand was blown out of the six-inch throttle valve. The roar of the escaping steam which enveloped the entire boat, the shrieks of women and children as they scrambled to the dock, made matters lively for a short time. It was fortunate that no one was injured-not from the steam, but in the stampede. After the fires had been "pushed back" and the steam had ceased its activity, the engineer was able to determine the damage. To make a long story short the engineer was dispatched to Boston where he procured a new valve, returning with it on the "boat train" at 8:45 p. m.

During a portion of that time the crew was entertained in Stonington by the Antiques and Horribles. 

During the night they helped put in the new valve. [21]

During the earlier days all of the coal, lumber, brick, pig iron and moulding sand consigned to Westerly was received by water. Now all of these commodities are shipped in by rail with the exception of a portion of the coal which is received by power canal boats. In the matter of coal much less in quantity is being consumed in town than formerly owing to the fact that the machinery in all the various enterprises, excepting one, is being operated by electric current; that hundreds of homes now use gas from the street mains as well as oil for cooking purposes, and that oil in large measure is used as a fuel for the heating of many business buildings and residences. The aggregate quantity of coal that these displace amounts to thousands of tons annually. Standard Oil Tankers 

The increasing use of oil and gasoline has given rise to a new and important feature on the river in regard to the transportation these commodities. of 

Until 1928 the Standard Oil Company of New York had received its products at the local plant in tank cars by rail. At great expense the company employed contractors in the work of blasting out "Flat 

Rock" and the construction of a pier on which are supported three 6-inch pipe lines extending from the out-board end of the pier to the tanks in the yards. Many thousands of dollars have been expended by the company in clearing the channel of the river throughout its entire length of existing obstacles. Tons of rocks that had been left during former dredging operations were "hooked" and hoisted to the decks of high-powered lighters on which they were transported to a resting place where no more damage can be done by them. All this has enabled the Socony to operate safely its own twin-screw tankers directly from its oil docks in Providence to the local pier. At first cargoes of oil and gasoline were shipped in tank barges which were towed from the oil docks and up the river by the tug "S. O. No. 7" in charge of Capt. Thomas Michaelson, who was at first piloted up the river by Capt. Harry Baker, master of the "Westerly." After the task of clearing the channel of rocks had been completed and it had been nicely buoyed, they began to ship in cargoes by either the "Oswego Socony," "Boston Socony," "Providence Socony" or Hartford 

Socony." These craft are 252 feet in length by 40 feet beam, and are driven by internal combustion engines. They are never loaded to the limit of their capacity for the 

Westerly trip, however, there being in 8500 barrels the equivalent of 

425,000 gallons of mixed cargo.

These loads are discharged by their own deck pumps in five hoursabout 1400 gallons per minute. 

Some of the later vessels to arrive were piloted up the river by Capt. 

Johansen, who was the master in charge. 

To survey one of these hookers with the eye, even a boatman, as well as a casual observer, would assume that they represented the limit of length of vessels that could be headed around in the narrow river. 

However, this feature has been handled handsomely with two or three fathoms of width to spare.

This is evidenced by the fact that on March 30, 1932, new and beautifully equipped vessel of the fleet, the "New York Socony," 265 feet in length, came creeping into the berth with a partial cargo. She was in charge of Capt. Charles S. 

Pickett and was piloted by Capt. Michaelson. [39]

The longest craft that ever doubled the river is seen here creeping in to her berth at the pier of the Standard Oil Company of New York. It is the "New York Socony," 265 feet in length, with only a light cargo of about half a million gallons. Captain Tom Michaelson was successful in running her up this far but he was surely gratified when she was made fast to the dock. These pictures were snapped by Herbert P. Clark. Even the most optimistic steamboat man of former days would not have risked his reputation by declaring that a vessel 265 feet long would ever run the Pawcatuck River under its own power. But such was the case even under adverse conditions, for when she "steamed" up the river the tide was subnormal, and on the return trip a southeaster was piping high. Anyway, she ran up-she turned around-and she ran out. Now! Who will dare predict the possibilities that may accrue to the Pawcatuck as petroleum continues in its ascendency?

The 

Steam Yachts 

There was one class of steam vessels that afforded a world of pleasure to many residents of this community during the summer months for quite a period of years, beginning with those of 1877. These handsome craft were not built in yards along our riverside; however, they were owned here and made its placid waters their summer home, hence we are pleased to record their names among the "Steamboats of the Pawcatuck" and through their agency to preserve in mind and to speak of the magnanimous spirit in the matter of entertainment which time and time again was showered upon the acquaintances of their owners-the late Stephen Wilcox and his nephew, the late William D. Hoxie, each in his own period of years. 

The first steam yacht that Mr. 

Wilcox owned was named "Delight." She came into the river under the command of Captain 

John Hoxie, a former master of deep sea square riggers. Captain 

Hoxie was a brother of Mrs. Wilcox and the father of William D.

Hoxie. 

It was in 1882 that the second steam yacht was completed for 

Mr. Wilcox. This was the "Sophia."

Her dimensions were greater than those of the "Delight." After she had undergone the necessary trials in the waters around New York and was ready to make the run to Westerly, Captain Hoxie brought her around from the North River to a landing on the East River side. Here he went ashore to a ship chandlery for the purpose of making some purchases for the yacht. While thus engaged he sank to the floor and never regained consciousness. 

Finally the "Sophia" came through with another master in charge. She proved to be a smart little yacht but was not exactly the craft that fulfilled the mechanical hopes and aspirations of Mr. Wil- 

COX. It was in 1856 that he perfected a water-tube boiler for stationary service. Late in the sixties 

Babcock & Wilcox modified this creation and as the years Wore on they made wonderful improvements combining simplicity, safety and efficiency. However, as late as 1888 no steam producer of this class had been constructed by them for marine service.

This fact had been an uppermost thought in the mind of Mr. Wilcox for a long period and during the development of this idea he traded the "Sophia" for a smaller yacht called "Arrow." This was only a makeshift, for in 1889 he produced a marine water-tube boiler which was installed in a yacht then under construction after a design by Gustave Hillman, N. A. This was the "Reverie." Her length was 112 feet by 17 feet beam. The new boiler was built entirely of forged steel and furnished steam at 225 pounds pressure to a quadruple expansion engine having cylinders 8, 11, 16½ and 26 inches in diameter by 12 inches stroke,

This power plant proved to be a great success as well as a source of unlimited gratification to Mr. Wilcox. 

It might be of interest to note that since that experiment the 

Babcock & Wilcox Company have built about 4250 marine watertube boilers embracing 17,440,292 square feet of heating surface. 

This does not include the production of their plants in foreign countries. [43]

The Sailing Vessels 

It seems especially fitting and affords much pleasure in being able to append the record of the building of the many sailing vessels along the banks of the Pawcatuck over a long term or years.

This list was compiled by the late Herbert A. Babcock during his days of service on "The Westerly Sun." Mr. Babcock bore an unusual interest in the matter of commercial boating on the Pawcatuck and it is due to his persistent and painstaking labor that this authentic record was completed and preserved. Evidently this work of Mr. Babcock was prepared in the year 1896; hence we have taken the liberty to change the names of a few of the localities in order that they may appear more familiar to the present day reader.

During the years in which sailing vessels held the supremacy shipbuilders of Westerly were active in the matter of construction of craft of various characters, size and rig.

The demand was continuous and all of the vessels were built upon orders. There appears to be no authentic record of those that were built during the earliest days of the enterprise, but Mr. Babcock says: "Probably the first shipwright on the Pawcatuck was Joseph Wells who in 1681 built a vessel near 

Pawcatuck Rock. She was named 'Alexander and Martha,' the owners being Alexander Pygan, Samuel 

Rogers and Daniel Stanton. Her length was forty feet and she hailed from New London."

From the records it appears that between the years 1800 and 1867 vessels of various dimensions were built at several points on both shores of the Pawcatuck. After that shipbuilding was confined to the yard of Silas Greenman & Co., which occupied the space along the river and fronting the residence of 

William W. Wilcox on Margin

Street and extended southward to a line running west from the residence of the late Capt. William · 

Greenman, now the property of J. 

Clifford and Jenni M. Steadman. 

Good native white oak timber was largely supplied by farmers who were fortunate enough to possess virgin forest lands. Their timber rigs thus loaded and drawn by oxen with one, and perhaps two, horses in the lead as they proceeded down Main and Margin Streets were familiar sights in those days. Then the shipyard was a scene of public interest. It was a spot where the town folks were wont to visit and watch the progress of the structure as it rose from its heavy keel to a creature of shape and beauty. Finally when the day for launching arrived hundreds of men, women and children gathered on either side of the river in order to witness the spectacle. The crowd was especially pronounced when a vessel of larger dimensions was slid down the ways into her future element and voluminous were the cheers that voiced an accompani- ment to the surge of the waters. The revolution or evolution or whatever you may call it in the matter of transportation by water shifted the traffic from fleet afer fleet of sailing craft to string after string of tug drawn barges, boxes or scows. The cargoes in single tows represented the equivalent of loads that were formerly carried in from fifteen to thirty ordinary two masted schooners. Therefore, it was impossible for the white winged carriers to hold their own against such competition. Consequently many of them were laid by and rotted out. 

Some of them found their way to the bottom and others were stranded on some reef or shore as in the instance of the "Altana M. Jagger." The picture which is herein presented of this well known vessel was made by Lawrence M. 

Crandall on July 2nd, 1932, the day following her stranding on the sand: of Misquamicut Beach, strangely enough near the summer home of the lady in whose honor she was christened. Since then the churning and pounding of the sea has reduced her to a sparless, deckless hulk.

Far more cheerful is the tone of the news item printed in the Providence Journal from my regular correspondence May 24, 1890. headline given it by the editor read as follows: 

The "The Altana Jagger Is a Good Un” "Capt. Charlie Emmet's new schooner the 'Altana M. Jagger," named in honor of a daughter of Mr. Foster Jagger of this town, arrived at the wharf of Messrs. C. B. Cottrell & Sons early on Friday morning with her first Westerly cargo-225 tons of moulding sand for the above mentioned firm.

The new vessel attracted considerable attention while she was being unloaded, for from all points aloft her brand new bunting was flying during the entire day. She is owned by

Capt. Emmet and several Westerly gentlemen, and was built for them by Jackson & Sharpe of Wilmington, Del. The length of the keel is 98 feet and she is of 22 feet beam.

The depth of the hold is 7½ feet and on a draught of seven feet she floated her cargo of 225 tons up the river, although she can easily carry 250 tons.

The vessel is rather a novelty for the lads hereabouts, for it is constructed somewhat on the canal schooner principle in regard to shape, although both ends are nicely trimmed down, but above deck everything is very yacht-like, wire rigging and all the patent appliances with iron davits, etc. She is rigged without a fore topmast but has a flying jib-bom, all combining to produce a pleasing effect. The cabin is a model of neatness and comfort, and as old Uncle' Fred Dunscan, the veteran sea cook, used to say, when asked for his opinion of a vessel-whether new or old: 'I guess she's a purty good un. She's got good anchors and good chains.' 

During the good old sailing vessel days there were scores of schooners belonging to other ports that were towed up the Pawcatuck from time to time consigned to various tradesmen and manufacturers. There was the fleet comprising the Trenton canalers. They were built like a canal boat, almost square at both ends, which gave rise to the oft told yarn that they were built by the mile and cut off to order, or to the length of a canal lock. They were schooner rigged without a fore-topmast and with a stub of a bowsprit and a barn-door rudder. As a rule they brought about 200 tons of coal from Philadelphia and their route lay through the Delaware and Raritan Canal, being towed by horses or mules as far as Newark Bay. We towboat boys always referred to them in a kindly spirit as "Horse Oceanmen."

Their names as I remember them were "Blast," "Gale," "Surge," "Mist," "Wave," "Storm," "Gust," "Breeze," "Tempest," "Sparkle," "Twilight" and "Dawn."
