INTRODUCTION 

This memorandum reports the results of a pilot study of German press reactions to U.S. air power in Korea. 

Six papers published in Germany were selected for inspection in this study: two Communist and four non-Communist. While complete files of the papers in question could not be obtained, a sufficiently large number of issues for June, July, August and September were available to indicate the nature of their treatment of air power in Korea. All references to the air war were noted. A quantitative analysis was not undertaken, however, because the files were incomplete.

-2- 

SUMMARY 

1. 

The Communist press consistently presented the following image of the U.S. use of air power which tied in with other Communist propaganda: 2. (a) Bombing is of no military value (b) Bombing is directed solely against defenseless civilian targets (c) Bombing is a sign of the vengeful and impotent rage which Americans feel because they are being driven out of Korea (d) American "terror bombing" in Korea is indicative of what the

U.S. will do again in Germany, if given the opportunity. 

The non-Communist press presented no coherent picture of the role of air power to its readers. Two papers mentioned the air war in Korea so rarely that they seemed almost to want to avoid the subject. 

One paper gave fairly good coverage and at least mentioned, even if it did not stress, the military value of bombing. The fourth non-Communist paper supported U.S. action in Korea in only a very limited sense, and failed to provide its readers with information which would enable them to refute Communist propaganda on the subject. 

It is concluded that the German non-Communist press, insofar as the four papers selected are representative of it, presents only a very weak picture of air power as a military weapon. This leaves the field fairly clear for the Communist allegation of U.S. air barbarism. Non-Communist editors have apparently been reluctant to present a positive image of air power. Many of them must feel that such an undertaking is futile in a country which has suffered so heavily from bombing. This conclusion is reinforced by the fact that the detailed and exhaustive coverage given the air war by the U.N. command in the Far East, particularly after the middle of July, has not resulted in a more balanced selection of items on the part of the German press. However, an increasing number of references to leaflet operations seems noteworthy.

GENERAL CONCLUSIONS AS TO THE COMMUNIST PRESS TREATMENT OF THE AIR WAR IN KOREA 

1. 

The continuing Soviet peace campaign which had reached new heights of intensity since the Stockholm meeting (Spring 1950) harped not only on the outlawing of the atom bomb but emphasized also the imperialist nature of the West which was alleged to be preparing for a new conflict. The Korean war was utilized by Soviet propagandists as material for this campaign. It was exploited also for the purpose of driving a wedge between Asiatics, Europeans, and Americans. Finally, in Germany, the Korean

2. war was used to create animosity and resentment against the Americans. It is with this latter purpose in mind that the strong emphasis on the air war in Korea must be seen. For the memories of a badly bombed German population seemed particularly susceptible to resentment over air attacks only recently suffered and made it even more apprehensive about future air attacks with atom bombs which seemed imminent if the Soviet analysis of an imperialist America was accepted. 

The links with the past and the attempted projection of the Korean war into Germany are the two pillars which support . the Communist onslaught on German morale. 

The abrupt reversal of the war in the middle of September raises the question how the Communist Press resolved a defeat situation. Insofar as the peace campaign is concerned, the impact of the events did not seem to influence their strategy. The minimization of air power, however, and the insistence on its mere terror function may very well have been given a severe jolt.# *The Communist papers dealt with here remained silent for a few days and did not report the landings of UN troops until the 19th of September. Even then, these news stories were buried in the back pages of NEUES DEUTSCHLAND and TAEGLICHE RUNDSCHAU. Moreover, the Korean war which had been on the front page until this date was quickly relegated to an inside page of the papers, and the forthcoming election took over completely. 

The landings themselves were minimized although the numerical superiority of the enemy was acknowledged. While there was first a tendency to turn the defense of Seoul into another Stalingrad where Fascism would meet its end, the admitted superiority of the UN forces offered a big enough loophole for the papers to back out of their exposed positions in the face of further North Korean reverses.

E. 

GENERAL CONCLUSIONS AS TO TREATMENT OF THE KOREAN AIR WAR IN THE NON-COMMUNIST PRESS 

1. All six Western German papers supported the United States action in Korea. None of the papers accepted the Soviet interpretation of the course of events in Korea.

2. Although not all the papers which were examined gave equal coverage to the Korean war, the fact remains that with the exception of the SUEDDEUTSCHE ZEITUNG every one of them printed some news about the Truman statement that the atom bomb would not be used in Korea. Moreover, every paper in cluding the SUEDDEUTSCHE ZEITUNG paid increasing attention. 

in its columns to leaflet operations.

(One reference in the 

BERLINER STADTBLATT to several in the other papers). Only the FRANKFURTER ALLGEMEINE ZEITUNG had referred to leaflets before. 

3. Even though leaflet operations were reported by all papers, it was only the FRANKFURTER ALLGEMEINE ZEITUNG which in cluded an explicit refutation of the Communist line in its news reports. In the dispatch of Sept. 23rd this paper also gave a good illustration of the problems which confront allied commanders when specific tactical decisions conflict with long-range political objectives. 

4. The tendency to refer to operations as American, to bring pictures of U.S. soldiers and military equipment rather than emphasize the United Nations character of the operations in Korea threaded itself through the entire period for which issues were examined. This was particularly noticeable in those papers where pictorial coverage supplemented news reporting. (The picture supplement of the 

FRANKFURTER RUNDSCHAU on Saturdays). 

5. The SUEDDEUTSCHE ZEITUNG, however, permitted the air war in Korea to appear in its worst light. The dispatches and the very few pictures on its pages tended to emphasize the horror attendant on air war but paid only slight attention to the ground war. The fact that only the Allies have air power helped to reinforce the one-sided picture of the sufferings which the North Koreans had to endure. The cumulative effect of news items unfavorable to the air war is not neutralized by the fact that the SUEDDEUTSCHE ZEITUNG gave leaflet operations space in its columns and argued emphatically against the Communist line in the Korean war in its editorials. 

6. Even though this study is incomplete and impressionistic, the following emerges quite clearly: The Communist Press in Germany has a clearly 'defined' line in the treatment of the Korean war. Its readers are given the impression that air attacks are: (a) directed primarily against peaceful civilian objects; (b) an expression of American barbarism; (c) of little or no military value; (d) a manifestation of the

7。 impotent rage of the Americans on being expelled from Korea. 

The non-Communist press, on the other hand, furnishes the reader with no consistent interpretation of the air war in Korea. In some instances he does not receive sufficient news to enable him to form his own opinion and where he may do so, it is without an adequate frame of reference. In particular, the non-Communist press failed to establish a link between air operations and those of other arms. The news gave some inkling of the odds which the ground troops faced in Korea but very often left the reader completely in the dark as to where air action fitted into the overall picture or whether it had a place at all in overcoming the resistance of the enemy. The upshot of this may well be that the non-Communist in an encounter with a Communist does not have the tools to combat the latter's arguments and presentation of the air war in Korea. 

8. The presentation of the air war in Korea in the Western German non-Communist papers can be explained in several ways. The memory of the bombings of the last war may make it impossible for German editors to deal more fully with air warfare, particularly in a positive way. Readers may dismiss such argumentation or even resent it. That may explain the de-emphasis of the air war in some papers. Wherever it has been presented more fully, however, the tendency has been to select those aspects of the air war which agreed with the bombing experiences of the readers. The resultant ambivalence has, therefore, tended to weaken editorial endorsement of the United States action in Korea. Unwillingly then, many non-Communist papers have, by catering to their readers, failed to create an effective image of the air war in Korea which competed with the 

Communist image.
