INTRODUCTION 

IT IS a privilege to commend this fresh and stimulating book, which I have read with deep interest and appreciation. In it the great prophets of Israel live again, move through our modern world, and apply the eternal principles they saw so clearly to the social conditions which confront us.

It would be difficult to find one more competent to write such a volume than Rabbi Isserman. He himself is in the Prophetic Succession. The spirit of the prophets of Israel is in him. The passion for social justice which fired them kindles his own soul, and has made him an outstanding leader of the forces of social righteousness in this community. He will not ask that the readers of this book agree with all his conclusions, but it is difficult to see how anyone can escape his challenge. 

St. Louis 

August, 1933.

WILLIAM SCARLETT.

FOREWORD 

BELIEVING that the rabbi is the heir of the prophets and must attempt to carry out their functions in modern society, I could not, though tried with innumerable duties, refuse the invitation that the editor of Front Rank extended to me to write a series of articles on the social message of the ancient prophets of Israel. 

Hence this little book.

Perhaps no more propitious time than the present ever existed for the re-enunciation of prophetic ideals. 

Around us we see a weary, desperate civilization, regarding the visions of the prophets as impractical utopias, driven to the brink of chaos, if not annihilation. With nationalism rampant, with barbarian ideals frankly championed by responsible leaders, with social injustice respectable, with machinery dominant, and man hopelessly caught in its meshes, many there are who believe that man has failed to maintain his civilization and that its end is at hand. 

I believe that there is hope for man, hope that he may rise above his failure, and socially

NE of the oldest Jewish congregations in the United States, organized at the close of the Revolutionary War, had prepared for the inauguration of a young, progressive rabbi. Led by the officers, the rabbi stepped into the pulpit.

The congregation gasped. He was hatless. In accordance with a centuries-old custom, Jews at worship wore hats. This young rabbi, a reformer, had removed his. Many of the congregation followed his example. Others did not.

A few resigned and organized a new congregation. One of those who resigned built a large synagogue as a protest against the radicalism of this spiritual leader. Sixty years have passed since this incident and the disaffection over it has not yet completely subsided. When I served in that congregation a few years ago, some of its leaders persisted in wearing their hats during worship. The custom of praying with covered heads was copied by Jews from Mohammedans. In the course of centuries, it acquired divine sanction. The conscientious ob- 15

jectors to its abolition may have felt that a custom instituted by God may not be discarded by man. They may have regarded it as another concealed attempt to destroy the distinctiveness of the Jew. Whatever their reasons, a very unimportant item in the ritual of a congregation stirred up a tempest. Its elimination was bitterly opposed.

The experience of this congregation has countless parallels. A Christian denomination split into two because of a diversity of opinion on partial and total immersion at baptism. A 

Protestant sect was organized because of a difference over the technique of church organization. Several Presbyterian assemblies rose in thunderous protest because a gifted Baptist preacher graced and distinguished one of their church pulpits. Complaints are still being voiced far and wide because an Episcopal bishop permitted the use of his cathedral for worship by men of other denominations. 

Men resist change. They resent the new in everything, but especially in religion, because they know least about it. The tendency to resist the novel is shared by all. 

Men live in constant dread that the unforeseen may jeopardize their

security. The new, the untried, they instinctively fear. Its effect upon their futures is uncertain. The greater the coward, the more intense his hostility to change. Fear of the displeasure of God or gods has been the brake which has prevented change in religion. The above illustrations indicate how in an age of science and education, of communication, of transportation, when men's minds are being deprovincialized and deparochialized, when world contacts and associations are thrust upon all, there yet can remain that vehement, fierce resentment against the new, even among the inessentials in religion. 

Understanding that, we can appreciate the tremendous task which confronted the prophets of Israel. Their concerns were not merely with changing commas in the ritual. They were determined to remould and to recast the ritual, the religious emphasis, and the religious philosophy of a people. They essayed upon that heroic venture in an age when literacy was rare, when it was difficult to reach men's minds, when religion dominated the state and society, when religious loyalties were intense. When, a few years ago, Percy Stickney Grant from his pulpit

in a fashionable New York church attacked some orthodox doctrines of the Episcopal communion and stated that they were untenable in our age, the great denomination to which he belonged, many of whose communicants and whose lay and religious leaders shared his views, found it expedient to regard them as heretical. Imagine, therefore, how blasphemous the prophetic attack upon cult, upon tribal gods, must have seemed to the people of the eighth century before the present era.

There are those who, reading the hostility with which ancient Israel regarded their prophets, may look with scorn upon that people. How insensitive to spiritual values they were! How idolatrous! How avaricious! How heartless!

Such critics are guilty of an error. They are judging the people of Israel by modern and not by ancient standards. To understand the shock that the utterances of the prophets brought to the ancient religious Hebrew, one must endeavor to put oneself in his frame of mind. The prophets denounced everything that was sacred to him.

Imagine the fate of the preacher in many a church in our country who would declare as mythical what most Christians regard as funda-

mental in their religion, and would add that religion was foisted on man by artful priesthoods. That he would be unfrocked, ostracized, is indubitable. Yet many of these assertions have already been made by scholars of integrity, by philosophers, by thinkers, by scientists. Such a preacher would not stand alone.

But the prophets did stand alone. No one had voiced their views before. No one had written in defense of them. The sanction for them was the consciousness that their inspiration came from God. Standing with God, they had the courage to face their fellows with their new ideals. That the prophets were permitted to preach at times, that their messages were preserved in the national library, the Bible, that they influenced religion and morals, the law and the state, is to the credit of the ancient Hebrews, and is an index of their comparative hospitality to new ideas and of their religious genius. Rather than cavil about the hostile reception received by the prophets, when we consider their radical, heretical mission, we cannot but appreciate the forebearance of the people before whom they pleaded. How hospitable would we in America be, aye, how hospitable are we in America,

to those who openly declare their intention to tear our constitution to tatters? The prophets attacked not merely the political constitution but also the religious faith of their believing age. 

At times kings and peoples listened to them. 

Jeremiah denouncing the temple-cult was a thousand times more heretical in his age than the wildest communist is in ours..

In the popular mind, the prophets are apt to be confused with soothsayers, with fortunetellers, or with predictors of events. There were these in ancient Israel. There were the 

Urim and Thummim, the oracles which men consulted. There were the witches, to one of whom Saul went. There must have been attempts to communicate with the dead; Sir 

Oliver Lodge's spiritualism was anticipated.

There were also professional prophets who served as court advisers. But these must not be confused with the prophets, the literary prophets of Israel. The word for prophet in Hebrew is Nabi. It means "to speak forth." The prophets were forth-tellers. They spoke boldly and forthrightly on spiritual and moral issues. 

They were the world's first preachers. They believed themselves divinely inspired and under

compulsion to speak out. They were not members of existing professional prophetic guilds. 

Amos, replying to the priest at Bethel, who had challenged his right to speak, said: "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was a herdsman, and a dresser of sycomore-trees; and the Lord took me from following the flock, and the Lord said unto me, Go, prophesy unto My people Israel." (Amos 7:14, 15.)

Amos did not train to be a prophet, nor was he one by profession. He was a shepherd and heard the voice of God, whose clarion call he could not gainsay. Isaiah likewise felt this divine stirring within him. He, too, did not feel qualified to become a moral teacher in 

Israel. Only after glowing coals had purified his lips and God had reassured him did he feel prepared to embark upon the prophetic ministry. 

Jeremiah, too, could not resist the fire within him and was driven to his thankless but immortal work. The prophets were God-conscious men, sensitive to spiritual values, who could not keep within their souls the moral and spiritual truths that they perceived. They had to speak. 

The divine urge mastered and drove them.

They spoke regardless of consequences. It was

as if a being other than themselves dwelt within them and coerced them to prophesy. They were but the voices through which God made known his truths, God's passive instruments. For their age they redefined God and his demands upon men. How beautifully the prophet Micah expressed it: "It hath been told thee, O man, what is good. And what the Lord doth require of thee, only to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God."

The founder of this new line of prophets was 

Nathan, court prophet to King David. What 

Nathan's functions originally were is a matter of speculation. Very likely he consulted oracles for the king and performed similar duties. But he marched out of the line of his regular routine when David sinned by bringing about the death of Uriah, the Hittite. Then Nathan the prophet burst the bonds of his profession, told the king the parable of the rich man who took the poor man's lamb, and when David, seething with indignation, demanded to know the name of the criminal, Nathan in tones that have reverberated throughout the ages said, "Thou art the man" (2 Sam. 12:7). Thus did Nathan upset the traditions of existing prophetic guilds and blaze

the trail in which Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Micah, and Jesus have walked. Nathan became a moral teacher and a truth-teller, boldly accusing a powerful ruler of moral perfidy at the height of his career. Nathan may be called the father of the prophetic movement in Israel. Elijah and Elisha followed in his footsteps but left no literary records. Amos was the first of the literary prophets whose ideals have weathered the ages. The movement begun by Nathan in the reign of King 

David flowered in the prophets of the eighth century, the first of whom was Amos, the shepherd of Tekoa.

Why did this prophetic movement arise in 

Israel? A generation less steeped in psychology than ours might have answered by urging that the appearance of genius in nations and individuals is inexplicable. This may have ultimately to be our answer. There are biblical scholars who believe that certain environmental factors made inevitable the appearance of prophecy in Israel. Ernest Renan, regarding the

Semites as an inferior race, attributed the prophets' discovery of ethical monotheism to the geography of Palestine. Others attribute it to

historical and social conditions plus. The plus is the unknown quantity, the genius element which thus far defies human research. These scholars contend that the prophetic movement in Israel arose as a result of the clash between the civilization of the desert, where the Hebrew tribes originated, and the civilization of Palestine, between pastoral and agricultural customs, between the simple life of the shepherd and the complex, complicated life of the city-dweller, between the religion of the desert and the religion of Canaan.

Yahveh, who became the God of Israel, was a desert deity. He dwelt on Sinai or on Horeb, desert mountains. Life in the desert was simple and harsh. It was unsettled. The nomad had only sheep for sustenance and these he had to lead to green pastures and fresh water. If his sheep perished, the shepherd perished with them. Abodes were temporary and hence no elaborate homes could be built. None of the civilized arts could flourish. Food was scarce. 

The law of hospitality was dominant. To refuse to shelter a stranger in the vast expanse of the desert, was to condemn him to death.

Nomads dwelt in clans. The clans owned their

herds in common. The tribe ate or starved together. There were no rich. poor-or rather, all were poor. 

There were no 

There was the camaraderie which poverty inspired. The simplicity, the genuine fellowship, the social ownership of all goods, the conditions which living in the desert made necessary-these composed the way of life which Yahveh their desert deity offered, sanctioned, and championed. 

When the Hebrew tribes, wearying of their precarious existence, began slowly to infiltrate into Palestine, their joys at the comparative ease of life were mitigated by their revulsion against those practices which that life made possible. 

There was greater leisure. There was more food. There was even a surplus of food. There was speculating in land. Some men began to own all the land. Others worked for them. 

Soon the workers were exploited. Human beings were avaricious then as now. Castes arose. 

There were rich and poor. For debts, some Hebrews became slaves to others. All these new social phenomena challenged the desertdwellers. Further, the Baals, the Canaanitish gods, products of a richer civilization, with grander rituals, made lavish demands. Deities

of the soil and fertility, through their priests they encouraged and demanded licentious practices. 

Their temples were provided with sacred prostitutes, who were the symbols of fertility, and whose practice of their profession was believed to stimulate the richness of the soil. 

The desert God Yahveh demanded no intricate ritual and permitted no such indulgences in passions. Yahveh knew nothing about crops and fertility. Thus the scene was set for a conflict-between two cultures and two civilizations. Yahveh and Baal were fighting a duel for the soul of a people-aye, for the soul of a civilization. The prophetic bands were the champions of Yahveh, not merely maintaining his supremacy, but also championing the simple, wholesome, socially co-operative way of life, hating luxury and extravagance in living and in religion. Arrayed against these bands were the tendencies of the new civilization, the human love for leisure and the desire of the strong to rule and to take advantage of the weak. The prophets, champions of Yahveh, denounced the cult, championed the underprivileged, hated the castes, defended the weak, the widow, the orphan, the stranger, the slave.

How well Amos fits into this picture! He was a shepherd. He came to the cities, saw the inequalities, the vices of the powerful, the suffering of the weak, the elaborate ritual accompanied with cruel tyranny, and he burst forth into indignation which stirs to this day. "Not this does the Lord require, but let justice well up like water and righteousness like a mighty stream." Thus do scholars explain for moderns the drama in which were forged the souls and the minds of the prophets of Israel. 

The prophets were more than merely champions of Yahveh, the desert-god. They changed the popular conception of him completely, so that his earliest worshipers could not have recognized him. As they redefined the character of Yahveh, they revolutionized the religion of their people. With them, Judaism is born. Moses may have presided at its conception, the prophets at its birth. Before the prophets the religion of the ancient Hebrews was hardly to be distinguished from the religion of other semitic tribes. Their gods were considered to be tribal or national. They lived in sacred stones or mountains. They fought for their people. They were ruthless to other peo-

ples. 

They demanded no ethical conduct.

They were appeased with bribes of sacrifice and of frankincense. They were vain and at times fickle. They had to be courted constantly, not only with the choicest of cattle and of fruits but even with the choicest of life, the first-born. Such simple, primitive gods were the gods of the ancient Hebrews. The prophets led the revolt against these gods. Judaism begins with that revolt. Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, Deutero 

Isaiah, the author of the Book of Jonah, and Jesus, were leaders in that revolution. The result of their efforts was that the nationalistic, ruthless Yahveh became the God of love of all men, the God as defined in the Book of Jonah, the God who demands "lovingkindness and not sacrifice," the God who loves Nineveh as well as Jerusalem. 

It is a commonplace on the part of Christian preachers, theologians, and polemics to contrast the God-idea of the Old Testament with that of the New, and thus to illustrate the superiority of Christianity over Judaism. Equally common is the habit of many writers, even liberal writers, to speak of the ethics of the Jew as summed up by the verse, "An eye for an eye, a tooth for a

tooth, a life for a life," in contrast with the merciful ethics of Jesus. Not only does the ethics of Jesus come from Jewish life and teaching and environment, not only is the ethics of the Jew not limited by the Old Testament, but the ethics of the Old Testament cannot be limited to the law of retaliation. Against its ethics the prophets revolted. The lex talionis was universal among all peoples at that time in world history. Long after the Jews under the influence of prophetic leadership discarded it, it was retained by many peoples, now Christian, and to this day it is written in the philosophy of law of many nations, including our own. The cruel, avenging deity, found in the pages of the Bible, is no more the God of the modern Jew than he was the God of Jesus of Nazareth. 

That God-idea represents a step in the evolution of religion of the ancient Hebrews. All other religions passed through similar stages. 

It was the accepted God-idea until the prophets redefined God for the Hebrews and for the Western world. Not the avenging, tribal, narrow, cult-demanding deity of primitive peoples, but the just, loving, merciful, universal, socialjustice demanding God of the prophets, of the

author of Jonah, of Jesus, is and has been the professed God of the Jew, centuries before the birth of Christianity, before the New Testament was written.) This God-idea, challenging the world, was developed and defined in the souls of the prophets and given by them to their people at the expense of their life, of their liberty, and of their happiness.

Though the prophetic teachings shook the ancient Hebrew religious world, and though the prophets were lonely, suffering men, yet their work bore fruit, as their ideals were written into the constitution of their people. (The Book of 

Deuteronomy with its attempts to establish monotheism, by outlawing all shrines except the temple in Jerusalem, reveals the effect of prophetic teachings. The biblical law to protect the poor, the widow, the stranger (Lev. 

19), the laws about the freedom of slaves, the laws about the return of the land to the original owners after fifty years, the Sabbath day of rest for master and for slave and even for animals, the command to "love thy neighbor as thyself," all coming after the prophets, prove that their teachings were not in vain among their own people.

Even as the legislation in Israel was affected by the prophets, so also the national literature. 

The Bible, the national library of the ancient Hebrews, commences with the story of creation of the entire world. Rabbinical commentators point out that God did not create Jews first nor white men first. (He created Adam, man, so that no man, no race, and no nation should ever lay claim to superiority. The creation story in the first chapter of the Bible assumes the existence of a God of the universe, who is spiritual, and also assumes the equality of all races before him. This reveals the effect prophetic teachings had on Israel. The Jacob-Israel story likewise testifies to that. Jacob is a very despicable character. He cheats his aged and blind father. He mulcts his patrimony from his brother. Such a trickster becomes the founder of a religious people and their hero. Prior to prophetic teaching, stealing from other tribes other than one's own (Jacob and Esau were symbols of tribes and nations) not only was sanctioned but the successful stealer became a tribal hero. After the prophets had taught, and their teachings had penetrated the masses of Israel, a hero like Jacob was unpalatable and unsavory to the new

Hebrew conscience. By prophetic writers his story was rewritten. He was given a new name, Israel, the champion of God. A change of name implied a change of character. That Jacob had to become Israel, the deceiver a champion of God, testifies that the spiritual and ethical seeds of the prophets had fallen on fertile soil among their own people.

Why is Jesus among the prophets of Israel? 

Christian scholars will say that he is the greatest of the prophets and Jewish scholars will insist that he added nothing to the religion of the author of Jonah. It is difficult to measure the comparative greatness of men. Without Amos, there could have been no Jesus. If we judge by the influence men have exerted, then we have an inadequate standard. The influence that men have exerted may have been due to extraneous circumstances, or to gifted followers. As far as the Jewish scholars' views are concerned, it may be said that after Amos and Hosea had spoken, everything in the prophetic message was implicit in their words. The prophets not only amplified the implications of ethical monotheism. 

They contributed themselves, their personalities, their way of saying their message. Their bold-

ness and courage and life gave the prophetic message reality and vitality. Surely Jesus did that, and therefore belongs in the ranks of the prophets of Israel. 

Difficult as it is to make changes in ritual and theology today, even more difficult was it in the eighth century before the present era. The prophets boldly assumed the mission of converting the religion of their people from primitive Semitism to ethical monotheism. They thus became immortal figures, benefactors of the race. 

Though the task they undertook was gigantic, they neither faltered nor feared. For us remains the task of weaving their aspirations and hopes into the fabric of our civilization.

AMOS Spiritual Pioneer, Monotheist, and Prophet of Social Justice

AMOS Spiritual Pioneer, Monotheist, and Prophet of Social Justice 

IF ANYTHING might be called the symbol of America's prosperity, it was the Easter 

Parade in Atlantic City in 1929. To that shore resort, the social élite of the nation had thronged for an interlude of play to herald the advent of spring. Weeks before the festival, hotel reservations had been oversubscribed. When 

Easter dawned, the boardwalk must have been crowded. Tailors and designers had been busy for months and months to prepare the new styles which a wealthy clientele demanded. Jewelry, silks, all the signs of luxury and wealth must have been in evidence. Imagine if some unkempt factory toiler, haggard from overwork, dressed in tattered garb, had mounted a box on the boardwalk and had commenced to berate the crowd. Imagine him, having seen his coworkers laid off, and machinery replacing them, his wages remaining static, while the profits of his firm trebled and its stock rose in value on 37

the exchange boards. Envision him brooding over the prosperity of a nation which masked the suffering of his comrades, proceeding to air his grievances publicly. Think of him denouncing the religion of the crowds, who were satisfied to carouse on unearned profits, while slums and poverty existed about them. Conceive of him attacking churches, synagogues, and cathedrals, all religious institutions and leaders, for their complaisance in the face of social iniquities. See him denouncing the prevalent political corruption and scorning the society which encouraged greed and avarice, applauded the strong, trampled upon the weak, and yet believed its conduct to be just and ethical. How long would such an infuriated speaker have been tolerated by these gaily appareled, virtuous, and smug crowds? Would he not have been arrested for creating a public nuisance, branded as a "Red," or sent to a sanitarium?

Such must have been the crowd's reaction to Amos, the shepherd of Tekoa, the dresser of sycamore trees, who at Beth-El, where the élite of Israel had gathered to celebrate a great festival midst revelry and feasting, rose and de-

AMOS 

nounced the religion, the morality, and the manners of his countrymen. How could anyone accuse them of impiety? Had they not just brought their fat rams to the altars of Yahveh? 

Why assail them? Had their armies not been victorious, an evidence of divine favor? Did they not enjoy prosperity? How dare this simple shepherd rise and excoriate them at the moment of their greatest triumph? 

To Amos' simple shepherd's soul, the revelry and misery which then as now dwelt side by side in populous cities, must have come as a shocking revelation. In the desert, life was hard and simple. Surplus was unknown. What a man had, he shared with his dear ones, with his clan, with his tribe, with the stranger who came into his tent. To refuse so to share meant to doom one's fellow-man to death. The barest necessities of life were available to all. To feast while one's neighbor starved was unknown in the desert moral code. Holding such a philosophy, Amos' righteous indignation at the class distinctions and inequalities of wealth which he saw in the crowded cities of Israel is understandable. His moral nature rose in thunderous eloquent revolt. These crowds trampling upon

the poor, selling the needy for silver, exploiting the weak; these fashionable, luxury-loving women, indifferent to the miserable slums at their gates; these barons driving hard bargains with the humble and carousing with their illgotten gains these are immoral, ungodly. 

These cannot win the favor of Yahveh no matter how rich their burnt-offerings, for Yahveh covets neither feasts nor solemn assemblies, neither peace-offerings nor fat beasts, neither robed choirs nor elaborate ritual, but that "justice well up like water and righteousness like a mighty stream." 

When Amos saw that wherever he went the situation at Beth-El was repeated, rich living side by side with the poor, religion and cultobservance considered synonymous, indifference to human suffering, social iniquities sanctioned and approved by the nation, he knew that its punishment was certain and its doom inevitable. 

His God, a moral God, demanded obedience to his moral laws above everything. His God would punish social immorality. Amos saw that there were moral laws in the universe which, like natural laws, carried penalties for those who violated them. So thoroughly foul

AMOS 

and rotten seemed the social fabric of his people that he believed its destruction was imminent. 

To those who said that Yahveh, the God of Israel, would not afflict Israel Amos thundered that Yahveh is a God of justice and would punish all wrongdoers, even his own people. Amos hastened to add that his God deemed the Ethiopians and Egyptians as precious as the Hebrews. 

For to him it appeared that God ruled over the universe and not over one nation, and that all peoples were his children. With these ideals of 

Amos, the tribal semitic God of the ancient Hebrews was doomed. He was replaced with a God who rules over all humanity. Amos' God was universal and with his teaching of the universality of one God, Judaism was born. Thus did this bold prophet destroy forever the primitive religion of the ancient Semites and sow the seed for the development of ethical monotheism. 

Even as in Amos' generation, so too in our own, prosperity is not a sign of godliness. Like

Amos, we have learned through bitter experience that a nation which violates the moral laws of the universe cannot avert the day of reckoning. Poverty and luxury cannot continue to live side by side. Palatial residences and

miserable tenements cannot continue in one community. Increasing capital and decreasing consuming power are violations of moral as well as of economic laws. We had no prophets like 

Amos in 1929 to warn us of impending disaster.

If we had them, their still small voices were unheard in the mad hysteria of prosperity. But the day of God, of reckoning, has come. We as a nation have tolerated social iniquity and the doom has descended upon us. Our suffering is due to a lack of moral will and of spiritual vision. In the words of Amos our famine is "not a famine of bread nor a thirst for water but of hearing the words of our Lord." Our famine is spiritual and moral. We have more food, more clothing, more fuel than we can possibly consume and men die of starvation, go clad in rags, and freeze in the winter. We have brains, capital, machinery-everything to make physical life comfortable and secure. We only lack godliness, moral character, spiritual vision. We are a nation of sinners and now we pay the penalty. If only like Amos, modern prophets could thunder forth to a people whose eyes would see and whose ears hear, and whose hearts feel and minds understand, "Seek ye

AMOS 



the Lord and live" "Establish justice in the land." Thus might we atone for our sins and avert the impending doom and chaos.

Nor can this social justice be limited to America. It must include the entire family of nations. The moral laws of the universe apply not to America alone. Even as God is not only the God of Israel, so he is not only the God of 

America. Those who cry for isolation, for

American self-sufficiency, are reverting to the worship of national gods, however noble the creeds they utter or however universal the inscriptions over their sanctuaries. The moral law applies to all nations. (Justice for mankind must be the new slogan. Not Israel first or alone, not America first or alone, but Ethiopian and Egyptian, German and Japanese-all these have a right to justice, and no human being who claims to worship the God of the universe can ignore that right.

Who Through the Parable of Jonah Speaks an Immortal Truth 

THERE have been times when a sideshow was so successful that it became the circus. 

This is true of the Book of Jonah. The whale's swallowing of Jonah, intended by the unknown author to be an incident in the preaching of a great message, has become for many the Book of Jonah. That the whale swallowed Jonah is believed by multitudes. The whale did more than that. 

He swallowed the moral of the

Book of Jonah. Its author endeavored to sum up, in a tale, the spiritual teachings of the prophets. His readers loved the story, his sideshow, so much that they have ignored his circus, his unexcelled vision. If a fraction of the energy still expended in some theological circles to establish the historicity of the Jonah narrative would be utilized toward realizing its lofty philosophy, what a finer world would result. 

The Jonah parable was but a vehicle for a great spiritual truth. Multitudes are concerned but with the vehicle while its truth slumbers. 117

The prophet of the Exile, the second Isaiah, had tried to induce his people to accept a world mission. This mission was not to bring glory or power, but suffering and perhaps disgrace. 

It was to be assumed humbly and not arrogantly. 

It was to be the service of God, by testifying to his desire for social justice, world peace, and spiritual values. But the prophet, of course, was a visionary. When the Babylonian captivity was over and some exiles returned from Babylon to share in the rebuilding of Jerusalem, they had no time for Utopian dreamers. Practical men were needed, priests and not prophets. 

Practical measures had to be taken to save the people from the fate which had overtaken Samaria. Ideals had their place, but the time demanded that ideals be scrapped temporarily. 

What familiar language! Men have always lacked the courage to give ideals a chance. They have always catered to the lowest phases of human nature. They have feared to put their trust in the highest, in spiritual values, in God.

That choice was not a challenge to service as Isaiah interpreted it, but a privilege which assured eventual national prosperity. With this intense nationalism came a feeling of superiority to other peoples, always a product of chauvinism. 

The author of the Book of Jonah by his beautiful tale challenged the narrow nationalism. which was destroying the spiritual fibre of his people, summed up the universal teachings of the prophets and again pleaded that the narrow, arrogant nationalism be supplanted by the consecration to human service and by the recognition that the love of God is extended to all peoples, even to Babylonians, their most hated foes, who had destroyed their kingdom. Truly Jesus lived as a Jew. 

Even as Jesus lived as a Jew, so did he die as a Jew. His tragic death fits in perfectly with the historic Jewish conception of the messiah. 

The word messiah was initially applied to the kings of Israel. It means "anointed one." It was the duty of the ancient kings of Israel to save their people from their foes. Whenever 

Israel lived in exile or whenever tyrants cruelly oppressed them, the people hoped and prayed for a redeemer, or a messiah, who would restore their national independence and who would expel from Palestine the conqueror or the oppressor.

During the reign of the cruel and brutal Pontius Pilate in Jerusalem, the poverty-stricken masses of that city, suffering and groaning under his tyranny, were praying and hoping

137 that God would send them a redeemer who would liberate them from the Roman and his legions, and that a descendant of David would again sit on the throne of Israel, and make possible the renewed grandeur and glory of the kingdom of Judaea. When Jesus, therefore, came to
