RESEARCH DEPARTMENT LOUIS C. DORWEILER, JR., Director 

The Legislative Research Committee is a joint committee of the legislature, meeting quarterly at the State Capitol and giving advance consideration to problems expected to confront the next legislature.

The Committee (1) acts as a clearing house for current legislative problems by receiving proposals for research studies; (2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all proposals; (3) disseminates advance information on these problems to other legislators, the governor and the public by means of committee and research reports; and (4) reports to the legislature one month in advance of the regular session. 

The Research Department of the Legislative Research Committee is organized to provide an unbiased, factual source of information with regard to problems which may be acted upon by the legislature. This department is engaged in objective fact finding under the general supervision of members of the Committee.

INTRODUCTION 

On March 8 and 9 the Wisconsin Legislative Council, the North 

Dakota Legislative Research Committee, and the Minnesota Legislative

Research Committee assembled in the House Chambers of the State Capitol in St. Paul to discuss common problems and exchange information on various programs conducted by their governments. The attendance at the conference was excellent. All eleven members of the North Dakota delegation were in attendance; eleven of Wisconsin's twelve delegates (the other member was confined to his home by illness) were present; and all of the Minnesota Committee, with the exception of one who was out of the state, attended the meeting. Other Minnesota legislators availed themselves of the general invitation extended them to attend and participate in the conference. In addition to Committee and 

Council members, Wisconsin and North Dakota delegations each included state administrative officials to explain in detail some of the programs being discussed. Minnesota also called upon some of its administrative officials for similar information. 

The conference was opened with an address of welcome by the 

Honorable Luther W. Youngdahl, Governor of the State of Minnesota. 

Appropriate responses were made by Representative E. J. Langley for the North Dakota members and Senator Gordon Bubolz of the Wisconsin delegation.

The conference was very informal in nature and consisted of a series of six round table discussions, each covering a broad area of governmental activity. A representative of each state acted as a discussion leader at each round table. Three panels ran simultaneously on the afternoon of March 8, and three others the next morning which members were free to attend according to their primary interests. On the afternoon of March 9, the entire body assembled in the House Chamber where a summary of each panel discussion was presented by a discussion leader. Prior to the presentation of these summaries, Minnesota's Commissioner of Administration fulfilled a request from Wisconsin and North Dakota delegates by explaining the Minnesota Reorganization Act. 

The following report summarizes briefly the discussions which took place. It will be noted that there is rather wide variation in the course and form which the discussions took, due largely, perhaps, to the absence of restrictions and the generally informal nature of 8/9/1 858464

the panels.

In some cases, problems common to the states were examined in discussion and attempts made to reach a common understanding on the factors involved and possible solutions. This is perhaps best illustrated in the game and fish discussion between Minnesota and North Dakota members at the round table on Conservation, Forestry and Public Domain. In other cases, one state gained valuable information from the experience of another as in the case of the Wisconsin presentation of its staggered motor vehicle registration system, a new development in the field which was of great interest to Minnesota and North Dakota members. Some panels arrived at definite conclusions, while in others no conclusions were drawn. What may be called the general benefit of the conference, that is, the opportunity to know the legislators of other states facing the same or similar problems, was perhaps most valuable of all.

The rules of the Minnesota Legislative Research Committee require that a report be made to all members of the Minnesota Legislature following each regular or special meeting. This conference was part of a special meeting of the Minnesota Committee. Hence this report has two separate sections, the first summarizing the tri-state conference, and the second reporting on the transactions of the Committee at its own business meeting.

SECTION I Tri-State Conference 

The conference was opened with an address by The Honorable Luther W. Youngdahl, Governor of Minnesota, extending a warm welcome to the visiting legislators. "Your joint meeting here is, in my opinion, an important stride in the science and art of government", he said. "It is wonderful that the states are meeting because of the importance and necessity of uniform action in meeting our needs. We can learn much from each other". In continuing, Governor Youngdahl pointed out that the three states have similar problems in roads, forestry, conservation, public welfare, and taxation; and that the combined efforts of the groups assembled here will assist in passing proper legislation to solve these problems. Such meetings as this can point the way toward eliminating difficulties such as the one which arose during the last session of the Minnesota legislature between Minnesota and South Dakota over pheasant hunting.

The Governor pointed out that people are becoming more and more interdependent and that meetings of this kind can perhaps point the way for similar relationships on the national and international scale. 

In concluding, Governor Youngdahl said that a lot of confusion has resulted from the lack of careful study, thought, and research. "Groups such as those assembled here", he stated, "have an opportunity to eliminate many of the confusions that exist during a legislative session". 

Senator Gordon Bubolz responded on behalf of the Wisconsin Legislative Research Council, thanking the Minnesota Committee for the hospitality extended and the Governor for giving of his valuable time to counsel with the delegates. He then discussed briefly the Wisconsin 

Council. Created by the 1947 legislature, it is composed of five senators and seven assemblymen and operates through sub-committees covering various fields of state activity. 

Representative E. J. Langley responded for the North Dakota

Legislative Research Committee. He remarked on the splendid reception which had been accorded its members and extended thanks to Governor Youngdahl for his welcoming remarks and attendance at the opening session of the tri-state conference. He discussed briefly the work of the North 

Dakota Committee and what could be achieved by the states in working together on common problems. -1-

Participants in this round table discussed Bang's disease control measures, compulsory pasteurization laws, hoof and mouth disease, and weed control. Such interest developed in these common problems that the group agreed to meet again at 8:30 Tuesday morning for further discussion before the day's assigned schedule of panels convened. Bang's Disease Control 

Attention was first directed to the Wisconsin program for Bang's disease control which is essentially similar to the programs of the other states, consisting chiefly of test-and-slaughter program and calfhood vaccination. It was emphasized in the presentation that in a country where government is by the consent of the governed any program for the control of Bang's disease, though it may not be what science considers ideal, must be acceptable to the farmer in order to be practicable.

In 1934, the initial testing in Wisconsin disclosed an infection rate of about 4.8%. From 1934 to July 1, 1945, Wisconsin's program for the control of Bang's disease cost the state $462,621.69 in operating costs and $2,104,859.39 in indemnities. In addition, the 

United States Bureau of Animal Industry spent in Wisconsin for the same purposes $1,630,815.36 and $7,676,354.39 respectively. 

In 1945, after a combined expenditure of approximately $12,000,000, the incidence indicated by testing was 5.2%. Such discouraging results cast doubt upon the effectiveness of test-and-slaughter as a control -2-

measure. However, factors in testing were present which no doubt influenced this apparent rise. In later years fewer cattle were tested, and those tested were very largely members of herds which at one time or another had been infected. Regardless, however, it was felt that in terms of public health the money spent was not wasted. 

Other figures on the cost of the control program were also presented. In Wisconsin the personal property tax on cattle yielded $5 million in 1945 and $6 million in 1946. About 5% of this tax revenue from cattle was spent for Bang's disease control. The current appropriation of $323,000 is divided three ways: $173,000 for vacci-. nation, $100,000 for indemnities, and $50,000 to the University for research. Present research is directed at three phases of the problem: determining whether Strain 19 vaccine may transmit infection to humans, the developing of better means of diagnosis of Brucellosis, and ascertaining what portion of reactors are actual carriers of Bang's disease. 

Wisconsin has had some experience with groups of farmers deciding they were unwilling to go along with the test-and-slaughter program and subsequently taking the steps withdrawing their county from the Area Plan. Portage County, for example, withdrew. Withdrawal requires agreement of 65% of the cattle owners in an area, whereas coming under the plan requires 75% agreement. 

Official vaccination in Wisconsin is of two kinds: vaccination, after testing, and vaccination without prior testing. The pre-vaccinal test is required for sales outside the state. The opinion was expressed that the pre-vaccinal test is not worthwhile, especially in the case of young calves. It is anticipated that a bill will be introduced at the next session of the Wisconsin legislature exempting vaccinated animals from blood tests for the 12 months immediately following vaccination.

Wisconsin has requirements for testing prior to the sale or other such movement of cattle which are stricter than usual. All cattle sold, all cattle moved to public pastures, and all cattle moved from farm to farm must have had a negative reaction to the agglutination test. stock.

In connection with indemnities, it was pointed out that Wisconsin has no limit on the appraised value of animals but the indemnity itself is limited to $25 from the state and $25 from the Bureau of Animal Industry. 

In Wisconsin it was felt that the eventual method would be eradication of Bang's disease through test-and-slaughter but, until that goal is reasonably near, vaccination has much to recommend it. 

However, it was recognized that it may be dangerous to make vaccination compulsory because Strain 19 vaccine contains a living organism and, if vaccination were compulsory, the state might be held liable for an outbreak of the disease.

In general, the Bang's disease control programs are much the same in the three states. This may be attributed to the fact that participation by the United States Bureau of Animal Industry in combatting the problem, together with payment of Federal indemnities, provides an incentive for the use of the test-and-slaughter program, the approach which has become basic in Bang's disease control in most states.

North Dakota also does some vaccinating at state and federal expense. In Minnesota all vaccination is done at the expense of the owner. North Dakota lists lower maximum appraisals of condemned cattle than does Minnesota; Wisconsin has no limit on appraisals, but has the same limits on indemnities which may be paid as Minnesota has. North 

Dakota, in common with Minnesota, is having difficulty in hiring veterinarians. Perhaps for this reason it permits supervised laymen to conduct the agglutination test. 

It was pointed out that experiments are being conducted in Minnesota to determine the validity of a ring test for Brucellosis developed in Germany and Denmark. This test is applied to milk to indicate whether or not it came from infected animals. Carlton County is an experimental area and early indications are that there is a high degree of agreement between the results of the ring test and the agglutination test under the area plan. If the ring test proves valid, it is expected to be an economical method of testing milk at collection centers and so determine if infection exists in any of the herds supplying milk to that center. Suspected herds can be blood tested to determine which animals are reactors.

Compulsory Pasteurization 

Closely related to the discussion of Bang's disease control was the question of compulsory pasteurization of milk. The delegation -4-

from Minnesota pointed out that a demand exists to require pasteurization of all fluid milk (existing Minnesota laws require pasteurization of milk and cream used in the manufacture of butter; and pasteurization, heat processing, or aging of cheeses). However, the delegations from North 

Dakota and Wisconsin, while approving pasteurization, were reluctant to favor making it compulsory. It was pointed out that Wisconsin now, through local ordinances, requires the pasteurization of 93% of its milk. Fears were expressed that compulsory pasteurization would scare consumers and perhaps lead other states to boycott the milk from these dairy states. Wisconsin has a compulsory pasteurization bill pending which would apply only to fluid milk sold for consumption as such. It would exempt certified milk. In addition, the bill would not affect the small producers who sell from their farms, if their herds are state and federally accredited as disease-free. It is estimated that this bill would boost to 98% the proportion of milk that is pasteurized in Wisconsin. 

It was generally agreed among the three state delegations that sound sanitation practices and disease-free herds are of prime importance that it is wrong to use pasteurization to cover up failures in these essentials. However, it was felt that pasteurization is needed to protect against any failure to discover diseased animals and to protect the consumer against other sources of contamination.

It was emphasized that money indemnities are of little use to the Mexican peon because his cattle are needed and used primarily as draft animals. It was also stated that mules are ill suited to filling these needs.

Under present conditions of high mobility of population, both human and animal, it may not be possible to prevent the wholesale spread of hoof and mouth disease into this country. Weed Control 

Attention was next given to the matter of weed control. The view was expressed that there is a need for a change of philosophy in this matter. Present laws are directed toward private action on private lands, while public lands and railway rights of way are largely neglected. However, general feeling was that present laws in the three states are adequate but fail through poor enforcement. It was pointed out that the members of town boards are often reluctant to act against their neighbors. In addition, it was noted that recent developments in weed sprays may greatly reduce the problem.

ASSESSMENT AND TAXATION 

Monday, March 8, 1948 1:30 p.m. 

Discussion Leaders: 

Assemblyman Grover C. Broadfoot, Wisconsin

Senator John Conrad, North Dakota 

Representative Joseph J. Daun, Minnesota

Three major subjects were discussed at this round table, namely, Minnesota's county assessor and supervisor of assessment system; Wisconsin's experience in taxing natural gas; and North Dakota's attempt to tax cooperatives. With the exception of a detailed presentation of Minnesota's assessing system by G. Howard Spaeth, Commissioner of Taxation in Minnesota, the discussion on these three subjects was carried on by delegates to the conference. 

Minnesota's County Assessor and Supervisor of Assessment System

Mr. Spaeth pointed out that for many years the Department of 

Taxation and others in Minnesota had recognized the need for improving the law governing the methods of assessing property, but it was not until 1947 that such legislation was enacted. 

One of the major factors contributing to the enactment of this legislation was consideration during the same session of a new school aid law. This law provided for the distribution of a substantial amount of state funds according to a method based on the assessed value of property in the respective school districts. Thus for equality and fairness some coordination and uniformity of assessment among the 2700 local assessors was necessary. The objective of the act is to place the assessment of property on a more scientific and uniform basis throughout the state.

The act requires that each county employ either a county assessor or a county supervisor of assessment. In either case, local assessors are retained. Thirty-seven counties have selected a county assessor and fifty have chosen a supervisor of assessment. The authority to select the assessor or supervisor of assessment and establish the salary to be paid for this work rests with the County Board, but the appointment must be approved by the State Commissioner of Taxation. 

With few exceptions, the men selected by the local boards have received this approval. First appointments expire on December 1, 1948, and -7-

succeeding appointments are for a term of four years. The County Board of Commissioners may terminate these appointments at any time on charges by the Commissioner of Taxation of inefficiency or neglect of duties. 

After appointing a county assessor, the county may, if he has served two years, abolish the office and appoint a supervisor of assessors if it is determined that the county would be equally well served. 

Some of the important duties of the county supervisor of assessment are as follows: (1) confer with and advise local assessors as to their duties under the law and keep them informed of changes in it; (2) assist and instruct local assessors in the preparation and use of proper land maps and record cards; (3) assist and instruct local assessors in the preparation of assessment books; (4) prepare detailed topographical maps and land value maps; (5) examine conveyance of land and, from the information obtained, recommend to the County Board of Equalization necessary changes in individual assessments or aggregate valuations; (6) keep informed on the true value of various items of personal property and furnish such guidance to local assessors; and (7) assist the County Board of Equalization at its request or that of the Commissioner of Taxation by investigating requests for (a) reduction of valuation, (b) abatements and settlements of taxes, and by searching for omitted property.

Many of the duties of the county assessor are the same as those of the supervisor of assessment. However, he is given more direct and definite responsibility. Whereas the relationship of the supervisor of assessment to the local assessor is an advisory one, the county assessor is required to make all assessments, basing them upon appraised values reported to him by local assessors. He is also required personally to view and determine value of property difficult to assess, to make all changes ordered by local boards of review, to enter all assessments in the assessment books, and to prepare all charts, cards, maps and forms requested by the Commissioner of Taxation, etc.

To aid further in improving assessment practices the Department of Taxation conducts assessing schools in all counties of the state and, in conjunction with other organizations, sponsors an annual assessing school at the University of Minnesota. It also prepares a detailed assessors' manual and other material to aid in placing assessment on a more uniform basis.

Although the law has been in effect for only a short period of time, it is already bringing in considerably more revenue, especially through the discovery of omitted property, and is resulting in greater uniformity of assessments throughout the state. -8-

Some discussion was devoted to the Iowa system under which the County Auditor is the ex-officio assessor in each county. Under this system there are no local assessors except in towns of 10,000 population or more.

North Dakota delegates pointed out that in 1919 their state enacted a law providing for a tax supervisor in each county, appointed by the Board of County Commissioners and approved by the State Tax Commissioner. In 1923 this law was amended to provide for such an official in each of the six judicial districts as it was felt that one in each county was not necessary. Provision for supervision of local assessors either at the county or district level was repealed by the 1927 legislature.

It was the consensus of this panel that Minnesota's new law will result in better assessment. The group also felt that the county assessor was more desirable than the county supervisor of assessment, and that when units of government were of sufficient size to employ full-time assessors, they might well be exempted from the application of either the county assessor or county supervisor system. 

Wisconsin's Experience in Taxing Natural Gas

The experience of Wisconsin in taxing natural gas was the second subject discussed by this group. Railroad interests, the Solid 

Fuels Institute, and coal distributors were the principal interests behind the legislation to tax natural gas which was enacted prior to entry of this commodity into Wisconsin. The tax was first considered by the 

1939 legislature but was not enacted into law until 1943 when a tax of 74 per 1,000 cubic feet of natural gas distributed was instituted. The law also included what was termed a "home rule bill" which provided that a franchise from each township or municipality through which the pipe line passed was necessary in addition to a certificate of convenience and necessity from the Public Service Commission. 

The tax measure provided for collection of the tax by the distributing company. The state-retained 35% of the proceeds to meet the cost of administering the law, and 65% was returned to the municipalities on the theory that it would help care for the unemployment allegedly occasioned by the use of gas for fuel. Natural gas did not enter Wisconsin until 1946 and then reached only the city of Burlington in the southern part of the state. Inasmuch as the law was repealed in the 1947 session, only $15,056.72 was collected. 

Natural gas property is now subject to assessment by the Department of Taxation which also collects the tax and apportions it to the various communities.

Repeal of this tax was advocated by housewives and utilities who pointed out that the product was cleaner and had more heat units than other fuels, and that it would be in the better interests of the consumer if the tax was repealed.

The consensus of the Wisconsin group discussing this tax was that it had been poor legislation. Their point of view is perhaps best summarized by one delegate to the conference who stated, "we would not have many garages or filling stations if we had taxed the automobile to protect the village blacksmith".

The home rule bill was also repealed by the 1947 session as impracticable in its application because it made it possible for any local unit of government to stop further extension of natural gas facilities. 

North Dakota has one gas pipe line entering the state. The gas consumed there is used principally as fuel. The state has levied no special tax on natural gas. The facilities that transport the gas are subject to an ad valorem tax and any gas sold, of course, is subject to North Dakota's 2% sales tax. 

North Dakota's Attempt to Tax Cooperatives

North Dakota was the only one of the three states represented at the conference that has attempted to tax the earnings of cooperatives. 

In the discussion of this type of taxation North Dakota delegates pointed out that an attempt was made during the 1947 legislative session to apply the state's graduated corporate income tax to the savings or profits of cooperatives which were not distributed in cash to patrons. This legislation passed the North Dakota Senate but was defeated in the House of Representatives.

In the general discussion on taxing cooperatives there was considerable debate on the legal aspects of taxing their retained earnings. The known and often cited advantages accruing to them by virtue of their tax exempt status were pointed out, as was the determined opposition of the cooperatives to attempts to tax such earnings, savings, gains, profits, etc., as are retained by them and not distributed to their patrons.

The major part of the discussion evolved about the question of whether co-ops could continue to evade their full income tax responsibilities in those cases in which they are in direct business competition with other business corporations who must pay the state -10-

corporate income tax. It was mentioned that corporate practices in the past in business operations and tax evasion had brought about very strict regulation and control of such corporations. In the light of this observation, the question was raised as to whether continued refusal by some co-ops to accept their full tax responsibility may not eventually result in the same strict regulation and control of all cooperatives. 

Tax Sharing of State-Collected Revenues in Minnesota 

After the discussion on the three subjects above, Minnesota's sharing of cigarette and liquor tax receipts with municipalities, provided for by the 1947 legislature, was explained by Mr. C. C. Ludwig of the League of Minnesota Municipalities. 

One-third of the three cent tax on cigarettes is returned to the several counties, cities, villages and boroughs of the state on the basis of the ratio of their populations to the state population. For the purposes of apportionment to counties, population not residing in incorporated places is used as the basis for distribution. Incidental to the cigarette tax discussion, a North Dakota delegate mentioned that current estimates were that revenue from North Dakota's cigarette tax has increased $30,000 per month since the enactment of the Minnesota tax.

Under a 1947 Minnesota law, 30% of the receipts of the tax on liquor and wine are shared with the counties, cities, villages and boroughs of the state. The basis of apportionment used is the ratio of their population to the state total. This sharing differs, however, from that under the cigarette tax law in that counties are required to distribute their apportioned share among their constituent organized townships on the basis of the ratio of each township's population to the total township population in the county. * This tax sharing was enacted in connection with an increase of state liquor taxes. -11-

Monday, March 8, 1948 1:30 p.m. 

Discussion Leaders: 

Senator Gordon Bubolz, Wisconsin

Representative Donnell Haugen, North Dakota 

Senator A. L. Almen, Minnesota

The panel discussion on Education devoted most of its time to school district reorganization problems and the legislation adopted to meet them by each state during the last session. Consideration was also given to the matter of expanding higher education facilities and meeting the teacher shortage, particularly in rural elementary schools. 

School District Reorganization 

Representatives from each state summarized reorganization legislation and its effects to date in their home state. The discussion revealed substantial similarity in the approaches of the three states to the problem.

In summarizing the Wisconsin law, it was pointed out that the county boards of supervisors were required to appoint county school committees of six members each to study school district organization, draw proposed plans, hold hearings, and effect such changes as in their opinion would improve the educational facilities. Wisconsin does not require submission of the plan to any state agency, nor to voters, for approval. In effect, however, the State Educational Commission has considerable influence through advice and expert information which they are frequently called upon to give. The plans of proposed new districts were not made subject to referendum because of the experience in some other states, notably Oregon and Kansas.

Mr. Toepel, Executive Secretary of the Wisconsin Educational 

Commission, pointed out that all of the Wisconsin counties had appointed committees and all of them were working. Efforts have been made to have the committees think on a countywide basis, rather than along sectional lines. The Wisconsin State Commission has attempted to stimulate an overall survey through a research project, that is, by asking the county school committees to get together information on the enrollment tax base, population trends, transportation, capital investments, and other such factors which materially affect the school situation. -12-

Mr. Engum of the Department of Education outlined the procedures under the Minnesota law. At the state level, the Board of 

Education appointed a nine-member Statewide Advisory Commission on Reorganization. The Advisory Commission was set up to advise on goals and procedures. 

Locally, the law required the county superintendent to call meetings of all school board members within the county to vote on the question of creating a county school survey committee. Sixty-three counties have organized these survey committees, four are exempt from the provisions of the law, and twenty voted against organizing for the study. There is feeling now that many of these counties would probably vote in favor of electing a survey committee if given another opportunity (which is now precluded by the law). In those counties which voted to organize and study, a committee of nine, five of whom were from the rural areas affected, was selected. The survey committee thus chosen is required to study, hold meetings, and make a tentative report to officials in the affected areas and to the State Advisory Commission. 

After holding hearings on the tentative report, and receiving and considering the suggestions of the Advisory Commission, the County School 

Survey Committee will submit (on or before November 1, 1948,) a final report with recommendations for reorganization. It is within the power of the Committee to recommend that no changes be made. If reorganization is recommended, the superintendent of the county in which the major portion of the proposed district is situated must arrange an election within nine months. Rural and urban groups will vote separately for or against reorganization. Majority vote of both groups in the affected area is necessary to carry reorganization. Three changes were suggested which might improve the operation of the law. The opinion was expressed that inadequate time and effort had been spent in acquainting the school boards with the provisions of the law, a factor which probably was responsible in some instances for decisions not to organize survey committees. In addition, the law, which gave the counties only a single chance to organize and study, might be changed to allow another vote. There is evidence that sentiment for a reorganization survey has grown in counties which rejected their first and only opportunity. Another criticism of the law is that its tempo was too fast, resulting in inadequate time between dates when steps in the studying, reporting, and voting process must be completed. 

Representative Haugen, Representative Langley, and State Commissioner of Education Grimsrud outlined the North Dakota plan. It requires the creation of county committees with as many members as there are county commissioner districts. Usually there are five. In common with the legislative intent of the other states, the North Dakota legislature desired that the administrative reorganization act be as -13-

democratic as possible.

To this end it has only one mandatory feature, namely, that the county committee must make the survey. The North Dakota law allows county committees nine months for study and nine months to develop and evaluate plans which will establish school districts of sufficient size and resources to afford modern educational opportunity. 

The plan as developed must be approved by the state committee. The voters of the proposed new districts must return majorities in both an urban and a rural election to put the plan into operation. 

It was realized in North Dakota that successful reorganization throughout the state was contingent upon local understanding of objectives and popular support. For this reason an intensive educational campaign was launched even before the bill was submitted to the legislature. The state organizations interested in education and local county officials were contacted and the campaign was capped by eight regional meetings at which all but three counties were represented. It was concluded by the panel that North Dakota had conducted the best educational effort on behalf of its reorganization plan. 

It will be noted that all three states are keeping their programs as local as possible by insisting that county committees supply most of the initiative and action required in reorganizing their own districts in accordance with their own views of local needs. However, in all instances state level educational men were playing important parts, upon local request, in providing guidance and advice. 

There were some differences in the approaches used by the states. While North Dakota and Wisconsin require the making of studies (the only mandatory feature in their respective laws) Minnesota does not make even this requirement. Other variations among the states are that North Dakota requires state approval of locally developed plans, and Wisconsin does not submit reorganization plans to direct referendum. 

The tri-state group reached some general conclusions regarding school district reorganization. 

A. The panel agreed that the objectives of these laws in the three states were equalized educational opportunity, more efficient school administration, and more nearly equitable distribution of tax costs of education. Game and fish laws which discriminate against non-resident sportsmen were considered first by the members of this panel. 

It was pointed out that such restrictions create difficulties for many people, especially those who live near state borders where wild life conditions make it natural to go into another state for hunting or fishing. Such a situation exists in the case of Minnesota and North Dakota and to a much lesser degree between Minnesota and Wisconsin. In the latter case wild life conditions are in general much the same in both states. 

Many North Dakota residents come into Minnesota to fish, while numerous Minnesota citizens hunt pheasants and ducks in North Dakota.

For these reasons North Dakota sportsmen are concerned with the fishing laws in Minnesota, while Minnesota hunters are interested in North 

Dakota's hunting laws.

There was considerable agreement that non-residents who buy licenses and abide by the law should be granted the same privileges as residents. However, it was felt that non-residents should be required to pay more than residents for licenses. 

From the point of view of ease of enforcement, a possession limit which equals the daily limit is desirable. Such a limit would do away with the necessity of tagging game or fish, and would make it more difficult for game hogs to operate. However, many out-of-state sportsmen are inclined to dislike such a limit. It works no hardship on those who live near the border and are in a position to cross over and back with many daily limits during a season. Those who must travel far to get into the other state, however, are unlikely to be able to do so. Therefore, there would be considerable disparity in the total take of game or fish by these two groups of sportsmen. It was pointed out that hunting and fishing are enjoyed for their intrinsic values as sports as well -17-

as a source of food, and that out-of-state hunters and fishermen should not take back large amounts of game or fish. It was admitted, however, that the hunter or fisherman who makes one trip a year is justified in wanting to take back more than one day's limit. It was suggested that the principle of the Minnesota fish laws might also be adopted as a guide for game laws. It has resulted in a measure which permits non-resident fishermen to send out a certain number of pounds of fish during a season which must be tagged and shipped by common carrier. In addition, the non-resident is permitted to take out a daily bag limit as often as he comes into the state to fish. Thus the fisherman who makes one trip into the state can take out more than one day's limit, and the frequent visitor can take out a legal catch as often as he visits the state. 

Furthermore, non-resident hunters and fishermen alike can consume as much of their catch as they want while in the state and still take back a bag limit.

North Dakota residents feel that they are being discriminated against with respect to spearing fish in Minnesota. Minnesota does not issue permits for spearing to non-residents. Spearing by Minnesotans is sharply restricted. Inasmuch as North Dakota does not permit any spearing within its borders, it was pointed out that its citizens want permission to do something in Minnesota which they cannot do legally in their own state. However, the opinion was expressed that if spearing is to be allowed in principle, it should be open to resident and nonresident alike. This brought forth the comment that it was hoped that spearing would be abolished entirely as a conservation measure. Attention was called to the fact that Minnesota permits winter angling by both resident and non-resident alike.

He would not like to have farmers come to feel that the pheasants on their land are their property and on this basis, exclude hunters from their land except on payment of a fee. 

It was also pointed out that sportsmen who live near the border sometimes travel on the highways of adjacent states on their way from their homes to hunting or fishing grounds elsewhere in their home state.

Forestry 

The The second subject discussed by this group was forestry. discussion of this subject and related problems was mainly between representatives of Wisconsin and Minnesota because North Dakota does not have extensive forest areas. Minnesota and Wisconsin have similar problems with respect to forests, and much was gained from the inter-change of ideas and approaches toward the solution of these problems. 

Mr. H. G. Weber, Director of the Forestry Division of the 

Minnesota Conservation Department, was called upon to introduce the topic. He pointed out that game and fish enthusiasts should be interested in forestry because forestry, wild life conservation, soil conservation, and stream pollution are all inter-related and need to be considered as parts of a single pattern, not as separate entities. He stressed the fact that conservation needs a large view and that specialists tend to look at their own specialties to the exclusion of the rest of the picture. It was emphasized that forestry involves more than production of timber as a usable product. 

In Wisconsin a selective cutting bill was introduced in the last session but because of its controversial nature it failed to pass. 

Doubts were expressed as to the advisability of giving a government agency the power to tell land owners what to do with the products of their own land. Furthermore, great difficulties were encountered in defining what constitutes "mature timber".

Minnesota has a law regulating the cutting of timber on private lands. It was pointed out that the first step in the establishment of a forest policy is a determination of land use which land is best suited for agriculture and which is best adapted to forest. This involves zoning and also a tax adjustment for forest lands. 

-19-

There are three approaches to the administration of selective cutting laws: (1) establish physical limits for mature timber, (2) create state boards for supervision, and (3) set up local boards. Minnesota has taken the first approach and has established a minimum diameter limit with further regulations covering such matters as the number of trees per acre which shall be left for seed production. Land which is being cleared for agricultural purposes is exempt from this law. It was indicated that the Minnesota timber cutting law is operating quite satisfactorily and that large corporations are cutting timber only on a sustained yield basis.

Another important problem discussed by the group was the compensation of local communities for loss of taxes from public forest lands. 

The view was expressed that when certain lands are removed from the tax rolls by a public agency, some remuneration should be made to the affected locality in lieu of taxes. In Wisconsin, the localities receive ten cents per acre on lands in county forests. It was felt that the Wisconsin law which provides for a definite payment in lieu of taxes to the localities is preferable to the Minnesota provisions which depend on the yield of the forest land to determine the amounts to be paid to them. 

It was also pointed out that localities face a similar tax revenue loss on federally owned forest lands. 

Attention was called to the fact that much of the area incorporated into public forests is tax-forfeited land, and actually, therefore, local governments were losing no property tax revenues as a result of this shift to public ownership. However, it was agreed that local units in heavily tax-forfeited or public forest areas need some system of in lieu payments to help provide revenue for the carrying on of local government. It was felt that these factors must be considered in the establishment of any such system of payments in lieu of taxes to localities. 

In the development of a sound forestry policy it is necessary to give serious consideration to the changing and varied uses to which forest lands are put. This is a complex problem and sustained attention is necessary in order that changing conditions will be reflected in policy adjustments. For example, if Minnesota had established a forest policy based on conditions as they were when the state was the second largest producer of saw timber, such policy would be ill suited to the present when timber production is largely confined to pulpwood. 

Sentiment was expressed that Congress should consider the problems of forestry and especially the matter of payments in lieu of taxes to local subdivisions. -20-

The County Unit Health Plan 

North Dakota in 1941 and Wisconsin in 1947 enacted enabling legislation providing for the establishment of district health units. 

Minnesota has not enacted such legislation. Wisconsin delegates pointed out that health legislation in their state was readily adopted as the bill contained no mandatory features with reference to establishment of district health units. The original bill had been drafted by the 

Department of Health but was revised by the Welfare Commission and again by the Welfare Committee in the Assembly. 

The Wisconsin act provides for the establishment of full-time county, multi-county, or city-county health units. The governing board of the county unit consists of 5 or 7 members; the multi-county units, 9 members; and the city-county unit, 7 members. In all cases the law provides that laymen shall be in the majority on the board. 

The governing board is empowered to select a full-time health officer from a list furnished by the State Board of Health. His appointment shall be for a term of five years. In fulfilling its functions the district health unit assumes all the powers and duties, including the power to make (and the obligation to publish) rules and regulations, of the health officers of governments comprising the unit. Thus complete jurisidiction over the health function in its area (except for cities which choose to retain municipal health departments) is vested in the district health unit. The Wisconsin act also contains provision for possible withdrawal of areas from the district unit. 

The duties of the local health officer include appointment of personnel to the staff, subject to the approval of the governing board. 

Established county nurses, who are subsidized to the extent of $1000 per -21-

year by the state, are transferred to the district unit when it is organized.

The budget for the district unit must be approved by the board of supervisors of the county or counties comprising the unit, and in the case of a city-county unit, by the board of supervisors and the city council. All staff salaries are fixed by the county board of supervisors, or by this body and the city council in the case of a joint unit. Costs of the health unit are shared among the units comprising the district on the basis of equalized valuation. 

Funds for the district units must be raised within the ten mill limitation on county levies which exists in Wisconsin. 

To date only one unit has been formed under the law, and it had previously been operating on a similar though more informal basis. 

The city of Eau Claire and Eau Claire County are the members of this joint health unit and results are reported to be satisfactory. No other 

Wisconsin counties, are considering forming district health units at this time. In many instances the cost of the project will preclude their formation.

North Dakota passed its County Health Unit Plan in 1941.

Motor Vehicle Reciprocity LawS Mr. J. P. Bengtson, Director, Motor Vehicle Division, Department of State, summarized Minnesota's reciprocity provisions and the state's experience under them. -24-

The substance of reciprocity is the granting of the rights and privileges to the vehicles of another state which that state accords similar vehicles registered in Minnesota. Minnesota honors any foreign registration tag on automobiles as long as the owner does not become a Minnesota resident. All foreign vehicles, other than automobiles, are classed as trucks and, if reciprocity arrangements exist between their home state and Minnesota, are given the use of state highways without payment of Minnesota registration taxes.

There are, however, certain conditions and limitations. Only vehicles operating in interstate commerce are eligible for reciprocal treatment. Any vehicle making more than one stop in a state in order to discharge or take on passengers or cargo is intrastate commerce for the purposes of state taxation and does not enjoy reciprocal exemptions. 

Other conditions of reciprocity require application for a permit (at no charge) for informational purposes, display of a windshield sticker, agreement to be suable for damages resulting from operation within the state, and adherence to Minnesota highway law and load and size restrictions. Minnesota has full reciprocity agreements which are in satisfactory operation with both North Dakota and Wisconsin.

It was pointed out that no reciprocity exists between North Dakota and Wisconsin. Wisconsin delegates explained that there had been some reluctance to enter into reciprocal agreements because of the state's position as a through route between the Twin Cities and the Chicago area. 

It was added, however, that the necessity of expediting transportation of war materiel had speeded reciprocity progress throughout the nation and Wisconsin now has an enabling act permitting such agreements. 

The question was raised whether differences among the three states in load and vehicle size restrictions did not nullify the workings of reciprocity. Discussion, however, revealed that the requirements of the states were substantially similar. 

The Wisconsin Staggered System of Automobile Licensing 

Mr. B. L. Marcus, Commissioner of the Wisconsin Motor Vehicle

Department, summarized Wisconsin's experience with the staggered licensing system devised and inaugurated by that state. 

He explained that the new plan was developed to combat the extra costs arising from the last-minute rush each March of thousands of motorists applying for re-registration. This peak workload required the hiring of a large, inexperienced, temporary staff each year which cost much time, money, and patience. -25-

The Wisconsin solution to the problem is basically the application of the business-tried principle of cycle billing to automobile registration by re-registering roughly 1/12 of the state's vehicles each month. Each such monthly registration group is designated a "series", with January being Series 1, February Series 2, etc. Permanent plates, which belong to the car owner and do not follow the vehicle in case of its sale or transfer, bear, in addition to the registration number, the number of the series in which renewal is due. On January 2 of each year bills are mailed to all owners of Series 1 plates who, during the month, make their remittance and receive in the mail new inserts to be attached to their permanent plates. The same procedure is followed for Series 2 in February, Series 3 in March, and on through the year.

Permanent plates, it is felt, make for readier identification, less confusion over titles, and greatly reduced manufacturing, handling, and mailing costs. Worn or damaged plates are replaced free of charge by the state. The 1947 replacement rate was 5,400 plates, or approximately .7% for the 800,000 automobiles registered. It is anticipated that the replacement rate will rise, but even a 20% annual turnover would result in total replacement only once every five years.

A previous consolidation of all state functions dealing with motor vehicles into one department has made possible the shifting of motor vehicle personnel from one division to another when the press of work makes it advisable. The department also has adopted a policy of using efficient office procedures and business machines to speed the work and cut unit costs. These factors, together with the advantages of the staggered system, itself, permit a relatively small, permanent, trained staff to administer motor vehicle registration. All are important in the economy of operation Wisconsin is achieving. Introduction of a $16 flat fee for automobiles by the 1947 legislature has further simplified registration and reduced operating costs. Projected further changes, some contingent upon legislative approval, include revision of truck registration practices, and even greater mechanization of office procedures. Estimated savings to Wisconsin under the presently operating system are approximately $1,000,000 per year.

Government This discussion began with a brief statement of North Dakota's practices and problems in sharing motor vehicle revenues with local government units. The discussion then moved on to Minnesota and Wisconsin experience and a comparison of the practices in the three statés. -26-

MINNESOTA'S REORGANIZATION ACT 

At the request of the Wisconsin and North Dakota delegates, Minnesota's Commissioner of Administration, Mr. Earl L. Berg, spoke to the full conference Tuesday afternoon on the Reorganization Act. He traced the history of the Department of Administration by pointing out that its forerunner came out of the reorganization of 1925 and that the department as now constituted was created by the 1939 legislature. The latter reorganization placed in the hands of one man, the Commissioner -27-

of Administration, the responsibility for budgeting, purchasing, supervision and control over all state contracts, and supervision and control over the operation of state buildings. Mr. Berg pointed out that in addition to carrying out these responsibilities his department operates a central store and a central duplicating division to service the respective needs of the various departments. 

Budgeting practices were inaugurated in Minnesota in 1925. The current budget procedure can be presented in three distinct steps: (1) preparation and executive recommendation; (2) budget execution; and (3) reporting. He explained that during October prior to the convening of the legislature each department submits to the Commissioner of Administration a complete report of its expenditures for the preceding two years, and estimates for the current year and for the coming biennium. 

From this information the Budget Division prepares for the Governor the annual budget which is presented to a joint session of the House and Senate during the first two weeks in January. 

During the legislative session the Department of Administration is given the privilege of working closely with the Senate Finance Committee and the House Appropriations Committee, as well as with the departments making requests for funds. This familiarizes the department more thoroughly with legislative intent in making the appropriation and permits better supervision of subsequent expenditures by state departments.

All state departments are allotted funds quarterly by the budget division. In addition to operating under a quarterly allotment system, departments are required to encumber funds before they are spent, a procedure which prevents expenditures from exceeding appropriations. 

The Commissioner is empowered to cut back appropriations in order to maintain a balanced budget if revenue receipts fall below estimates.
