ALVALL WHAT WE KNOW ABOUT HEREDITY AND ENVIRONMENT BENJAMIN C. GRUENBERG 

On the average, breast-fed babies stand a better chance of surviving the first year of life than bottle-fed babies. Yet some mothers will not yield sufficient milk, whereas others produce enough for two or more babies. Moreover, some mothers have plenty of milk, but the milk does not agree with their respective babies. Mothers differ, one from another. Babies differ. The individuals of every species differ from one another, human beings perhaps most of all.

It is well established that there is a close connection between the operation of various glands on the one hand, and the temper, mood, responsiveness, excitability, and so on, on the other hand. We are not often moved to exclaim about a person, "What a splendid liver he (or she) has!" But we cannot overlook some of the manifestations of a deficient liver, even if we usually lay the blame elsewhere. There is probably also a correspondence between the structure and other physical and chemical properties of the central nervous system, and the quality or rate of thinking, sensitiveness to stimulation, and susceptibility to various social influences. Inasmuch as some of the characteristics of men and women are of great significance for individual or for social welfare, it is important to answer the question, "What causes the differences?" Are higher mentality and resistance to disease acquired, or are they inherited? Is criminal conduct or musical ability the effect of environmental influences or a manifestation of heredity?

The controversy as to the relative efficacy of heredity and environment is interminable because it rests upon the false assumptions that differences in general must be due to either heredity or environment, and that heredity and environment act independently. The breed of duck under which a hen's eggs are. hatched will not influence the character of the chicks that come out, but the breed of hen from which the eggs came, will. The uniform conditions supplied by a setting hen or an incubator are necessary for the development of the embryo: they are in no sense the causes of the Plymouth Rock or Leghorn qualities which subsequently appear in the animals. 

In an extended series of experiments on the development of a stickleback fish, fertilized eggs of the same parentage were distributed in vessels containing sea water, modified by the addition of varying quantities of calcium and magnesium salts. In certain solutions the eggs developed into the familiar stickleback. In other solutions the eggs started to develop, but did not complete the process, dying at various stages along the way. Particularly striking is the observation that when the proportion of magnesium exceeded a certain point, the eyes of the animal, instead of appearing on opposite sides of the head, as they do when the eggs develop in normal sea water, became fused into a single eye. Here the capacity to develop into any one of several types of fish is inherited, or borne by the fertilized egg, but the particular type actually developed depends upon the particular type of environment to which a given egg is exposed.

Many human malformations and monstrosities are undoubtedly due to mechanical or chemical injuries sustained by the foetus in the course of its development. There are recorded, for example, babies with defective head development born to women who had been treated with X-rays during pregnancy. 

To be sure, after does not mean because; but the evidence of causal relation is in many cases unquestionable. Diseased mothers often give birth to children that depart more or less strikingly from our conception of the normal. Disease in childhood brings about more or less permanent and more or less serious malformations. Some children are born deaf, but others attain deafness through scarlet fever. In still other cases deafness seems to be acquired in the course of individual development without relation to external factors, just as dark hair is frequently found among people who were fair-haired during childhood. 

In such cases we say that the deafness, as a condition of the adult person, is somehow inherited: it is due to something inherent in the same sense as the capacity to become a contralto rather than a soprano, or blue-eyed rather than brown-eyed.

We are so familiar with individual variation in physical traits, as well as with resemblances in physical traits, that we have no difficulty in accepting the doctrine that traits are inherited. We are so familiar with the effects, on the intellectual and emotional levels, of learning or of neglect that we find it difficult to think of intellectual and emotional characters as being inherited in the same way. It would be altogether misleading to look to the relative proportions, or the relative vigor, of the notorious "glands" for an explanation, in terms of physical structure, for behavior differences among people. Yet there is undoubtedly an inherited structural foundation for many of the characters that are humanly or socially of greatest importance. This, at least, would seem to be just as true as the generalization that character and conduct can be modified by various effects produced upon the blood, the glands and the nervous system.

Hundreds of species or plants and animals have been studied experimentally, especially within the past thirty years; and in each species studied from a few of many dozens of characteristics, both functional and structural, have been found to be transmitted from parent to offspring in certain ways. These uniformities have come to be known as Mendel's laws, after the experimenter

who first observed and recorded them. Mendel experimented with garden peas, and he fixed his attention upon traits that are easily contrasted with their opposites or with their absence-tallness versus dwarfed growth, yellow seed versus green seed, wrinkled seed coat versus smooth seed coat, and so on. Mendel's results, subsequently confirmed and extended by others (after having been forgotten for about thirty years and later rediscovered independently by three other investigators) required for their explanation the assumption that there exist in the eggs and sperms some material entities which correspond in some way with the characters observed in the individual plant or animal. 

It seems reasonable to look to the germ cells for whatever material basis there may be for the inherited characters that make up the individual. With improvements in the microscope it became possible, more than a generation ago, to say with confidence that the hereditary material is carried in the chromosomes. Today it is possible to go further and say that certain particular traits emerge in the developing organism because of the presence or absence of more minute structural entities, called "genes," in particular chromosomes. 

More recent research has actually traced hundreds of plant and animal structures and functions to their sources in definitely localized areas of the chromosomes in the germ cells, making possible the charting of genes, or the construction of "chromosome maps."

Among human beings the ordinary cells of the body contain 48 chromosomes, 24 pairs. The two members of each pair are indistinguishable with the most powerful microscope. During the formation of germ cells, the members of each pair become separated with the the result that each egg and each sperm contains but 24 chromosomes. Later, during fertilization, the 24 from the sperm unite with the 24 from the egg and the full complement of 48 is reestablished in the cells of the new individual. It is important to note that the chromosomes are individualized and that they combine in mated pairs. 

This behavior of the chromosomes in man and in other species of living things corresponds perfectly with the major facts established by Mendel and his followers regarding the inheritance of certain kinds of characters. When the parents bring to a union a contrasted pair of characters, the offspring will all show one of the two characters, and this without regard to the sex of the parent having the "dominant" trait. Among human beings this is illustrated by the dominance of curliness of hair over straightness, of dark hair and eyes over light, of shortness or dwarfing of limbs over normal length, of normal general proportions or dimensions over general dwarfing, and many other structural and functional characters.

Hybrids showing the dominant trait will give rise, in succeeding generations, to individuals in which the recessive character reappears in a certain proportion of cases. Brown-eyed parents of mixed ancestry, for example, may

have one blue-eyed to three brown-eyed children. This segregation takes place for hundreds of pairs of characters in apparent independence of one another. 

For example, tallness or shortness may be segregated in the offspring of mixed types, independently of eye-color, or of the shape of the chin. Since practically all human populations are mixed, each individual born will show characters derived from several lines of ancestry as well as clear resemblances, in regard to several details, to the parents and to the grandparents. The fact of dominance and the fact of segregation both agree with the assumption that the chromosomes are the carriers of whatever it is that determines the several characters of which the organism consists. If each chromosome in the human germ cell determined the development of a single character or group of characters, the distribution of chromosomes during the formation of germ cells and their recombination during fertilization would yield, potentially, over 16 million distinguishable individuals as the offspring of a single pair of parents.* 

This explains roughly why no two individuals are alike, even if of the same ancestry or parentage, except socalled duplicate twins. Such twins are supposed to result from the complete separation of the two halves of a fertilized egg, each half developing into a complete individual. The twins are identical because they start out with identical sets of chromosomes. 

The members of each pair of chromosomes appear to be interchangeable, as well as indistinguishable; but there is one exception. In the male, one of the pairs, the "sex chromosomes," is made up of two unlike units. The larger we may call X, the smaller Y. In the female the corresponding two are indistinguishable, both like X, the larger. When egg cells are being formed, the two members of this pair, as of the other pairs of chromosomes, become separated so that each egg contains one X. When sperm cells are being formed, the separation of the sets of chromosomes results in producing two kinds of sperms-one-half contain the larger or X chromosome, the other half contain each the small or Y chromosome. Accordingly, there are possible two kinds of fertilization. An egg cell may combine with a sperm containing the X chromosome; the resulting individual will contain two X chromosomes in each cell and will be a female. Or the egg may combine with a sperm having the Y chromosome, and there will ensue a male, with the XY combination in each cell. 

The determination of the sex of the individual by a difference in the chromosomes has been established for many species of animals, although the mechanism is not the same in all. We may say, therefore, that sex is inherited, not in the crude sense that the individuals in each generation show the same sex characters as did their parents, but in the sense that the sex is already determined at the moment of conception. Disregarding monstrosities and mutilations, we know that the maturing of the individual into a definitely male or a definitely female organism is contingent upon the normal operation and balance of the entire endocrine system. Recent experiments in the reversal of sex are impressive confirmation of such interpretations. The discovery of the connection between the chromosome mechanism and the determination of sex is not alone of interest in itself, but served to establish the principle of the gene through research upon the so-called sex-linked characters. These are illustrated among human beings by the common or 

Daltonian color-blindness, an inability to discriminate between green and red. 

Color blindness is about three or four times as common among males as it is among females. A study of family histories shows that it is never transmitted from father to son, nor from father to daughter; yet the daughter of a colorblind man will transmit the trait to half her sons. Many other characteristics have been found to follow a similar course, including haemophilia, a certain type of bleeding, in human beings.

From numberless experiments and from a study of family histories, it becomes increasingly more certain that it is not the chromosomes as a whole that are involved in the determination of particular characters in plants and animals but the definitely localized, though invisible, units, the genes. It is not to be supposed that each trait is the manifestation of a particular gene; or that each gene manifests itself in a distinctive trait. Each character is probably the resultant of the interaction of a number of genes; and the action of each gene is probably influenced by several or many, if not all, of the other genes. 

We do not know how one of these genes, or a combination of two or more genes, operates in the course of development to bring about a brown pigment in the eye, or a relatively longer or shorter thighbone, or an increase or reduction in the number of joints of the fingers; but we know that such things do happen. This knowledge is as definite, although inferred, as is our knowledge about the behavior of chemical elements and radicles in the formation of synthetic dyes and drugs. We check also in both cases by being able to reproduce the desired or predicted results experimentally. On the other hand, we do not know how an excess or a deficiency of sunlight or of magnesium brings about one or another peculiarity in the bones or in the eyes. We do not know how a shortage of iodine in the soil of the Great Lakes region brings about an excessive number of goiters among young people living there. We do not even know how the sunshine tans the skin. But we do know that such effects are produced.

For practical purposes we distinguish and name human qualities of body, of personality, of character-longevity, enterprising, courageous, imaginative, rheumatic, intelligent, absent-minded. There is, however, no necessary connection between these convenient units of description or identification and the actual mechanism that maintains the species as an organic continuity in which the constituent members are nevertheless unique individuals. The refinement of our knowledge regarding heredity produces in many the impression of a fatalistic predestination. Are not developing individuals subject to modification by environmental factors. Does not environment count? Is it all the same what happens to children?

About the transmission of the genes as physical facts there can be no doubt. About the determination of thousands of characters by virtue of these genes there can be no doubt. The person in whom we are interested is, to begin with, quite unpredictable, however, because, although a bundle of inheritances, he is one of several thousand times 16 million theoretically possible recombinations of genes. Moreover, the inheritances include not alone structural details, but hundreds of capacities that cannot all, in the very nature of things, be brought to realization. We may make a logical distinction between heredity and environment, but in a living thing whatever is inherited, and however it is inherited, comes to manifest itself only under special conditions favorable to development to development of the individual as a whole, or of the trait in question. Most of us inherit susceptibility to rabies, or to the plague, but most of us never manifest these diseases; most of us inherit the capacity to learn 

Chinese, but most of us never do learn Chinese. 

Environment and experience do count; it does matter what happens to children. An epidemic invades a community in which some of the children have been immunized, others not. Of the latter, some are naturally immune or resistant: the rest bear the whole brunt of the attack. It matters whether children are exposed to infection or protected from it. It matters how infected children are treated. Of those who recover, some will be permanently injured or crippled. Problems of nutrition are so familiar that we need only be reminded that adequately nourished individuals differ from starvelings. Studies of delinquency frequently show how important are the early experiences and privations of children. Blights and bruises leave their marks; guidance and training and more subtle influences evoke organic responses. And yet there is a limit to what we can do to modify the developing individual, as there is a limit to what the individual can do under adverse conditions. 

Where conditions are identical for a given group of individuals, we should attribute the differences that appear to constitutional or inherited factors. 

Where the heredity is identical the observed differences may be attributed to environment. This is a simple and reasonable formula. Its application, however, is not so simple. What constitute identical conditions? Are all the chil-

dren brought up in the same home, on the same diet, with the same schooling, with the same playgrounds, the same books, movies, newspaper funnies, in the same environment? Again, what constitutes identical heredity? Are the children of the same parentage identical in heredity? Apparently not. 

The most promising material for unraveling. this intricate relationship of heredity and environment is to be found in twins that have become separated early in life, brought up in distinct types of surroundings and observed after many years. The number of such cases is naturally very small. The few that have been studied reveal remarkable persistence of physical, emotional and intellectual traits, of taste, and of temperament. At the same time they show also that even so-called identical twins commonly differ in some details, from the very first. Differences in environronment appear from these studies to be chiefly differences in opportunity to develop favorably. 

Another problem that has preoccupied the interest and curiosity of mankind is that of the transmission of modifications. Are the acquisitions of experiences and training, of disease or achievement, ever passed on to the offspring? Does each generation start at scratch, or are the successes and failures cumulative? Many experiments and other studies have been made to answer these questions, but the net result to date is entirely negative. Most of the experiments turn out indeed to be irrelevant; for example, amputating the tails of rats for hundreds of generations proves nothing because the removal of the tails produces no organic change in the animals. One of the most significant experiments is that of Castle, here at Harvard. An albino guinea pig had her ovaries removed, and replaced with the ovaries taken from a black female. 

Later the albino was mated with an albino male. Now, there is no more intimate environment than that which the mammalian foetus finds in the uterus within which it develops. Will the eggs from the black animal, developing in the body of the white animal, be influenced by the blood stream of the latter? 

The offspring were all black, unlike the father, unlike the foster mother. We know that in these animals albinism is recessive and pigmentation is dominant; there can be no question of hybrid ancestry or atavism influencing the result. 

Those who oppose the doctrine of transmission of acquired traits are at the disadvantage of trying to prove a negative. Such positive knowledge as we have points to the genes as the determiners of inherited characters, and until we can show experimentally the environment which influences the development of the body at the same time influences the genes, we must avoid counting upon the transmission of modifications.

When we turn to biology to guide us in the social policy, we can learn much regarding conditions favorable or unfavorable to the healthy development of human beings. Measurable results have been obtained through sanitation, through improvements in diet and housing, through maternal and infant care, through immunizations, through regulation of industry and transportation.

Infant mortality has been sharply reduced; typhoid, smallpox and malaria have been eliminated from vast areas; yellow fever and diphtheria are being pushed back, rickets and goiters are coming under control, the average duration of life is substantially increased. Similarly we find results in the perfection of skill, the refining of manners, the cultivation of tastes, the modification of attitudes, the lifting of the whole level of living. Whether people are any happier because of these cultural changes, whether lives are worth saving at all, or whether indiscriminate saving of life is socially wise, biology cannot answer, at the moment.

Making use of our knowledge of heredity is much more difficult, notwithstanding the brilliant achievements of plant and animal breeders. Aside from the impracticability of applying breeders' methods to mankind, for reasons inherent in the nature of man, is the further difficulty that we really do not know how to select qualities desirable for race improvement. Certain characters, both desirable and undesirable, run in families, but there is no necessary connection between one set of desirable-or undesirable qualities and another. 

The next musical genius is just as likely to be a blond as a brunette; worse still, he is just as likely as not to be susceptible to tuberculosis, or deficient in business ability. A successful financier may turn out to be a diabetic, or an epileptic.

It is especially difficult to deal with phenomena of social success and failure without making certain tacit assumptions regarding values, priorities, rights and wrongs.

There is the constant danger of assuming that God, or Nature, prefers our kind of people-which may be true; and that God, or Nature, needs our help in getting rid of the other kinds-which is highly improbable. 

For seventy years the doctrine of natural selection has served as the scientific confirmation of the righteousness of prevailing social and economic practices, and of the exploitation of the weak by the strong. Formerly we said that the mighty are so by the will of God; since Darwin we say that Nature herself selects the fit. This doctrine persists, being a source of comfort to those in power. But recently it is called upon by groups that feel power slipping away from them. For example, the birth rates of some portions of the population are such that in a thousand years there will be no Nordic descendants of the Mayflower immigrants; and something should be done about it. 

If it is true that the feeble-minded and the perverse are multiplying more rapidly than our kind, that may be important; but certainly devout followers of a rather crude darwinism have no ground for complaint. Curiously, however, most of our eugenists who let themselves be heard are such darwinists. 

They say, for example, when you speak of child labor or factory hours, leave it to nature. They say, when you speak of maternal health and tenement laws, the fit will survive. Yet they clamor for a law that will save the earth for a group that cannot replace itself through an adequate birthrate.

We cannot separate heredity from environment as forces acting independently. This is not because our knowledge is inadequate. On the contrary, it is because we already know enough to see that the manifestation of the potentialities which the genes carry is inseparable from the conditions under which the individual develops. Whatever develops in a growing organism is possible because of its inheritance; the process of development depends upon the environment. In the meantime, we know enough to make possible a fuller life and a fuller realization, nay, a fuller revelation, of innate capacities than the race ever enjoyed in the past. We do not know enough about heredity to formulate with confidence a comprehensive social program. We do know enough about heredity to scrutinize suspiciously various proposals for a quick regeneration of the race, and enough to realize that much further research is greatly needed.
