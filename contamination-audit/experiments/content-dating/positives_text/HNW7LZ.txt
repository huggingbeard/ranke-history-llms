INTRODUCTION 

HE people of the United States will not understand China and The friendly toward the Chinese people in the years that lie ahead unless the elementary schools of the nation build a strong foundation of knowledge and respect for China. Such knowledge and respect are essential to cooperation between the two countries. They are essential to education for citizenship in a twentieth-century democracy. They are essential for the well-rounded growth of individual pupils whose education is to enrich their lives with the achievements of man's social heritage. Many elementary schools in the United States are beginning to include the study of China in their programs. Their work needs to be increased and improved. Other schools, which have not yet begun to study our magnificent neighbor across the Pacific, need to be stimulated to such study, not alone because of the present international emergency but also as a long-term program for widening pupils' horizons and for making school training more realistic and useful. Citizens of any American community who have sensed the increasingly close relations of East and West in the modern world should stimulate schools to carry on more effective and thorough education about China and her Asiatic neighbors. 

This pamphlet suggests a number of ways in which American elementary schools may enrich their programs by increased study of China. It first lists a series of topics which seem especially appropriate for elementary-school pupils. The second section of the pamphlet lists a number of activities and projects illustrative of the range of exercises about China which will be stimulating and profitable for pupils. Included in the sections are references to various types of teaching materials. The pamphlet is brief and by no means exhaustive, but it does contain practical approaches and materials which may be useful to elementary-school teachers, supervisers, and principals. 1

The suggestions in the pamphlet grow out of the experiences of the authors, of schools throughout the nation which have done good work in this field, and of the Committee on Asiatic Studies of the American Council on Education. Readers of the pamphlet who are interested in the study of China and in the fundamental improvement of elementary education in this country are urged to bring the pamphlet to the attention of school leaders and to cooperate in improving the study of China in the schools of their own vicinity. 

With such help as the pamphlet suggests, elementary schools may make a valued contribution to the enrichment of pupils' lives and to the welfare of democracy in an interdependent world.

What to Teach About China 

AMERICAN elementary schools should develop in the minds of pupils clear pictures and concepts of Chinese life. Study of China at the elementary-school level should deal first with tangible rather than with abstract matters. It should lay the foundation for later understanding of complex problems and conflicts in Chinese development and of China's place in modern world affairs. Four topics are here suggested for study. They may be studied separately in classes in reading or history or geography, or they may be combined in a single unit on China to be studied for several weeks at some point in the intermediate grades. 

1. WAYS OF LIVING IN CHINA: Chinese customs, clothing, home life, foods, work, recreation, education - these are the most significant areas for study in the elementary school. These ways of living are an introduction to the study of Chinese culture. Pupils should study first about homes and home life, farms and farming, Chinese villages and community life, celebrations and festivals, schools, and shops, and the work of craftsmen. They should read vivid descriptions of these fields of day-by-day activity, and should come to understand that Chinese ways of living are perfectly natural products of distinguished tradition and of geographic influence. 

A warning should be given. All too often boys and girls gain false ideas because only those customs which are different from ours are emphasized. Customs which are merely strange and unusual have no place in well-planned study. Similarities between Chinese ways of living and our own should be brought out. And in the study of all aspects of Chinese life, it should be made clear to pupils that Chinese ways of living serve the same basic human needs as do our own. 

A second matter to watch is the danger of over-generalizing about Chinese customs. Ways of living in China are fully as varied as they are in the United States. Not all Chinese raise rice and eat it daily. 

Although agriculture is the most important industry of China, not all Chinese are farmers. There are striking differences between 

North China and South China, between mountainous China and the fertile lowlands, between city life and country life. These differences, and the rich variations in Chinese customs, should be pointed out. 3

A third warning concerns the element of time. We often speak of "changeless China," but it is at least equally correct to speak of "changing China." Too frequently we teach of ancient Chinese customs as if they were also universally characteristic today. To teach that all Chinese wear queues or that the feet of well-to-do girls and women are bound is as foolish as to teach that Americans still wear Pilgrim costumes and cook at colonial fireplaces. Rapid changes in recent years make it especially important to recognize distinctions between the ways of living of Old China and of New China. 

2. THE LAND OF CHINA: Study of China's geography is important in the elementary school. China is a vast land, reaching from a cold 

North to a tropical South, from the Pacific on the east to high mountains and dusty plains to the west. Chinese life cannot be understood without reference to China's three great rivers - the Hwang Ho or Yellow River, the Yangtze-Kiang or Great River, and the Sikiang or West River. "Brown China" to the north and "Green China" to the south should be explored by all pupils. The major cities of 

China, their location and economic activities, as well as the trade routes and lines of communication should be included in pupils' study of geography. Pupils should acquire a mental picture of the map of China, omitting details and all except the most important names and places and areas. 

Human geography, stressing the relationship between men and their physical enviroment, should be a theme in all elementary-school work in the social studies. Geographic influences on Chinese ways of living should be stressed. Many schools will find it profitable to minimize the general, somewhat encyclopedic, study of the geography of China and to concentrate on the study of human geography in selected "type regions." Such studies as those of life in a ricegrowing area or on the loess plains or in a mountainous mining region or in a busy seaport afford excellent opportunities for understanding the basic relationship of human life and the earth. In these type-studies the interrelationships of the environment and people's work, play, and living conditions should be brought out. 

3. THE CHINESE PEOPLE: In studying the people of China, pupils should note (a) their great number a population of more than 450,000,000; (b) their location - over three-quarters of the people ordinarily live on one-fifth of the land, making congestion in the

fertile valleys and in the cities very great; and (c) their racial and cultural characteristics. The relative mobility of the Chinese people in recent years, especially in connection with the great trek westward before the Japanese invaders, should be noted. Recent attempts, through industrialization, cooperatives, new types of farming, and social measures of the government to raise the standard of living may be suggested to pupils. 

Pupils should learn that not all Chinese look alike any more than all Americans look alike. As a rule people living in the north of 

China are taller and more vigorous; southerners are slimmer, shorter, and likely to be more independent. Variations in physical appearance, in cultural background, and in standard of living are great, and should warn pupils against over-generalization about the Chinese as a people. Pupils should come to regard Chinese in democratic terms, that is with basic regard for individuality and for personal worth and character. 

4. THE RISE OF MODERN CHINA: The age and rich tradition of 

China should be impressed upon pupils, but of equal significance for civic education is the rise of "New China" in recent decades. 

The China of today and tomorrow is the China that American citizens of today and tomorrow need to understand. 

The history of China is a succession of dynasties which have risen to great power, declined, and decayed. The last of these dynasties was the Manchu regime, which, during the nineteenth century, was increasingly corrupt and ineffective. During that same century the great power of western imperialism seemed about to divide China into provinces and colonies. Against outside control and against corruption at home, the new Republic of China was born. 

For young people the story of the Republic can be centered around two great figures - Sun Yat-sen and Chiang Kai-shek. The first set the ideas of new China and is today revered as the founder of the Republic. The second led China through the period of the war lords and the invasion of the Japanese. With consummate skill he has combined the forces of China and carried on a long, hard war against the Japanese. Under his government have been carried out many reforms in social and political organization. New developments in industry and agriculture, in education and medicine, promise well for the future of China. 5

Projects and Materials 1. DISCUSSION OF "BELIEFS": An interesting activity may be de veloped by class discussion and comparison of American "beliefs" about the Chinese and Chinese "beliefs" about us. To start the discussion the teacher may place on the board the following compara tive lists: 

Many Americans Believe: 

a. All Chinese look alike. b. The Chinese do everything backward. c. The Chinese are a nation of laundrymen and restaurant owners.

Many Chinese Believe: 

a. All Americans look alike. b. The Americans do everything backward. c. All Americans are very rich playboys or very tough gangsters. 

Discussion of such ideas as these and of how they originated may be an excellent introduction for a unit on China. By keeping a record of the beliefs about China held by pupils at the beginning of their study of the subject, the teacher may construct an informal test of attitudes.

7. SHADOW PLAYS: Shadow plays have been known in China for centuries. They have been popular at the courts of emperors and also village festivals. They are like motion pictures without photography; in fact the Chinese call motion pictures "electric shadows." 

At shadow plays the audience sits on one side of a screen and a bright light shines against the other side. The screen is decorated like a stage, and the players hold cardboard or parchment figures up against it on the side toward the light. Sometimes the figures are colored; always they are jointed, and are made to move around and act and seem to talk like puppets. The audience sees the figures silhouetted against the light and watches them act out plays and scenes. 

*Available from United China Relief.

The making of shadow figures and stage settings and the writing and acting of shadow plays may be very popular with elementaryschool classes. The plays offer opportunity for creative experience related to art, literature, costumes, and traditions of China. The use of shadow plays as part of an activity program is very much worth while.

Miss Pauline Benton (The Red Gate Players, 454 Riverside Drive, New York) is the best American exponent of shadow plays. Many schools have found it worth while to engage her and her company of players to put on authentic shadow plays for clubs and special occasions. Miss Benton has prepared a pamphlet on A Chinese Folk 

Drama: the Shadow Play which is shortly to be published by the 

American Council on Education. Its sections on "Shadow Plays in 

American Schools" and "Instructions for Making the Shadow Figures and Stage" will be especially helpful for teachers and pupils.

9. CHINESE FESTIVALS: Many elementary schools will find it enjoyable to celebrate some of the Chinese festival days. Assembly programs and school parties can be arranged for such occasions. In Edwards and Sung, Ling, Ling, Child of China (Harr Wagner. 1939) as well as in the books already referred to will be found many suggestions for appropriate observance of festivals. 

The most elaborate festival is the New Year celebration which takes place between January 21 and February 21. In China it lasts for fifteen days, with special ceremonies to be performed each day. 

In preparation for New Year's the Chinese try to pay all their debts, and then array themselves in new clothes as we do at Easter. The climax of the New Year's celebration is marked by parades. Gaily colored lanterns light the streets. Then comes the parade of the dragon - a long, paper dragon carried on the shoulders of dancing men. There are firecrackers and fireworks, and floats in the parade. 

The dragon-boat festival, on the fifth day of the fifth month, dates back to two hundred years before Christ. An old legend says that a beloved statesman drowned himself then as a protest against cruel and corrupt government. At this festival the river is crowded with gaily decked boats. Some boats - the dragon-boats with a dragon's head at the bow and tail at the stern race to a given point to make sacrifices in honor of the beloved statesman. 

The Moon Festival is another time of feasting and merry-making. 

The moon shines brightly during the eighth month, as does our harvest moon. Special cakes are made in the forms of the moon, a horse and rider, a pagoda, or a fish. Family parties are held in the light of the brilliant moon.

Alert pupils may find Chinese motifs in the illustrations and advertisements of American magazines. 11. PERSONALITIES OF CHINA: The study of biographies is an im portant part of civic and character education. American children are not as familiar with the lives of outstanding Chinese heroes and leaders as they should be. There are many individuals worth studying about, both for what they teach about China and what they teach about ethics. CONFUCIUS 

CONFUCIUS: About 500 years before 

Christ, a great teacher was born in 

China. His name was Kung-fu-tze, Confucius. During the past 2400 years he has probably influenced more people than any other individual in the history of the world. 

He was not a religious leader, but he tried to teach his followers to live a better life by being honest, faithful, obedient to authority, and worthy members of their families. Many of Confucius' wise sayings have become proverbs of the Chinese. He stated the Golden Rule by saying, "Do not unto others that which you would not wish them to do unto you."

WU TAO-TZE: Long ago a famous painter lived in China. Most of his paintings were made on the walls of Buddhist monasteries and temples, which have long since fallen into ruins. Wu Tao-tze, like many other 

Chinese artists, did not paint with his models before him, as most of our artists do. He first spent many hours studying the scene or the people he intended to paint. Then he went away by himself, thought long about what he had seen, and painted from memory. His pictures were so beautiful and life-like that many legends have grown up telling how dragons and fish came to life from his pictures. One of the most Yun Jee WU TAO-TZE 12

famous of the legends reports that Wu Tao-tze once painted an especially beautiful landscape. The emperor came and stood in silent admiration before it. The artist pointed out the mouth of a cave in the picture and said that in the cave lived a spirit among surroundings even more beautiful than those of the picture. Thereupon Wu Tao-tze walked into the cave in his picture and was seen no more.

12. AN ASSEMBLY PLAY: Many excellent assembly programs can be developed from the study of China. One ambitious program may be based on the play New Walls for China, written by six outstanding American playwrights for broadcasting purposes. (Trans-Pacific 

News Service, Inc., 1250 Sixth Avenue, New York). The play is arranged in the following parts:

Part I introduces China as a land planning for a future which will match the magnificence of its past.

Part II tells of the struggle the Chinese are making to keep their hospitals and medical centers operating in spite of the dearth of medical supplies and personnel. 

Part III pictures a bombing crew ready to depart on a raid. 

Part IV dramatizes recruits who are having the purpose of their work explained to them.

Part V dramatizes the story of Chai Yu-tang, a sixty-year-old peasant woman who has done much to organize and help the guerrillas. 

Part VI compares old schools with new types of education in China. 

Part VII describes the work being done by the cooperatives in interior China.

Part VIII tells what is being done to care for the thousands of war orphans in China today.

Part IX describes the program outlined by Sun Yat-sen for the future of a China built on democratic foundations.
