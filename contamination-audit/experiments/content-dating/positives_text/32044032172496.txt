FOREWORD 

The former edition of this booklet published in 1933 has been distributed among many grand jurors of the past decade. We think from the improved procedure of our grand juries as well as from favorable comments of many grand jurymen, that this work has been of value to all good citizens.

Upon request of the recently organized Grand Jurors Association of Fulton County, this new edition is being issued. It contains the revisions necessary to cover the changes in our laws, as well as some additional statements of law and procedure. 

The Grand Jury is an important Governmental agency for preserving the peoples' rights and liberties; their sole fortress against official oppression, extravagance and waste in the conduct of public affairs. Its vitally important powers of wide searching investigation into the conduct of all Governmental agencies-City, State and County-are powers secured through generations of sacrifice; they should be zealously guarded and not lightly surrendered to "king or potentate."

We hope this, the second edition, may be continually helpful to grand jurymen, conscientious public officials and of interest and value to all citizens.

Preface To The First Edition THE GRAND JURY HISTORY, LEGAL RIGHTS AND DUTIES 

The liberties of the people are not secure merely because our national government is called a Republic, because we have free schools, elect by popular vote our representatives, assemble the grand jury, speak glibly of freedom, mercy, justice and equal rights. But rather good government and consequent liberty depend upon the proper functioning of governmental agencies set up by the people to handle their affairs and in the character and capacity of their chosen officials. 

One of the most important of these agencies is the grand jury, regarding which there has been frequent misunderstanding on the part of the average citizen and some public officials. It is well to remember that all public officials and employees are subject to its inquisitorial powers, and whenever abuses are manifest in the conduct of the public business it is the sworn duty of the grand jury to demand through the courts and the legislature that they be corrected. 

This booklet is presented to the grand jurors of Fulton County with the hope that it may prove useful in giving them a better understanding as to the legal rights, duties and methods of procedure of this important tribunal of the people. It is offered with no ulterior motive and purely as a contribution to good citizenship. Please accept it with our compli ments.

ORGANIZATION OF GRAND JURY 

The first duty of a grand jury is to organize—to elect a foreman, assistant foreman, a secretary and assistant secretary. The work of the jury can be greatly expedited by the election of experienced officers. Too often, the first man nominated is elected. If a little time is taken and capable men elected, the work of the jury can proceed without friction or delay.

Immediately after organizing, it is customary to consult the solicitor regarding the time of the sessions. Usually 

Tuesdays and Fridays are selected and hours from 10:00 A. 

M. to 1:00 or 2:00 P. M. are designated. Special sessions are called by the solicitor, or by a vote of a majority of the grand jury.

The spring term (March-April) and the fall term (September-October) are designated by law as the investigating terms, although the jury can make investigations any time they so desire by a majority vote.

At the spring and fall terms, committees are appointed by the foreman to examine and report on various county activities. Committees may be appointed by any jury at any time. These committees consist of from 3 to 5, or more, members as desired. The jury may have as many committees as it desires. It is customary for the foreman to place the jurors on committees in which they are interested. A good way is to place all names of committees on a paper and pass it around and ask the jurors to designate the committees upon which they would like to serve. The foreman then names the committee members from this list.

The following committees are usually appointed with such others as may be necessary. These committees should be named early in the session, so they will have time to do something.

County Records, Justice of Peace Courts, Roads and 

Bridges, County Jails, County Reformatories, County 

Schools, Convicts and Convict Camps, Almshouse, Police,

Sanitation, Finances, Registration Lists, Purchases.

USUAL PROCEDURE 

It is customary for the solicitor to first state the case, and then interrogate the witness. Additional questions may be put by the foreman, who usually asks the jury if any others would like to interrogate the witness. After all testimony has been presented, and this evidence should be the best obtainable, the solicitor retires and the foreman asks for a motion, which is either "for" or "against" a true bill. After the motion is seconded, the foreman asks, “Is there any discussion?" After all jurors have had an opportunity to express their views, the vote is taken. Care should be duly exercised in voting, remembering that much "weal or woe" depends upon the verdict. 

Remember, that not all indictments result in trial and conviction. Some are dismissed without trial.

The finding of a "no bill" is just as important as the finding of an indictment. It is better to err in dismissing a charge than to indict where there is insufficient evidence. 

The Grand Jury ought, as far as the evidence before it goes, be convinced of the guilt of the defendant, before a true bill is found. (See further about evidence on page 6). 

The jury is usually advised by the solicitor upon legal questions but can ask the court for special instructions as occasion may arise. If the interests of either one or both were felt to be antagonistic to the public interest the grand jury would of course act independently.

The jury may subpoena any public official to appear and bring such records as may be desired. They may also subpoena any other persons as witnesses during an investigation. 

During a special investigation the solicitor may or may not be requested to be present, depending largely upon the convenience of the solicitor and the desires of the jury. 

The foreman in the absence of the solicitor should administer the following oath to all witnesses: "The evidence you shall give the grand jury on this bill of indictment (or presentment, as the case

may be here state case) shall be the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, so help you 

God."-Penal Code, Section 835, 8. 

The grand jury cannot find a bill or make a special presentment except upon the testimony of a witness to whom has been administered the oath as prescribed in the Penal Code. In other words, to make the findings legal, the witness must be sworn on a bill of presentment or indictment charging a specific offense against the party. 

All public offenses as come to light while the grand jury is considering other matters or may have come to the knowledge of other jurors together with all things affecting the public weal or injurious to the entire community are within the province of the grand jury and if upon inquiry these matters seem to require prosecution it is the duty of the grand jury to state them in the form of a presentment.

Section 778 of the Penal Code makes it the duty of the solicitor general to draw all indictments or presentments when requested by the grand jury and to prosecute all indictable offenses. The grand jury may, if it sees fit to do so, act independently of the solicitor in the preparation and filing of presentments. 

No body of men exercise more important public functions than the Grand Jury, so the recommendations made should be carefully considered and the reasons for them should be such as would appeal to reasonable men and not overzealous advocates of some particular reform. 

Considerable time of a grand jury is consumed by the solicitor in taking notes on the testimony of witnesses. 

Stenographers are not permitted. The law in this respect should be amended in order to conserve the time of the jurors and the solicitor. Accurate minutes of the jury's proceedings should be kept as a permanent record. 

EVIDENCE BEFORE THE GRAND JURY 

The number of members of the grand jury in Georgia is fixed by

statute at not less than eighteen nor more than twenty-three. Unanimity is not required, but twelve must concur in the presentment or indictment. 

Blackstone puts the principle as being that no man shall be convicted except by the unanimous voice of twenty-four of his equals or neighbors-twelve on the grand, and twelve on the petit jury. Such a responsibility, however, is not always considered by the members of the grand jury, as bills are frequently voted without serious consideration, and with little effort being made to get all of the available evidence. 

The evidence in such cases is often one-sided, flimsy and illegal, and falls far short of making out a prima facie case against the accused who, except in the case of public officials, have no opportunity either to be heard or to call witnesses. Careful consideration on the part of the jurors as to the kind or character of evidence adduced, as well of evidence presumably available but frequently not offered would obviate many prosecutions and result in a substantial saving of both money and phys ical and mental suffering. (THEN, TOO, THE NATURE OF MANY OFFENSES BROUGHT TO THE ATTENTION OF THE GRAND JURY ARE NOTHING MORE AT BEST, THAN SIMPLE MIS- DEMEANORS, WHICH EITHER SHOULD BE HANDLED IN THE POLICE COURT OR PROBABLY BETTER STILL, DISMISSED AT THE PRELMINIARY HEARING). It is not enough for the grand jury to exercise its veto power on such criminal prosecutions. There should be more careful consideration all along the line as to what truly constitutes an indictable offense, and more caution exercised at preliminary hearings.

Judge Cowles of N. Y. Supreme Court quotes as follows: 

"(575) In 1 Chitty's Criminal Law, 318, it is said: 'Formerly it was laid down that a grand jury ought to find the bill, if probable evidence were adduced to support it because it is only an accusation, and the defendant will afterwards defend himself before a more public tribunal. But great authorities have taken a more merciful view of the subject, and considering the ignominy, the dangers of perjury, the anxiety of delay, and the misery of a prison, have argued that the grand inquest ought, as far as the evidence before them goes, to be convinced of the guilt of the defendant." 

Judge Cowles concludes at page 575: "The rule, as thus laid down, I believe to be the true

one.

No other, in my judgment, is safe, nor is it to be tolerated that a citizen shall be charged with a serious offence, and sent to a petit jury for trial, unless the ex parte testimony taken in his absence, with no power of cross-examination, shall at least, if unexplained, show him guilty."

THE GRAND JURY AND THE COURT 

After the grand jury has been empaneled it is proper and usual for the court to instruct that body as to its duties, and the method of performing them. The grand jury is a constituent part of the court, and its functions, although always proceeding ex parte are of a judicial nature. The Court is PRIMARILY the advisor of the grand jury, as to legal matters on which it may desire information. It cannot, however, restrict the grand jury in its investigation of public affairs, nor limit its inquisitorial powers. Should public officials prove unfaithful to their trust, incapable or corrupt, or should they tolerate practices injurious to the community as a whole then it would be the duty of the grand jury to correct such a condition by the filing of proper indictments.

NUMBER OF GRAND JURORS 

An Act of February 9, 1797, provided, "That no grand jury shall

consist of less than eighteen or more than twenty-three, but twelve may find bill or make a presentment.” That number has been preserved through the various digests and codes of laws to this date. 

To find a true bill or make a presentment, it is necessary that at least twelve should vote for that purpose, whether the number present be eighteen or more. A majority short of twelve is not sufficient.

A judge of the superior court is authorized to fix the amount of compensation to be paid citizen or citizens for services rendered under 

Sections 841 and 842. Chatham County vs. Gaudry, 120 Georgia, 121, (47 S. E., 634). The amount designated is paid as an expense of the court by the county treasurer. In the case cited the commissioners of 

Chatham County were given "exclusive and original jurisdiction" to examine and audit the accounts of county officers. The court held that this did not prevent the grand jury of the county from exercising its powers under Sections 841 and 842 of the Penal Code. 

The Court of Appeals, in the case of McLarty vs. Fulton County, 52 Ga. App. 445, held that a Committee appointed by the grand jury to examine County affairs was entitled to reasonable compensation fixed by a Judge of the Superior Court as part of County expense for their services, although the Committee intended to use its compensation to pay for clerical help in the investigation, for which the County is not liable.

In some states it has been held that a grand jury has no general inquisitorial power to inspect the books of officers, and to subject the officers themselves to examination for the purpose of ascertaining whether 11

or not there has been any official misconduct in any public office, but in Georgia this power is granted by statute. Chatham vs. Gaudry, 120 

Georgia, 121, (47 S. E., 634).

In Georgia grand jurors are by statute expressly empowered to make presentments of offenses which are within their own knowledge or observation or are of public notoriety and injurious to the entire community. Kirby vs. Long, 116 Georgia, 187; 77 Georgia, 143-205; 28 

Corpus Juris, 799.

Georgia does not limit the power of the grand jury investigating the acts of public officials to cases where there may be a prosecutor, and does not prohibit them from prosecuting by presentments on their own motion. Groves vs. State, 73 Georgia, 205.

*THE ORIGIN OF THE GRAND JURY 

The origin of the Grand Jury was in the middle ages and has been claimed by different places. There was something not unlike it in Anglo- 

Saxon times, but its present form can be traced to the Norman institution "Recognito" or "Inquisito" introduced by William the Conqueror in England. Coming as he did to a conquered country he had no way of knowing conditions there. To find out, he applied the Norman device of calling together certain men from the vicinity and asking them about the customs of the district, the King's rights therein, who the landowners were, who were the persons suspected of crime, etc., etc. 

Henry II (1166) extended and systematized this procedure. Whoever was accused by the men from the county had to go to the "ordeal." 

Later with the disappearance of the "ordeal" persons summoned from the vicinity determined the question of guilt or innocence. Sometimes the same body of persons both accused and tried. The professional police and the prosecuting attorney did not exist. Objections arose to have the judgment made by those who had brought the accusation. So grad- 13

ually there came about a separation between the accusing (Grand) jury and the trial (petit) jury. The accusing jury was definitely the body whose duty it was to find out who was suspected of crime and report. 

They found this out by inquiry and investigation before the judges came to hold the sessions. The Grand Jury then, in its origin, was a device for enforcing the law, putting upon definite persons the responsibility for ascertaining those suspected of crime. 

From earliest times the Grand Jury has not been simply a factor in the prosecution of criminals. It has stood in the place of the public. 

It has embodied the ancient concept in English law that no power on earth except the public could say that a person should be charged with a crime. Not king, not attorney general, not crown counsel for the county ever could or can now charge a person with a serious crime in England. That right was and is reserved to the public alone. 

GRAND JURY IS THE PUBLIC 

It cannot be emphasized too strongly that the Grand Jury is the public, and reflecting public sentiment, may refuse to charge a crime even though it has ample facts upon which to bring an indictment. 

The Grand Jury is absolutely independent. It is chosen from the people of the neighborhood, nowadays the people of the county. The people, that is, the Grand Jury, have the power on their own initiative to inquire into all crimes committed within their neighborhood (county) and to say whether the facts adduced are prima facie evidence of a crime, or whether the act should be treated as a crime. The people of the neighborhood—the Grand Jury-have a right to conduct prosecution. It shares with the office of the sheriff the distinction of being an institution of the locality.

It was the attempt of the king to dictate to Grand Juries which hastened the downfall of the Stuarts in England in 1688. The Grand 

Jurors of Middlesex in 1681 refused to indict Lord Shaftsbury at the wish of Charles II, and when the Lord Chief Justice berated them for their action he was taken before the bar of the House and warned never again to attempt to coerce a Grand Jury.

Ordinarily the Grand Jury is drawn from "men of character and position" in the community. It is not merely a part of the court, as most Grand Jurors themselves believe. 

It can accept or reject the admonitions or advice of judge 14

or public prosecutor. What a few Grand Jurors know is that the Grand Jury can not be dismissed before the end of its term.

CITIZEN MAY ASK AN INDICTMENT 

Any citizen may present a bill of indictment to the Grand Jury and ask that it be acted upon, regardless of the wishes of the judge or the public prosecutor.

One of the earliest developments of the Grand Jury theory was that no officer of the government should be a member. The Grand Jury represents the people and officials are jealously excluded. It is untrammelled by official connections. It comes from the people of the neighborhood (county) in which the crime was committed and from precommon law days has been "the people.” *From articles published in the PANEL by the Hon. John D. THE ASSOCIATION OF GRAND JURORS BY MORGAN Blake 

Well-known columnist of the Atlanta Journal and Foreman of the 

July-August Grand Jury of 1943 

A project very close to my heart is that of the Permanent Grand Jury group which has recently been organized, and which promises to become a most helpful influence in civic life, and a stalwart champion of good government.

It is easy for me to visualize what a power for good such an organization of former grand jury officers can be in effecting a continuity in the presentments of the active grand juries, and preserving all the records, including reports of committees. We have already made arrangements for a permanent file for all such reports in the grand jury Not only this but my hope is for such an organization as the one we have brought into being, to grow into a formidable citizens room. 15

committee like unto that one in Milwaukee through whose vigilance and unremitting pressure this city is rated a model for law enforcement and good government.

Too often heretofore some grand jury has furnished blood and sweat and toil in disclosing deplorable conditions in government only to see these disclosures, after the first excitement dies down, disappear into the limbo of forgotten things. With a permanent group functioning behind the scenes and on the alert such disclosures will not be forgotten, but will be kept alive until something is done about them. 

Therefore let those of us who have begun this work refuse to become weary and let such an organization die through lack of interest. 

I feel it is much needed to wield the weapon of public opinion, which is Democracy's strongest force.

AIMS AND OBJECTS OF THE GRAND JURORS ASSOCIATION BY E. L. EMERY Foreman of May-June 1943 Grand Jury and First President of Grand Jurors Association of Fulton County 

This pamphlet will be very helpful to the foremen, and the individual grand jurors, some of whom are not familiar with their broad rights and duties. It is therefore urged that it be examined and studied very carefully so that each man will familiarize himself with its contents. 

Past experience indicates that presentments eminating from grand juries have not at all times reecived sufficient attention, caused partly by lack of specific recommendations and partly by lack of necessary measures, to put in effect recommendations and suggestions contained in the presentments.

Therefore: The Grand Jurors Association of Fulton County was formed for the express purpose of increasing the effectiveness of the grand jury system by:

FIRST: Encouraging the grand jury to act upon its own responsibility as contemplated by law. 

SECOND: By raising the standard of the grand jurors work by supplying adequate and accurate information as to their powers, duties 16

and responsibilities. The grand jury is one of the most potent agencies under our system of government, and when properly functioning, is an institution for the safeguarding of private rights, and an indespensible aid to the administration of public justice. 

THIRD: By reminding its members of their duty "diligently to inquire and true presentments make" of all matters that are given to them, or "to come to their knowledge.”

FOURTH:-By urging the importance of each grand jury keeping an accurate written record of all investigations and indictments in a permanent, well protected file in the grand jury room, to avoid duplication of work, greater continuity and a means of checking ultimate disposition of indictments and investigations which have been developed at great public expense.

FIFTH:-By encouraging the foreman of each succeeding grand jury to make full use of these written records, and to call, for consultation, such members of committees of past grand juries as the jury desires. 

SIXTH:-By advocating measures to put into effect recommendations and suggestions in presentments, from grand juries, which, point to conditions that should be remedied.
