PREFACE 

IT T is admitted by most lawyers that the Court System of New Jersey is more complicated than that of most of the other states. No book has been published that gives the desired explanation of these Courts. 

The result has been that most teachers of Commercial Law and Civics have either treated the subject in a very general way, or omitted it altogether. A few teachers have delved into the Statutes, but it takes a brave person who has not made a formal study of Law, to attempt to secure definite and reliable information from the ever changing Statutes.

Then again, very few teachers have access to the 

Statutes. 

For these reasons this little booklet is printed. If it gives students a more complete understanding of the Courts, and gives teachers less hesitancy in approaching the subject, the long hours spent by the author in hunting through the Federal and State Statutes for the facts presented will be amply repaid.

Grateful acknowledgments are made to Dean George 

S. Harris of New Jersey Law School, Joseph A. O'Brien, A. M., LL.B., of Pfaff & O'Brien, Counsellors at Law, Schuyler M. Townsend, A.M., Department of English, Jefferson

COURTS IN NEW JERSEY 

"Oyez! Oyez! Oyez! All persons having business before the Honorable the Supreme Court of the United States are admonished to draw near and give their attention for the Court is now sitting. God save the United States and the Honorable Court."

In these words the Crier announces the opening of the highest Court in the land every day it is in session. 

The Constitution of the United States, Art. III., Sec. 1, declares: "The Judicial Power of the United States shall be vested in one Supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish.' As business increased Congress has added other 

Federal Courts until now the Federal system of Courts covers the entire country and is being constantly expanded. .

Congress cannot change or abolish the Supreme Court as it is established by the Constitution. It may, however, increase the number of justices at any time, which it has done several times.

A Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices compose the Court. They are appointed by the President for life by and with the advice and consent of the Senate. The salary of the Chief Justice is $20,500.00; and that of the 

Associate Justices $20,000.00 a year.

The Constitution of New Jersey, Art VI., Sec. 1, provides for the Courts of this State as follows: "The judicial power shall be vested in a Court of Errors and Appeals in the last resort in all causes as heretofore; a Court for the trial of impeachments; a Court of Chancery; a Prerogative 

Court; a Supreme Court; Circuit Courts, and such inferior 

Courts as now exist, and as may be hereafter ordained and established by law; which inferior Courts the Legislature may alter or abolish, as the public good shall require." [5]

The Legislature has created many Courts, and may abolish and change them at will. Every time it meets there are laws passed affecting the Courts; so it is difficult to know exactly what powers and jurisdiction the inferior Courts have at any given time. 

Two definitions of Courts that seem to be of more than average clarity are as follows: "An organ of the government belonging to the judicial department, whose function is the application of the laws to controversies brought before it, and the public administration of justice." White County V. Gwin, 136 Ind. 562. "A Court may be more particularly described as an organized body with defined powers, meeting at certain times and places for the hearing and decision of causes and other matters brought before it, and aided in this, its proper business, by its proper officers, viz., attorneys and counsel to present and manage the business, clerks to record and attest its acts and decisions, and ministerial officers to execute its commands, and secure due order in its proceedings." Ex parte Gardner, 22 Nev. 280.

FEDERAL COURTS 

The Federal Courts of most importance to the people of New Jersey are the United States District Court and the 

United States Circuit Court of Appeals. The other Federal 

Courts, such as the United States Supreme Court, Court of Claims, and the Court of Customs Appeal have jurisdiction over all the Country and sit in Washington.

UNITED STATES CIRCUIT COURT OF APPEALS 

The Judicial Districts of the United States are divided by groups of states into ten circuits, in each of which a 

Circuit Court of Appeals meets annually.

New Jersey is in the Third Judicial Circuit, which consists of New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware. There are three Judges in this Circuit, who hold terms of Court [ 6 ]

in Philadelphia on the first Monday in March and the first 

Monday in October of each year.

This Court has appellate jurisdiction only, its decisions being final in most cases. Nearly all cases appealed from the United States District Court are brought up here. 

The Judges are appointed for life by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, and their salaries are $12,500.00.

One of the Justices of the United States Supreme Court is assigned to each Circuit. It is his duty to review before the Supreme Court all cases brought up on appeal from that Circuit, whether they be from the Circuit Court of Appeals or direct from the District Court. In case he attends any session of the Court of Appeals, he shall preside. 

The Judges in this Circuit are: 

Hon. J. Warren Davis, New Jersey 

Hon. Joseph Buffington, Pennsylvania 

Hon. Victor B. Woolsey, Delaware

UNITED STATES DISTRICT COURT 

The United States is divided into eighty-four Judicial Districts, in each of which there is a District Court. New 

Jersey constitutes one Judicial District, and Court is held at Newark on the first Tuesday in April and the first. Tuesday in November of each year; at Trenton on the third 

Tuesday of January and second Tuesday of September; and at Camden on the first Tuesday in December. There are at present four Judges sitting in this district, who divide the business by agreement. They are appointed by the President by and with the advice and consent of the Senate. 

Their term is for life. The salary is $10,000.00. 

This Court is the lowest Federal Court and, therefore, has original jurisdiction in most cases and controversies affecting the United States Government and in civil cases that come under Federal law. It has no appellate jurisdiction except in cases arising under the Chinese Exclusion [7]

Laws. Cases under this law are tried by United States 

Commissioners. It has original jurisdiction over all crimes and offences that come under the Federal laws, and the District Judge sits as Admiralty Judge in all cases of crimes or civil suits of admiralty and maritime jurisdiction. All bankruptcy matters come to this Court through the Referees in Bankruptcy who are officers of the Court. 

Appeal must be taken to the United States Circuit Court of Appeals, except in a limited number of cases where an appeal may be taken direct to the United States Supreme Court. Under this head come all cases where there is a question of the constitutionality of a Statute, appeals in suits against monopolies, appeals upon a writ of error in convictions of crime punishable by death and appeals in certain cases coming under the interstate commerce laws. 

The Judges of this court in New Jersey are: 

Hon. William N. Runyon, Plainfield 

Hon. William Clark, Princeton

Hon. Guy Laverne Fake, Rutherford 

Hon. John Boyd Avis, Woodbury

UNITED STATES COMMISSIONERS 

United States Commissioners are appointed by the 

Judges of the United States District Court. Each Judge may appoint as many in his district as he considers necessary. In congested areas, especially near the seaboard, it is common to find one in each city. 

The Commissioner is a judicial officer and exercises, within very narrow limits, the functions of a United States Judge. To him are committed some of the minor duties of the Court appointing him. He does the preliminary work on cases to come before the Court. His powers are, in the main, in United States cases the same as those of a Justice of the Peace in State cases.

Some of his powers are: 1. To issue warrants for of fenders against the laws of the United States, cause them to be arrested, imprisoned, or bailed for trial. 2. To carry [ 8 ]

into effect the awards of foreign consuls. 3. To sit as arbitrator between the captains and crews of vessels and to enforce his decisions in regard thereto. 4. To take bail and affidavits in civil cases coming under Federal laws. 5. To administer oaths and take acknowledgments. 6. To issue search warrants in internal revenue, customs, and counterfeiting cases, etc., etc.

One of his greatest powers is in Chinese Exclusion cases. He sits as a United States Judge in such cases and may order a deportation. An appeal may be taken, however, to the United States District Court. 

There are seventeen Commissioners in New Jersey.

REFEREES IN BANKRUPTCY 

So many bankruptcy cases come before the Federal District Court that all the time of that Court would be taken up, if they were handled in the regular way. The Bankruptcy Law provides for Referees to be appointed by the Court in such numbers as seems necessary. The Referee does the work of the Court in these cases and his decisions are the judgment of the Court. 

Referees are appointed for two years and receive fees in lieu of salary. 

There are seven Referees in New Jersey.

JUSTICE'S COURT 

This is the lowest Court in the State with civil and criminal jurisdiction, and was established by the Constitution. Justices of the Peace are chosen at the elections held in November for a term of five years. They take office on 

May 1, following, after qualifying by giving a bond of from $500.00 to $3,000.00.

The civil jurisdiction of the Justice formerly covered the entire county, but the Act constituting District Courts restricts it in many ways. The civil jurisdiction has been taken away from it entirely in cities where there is a District Court. [ 10 ]

When a township or ward contains 2,000 inhabitants or less, it may have two Justices; when from 2,000 to 4,000, it may have four; and when over 4,000, it may have five. 

A township not voting in wards and containing over 7,000 inhabitants may have an additional Justice for every 3,000 inhabitants over 4,000. Justices are also elected for every ward in cities.

In civil matters the Justice holds a Small Cause Court, wherein actions may be tried involving not over $200.00, but even this power is limited very much by the Act constituting District Courts.

In criminal matters the Justice acts as a magistrate to have criminals arrested and held to bail or committed; to apprehend, convict, and imprison disorderly persons; to enforce the Acts concerning vice and immorality, cruelty to children and animals, motor vehicles, fish and game, and other similar Acts. This power has also been limited by statute in cities that have Police Courts or Recorders.

Appeals may be taken to the Court of Quarter Sessions. 

A Justice of the Peace in New Jersey can no longer perform marriages.

In all cases the Justice must hold Court in his regular office and in no other place.

POLICE AND RECORDER'S COURTS 

The Police and Recorder's Courts are created by Statutes. 

The real distinction between the duties of a Police Judge or Justice, and a Recorder is often in the designation only. 

Statutes sometimes give the one name or the other to these magistrates whose duties are similar. Statutes define the jurisdiction and authority of each. The main duties of both officials are to hear criminal complaints and complaints under ordinances for penalites, or to enforce fines. Whenever the Statute gives jurisdiction to "all magistrates," this term includes Police Justices, Recorders, and also those Mayors who have power of Justice of the Peace. 

Honeyman, in his book entitled Honeyman's Justice, says regarding Police and Recorder's Courts: "Unfortu- [ 11 ]

nately no attempt has been made by the Legislature to enact a uniform law respecting these magistrates or Courts, even in cities. By Statutes affecting only this or that class of cities, and this or that class of town and townships, the result is so great un-uniformity that it is puzzling to know what is the full or restrictive scope of these several Courts. 

In fact, the law on this general subject is in a state closely resembling confusion."

POLICE COURT 

The Police Court has criminal and semi-criminal jurisdiction only. The Police Justice hears cases concerning the violation of local ordinances, motor vehicle violations, and other small statutory and semi-criminal offences. He has about the same jurisdiction in criminal matters as a Justice of the Peace. The Court is in session whenever necessary. 

Appeals may be taken to the Court of Common Pleas or to the Supreme Court.

RECORDER'S COURT 

The Recorder corresponds to the Police Justice. In the small towns and rural sections the Police Court is known as the Recorder's Court, and it has the same powers and jurisdiction in its community as the Police Court has in the cities.

DISTRICT COURT 

The District Courts are created by a special Act of the Legislature and there is one or more in each county. They are also, under the law, established in some cities. There is one Court in cities over 17,000 and two in cities over 150,000. One Judge constitutes the Court and is appointed by the Governor, with and by the advice and consent of the Senate for a term of five years. This Court has jurisdiction in all civil suits up to $500.00. It has no criminal jurisdiction. The Legislature has also given this Court jurisdiction in many other matters, such as fish and game, cruelty to animals, pure food and drugs, etc. The bulk of its work, however, consists of cases involving contracts, negotiable instruments, landlord and tenant, and other cases [ 12 ]

of a business nature. Tort cases involving automobile accidents have of late years become more and more numerous. 

Salaries of the Judge vary according to the population of the county or city in which the Court is established. 

The Judge tries the cases coming before him without a jury, unless one is demanded by either party to the suit. 

Where a jury is demanded and the amount of the controversy does not exceed $50.00, it consists of six persons; over that amount twelve persons.

An appeal from the decision of this Court is taken to the Supreme Court.

DISTRICT COURT OF CRIMINAL JURISDICTION 

In 1926 the Legislature passed an Act permitting the Legislature to create Criminal Judicial District Courts in any county when it deemed it necessary. Thus far, only two such Courts have been created. Both are in Bergen 

County and are known as the First and Second Criminal District Courts of Bergen County. These courts have only criminal jurisdiction. They have exclusive jurisdiction in matters committed to them and also have concurrent jurisdiction with Recorders' Courts, Police Courts and Justices'

Courts in matters pertaining to the violation of local ordinances. 

Appeals may be taken to the Supreme Court.

The Judges of these Courts are (January, 1931), in the First District, Hon. Charles J. McCarthy, Hackensack, and in the Second District, Hon. J. Wallace Leyden, Hackensack.

COURT OF COMMON PLEAS 

The Court of Common Pleas is one of the busiest Courts in the State. It holds three stated terms a year and special terms when ordered to do so by the Supreme Court. It has jurisdiction in all civil cases, where title to land is not involved, throughout the county where it is located. There is at least one such Court in every county. One Judge constitutes this Court and the Justice of the Supreme Court [ 13 ]

holding the Circuit Court in the county is an ex-officio 

Judge of the Court.

All appeals are to the Supreme Court. 

Some of the matters under its jurisdiction are: 

1. Changing the name of any town or village in the county, or of any person on his request. 

2. Cases relating to insolvency. 3. 

Cases relating to roads and wrecks. 

4. Application for exemption from military duty. 

5. 

Granting of licenses.

It can also try Circuit Court cases referred to it by the 

Supreme Court. 

CIRCUIT COURT 

There is one Circuit Court in each county, sometimes called the County Court. It has a general civil jurisdiction in the county in which it is located, therefore, its authority is concurrent with the Supreme Court Circuit in civil matters. The Circuit Court has no criminal powers. 

The Governor appoints, with the advice and consent of the Senate, Circuit Court Judges who pass from county to county to hold Court. In the counties that have large populations the Judges have so much work that usually they are permanently located. They are appointed for seven years at a salary of $16,000.00. The work of these Courts has grown to such an extent that there are now fourteen Circuit 

Judges, holding three terms of Court a year in each county.

The Supreme Court Justice has to ask the Common Pleas Judge to hold the Cireuit, very often, which is permissible under the law. The Supreme Court Justice in whose district the Circuit Court is located, may also preside, but rarely does.

Appeals may be made either to the Supreme Court or to the Court of Errors and Appeals.

COURT OF QUARTER SESSIONS 

The Court of Quarter Sessions has original jurisdiction over all crimes committed within the county in which it is located, except murder and treason, and in counties having a population of 250,000 or more murder cases may also be tried. There is one such Court in every county. It has appellate jurisdiction in cases brought to it from Police 

Courts, Justices' Courts, and Recorders' Courts.

It holds three sessions a year, meeting on the second 

Tuesdays of January, May, and October.

The Judge of the Court of Common Pleas constitutes this Court.

COURT OF SPECIAL SESSIONS 

This Court has the same jurisdiction as the Court of Quarter Sessions, except that in this Court the defendant waives a jury, and the cause is determined by the Court alone. It is presided over by the Judge of the Court of 

Common Pleas, and all appeals are to the Supreme Court upon a Writ of Error.

COURT OF OYER AND TERMINER 

There is a Court of Oyer and Terminer in each county. 

This is the chief criminal Court and has jurisdiction over [ 15 ]

all criminal matters in the county including murder and treason. It has no civil jurisdiction.

The Court is composed of a Supreme Court Justice and the Judges of the Common Pleas Court of the county. In case the Common Pleas Judge is absent the Supreme Court Justice may sit alone. By Statute the Common Pleas Judge may hold Court alone in counties with 300,000 population or more.

Before a case can be brought into this Court, it is necessary for the Grand Jury to see whether or not the case is worth bringing up. This is done for two reasons: first, to expedite the work of the Court by refusing to refer cases that have no merit, or cannot be proved. Second, so that persons cannot be falsely accused of crime and held to trial for spite by some enemy. This would mean that an innocent person might be put to great expense to defend himself, and his character and good name might suffer. A Grand 

Jury, therefore, inquires into every crime and, if it thinks that there is a reasonable possibility that the person is guilty, indicts the prisoner, which means that it holds him for trial by the Court.

Appeals may be taken to the Supreme Court by Writ of Error.

SUPREME COURT CIRCUIT 

The State is divided into nine judicial districts. In each of these districts a Justice (including the Chief Justice) of the Supreme Court holds Court, commonly called the Supreme Court. This Court is really the same as the Supreme 

Court except that instead of sitting in Trenton, as they do for the Supreme Court sessions, the Justices sit in nine districts. The work of the Supreme Court Justices, however, is so heavy that it is customary for them to send all of their trial issues to the Circuit Court Judges. The 

Statutes allow this, and it is seldom that a Supreme Court Justice does more than open the Court each term and charge the Grand Jury. Then sometimes he appears on different days during the term to hear motions. At times he may sit to try some very important case. [ 16 ]

It has been ruled that a judgment of this Court is the same as a judgment of the Supreme Court. A judgment obtained in a case tried in the Supreme Court Circuit is entered in the Supreme Court. It has civil jurisdiction throughout the State to an unlimited extent. 

An appeal may be taken to the Court of Errors and 

Appeals.

SUPREME COURT 

The Supreme Court is composed of a Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices, appointed by the Governor, with the advice and consent of the Senate, for terms of seven years. The salary of the Chief Justice is $19,000.00 and that of the Associate Justices $18,000.00. It meets in Trenton on the third Tuesday in February and the first Tuesday in May and October. Special terms may be ordered by the 

Chief Justice or any two Associate Justices at any time.

This Court is now so busy that it has been divided into 

Parts I., II., and III., which meet at the same time.

It has original and appellate jurisdiction in all cases both civil and criminal, and appeals may be made to it from nearly all the lower Courts. It is not necessary for all the 

Justices to sit in any case. As stated above, the Court has [ 17 ]

been divided into three parts, three Justices sitting in each part, but any single Justice sitting alone may hold the Court.

One of the important powers of this Court is its superintendence over all governing bodies of municipalities through the writ of certiorari. peals.

COURT OF ERRORS AND APPEALS 

The Constitution provides that a Court of Errors and Appeals shall be the Court of last resort in all causes. It is, therefore, the highest tribunal in the State, from whose decisions there is no appeal except in two instances. These two exceptions are: If the parties are residents of different states, an appeal may be taken to the United States Supreme Court, and if there is a question involving the constitutionality of the law, as determined by the Federal Constitution, there may be an appeal taken to the United States 

Supreme Court. 

The Court is composed of the Chancellor, who is its head, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, the eight 

Associate Justices of the Supreme Court, and six Special 

Judges. The six Special Judges need not be learned in the law, and several times in the history of the State one or more of them have been laymen, but it is customary to ap- [ 18 ]

point lawyers. These Special Judges are appointed by the 

Governor with and by the advice and consent of the Senate, for six years, the term of one expiring each year. Their salary is $40.00 per diem.

This arrangement is unique in New Jersey and dates back to Colonial times when the Governor was the Chancellor, and he with his Council, met with the Supreme Court to hear appeals. The six Special Judges now appointed correspond to the Council and, when the Governor's duties became too many and complicated, a special Chancellor was appointed.

COURT OF CHANCERY 

The Court of Chancery is the only equity Court in the State. The function of this Court is to give such relief as is not given by the common law Courts. Lack of space forbids a dissertation on equity here, but it is necessary for the proper understanding of the duties of this Court that the student have knowledge of that division of the law known as equity. Any good textbook will give this information.

The Chancellor composes the Court of Chancery. As the work of the Court has grown he has been given assistance from time to time. There are now ten Vice-Chancellors, five Advisory Masters, numerous Masters in Chancery and several Special Masters. The Vice-Chancellors hold Court in various parts of the State and their decisions are the decisions of the Chancellor. [ 19 ]

SURROGATE'S COURT 

There is a Surrogate's Court in every county. The 

Surrogate is elected by the people at the general elections and his term of office is five years. Under the law any citizen may be elected to the office. It is not necessary that he be a lawyer.

The Surrogate's Court admits wills to probate when no objections are filed and where the will is regular on its face. If objections are made to the filing of the will by someone interested in the estate, the matter must be referred to the Orphans' Court at once.

A person that dies leaving property but no will dies intestate. Before the next of kin can divide the personal property, the Surrogate must appoint an administrator to settle up the estate and divide it according to law. It is the duty of the Surrogate to audit his accounts when requested to do so by someone interested in the estate. [ 20 ]

When a person dies leaving a will, an executor is usually named in the will whose duty it is to settle the estate and divide it according to the terms of the will. The executor must produce the will before the Surrogate and qualify. The 

Surrogate also audits his accounts when requested. 

If a person dies intestate leaving a widow and minor children, the widow is entitled to apply for the administration of the estate. If, however, there are children who are over the age of twenty-one, it will be necessary for the widow to procure a renunciation and request signed by the adult children.

Where there is no executor named in the will by the testator, the next of kin are entitled to select among themselves an administrator or administratrix with the will annexed.

A will signed by a testator without an attestation clause and having two or more witnesses to prove the will, will be accepted by the Surrogate if the witnesses appear to identify their signatures. If one (or more) of the witnesses is dead or cannot appear, other persons knowing his signature may identify it. Where there is a perfect attestation clause only one witness is necessary. 

The Surrogate also names guardians of minors. 

All appeals are taken to the Orphans' Court or the Prerogative Court.

ORPHANS' COURT 

The Judge of the Court of Common Pleas is the Judge of this Court and the Justice of the Supreme Court in each circuit is Judge ex officio. This Court hears all controversies respecting wills. A will that has been objected to before the Surrogate is brought to this Court and here its validity is determined. Appeals, however, may be taken to the Prerogative Court.

After a will has been brought to the Orphans' Court and found valid and if an accounting is filed in the Surrogate's Court, the Orphans' Court must approve the accounting. [ 21 ]

This Court meets once a week.

The Orphans' Court also makes decrees of adoption of children and may name guardians of minors.

When application is made for guardianship of children under fourteen, the father has the preference if he wishes, the mother's right is next considered. In case the father is dead, children over fourteen make their own applications and may choose any guardian they wish.

It may be of interest to know the fees that are allowed by the Court to administrators or executors for their work in connection with the settlement of an estate. These fees are fixed by law as follows:

When the estate is $1,000.00 or less, seven per cent of the amount of the estate; from $1,000 to $5,000, four per cent; from $5,000 to $10,000, three per cent; from $10,000 to $50,000, two per cent. When the estate is over $50,000 the Judge of the Orphans' Court decides what commission shall be paid, and it is usually calculated according to the amount of the estate and the work performed by the administrator. He usually allows from two and one-half per cent to five per cent of the entire estate. 

PREROGATIVE COURT 

The Prerogative Court hears appeals from the Orphans' Court and Surrogate's Court. It also has the same original jurisdiction over wills, letters of administration and settlement of disputes that these Courts have. Most of its work, however, is the hearing of appeals from these lower Courts.

This Court meets in Trenton and is in session whenever necessary.

The Ordinary is Judge of this Court. The Chancellor acts as Ordinary and the Vice-Chancellors as Vice-Ordinaries.

The Ordinary is also called the Surrogate General. 

Appeals are made to the Court of Errors and Appeals. [ 22 ]

JUVENILE AND DOMESTIC RELATIONS COURT 

Juvenile Courts were created by the Legislature in 1903, and an additional Act in 1929 created a Juvenile and Domestic Relations Court in every county in the state. In other than first class counties the Judge of the Court of Common Pleas acts. If the county has more than one Common Pleas Judge, the Supreme Court Justice presiding therein designates which shall serve.

This Court has exclusive jurisdiction over all cases against a child under sixteen years of age who violates a municipal law, commits what should be punishable as a crime or who is disorderly, habitually vagrant, incorrigible, immoral, associates with vicious persons, grows up in idleness and crime, goes to unlawful establishments, roams the streets at night, is habitually truant from school, etc., etc. 

If the child is convicted or pleads guilty, and so becomes adjudged a juvenile delinquent, the Court may commit such child to the State Home for Boys or Girls, as the case may be, or may commit it to any public institution in the county which is established for the reform of juvenile delinquents. The Court may suspend sentence or place the child on probation, or both. If the child is placed on probation the county probation officer shall have charge of him during the term of probation. The Court may also sentence the offender to imprisonment or may fine him, if in its judgment the case warrants it.

A child accused of murder or manslaughter cannot be tried in this Court but must be bound over to the criminal Courts.

COURT OF PARDONS 

The Court of Pardons consists of the Governor, the Chancellor and the six specially appointed Judges of the Court of Errors and Appeals. It meets on the first Tuesdays in April and September in Trenton. A majority of the Court, of whom the Governor must be one, may grant pardons after conviction and commute sentences of death to imprisonment at hard labor for life, or for a stated number of years. It may also remit fines and forfeitures. It cannot grant pardon in cases of impeachment. [23]

COURT FOR THE TRIAL OF IMPEACHMENTS 

When the Governor or any officer of the State has been suspected of committing a misdemeanor while in office, the lower house of the Legislature, the Assembly, makes the formal charge of such offence. When the Assembly has voted that the suspicions are well founded, the Senate sits as a Court for the Trial of Impeachments and hears the charge. A two-thirds vote is necessary to convict and there is no appeal from such conviction.
