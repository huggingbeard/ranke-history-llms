The government, through its federal, state and local agencies, has published detailed books and articles on the subject of air raid precautions, and every American should become familiar with that information.

Experience in foreign countries warns us that the nervous strain of an actual raid makes one forget just what to do. Here in concise form are facts you should remember -presented in a booklet which you can always carry with you and refer to when the emergency arrives. 

An "auxiliary fireman" has one of the most dangerous war assignments in England. For his personal protection, Mr. Loud, after volunteering for such work here, studied all the facts he could find on air raids. Although there is disagreement among the "experts" on a few details, he had enough confidence in the following information to want to have it always in his pocket. We believe other 

Americans will want to have it too.

VUVERSITY PURPOSE OF AIR RAIDS 

AIR RAIDS are designed to destroy life and property, and to break the morale of the defending communities. 

Shipyards, factories, airfields, railroads, and bridges are prime objectives, but no building or life is free from danger when bombs begin to fall.

TYPES OF BOMBS (1) INCENDIARIES: 

a. The most commonly used incendiary is a cylinder of magnesium with a thermite core, thirteen inches long and two inches in diameter, weighing about two pounds. 

Thermite is a mixture of two powders which when ignited burn with twice the heat required to melt steel. It seldom explodes and consequently is harmless.

(2) HIGH EXPLOSIVE (“HE") BOMBS: 

This type of bomb usually explodes when it hits the ground or a building, unless it carries a timing device to delay its discharge. 

(3) GAS BOMBS: 

a. Toxic (poisonous)—may cause physical injury or death. b. Irritant-result in personal discomfort and are designed to create panic and confusion. 

Imaginative authors of fiction have greatly exaggerated the effectiveness of gas. Up to now in this war it has not been used to any great extent.

ESSENTIAL PRECAUTIONS AGAINST BOMBS (1) INCENDIARIES: 

a. Find location of fire alarm box nearest to your home and learn how it is operated. b. Know how to shut off the electricity, gas and water in your home. Also if you have an oil burner know how to stop the flow of oil at the tank. c. Remove all inflammable materials from attics, cellars, and garages. d. Obtain as much of the following equipment as possible: 

1. Sand, sand barrels, and longhandled shovels. 

2. Garden Hose. 

3. Water Pails. 

4. Fire Extinguishers. 

5. Heavy Gloves.

6. Flash Lights (with extra batteries). e. Know the name, address and telephone number of your district air raid warden. 

(2) HIGH EXPLOSIVE BOMBS: 

a. Find the best bomb shelter in your neighborhood. If you are in your own home when "HE" bombs begin to fall stay away from windows, and lie down under a good stout table. b. Large steel-constructed office buildings make reasonably good shelters below the top three floors and above the bottom three floors. However keep away from elevator wells. c. When outdoors, and "HE" bombs begin to fall, Lie Down Immediately-

(3) GAS BOMBS: 

If gas is used and you have gas masks wear them. Otherwise go to the room in your home which has the fewest doors and windows. Paste paper over glass. 

Stuff rags in cracks and around doors.

Gas is usually heavier than air and consequently thickest at or below ground levels. 10

PREPARING FOR BLACKOUTS 

1. During complete blackouts villages and cities are as dark as deep forests. Trees, hydrants, poles and other obstacles can not be seen by pedestrians. Therefore become familiar with every inch of territory you might have to cover during a blackout. Be able to find the way home with your eyes closed. Know where every tree or obstacle is located. Be able to locate the nearest hydrants blindfolded so that you can lead fire fighters to them during an emergency. 

2. WINDOWS 

a. Tape to prevent shattering. b. Cover with long heavy drapes or darkcolored cloth, fastening tops and sides. 

3. DOORS 

Treat like exposed windows. 

4. SKYLIGHTS 

Cover with rigid non-reflecting materials.

5. LIGHTS 

a. Shade all sources of light to prevent direct rays on windows, even though these have already been blacked out. b. Remove bulbs from outside lights and and from unused rooms. One ordinary light bulb can be seen 15 miles from a plane on a clear night.

DURING A BLACKOUT 

1. Stay off the streets. 

2. Wear white clothing. 

3. If you must be out: a. Do not hurry. b. Cross streets at cross-walks c. Avoid crowds. d. Do not light matches. 121

HOW TO EXTINGUISH INCENDIARIES 

Permit a bomb to burn for about a minute before approaching it. They sometimes contain an explosive charge. 

1. USE OF SAND: 

a. A plentiful supply of sand should be available in pails, barrels or bags at locations where incendiaries may fall, -particularly in attics, every floor of the house, garages, and near outside door in the yard. b. Approach bomb carefully, holding a shield such as an ash can cover to protect against flying magnesium or embers. c. Place sand beside bomb with a longhandled shovel. Place sand on bomb. 

Then (unless there are other bombs requiring immediate attention) scoop 13

onto the shovel and place in a pail half filled with sand. Remove to a place away from all combustible materials. 

2. USE OF WATER: 

a. Don't apply a direct stream of water on an incendiary bomb. It would cause an explosion and spread the fire over a wide area. b. When using a garden hose or stirrup pump apply water around the bomb to prevent its setting fire. Then use a very fine spray on the bomb itself. This fog spray cools and smothers the burning chemicals. 

Note: Sodium and potassium bombs are ignited by water. If it appears that you have struck a bomb, control it by smothering with sand or a similar agent. 

3. USE OF FIRE EXTINGUISHERS: 

a.

Foamite and Pyrene extinguishers are 14

of value in smothering gasolene, oil, sodium, and potassium bombs. b. The type of extinguisher which mixes sulphuric acid and baking soda when inverted forms carbon dioxide gas which forces out water. Caution should be used not to throw the full stream at an incendiary. c. The ordinary 2½ gallon liquid extinguisher does not last long enough to be effective on fire bombs.

GENERAL CONCLUSIONS 

AIR RAIDS are dangerous to everybody. If a bomb falls directly upon a person, death is practically certain. However, except for the direct hit, danger is relatively small provided the necessary precautions are taken. Statisticians could divide the area of this country by the number of bombs likely to be dropped, and arrive at the mathematical probabilities 15

of being killed. An automobile, driven at high speeds by an inexperienced driver, is a very dangerous thing. But few of us hesitate to ride in automobiles. Drill yourself and your family in the things you should know and do when the "Alert Signal" sounds. Get acquainted with your local air raid wardens and follow their advice and instructions. 

Above all keep cool and you will probably keep alive. 

Undoubtedly as time goes on new bombs will be designed, and improved methods to combat them will be published. The best defense against air raids is the ability to anticipate the enemy's attack and the fortitude and self-discipline to follow the proper procedures while the planes are dropping their loads. 

The army, navy, and other defense forces will warn us before the enemy strikes. The rest is up to us. 16
