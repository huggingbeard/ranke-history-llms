AUTHOR'S APOLOGY 

This was not a born book. It originally was a set of short stories that the author had done in his own free style of traveling and calling a spade a spade. Somehow the news spread that Adventuring Alone in Europe made interesting reading. Getting tired of having new copies made as fast as the old ones would wear out, and of keeping dozens of friends waiting, I decided to put that and a few other stories into one book and let everyone who cared to read them. 

Everything in this book is the truth and nowhere have I resorted to a lie just to create a laugh. I do feel, however, that I owe that portion of the public that may read this book an apology, as I do not write for a living, so therefore, do not expect any eloquent flow of verbs, nouns and pronouns. 

I love to get a laugh out of my experiences when traveling; while a lot of people travel and take it too seriously, such as spending a great deal of time visiting old castles and museums. 

When they get home they have nothing to talk about as no one cares to listen to them. Most travel books are too dry, so in this book I have at least tried to make my readers smile. 

So forget your troubles, get giddy and travel with me.

June 17At Sea 

It is cloudy and spitting rain today, and a little rougher. I debated world troubles and topics of the day all forenoon with a New Yorker, a fellow from

Heavens, I have gone to hell. I just had tea and cookies at 3 P. M. after a big luncheon. Imagine taking tea like an old biddy. They serve tea every afternoon.

The crowd is tame and blue, as the sun is not shining. If we can only get some of these old babies half stewed it will start things. 

Tonight is "gala dinner"-and what a feast! I never saw such a layout, and all the old girls put on their evening dresses, without brassieres-glad I am not a boy! 

Sea is kicking up now. This boat is very seaworthy.

June 20At Sea 

What a day! Clear sky, blue water and glorious sunshine. I rose after five hours in bed-tired, but my bath steward had my bath of lovely cold sea water all ready for me. You see, on board there are three servants that watch over you like a motheryour bedroom steward makes your bed, does all your favors; your bath steward calls you every morning at a special time and makes your bath ready; and lastly, you have the same waiter and the same table at every meal-and he tries to kill you with food! 

This morning we-Denny, Murphy, Dorothy and

Tanette-go out and spend the forenoon in the bow of the boat, in our bathing suits, taking sun baths and pictures, while we plowed steadily on through the beautiful blue ocean.

The Captain tells me that the crowd was the nicest class of people that he has ever carried. We take all kinds of snaps, and, jokingly, I introduce Tanette to one of the officers as the future Mrs. Foley, and said that we were being married in Venice-but the truth is that she is getting off in England and staying in 

Europe until January, ending her trip in Sicily, whereas I am headed for Algiers, North Africa, via 

France, returning through Switzerland, Germany and Belgium. Tanette is gathering writing material and no doubt will also gather other boy friends, and

After a lovely meal, everyone attends a movie or a lecture-but I am tired, so go for my first nap, which I never get, as there are some lovely children on board with their parents and a nurse-they are from South America and have all kinds of toys, and they make a terrible racket outside my room. He repeated his request three times-finally I got a woman to translate for me and this is what he wanted-he had seen me give a man one of my corncob pipes, a huge affair, for a souvenir, and he wanted one of these, and just wouldn't be happy until he had one. Later on, as I passed his father and mother I could see him telling them that I was the man who had given him the wonderful pipe, and they thanked me.

Tonight there is no dance, as there are talkies in one room and a lecture in another, so my gang is all split up. Murphy has a new girl, and Denny has taken Tanette to the lecture, so I am alone for the first time. 

Watched a golden sunset over the blue ocean tonight. It was glorious! We lose an hour each night

for five nights, as the sun changes, so we have to set our watches ahead each night. Well, this is the end of a perfect day-clean fun, from 8 A. M. till midnight. Now I know why people want to go to Europe. 

We all retire early. 

June 21 - At Sea

Up early and had an invigorating bath of cold sea water. After another huge breakfast we go out to the bow of the ship with all my gang, where we lie in the sun for three hours in our bathing suits.

After luncheon I give a concert in the music salon, accompanied by a fine man pianist. I leave it to the audience they said it was good. I was called after this "the Irish Tenor from Boston." 

Next, Tanette and I play deck tennis on the upper deck. I lean toward her, as she is a one-man woman. 

Saw some sharks and whales today—also sighted our first ship since leaving New York six days ago.

The one thing I dislike on this ship is the late dinner hour-dinner starts at 7:30 and it is 8:45 before you leave the dining saloon. After dinner we take a little walk on deck. Tanette looked very chic in a black décolleté gown, which makes her look slimmer. I must tell her that, because women who are a little heavy in the sitting room like to be told that a certain dress makes them look thinner.

June 22 

At Sea

Up early again, and the usual routine of bath, breakfast, walk-and then down again for a huge luncheon. This luncheon is larger than the dinner. 

The favorite daily game on board this ship is called "Filling the gut," and a man would do well if he had a tapeworm. 

Today everyone looks dejected. It is cloudy and gray. It was a little rough and foggy last night, but we didn't go to bed until 2 A. M. We have had six days and nights on the go and everyone is dead tired. 

I didn't see any rough stuff in all that time. With so

much loss of sleep I feel like an old rubber boot, and Tanette is not so pleasant now and I don't know whether she is giving me the gate or not. This day isn't much- we will charge it off to profit and loss. 

Friday, June 23 — At Sea 

We forget yesterday and smile again today, even if it is still rough and foggy. You see, we are getting nearer England every day now, so what can you expect. 

My first laugh today was at breakfast-they have funny names for all the dishes. They certainly put on the dog. For instance, you might order something with a lot of big words and get roasted lamb's nipple surrounded by some watercress. While at breakfast, one of the dishes I ordered was an English dish, called "Bubble and Squeak," and when it came it was a vegetable hash. I said to the waiter, "I suppose if you were serving baked beans you would call them "Wind and Whistle." 

My next laugh was with the Captain. It being very foggy I said to him that I had a grand idea. He asked me what it was, and I told him that the English 

Shipping Board ought to take large hunks of Limburger cheese and put them in their lighthouses along the English coast so ships could smell their way-as they certainly cannot see the lights. He laughed at this until his belly shook like a jelly fish. 

It is still cloudy and a heavy sea is running, which keeps our masquerade ball inside tonight. I am made

Saturday, June 24 - Last Day At Sea

Well, I was up early, as we are now off the English Coast and are passing other vessels. Some of the boys and girls couldn't get up-too much brew. This is just a lousy feeling day. Everyone is packing. 

Half the boat is getting off at Southampton, England. 

I am still sore at Tanette and Murphy for giving me the dump, but it is our last night together, so when it gets to be 11 P. M. we all go to the tap room, forgive and forget, and drink highballs and champagne.

I went to bed at 1 A. M., but half the boat stayed up all night, as they are to be called to disembark at

June 25We Land 

This voyage is ending now, as we pull into Le 

Havre, France, at 3 P. M. today, and I will say this, that I met the cleanest crowd of 350 people that could be assembled. There was no rough stuff or drunkenness aboard. The ship "Pennland" is a marvelous sea boat. There was only one day and one night of heavy seas, and there weren't over a half dozen who were seasick.

I've just attended Mass, and thought of something I wouldn't leave out of this story for the world. On this boat is a General Chiriboga, of Ecuador, South 

America, with his family-a wife and four children and an Indian maid. There is a boy, about six years old, named José Ruben, who is highly attached to me because I play with him and show him tricks. He grabs me by the hand and runs me up two decks to a woman who translates for him-this was it. "He likes you very much and is afraid you are getting off here at Southampton, and you haven't taken his address to send him the picture of you two taken together." When I took his father's address, wasn't he happy. His father is consul from Ecuador to France.

We arrive at Paris at 7:30 P. M. and register at the Grand Hotel, where Denny is well acquainted. 

It is near the Gare de l'Est, at Boulevard de Strasbourg. This hotel has two women clerks who speak only French-Madame Jean and Madame Danays.

I had the women ice up three kinds of wine for me to try. The one that pleases my taste the most I will stick to. There are thousands of sidewalk cafés and interesting characters of all kinds from every part of the world-prostitutes by the thousands walk the streets at night entirely unmolested by the police. Of course these are only for the fellows who want that stuff. Ninety per cent. of France is made up of good, devout people, so why pay attention to the other ten per cent.? Even ill-bred men approach you and ask you if you want to be shown the "dirt." Only a moron or halfwit could fall for such stuff.

Well, leaving all kidding aside, the really nice

Monday, June 26 - Paris 

We slept late this morning-our first good night's rest in nine days since leaving New York.

Now I must devote a paragraph to this old-fashioned, second-class hotel. There are no servants dressed in gold braid, but there is an old man who acts as porter, shoe-black, and a large, blonde youth who acts as "Handy Andy," who does everything else. He greets you every morning with a smiling, "Bon Jour. 

Then there are also the two lovely women clerks. 

Denny doesn't know this, but I am falling for Madame Jean. 

I took a six mile walk alone tonight, and at least five babies-I call them babies, as they weren't any of them over seventeen-asked me to go with them, for what is the equivalent of an American dollar. 

Wasn't I glad to get back safely to Madame Jeanjust to hold her hand and say, "Bon Soir, Madame 

Jean," and hear her reply, "Bon Soir, Monsieur Foley."

Tuesday, June 27 — Paris 

Up at 8 A. M. after a fine sleep, called Denny, had our "chocolat," then we're off for a long walk.

This morning we take in the most beautiful part of Paris-starting at the Place de la Concorde, we walk down the Champs Elysées, to the Arc de Triomphe, where we pay our respects to the unknown soldier. We walked for miles through the beautiful 

Bois de Boulogne-as pretty a woods as I have ever seen, with a large natural pond in the center. We hired a boat for an hour to row on this lovely pondand found it very restful.

We then go and have our regular dinner, with wine. Augusto, from Peru, had invited me to have dinner with him and his mother at the swell Hotel

Normandie, but my good clothes are at the laundry and I couldn't dress properly, so declined. 

It is now 10 P.M. Denny has gone to a show and

I am alone, so I must go down and swap a few words with my bonne amie-Madame Jean. She is lovely.

I thought she was a widow with two children, so it makes me sad to find out that her husband is still living, but she left him five years ago because he used

to beat her up so. What I want to say to her I pick out of a French book, and she replies through Denny if he is around, if not she writes it on paper. Denny is fond of her, too, which makes us feel very much at home. 

Wednesday, June 28 Paris

Today I have an appointment with Augusto, which 

I keep at 10:30 A. M. We strolled through the fashionable section and the real pretty girls are all Americans. This bunk about Parisian beauties is a fallacy—the same goes for Paris styles. Only the most wealthy Parisians dress in style the rest, the middle class and the poor, have nothing but dirty clothes and soupy vests and wear bed slippers in the street. For example, I wore clean linen knickers on the boulevard and everyone looked at me as though I were a curio. When I returned to my hotel I asked 

Madame Jean, in mixed French and English, why the laugh, and she told me that no one wears linen knickers in Paris. So I changed and put on striped flannel trousers and a leghorn straw hat, turning the brim down, as they do in America. Again they stared at me, and I found out that they do not wear straw hats of this make in France because they come from Italy. Well, the moral of this is—when in Paris, do as Parisians do-don't take a bath, and wear a heavy vest and a heavy felt hat no matter how hot the day. 

And here is another kick I have. I haven't had a good meal since I've been here. We eat in the

So we take it all in for about an hour and at eleven we leave for our hotel. It is the same as New York's Greenwich Village was before prohibition. 

Thursday, June 29 — Paris 

Denny leaves today for Algiers, North Africa, stopping off at Lyons before taking the boat at Marseilles. I follow him in two days, going to Algiers, but by a different route taking in the Spanish border and the Pyrenees Mountains. 

Well, I had my first verbal battle today! So as not to be lonesome after Denny left, I planned to take an all-day trip to Fontainebleau. When I went up for

my bus seat I found I was left flat-all the seats were taken. I had told the man I would be there, but he hadn't saved me a seat. You should have heard me then-in English-it was good. 

This is Thursday afternoon, and I admit I am lonesome. Alone in the big city of Paris-and I don't know a soul, and no one speaks English! I snap out of it, because I can do nothing else, so I visit the sad but beautiful Cathédrale de Notre Dame, and finish the afternoon by walking all over the Island in the Seine River on which the original city of Paris first stood. I have walked from fifteen to twenty miles a day for five days over the streets of Paris, and I've probably seen as much as though I were here for a month. 

I changed restaurants today. This makes my fourth. I love roast beef, and in each place I ordered it but couldn't eat it-it tasted lousy. I know damn well it is horse meat and they gave me the butt. Tonight I will take the subway for the American quarter, just to see if someone will say hello to me. 

Well, here I am back again. No hello from anyone, and this is Paris! Sad, gay, good and bad. I guess if you're good you are lonesome. Tomorrow 

I will attempt to see Versailles. 

Friday, June 30 — Versailles 

Well, I got up early today and took a lovely bus ride to Versailles, to see the Palace of Louis the Fourteenth. I was seated beside a Miss Irene, from

BREEZY ADVENTURE 

New York, a nice girl, whose acquaintance I made and whom I am also going to meet tonight and do a little slumming. 

The Palace of Versailles is beautiful beyond description-over a thousand rooms, full of gold, marble and priceless art treasures. Louis was the bird that never took a bath, still he slept in gold and laces. 

He was lousy, but kept himself swathed in scented oils. He was the baby that invented the backscratcher for the women of his court. The women were also "alive." Old Louis got tired of having the women stop to scratch their backs at State Balls, so—the invention. It has a long, thin handle, about a foot and a half long, with a small human hand carved of ivory on the end. Now, when one of the old girls felt a "cooty" on her back, she simply put the scratcher down inside her corset and scratched until the little fellow turned over on his back. Then, at bedtime, he automatically jumped on Louis, as he was the filthier of the two. Old Louis was the father of fifty-nine children, by his own wife and kept women—too bad he didn't make it sixty, it would only have been the matter of another night.

After Versailles, we visit the beautiful palace at Malmaison-the favorite palace of Napoleon and Josephine. It was most interesting and very beautiful. All Napoleon's favorite books, swords, guns and furniture are there intact. The gold dining-room service set is priceless. Everything he owned seemed to be in gold. The paintings are marvelous-also the gardens and grounds around the palace are very beautiful.

My first good luncheon since I landed in France I had today. I ate veal, which I never eat at home, and that is practically the only meat in France that is any good.

168 Miles in 9½ Hours over the Swiss Alps by Bus Well, this is Friday, and I am up at six, as I leave at 7:30 for the world's prettiest mountain trip, and that's a big word-world's prettiest-but that's a fact, well known by world travelers. The American Express gets this trip up-in a lovely bus with open top so that one gets a perfect view of the mountains. We do the cream of the Swiss Alps and return in nine and a half hours. There are only nine in the party, seven passengers, all Americans-two from Chicago, two from Detroit, two from Cambridge and myself, a picked chauffeur and a wonderfully witty guide who speaks six languages. He was born in these mountains, knows them all, has hiked over all the big ones, has been around the world, and was a captain in the Swiss army during the war for four years. Switzerland has compulsory service for all men in time of need. This guide told me he was in South America when he got the telegram to come home at once. He then said, "We Swiss love our country."

Now we are off! I am bewildered at the gorgeous scenery. The roads are good, but narrow and very dangerous at times. We made hundreds of hairpin turns, where, if our expert chauffeur miscalculated one and a half feet, we would all be plunged to our death-sometimes these drops are 2000 feet into a ravine. Here is one thing about Switzerland that you don't learn in school, but the world's military men know. 

At the start of the World War, Switzerland sent out an ultimatum to all the European nations to this effect, "The first nation to attempt to cross our border is the nation we shall war on and annihilate in our mountain passes." And our guide, the Swiss captain, told us how they could do this. Dozens of times he would say to me, in slightly broken English, “Look, up there, what do you see on the right-high up?”

Under the mountain is a tunnel." Then on the right he would show us disappearing guns. Branching off from our road were narrow passes going up in places as high as 

10,000 feet, all bearing the sign, "For Military Use Only."

Now they have enough munitions, cannon, etc. stored in hundreds of these mountains of solid granite that nothing could penetrate, not even an airplane attack, sufficient to completely annihilate any army that might attack Switzerland. As England has her one Gibraltar of solid stone, so Switzerland has at least two hundred which I saw with my own eyes, pointed out by this army officer. There are also dozens of tunnels through the bases of mountains, for carrying munitions and troops. One tunnel was pointed out to me which was eleven miles long. You see, I had no companion but the guide, and he said to me, "We Swiss love our country and must protect her." And you can rest assured they will.

I might add that this day's trip, including dinner, from 7:30 in the morning until 6 at night, costs $13 in American money. It may seem a lot, but last year it was $16, and remember that it is only once in a lifetime, and is well worth every cent of it-so if you're ever in Lucerne don't fail to take this trip, even if you must live on wind the rest of the week.
